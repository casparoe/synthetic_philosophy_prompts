I am a sixth-form student, and I have recently read Francis Fukuyama's essay The End of History? in The National Interest (1989). I find the central argument more plausible than I expected: that after the collapse of fascism and communism, liberal democracy has no serious ideological rival, and that the movement of history towards it may be exhausted. But the part of the essay that has stayed with me is not the famous prediction. It is the passage where Fukuyama says that the end of history will be a sad time. As I recall it, he argues that the struggle for recognition, the willingness to risk one's life for abstract goals, and the daring and idealism of ideological conflict will be replaced by economic calculation, the solving of technical problems, environmental concerns, and the satisfaction of consumer demands. He suggests that there will be neither art nor philosophy, only the perpetual caretaking of the museum of human history.

I want you to treat my acceptance of the whole thesis—both the empirical claim about liberal democracy's lack of serious competitors and the normative claim that a post-historical world would be melancholy or diminished—as a position, and tell me what would count against it. 1. Which observable developments since 1989 should most trouble a defender of the thesis?
