I was in a historical methods course last week where the instructor put two sources about a colonial-era famine in front of us. One was a district officer's report: tables of dead, dated entries, grain prices, a death count. The other was a cycle of songs about the famine collected from herders decades later — songs that name villages the report doesn't mention and give a mortality figure something like ten times higher. What bothered me wasn't the disagreement but how the room digested it. Everyone agreed both sources were partial and interested; no one was naive about either. Still, when we got to the question of what could anchor a historical account of the famine, the report was treated as evidence — biased evidence, but the kind you correct, triangulate, and build around — whereas the songs were treated as evidence of how the famine was remembered, not of the famine itself. The songs needed the archive to corroborate them before they could say anything about what happened. The archive needed no such corroboration from the songs.

I can't tell wether there's a good epistemic principle under that or just a reflex in favor of documents. I can generate candidate justifications: the report is contemporaneous and fixed, the songs shift with each performance; the officer answered to some chain of superiors, however weakly, while the singers answered to no one; the report is one authored text with a date, while the songs have no stable provenance. But each of those could be turned around in a case we can imagine: a contemporaneous fixed report written by a party with every incentive to minimize deaths, versus a carefully transmitted song tradition whose whole social function is to keep the dead named. So here's what I want. Give me the strongest principle you can build that justifies treating the officer's report and the song cycle differently — one that does not boil down to documents are documents and therefore better — and then stress-test it against at least one case of your own where an oral source passed down over generations ought to beat a written record. Tell me how confident you are in the principle and which point in it you are least sure of.
