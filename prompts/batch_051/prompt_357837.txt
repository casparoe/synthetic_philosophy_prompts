I've been arguing with a friend about whether natural selection says anything substantive or is just a dressed-up tautology. The old worry is that if you define fitness as actual reproductive success, then saying the fit survive just means saying the ones who leave more offspring leave more offspring. I know the propensity interpretation is supposed to fix this by treating fitness as a probabilistic disposition an organism has before the outcomes are in — like a biased coin — so that selection claims become predictive and falsifiable. But I don't see how you'd tell whether that's a real fix or just relabling the bookkeeping.

Here is the task. Suppose you have a study organism where you can track individuals across generations and measure components like viability, fecundity, and mating success directly. Design a concrete measurement scheme or experiment to test the claim that propensity fitness is a real, causally relevant property of individuals — one that can be assessed independently of actual reproductive success — rather than a post hoc summary of who happened to survive. Say exactly what result would favor the propensity reading and what result would favor the skeptic's reading (fitness is just bookkeeping and the tautology objection stands). Then tell me what even a favorable result would and would not settle. In particular: if independently measured propensities predicted realized reproductive success well, would that show the propensity notion is doing explanatory work, or could the skeptic still say you just built the tautology into your choice of components? And how should drift enter the analysis, since a probabilistic disposition can be perfectly real yet fail to match outcomes in any particular population?
