You are a professional philosopher of religion with expertise in philosophical theology, the problem of natural evil, and the science-and-religion literature. I am drafting a one-page statement of my own position for a theology seminar, and I need a rigorous consistency audit rather than a sympathetic restatement. Please think carefully, and before settling on your final list, brainstorm all the ways the commitments below could conflict; then keep only the genuine ones and rank them. Here is the set:

(a) God is perfectly good, and creation as it came from God was very good.
(b) The biological world really has included predation, parasitism, disease, and animal death for hundreds of millions of years before human beings appeared.
(c) The Fall was a real historical event involving the first human beings, and human death — physical as well as spiritual — is a consequence of sin.
(d) The Fall had no retroactive effect on pre-human history: the suffering and death of animals across evolutionary time was not caused by human sin, but was simply part of the created order before sin entered the world.
(e) Animal suffering is intrinsically bad — it is bad for the creature that undergoes it — and a perfectly good God would not permit intrinsic badness of that kind unless some outweighing good required it.
(f) The whole evolutionary process, animal suffering included, was God's chosen means of realizing a very good creation; pre-human animal suffering was therefore part of the original very-good order, not a departure from it.
(g) The peaceable-kingdom imagery — the wolf dwelling with the lamb and no creature doing harm — depicts the redeemed creation that is coming, not a lost prehistoric Eden.
(h) The redeemed creation will be the fulfillment of what God originally intended for creation — not a second plan that abandons the first, but the same creation brought to its intended goal.

A friend has told me that (c) plainly contradicts (b), but my instinct is that this objection is too quick, since (c) is about human death and (d) explicitly denies any retroactive effect. I would like to know whether that instinct is right, but my real worry is that the overall position may come apart somewhere else and I cannot see where. Please identify which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension and which only seem to be; order the genuine tensions from most to least severe; and for each, trace exactly which commitments generate it, say what would have to give, and what the revision would cost. If some apparent tension dissolves on inspection, say explicitly why it dissolves rather than simply flagging it.
