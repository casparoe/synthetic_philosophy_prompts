An AI lab I follow has circulated a compromise between outcome-based and process-based supervision for multi-step reasoning models, under the working title *process escrow*, and I would like a mechanism-level analysis of it rather than a verdict. An agent is trained on tasks whose final answers are graded as a binary success O ∈ {0,1}. During a rollout, a separate, frozen process critic reads the transcript up to each step t and emits a score c_t ∈ [0,1], intended as its estimate of the probability that the episode will eventually succeed. The agent receives no reward until the episode closes; the return is then computed as G = O · Σ_t γ^t (1 + β c_t). A failed episode is therefore worth zero, while a successful episode is worth the usual discounted outcome sum scaled by how promising the critic judged its prefixes, with β small initially but scheduled to grow. The critic is trained purely to predict final success from prefixes of rollouts produced by the current agent, and is audited every n rounds on a held-out suite; if its Brier score or calibration error crosses a threshold, it is refitted from scratch on outcomes alone. Human process critiques are collected on a random 5% of steps and enter the critic's loss only as a soft regulariser, never as a direct reward, on the stated principle that humans stay out of the reward loop. The lab's internal note claims that the scheme cannot be reward-hacked because process only ever multiplies outcome, and the critic is scored for forecasting outcomes rather than for approving actions, leaving nothing for the agent to seduce.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral position. I would like you to work through what it actually incentivises in the agent, in the critic, in the task authors, and in the human annotators who know their critiques shape the regulariser. Where is it gamed first, and by whom? What does the process term change about the learned policy compared with plain outcome supervision, and under what conditions does the whole scheme quietly reduce to outcome supervision with extra steps? Which of the lab's stated guarantees fails first — the multiplication by O, the forecasting-only critic, or the humans-out-of-the-loop principle — and what would the first salient failure mode of a trained model look like in practice? Finally, which failure modes could be patched without abandoning the core of the scheme, and which can be fixed only by reintroducing genuine process information, at which point the scheme is no longer doing what was advertised? Please answer in British English.
