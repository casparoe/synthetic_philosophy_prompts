I'm a software engineer, and I'm making a decision tree for the betting/quant meetup I go to, to sort people by how they actually size a bet when they have an edge and expect to see similar spots over and over. People keep talking past each other because one guy means maximize expected value and another means don't go broke, and the disagreement only shows when you hand them the same case and they pick different stake sizes. So I want the tree built bottom-up from cases and answer combinations first, not from a list of labels; each branch node should be the concrete question or case, not the position name. The distinctions I care about are whether you're maximizing the average or the long-run growth rate, how much variance you'll take for a given edge, and whether non-money stuff like fun or avoiding regret should enter. Use concrete cases like these, phrased so someone can answer without already knowing which leaf they're walking into: (a) a coin that comes up heads 60% and pays even money, and you can bet it many times; (b) a one-shot all-or-nothing bet where you could put up $80k of a $100k bankroll on a fair coin that pays 2:1; (c) a venture-style bet that pays 40:1 and you think hits 8% of the time; (d) a negative-expected-value bet that's genuinely fun, like $50 on your favorite team at bad odds. Include both repeated and one-shot cases to see who applies their rule to both. Each leaf should be a named position, aiming for around six to eight leaves, and you should cover at least: the naive expected-value maximizer, full Kelly, fractional Kelly, someone who knowingly bets more than Kelly, and expected-utility maximizer with a risk-aversion parameter (which is really a family, so say that). Give each leaf a sentence or two on its most awkward commitment. Also mark any combination of answers that has no defender, and say whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And be straight about where the tree can't seperate having log utility from maximizing the long-run growth rate, since near as I can tell those give the same stake sizes in practice. The audience is smart engineers, not philosophers, so keep the prose short and write in American English.
