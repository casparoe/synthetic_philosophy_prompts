I'm in a reading group on hell, and I built the following argument for our meeting, but now I'm worried it proves too much and I want to know where it breaks.

(1) A punishment is just only if it is proportional to the offense.
(2) Human offenses are finite: any wrong is committed by a finite agent over a finite stretch of time, and its worldly consequences harm finite beings to a finite degree.
(3) The severity of a punishment is a matter of how much harm it inflicts on the person punished.
(4) Eternal conscious torment inflicts unlimited harm, because the suffering extends through endless time and has no finite total.
(5) An unlimited harm can't be proportional to a finite offense.
(6) So no human offense ever merits eternal conscious torment.
(7) A perfectly just God wouldn't impose a punishment that no one ever merits.
(8) Therefore no one goes to eternal torment; the traditional doctrine of hell is false.

The part that bothers me isn't really (8) itself—I can live with losing hell. It's that the same moves seems to take heaven with it: no finite life merits unlimited bliss either. And annhilationism may not survive either, since if what's bad about death is losing all the goods your future would have contained, then annihilating a person with a potentially endless future looks like an unlimited loss too. So the argument appears to leave only universal salvation standing, and it does so on the basis of a four-line proportionality slogan. I don't trust a slogan to settle all of eschatology, so I suspect a step is bad or the reasoning is invalid. But each premise looked fine to me in isolation.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? If it's valid, which premise is least costly to give up, and what do I pay for giving it up? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep the proportionality idea for ordinary criminal justice than keep either hell or heaven, but I'd like to know whether that's actually the trade I'm making.
