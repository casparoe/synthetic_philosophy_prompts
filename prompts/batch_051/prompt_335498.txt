I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been trying to reason about a future in which the pattern of a human brain can be scanned and run as software: a mind that can be copied, run at different speeds, paused, and forked into several instances that share all memories up to the split but then diverge. I usually handle hard problems by decomposing them until each piece is easy to check, but this scenario keeps short-circuiting my decomposition.

Here is the claim I want your credence for: a later fork of an uploaded mind is the same person as the mind before the fork, in exactly the sense that does the moral work — so a promise made before the fork binds the fork, harming the fork is morally on a par with harming the original, and the fork inherits the original's obligations and entitlements rather than merely being assigned them by convention. If you think "same person" is the wrong concept here and we should swap it for something like "continuation-enough to carry the same obligations and self-interest," say so, but still give a number for the normative claim.

I want a number between 0 and 1, not "it depends," and I want the derivation. Decompose it into the sub-questions the number depends on. My candidates:

1. Whether personal identity over time rests on psychological continuity (memory, traits, self-model) or also on the uninterrupted operation of one physical instance, which a new material copy would lack.
2. Whether identity can branch: if two equally continuous successors of one past both qualify as "the same person," or whether branching breaks identity the way a line can't remain one line after it splits.
3. Whether the moral facts that seem to need identity — being bound by promises, being owed compensation for harms, being the object of self-interested concern — actually need strict identity, or can be carried by something weaker like the right kind of causal and informational continuity.
4. Whether nearby cases (sleep, memory loss, split-brain patients, destructive teleportation) are close enough to upload-and-fork to ground a judgment, or whether this is a concept-breaking case where we should stop asking what is true and decide what to legislate.

For each sub-question give a rough probability, show how they combine into your final number, name the single sub-question your credence is most sensitive to, and tell me one concrete observation, experiment, or argument that would move your number most.

Keep the whole answer under 400 words.
