I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy and there's an asymmetry I keep fumbling to justify. In ordinary policy debate we accept real losses for some people when the total gains are big enough: a bypass takes someone's house, a vaccine mandate imposes a risk on millions, a tax funds a hospital somewhere else. Nobody thinks "but these particular people are harmed" is by itself a decisive objection. But when the many diffuse beneficiaries are replaced by one person—a Nozick-style utility monster who gets, say, a hundred times more welfare from each unit of resource than anyone else does—the same aggregative reasoning suddenly sounds like a reductio, and everyone starts talking about exploitation and rights. The welfare math doesn't care whether a fixed total is split across many people or concentrated in one, so it can't be the quantity of good alone. Is the asymmetry defensible, or is the horror at the monster just a heuristic? If you defend it, give me a principle that licenses the bypass but rules out feeding the monster, and test it on the case where the monster's utility profile comes from a freely chosen pill rather than anything blameworthy in their character. If you don't think it's defensible, tell me which side you'd give up.
