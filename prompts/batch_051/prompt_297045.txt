In the course of reporting a feature on the preservation of obsolete software, I put the same hypothetical case to two people I was interviewing, and their answers opened into a disagreement I have not been able to referee. The case concerns a mid-1990s word processor called Cogwheel. Its publisher went bankrupt in 1997, and a preservationist has since restored the program and now hosts a working copy in a noncommercial online archive, with full provenance notes and a public promise to remove the material if any rights holder ever emerges. Decades of searching have identified no rights holder.

The first source, Mara, runs a community software archive and defended the preservationist. The second, Julian, works in rights clearance for publishers and called the preservationist's conduct straightforward infringement. Their exchange went roughly as follows.

Mara argued that Cogwheel is an orphan work in the most practical sense: there is no one to ask, no one selling or licensing it, and the physical media and file formats are degrading. Waiting for an owner who may never appear means accepting the permanent loss of a small piece of computing history. In those conditions, she said, a noncommercial archive that preserves attribution and promises to remove the work on request does no harm and supplies a genuine public good.

Julian replied that this smuggles in an assumption: unknown is not nonexistent. Bankruptcy does not vaporize a copyright; it transfers the company's assets, including its intellectual property, to creditors, successors, or some other entity, even when no one has troubled to identify them. An orphan work, he said, is a work whose owner cannot be located, not a work that has no owner. The enthusiast label "abandonware" obscures the difference: abandonment in any legally serious sense would require some affirmative act by the rightsholder, and years of silence are not that. Hosting the program without a license is still taking what belongs to someone else, and the noncommercial nature of the archive changes the scale of the problem rather than its character.

Mara responded that a property right that has not been exercised, enforced, or made traceable for decades is not functioning as a right for anyone. She added that copying is non-rivalrous: the archive's copy does not deprive any hypothetical owner of the original, and no revenue is being diverted because there is no market. She granted that an owner might object to circulation in principle, but asked what weight should be given to an objection that no actual person has ever voiced.

Julian answered that copyright is not use-it-or-lose-it, and that the owner's interest is not reducible to lost sales. Part of what it means to hold the right is to decide whether and how the work becomes available, including the decision to keep it unavailable; the preservationist is making that decision on the owner's behalf. If a legitimate rights holder surfaced tomorrow, he said, they would find the decision had already been made for them, by a stranger with no better claim than a trespasser who repairs an empty house and opens it to the public.

Mara rejected the empty-house model, saying copyright governs copying rather than possession of a single object, and the cost of her preferred course is not that an owner loses a publication decision but that the decision is made earlier than the owner would like—if the owner ever appears. Meanwhile the cost of the cautious path is concrete and cumulative: the last usable copies vanish. She added that the law itself has long recognized orphan works as a policy problem, precisely because the ordinary rule produces deadlock, with the public losing access and no rights holder benefiting from the loss.

Julian granted the deadlock but said it cut the other way. The remedy for a bad rule is to change it, through an orphan works regime with a genuine diligent-search standard, a public registry, and a cap on liability, not to act as though the existing rule had already been repealed. Otherwise, he said, sincere preservationists and unscrupulous exploiters will both plead that they could not find the owner, and no stable way will remain to tell them apart. The promise to take the work down if an owner appears is, to him, the bootstrapping move at issue: the infringement occurs now, and the owner is left to respond to a fait accompli.

Mara's final point was that society already judges unauthorized use by surrounding circumstances—quotation for criticism, private copying, fan activity—so a flat obey-the-rule-first principle would condemn much of what Julian himself likely accepts. The choice, she said, is never simply between respecting rights and respecting access; it is between competing ways of weighing rights, access, and the preservation of the historical record. Julian replied that this invites the very arbitrariness he had warned against: once context replaces consent, the person deciding whether the archive is responsible is simply the person running it.

I would like the exchange adjudicated move by move. Please say which of each speaker's claims are sound and which are overstated or elide something important; identify the concepts they are using loosely—abandonment, harm, non-rivalry, the difference between changing the law and disobeying it—and say what, if anything, both parties are missing. I am less interested in what a particular country's statute happens to require than in whether the arguments themselves succeed, fail, or are valid but insufficient. On the arguments as actually made, which side is in the stronger overall position?
