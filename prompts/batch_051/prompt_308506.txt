so i have no philosophy background (i do backend infrastructure, mostly), but i've been reading augustine's confessions, book XI, because somebody told me it was the first serious western text about time and i wanted to see how far back the bugs went. i've come away with a view that i find weirdly compelling and also can't bring myself to believe. the view, put crudely: the past and the future don't exist. there is only now. the past exists only as memory, a present state of a mind, and the future exists only as expectation, also a present state of a mind. here's the passage that hooked me, augustine, confessions, book XI, chapter 20:

"But what now is manifest and clear is, that neither are there future nor past things. Nor is it fitly said, 'There are three times, past, present and future;' but perchance it might be fitly said, 'There are three times; a present of things past, a present of things present, and a present of things future.' For these three do somehow exist in the soul, and otherwise I see them not: present of things past, memory; present of things present, sight; present of things future, expectation."

i find this compelling because it seems to dissolve, in one move, a bunch of questions that otherwise feel like busywork. how do you measure a stretch of time that no longer is? you don't; you measure a trace that exists now. why does "now" follow you around like a cursor in an editor? because now is the only position that exists; the cursor is always at the live line, and every other line is either a log entry or a prediction. why can't you point at next thursday the way you point at the rack down the hall? because next thursday isn't an object, it's a pattern in present expectations. it even matches how a self-aware program would experience its own run: a program counter pointed at one instruction, memory holding a past that no longer executes, branch prediction half-committing to a future that hasn't been fetched.

but the intuition i can't shake: "there were dinosaurs" is true in a completely different way than "there are unicorns." the first is made true by how things actually were; the second is made true by how things aren't. if the past doesn't exist, then "there were dinosaurs" has to be made true by something present — fossils, records, memories, maybe god's present states — and then the cretaceous turns out to be a story the present tells about itself. that seems obviously wrong to me. it gets worse when the present disagrees with itself: if a fossil and a sloppy paleontologist's memory imply different dinosaurs, one of them is wrong, and wrong about what, if the past isn't around to be wrong about? same with fixing a regression: when git bisect tells me a fixed commit introduced the bug, i take myself to be finding out what the code actually was, not reconstructing a self-consistent story from present artifacts. the past doesn't feel like a model i maintain; it feels like the thing the model is about.

so the actual question. present both sides at full strength, no strawmen. for the theory: what's the best real argument that the past and future don't exist apart from present acts of mind, and what does it say about the "whose memory?" problem — is it god, the block universe seen from inside, or something less weird than either? for the intuition: what is it actually tracking if it's not just a psychological habit, and why can't the theory explain it away as an artifact of how we plan and navigate? then tell me which should give way, what would have to be true for the other one to win instead, and — the thing i most suspect — wether there's a reading of the passage on which both survive because augustine is talking about how time is measured, not about whether the past and future are real.
