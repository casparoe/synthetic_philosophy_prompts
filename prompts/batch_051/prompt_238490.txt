I'm working on a novel where a crew of nine on a small ship picks up a faint signal that might be a distress call or a lure. Each crew member has a private read on it, and each is right a bit more often then chance, but they are not all equally cautious. I keep reaching for two ideas that seem to point in opposite directions and I want to know if they really conflict.

The first is the Condorcet jury theorem: if everyone votes independently and each is more likely right than wrong, the majority of a large group is much more reliable than any single member. The second is the unilateralist's curse: if any one of them can act on their own judgment—say, anyone can launch the rescue boat, open the airlock, or broadcast the ship's position—then the group no longer acts on the average or the majority. It acts on whoever has the most optimistic or least cautious estimate, and adding more people makes it more likely someone crosses the danger threshold, not safer.

Are these two results genuinely in tension, or are they just about different decision rules? I want more than the answer that one is voting and the other is unilateral action. What exactly changes when you move from one setup to the other, and is there a unified way to say when private information makes a group wise and when it makes the group reckless? Give me a distinction I can actually use in the plot, and flag the places where you're hand-waving. If I've got either idea wrong, tell me so and correct me instead of humoring the novel.
