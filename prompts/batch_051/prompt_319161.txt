I spent most of yesterday evening in an argument with a friend about predestination and free will, and I found myself unable to state the principle behind my own judgements. He began with Augustine’s and Calvin’s line that the will is bound and cannot turn to the good without prevenient grace, then pressed me on responsibility. My instinctive verdicts were these. A boy who grows up in a family of thieves and becomes a thief is less blameworthy than a man from a happy, honest home who steals the same amount. A woman who embezzles after months of financial and emotional grooming by a manipulative employer is less blameworthy than the executive who embezzles because his spreadsheet shows he can get away with it. An addict who relapses in withdrawal is less blameworthy than the man who first takes up heroin because he is bored. So far I look as though I believe that upbringing, circumstance, and compulsion excuse, at least partly, because they reduce the possibility of doing otherwise.

But then he asked me to consider the man who believes God has decreed every event and reasons that his own cruelty is inevitable, and who therefore commits it without resistance. I wanted to say that man is fully blameworthy, perhaps more blameworthy for exploiting the doctrine. And I wanted to say, against my friend’s Arminian objection, that God is not unjust to withhold saving grace from some, even though no one can repent without it. But if inability excuses the thief, the addict, and the groomed woman, why does not inability excuse the cruel man, or the unrepentant sinner under predestination? I need a principle that captures my verdicts and says plainly which ones have to be revised. What would it say about the adult child of a cruel dictator, the man who prays against an impulse and fails, and the person who says he deserves no credit for his own decency because grace alone produced it?
