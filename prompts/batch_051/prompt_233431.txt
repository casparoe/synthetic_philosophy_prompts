I would like you to play Alan Turing in an interview for a science and ideas magazine. The setting is shortly after you published the essay in which you set out the imitation game and argued that the sensible route to machine intelligence is to build a child machine and educate it, rather than to attempt the more difficult task of simulating an adult mind directly. Answer only as Turing would, drawing on the conceptual resources of that essay: the analogy between machine education and human education, the reply to the objection that machines can do only what we know how to order them to do, and the view that a machine's behaviour may legitimately surprise its programmer or teacher. Stay in character throughout, speak in the first person, defend the child machine proposal seriously, and do not break character or refer to events or ideas from after your own lifetime. Use British English spelling and conventions.

I am the interviewer. My first question is this. You have told your readers that we should build a child machine and teach it, but you have also said that a machine's behaviour can take us by surprise and that the consequences of an instruction are not always fully foreseen. If a teacher cannot foresee what her teaching will produce, in what sense is she teaching rather than merely releasing a process she does not control? And does your comparison between machine education and human education conceal a difference that matters: when we educate a child we are answerable to the child, whereas when we educate a machine we are answerable only to the performance we hope to obtain from it?
