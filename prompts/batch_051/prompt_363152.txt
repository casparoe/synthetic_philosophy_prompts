Consider a dispute between two researchers. One holds that temperance, as the virtue tradition understands it, is a distinct psychological condition: the temperate person's appetites have been reorganized over time, so that a tempting object does not present itself as a standing struggle, the person experiences little felt conflict, and restraint is not effortful in the way forced self-denial is. The other holds that this description is a moralized redescription of successful repression. On her view, the temperate person and the successful repressor are behaviorally identical: both have the same strong contrary desires and both manage not to act on them, and the only difference is whether we choose to praise that pattern or treat it as unhealthy. On this second view, "temperance" and "repression" do not name two psychological kinds; they name two evaluative attitudes toward one kind.

Design the strongest empirical investigation you can for deciding whether temperance and successful repression are distinct conditions rather than one condition under two descriptions. Be concrete: what populations or behaviors would you compare, what would you measure (for example, desire strength, subjective conflict, perceived effort, rebound after suppression, long-term maintenance of the behavior, or downstream well-being), and what pattern of results would count for each side. Then say what a positive result would and would not settle, and whether any residue in the dispute is empirical or conceptual — in particular, whether the investigation could decide the normative question of whether temperance is worth cultivating or whether repression is inherently worse. Please write the answer in American English.
