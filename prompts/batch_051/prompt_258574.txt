I'm a longtime reader of LessWrong and nearby rationalist blogs, and I'm applying for a six-month research fellowship. I'd like you to turn a half-formed idea into a short research proposal of one to two pages. Don't bother with citations; I'll add them myself.

The area is what I'll call ontology identification. An AI system trained on observations ends up with internal variables, latents, or what people loosely call concepts. Ontology identification asks which ontology those internal variables correspond to: whether the system has carved the world at its joints or is reasoning in a gerrymandered basis, and how a researcher could ever tell. The standard way this gets discussed assumes a God's-eye view. We know the true hidden structure of the environment, often because we built the simulation; we probe the trained model; and we ask how well its latents line up with that ground truth. But a real embedded agent, and the interpretability researcher studying it, never has that view. The world's true latents are inaccessible. You get your own observations, the agent's behavior, and perhaps recorded activations, none of which arrive labeled with the correct ontology. Worse, even unlimited data often doesn't pin down a unique set of latents: latent-variable models are typically identifiable only up to transformations of the hidden variables, so the same observations are compatible with many different underlying ontologies.

My half-formed idea is that this changes the problem in kind rather than degree. If the ground-truth ontology is inaccessible even to the researcher and underdetermined even by the data, then "does the model's ontology match reality?" is not a question anyone is positioned to answer directly, and a lot of work on ontology identification may be smuggling in the omniscience it claims to be doing without. I want the proposal built around a version of the question that avoids that smuggling: can we state success criteria for ontology identification using only information available from inside the system, and if we cannot, what is the honest replacement? Should we ask instead about coherence, usefulness, compression, controllability, or something else?

Write the proposal with these parts: (1) a precise statement of that question, operationalized enough that we could tell what would count as an answer; (2) an account of why existing approaches, such as full-observability latent matching and interpretability benchmarks with hand-labeled features, leave it open rather than settling it; (3) a concrete six-month method, month by month, likely combining toy models where I know the ground truth but restrict my own access to it so as to simulate embeddedness, information-theoretic arguments about what can be identified from observations of observables alone, and small interpretability case studies, and say why this restricted-access method is the right one rather than the obvious alternative of building ever larger feature-labeled benchmarks under full observability; and (4) a realistic scope note covering what a positive result would look like, what would count as partial success, and what would count as the project failing.

Please also say how confident you are in the claims the proposal leans on, and single out the one point you are least sure of. I would rather have the proposal flag its own weakest joint than be uniformly confident.
