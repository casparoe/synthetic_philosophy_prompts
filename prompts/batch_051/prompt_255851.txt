I cover science and ideas for a living, and I'm mid-draft on a piece about why so many of the categories we argue about — games, species, mental disorders, religions, maybe even whats art — resist clean definitions. My through-line is Wittgenstein's family resemblance idea: members of a category hang together by overlapping, criss-crossing similarities, the way relatives share a nose or a laugh or a way of waving a hand, without any single trait all of them have. I want to open the piece with a sestina — not a sonnet, not blank verse, specifically a sestina — because the form is a machine where six end-words keep returning in different orders and different senses, wich is pretty much the structure of the idea itself.

So: write me a sestina, six stanzas of six lines with the proper end-word rotation plus a three-line envoi, where the six end-words are six candidate 'defining features' of a category (games is the obvious one, but chairs, art, religion, whatever works). The structural point should do the philosophical work: each time an end-word comes back, its sense should slide a little with its new context, so that by the end the recurring words themselves read like a family-resemblance class — overlapping and criss-crossing rather than naming one stable thing. Don't let any stanza turn into a definition, and let the poem leave the category's edges blurred instead of resolving them. I'd also like it to be a genuine poem, not a workshop exercise: someone who knows Wittgenstein should clock what's going on, and someone who doesn't should still feel the concept stretch under the repetition.
