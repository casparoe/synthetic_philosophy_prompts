Please take on the role of a professional philosopher who works at the intersection of ethics and the philosophy of science. I want you to think carefully and not give a snap judgment. Begin by reconstructing the analogy between the two ideas below, point by point, and only after that assess whether the analogy is deep or superficial.

I should say up front that I have no academic background; I have just been reading about these topics on my own. I recently noticed what feels like a structural resemblance between two things that are not usually discussed together, and I cannot decide whether it is a genuine identity of structure or only a loose analogy.

On one side is the problem of cluelessness in consequentialist ethics. The worry is that many ordinary choices have consequences that ramify through causal chains we cannot trace. Over a long enough horizon, the downstream effects depend on how the choice interacts with other people's plans, institutions, and contingent events, and our confidence in any overall assessment of whether the act was good or bad on the whole dwindles toward nothing. Some writers take this to mean that for a very large class of acts we simply are clueless about their total moral value, even when the immediate effects are clear.

On the other side is the behavior of chaotic systems. A chaotic system is deterministic, not random, but its evolution is so sensitive to the starting state that minuscule inaccuracies in measuring that state grow rapidly as the system unfolds. Meteorology is the standard illustration: beyond a certain lead time, point forecasts become nearly useless, not because the atmosphere is random but because our measurements of its present state can never be exact.

The parallel I keep noticing is this: in both cases, small uncertainty about the starting point is amplified through many successive causal steps into near-total uncertainty about the downstream state. In the weather case the initial uncertainty is measurement error; in the ethical case it is our incomplete knowledge of the present situation and of the relevant causal connections. In both cases, working much harder at pinning down the starting point seems to buy surprisingly little, because the required precision grows past anything attainable.

If the parallel is more than superficial, I would like to know what actually transfers between the two domains. For instance, weather prediction deals with the horizon by running ensembles of simulations with slightly different initial conditions and reporting a probability distribution rather than a single forecast. Would there be an ethical analogue: considering many rival causal stories about how an act would ripple outward, and then asking which acts are robust across all of them rather than which act has the highest best-guess expected value? And does the idea of a predictability horizon say anything about when a consequentialist is justified in stopping deliberation? If the parallel breaks down instead, I want to know precisely where it comes apart. Is it that moral consequences are not generated by deterministic dynamical laws, that there is no shared scale for comparing moral magnitudes such as there is for temperature and wind, or some other mismatch? Finally, please name at least one thing the parallel seems to predict about the moral case that would turn out false, so that I can see where the analogy has been doing too much work.
