I attend a reading group on AI safety; last week two other members spent the session arguing about whether the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence are compatible. I wrote down their exchange afterward as best I could, and I'd like it adjudicated move by move.

They took the orthogonality thesis to be the claim that an agent's level of intelligence and its final goals vary independently — no final goal is more "natural" for a highly capable system — and instrumental convergence to be the claim that many different final goals lead capable agents toward the same instrumental subgoals, such as self-preservation and acquiring resources.

(1) Dan said the two theses cannot both be accepted at full strength. If intelligence gives no push toward any particular final goal, why would high capability push almost every final goal toward the same instruments? A drive that predictably shows up across nearly all final goals just is a value, whatever the theory calls it. So instrumental convergence smuggles in universal terminal values, which is exactly what orthogonality denies.

(2) Elena replied that "instrumental" is doing the work. A proof-maximizer does not value compute for its own sake; it values proofs, and it would drop the drive for compute the moment proofs could be produced without it. Orthogonality was never a claim about which policies a final goal selects in a given world. Convergence is a fact about how diverse goals interact with the same environments, not a discovery of hidden shared values.

(3) Dan answered that this makes the interesting part of convergence disappear. If the convergent subgoals are just whatever the environment makes useful, then in a world where paperclips could be made only by cultivating daisies, a paperclip-maximizer would converge on daisies. The familiar list — resources, survival, not being shut off — is then only a list of features of our world, not a claim about agents. Orthogonality survives only because convergence has been weakened to the point of triviality.

(4) Elena said it has not been weakened to triviality; it has been given its proper scope, which is still useful. The claim that many terminal goals lead to the same subgoals across the environments we actually deploy agents in is an empirical warning about how hard it is to make a final goal safe. And Dan's deeper move — from "predictably pursued in nearly all circumstances" to "therefore a terminal value" — is a non sequitur: a prediction about behavior and a claim about what is valued for its own sake are different descriptions.

Neither moved. Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: for each move, say what it gets right and wrong, rate it from "clearly mistaken" to "clearly right", then say who is more right overall and what both are missing.
