Since I retired from experimental physics, I have been reading my way through the philosophy of science, and a parallel has begun to trouble me in the way good puzzles do. Many years ago I was fond of Bongard problems, those small puzzles in which six simple line drawings appear on the left and six on the right, and the task is to state the single rule that separates the two groups and would also sort further, unseen drawings. The rule might be that all left-hand figures are closed curves while all right-hand figures are open ones, or that exactly the left-hand figures contain one small shape. For any set of twelve such drawings, however, there are indefinitely many rules that do the separating: the clean rule, but also disjunctive gerrymanders that simply list the twelve drawings one by one.

This is the same shape, to my eye, as the underdetermination of a generalization by its finite instances, and in particular the situation in Goodman's grue puzzle, where the evidence supports the plain inductive generalization and a gerrymandered competitor equally well and something further must break the tie. In a Bongard problem the tie is broken by the solver's preference for compact, natural, projectible rules; in scientific induction it seems to be broken by a preference for simplicity or natural kinds. So the parallel I cannot decide about is this: Bongard concept-learning looks like scientific induction in miniature, with the intended rule playing the role of the true hypothesis, the twelve exemplars playing the role of the data, and the solver's bias toward clean rules playing the role of Ockham's razor or a prior over hypotheses.

Is that a genuine structural identity, or am I only pattern-matching on the word induction? The obvious disanalogy is that a Bongard problem has an author whose intended rule is the target by stipulation, whereas nature, so far as physics lets me see, has no comparable intention of its own. If that disanalogy defeats the parallel, what exactly does authorial intent contribute that an account in terms of simplicity would be smuggling in? If it does not defeat the parallel, then the comparison seems to cut the other way too: it suggests the scientist's simplicity preference, like the solver's, may track shared human cognitive bias rather than anything like truth. Which horn should I take, and where precisely does the analogy hold and fail? Please keep your answer to at most 500 words.
