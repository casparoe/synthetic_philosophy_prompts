I’m a journalist working on a piece about the way early modern philosophers thought about conceivability as a guide to possibility, and I’ve been trying to work directly through a passage in Hume that keeps getting cited but rarely explained. I want to be sure I’m not projecting a later modal framework onto it.

The passage is from Hume’s *A Treatise of Human Nature*, Book I, Part I, Section VII, “Of abstract ideas”:

> ’Tis a principle generally receiv’d in philosophy, that every thing in nature is individual, and that ’tis utterly absurd to suppose a triangle really existent, which has no precise proportion of sides and angles. If this therefore be absurd in fact and reality, it must also be absurd in idea; since nothing of which we can form a clear and distinct idea is absurd and impossible. But to form the idea of an object, and to form an idea simply is the same thing; the reference of the idea to an object being an extraneous denomination, of which in itself it bears no mark or character. Now as ’tis impossible to form an idea of an object, that is possest of quantity and quality, and yet is possest of no precise degree of either; it follows, that there is an equal impossibility of forming an idea, that is not limited and confin’d in both these particulars. Abstract ideas are therefore in themselves individual, however they may become general in their representation.

I think I see how this supports Hume’s line that abstract ideas are particular images doing general work, but I’m puzzled about the modal principle packed into the middle of it. Is Hume here endorsing a general inference from clear and distinct conception to real possibility, or is he using a narrower claim about what counts as an idea at all? If the former, how does he deal with the obvious problem of ideas that are clear enough as images but impossible as objects—for example, a mountain without a valley, or two touching spheres? If the latter, does the broader conceivability thesis actually do any work in his argument about triangles? I’d like a reading that distinguishes the local argumentative role this principle plays from the more familiar Cartesian version. And since I may be reading Hume too charitably or too uncharitably, please indicate which step of your interpretation you are least confident in, and what alternative argument or textual consideration would most likely change your mind.
