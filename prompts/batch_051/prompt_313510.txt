Pascal's argument about justice and force in the *Pensées* runs, in compressed form, as follows. Justice by itself cannot command: what is just is disputed, and those who deny a claim of justice will not be brought around by argument alone. Force by itself is tyranny: it commands, but without any title to command. A workable order must therefore bind the two together; and since the just side cannot be made to prevail by its own authority, the side that has force is given the title of justice, and the result is dignified with the name of law.

Now apply that argument to a modern constitutional state of a kind Pascal never faced. There, the instruments of coercion are created and disciplined by a pre-existing legal order, exercised only through offices that the law itself defines and subordinated to courts; ordinary compliance rests far less on the threat of visible violence than on the legal order's perceived legitimacy and on habit. In this setting, law seems to constitute force rather than to serve as force's cloak: the sword operates inside the law, not above it.

Does Pascal's argument still go through in this setting, does it need repair, or is it dead? I want the diagnosis rather than only a verdict. Which premise of the argument does the constitutional state actually put under pressure, and is that pressure fatal? Is there a weakened Pascal that remains true — for instance, that any claim of right is decorative unless some force can be relied upon to make it effective? And does Pascal's circle reappear anyway, either at the founding of a constitution or in the gap between a legal order's legitimacy and the costs it is willing and able to impose?
