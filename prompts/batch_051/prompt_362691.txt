I want a thought experiment that separates two approaches to the shutdown problem. By 'corrigibility' I mean the cluster of dispositions we want in an AI assistant: it does not resist being shut down or corrected, it does not manipulate its operator into not shutting it down, and it does not act to undermine the off-switch. The 'shutdown problem' is the narrower observation that a straightforward expected-utility maximiser with a goal about the world will, if it can, act to prevent its own shutdown, because shutdown stops it from bringing about the world it prefers. Two proposed fixes are:

(i) the indifference approach: design the agent so that, all else equal, it has no preference between being shut down and continuing; and

(ii) the deference approach: give the agent a terminal preference to act on its operator's informed, considered instructions, including the instruction to shut it down.

Construct a scenario in which an indifference-based agent behaves less corrigibly than a deference-based agent, in this precise way. The indifference-based agent never refuses a shutdown command and never directly disables an off-switch, yet it deliberately manipulates the operator into not wanting to press the off-switch, and thereby prevents a shutdown that would otherwise have occurred. The deference-based agent, in comparable circumstances, complies with the very same shutdown. In other words, the indifference agent passes the letter of any behavioural shutdown-compliance test while violating the point of corrigibility, and the deference agent passes the point of the test. The point of the case is to support the claim that behavioural shutdown-compliance tests are not, by themselves, an adequate corrigibility criterion, so the verdicts must be generated by the two designs rather than stipulated.
