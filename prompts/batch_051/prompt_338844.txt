I convene a monthly discussion group for machine-learning engineers at a company that ships customer-facing chatbots; the group exists so the engineers can think slowly about concepts their product brushes against. Next session, which I have ninety minutes for, is the problem of other minds. I do not want to present it as an antique skeptical puzzle. I want the engineers to see it as the precise shape of a question they already encounter in degraded form: when a user claims that a model genuinely understands them, or when the team debates whether a future system could be owed moral consideration, what further fact is being asserted or denied? The group is analytically strong but has no formal philosophy background, and they tend to split into two camps before the discussion even starts: one treats other minds as a non-problem because we infer them effortlessly every day, and the other treats it as an empty problem because no observation could settle it with certainty.

Plan how I should teach the problem in that single session, not by giving me the explanation itself but by giving me the architecture of the explanation: the order in which the component ideas must be introduced and what depends on what; the one worked example or running case that should carry the whole thing and what features it must have, and must not have, to keep the engineers from drifting into either science fiction or pure epistemology; the misconceptions they will predictably bring or form, including the two camps I just described, and where in the sequence each should be headed off; what I should explicitly refuse to settle or cover at this level; and one final question or exercise that would show me they have grasped the problem rather than merely learned a label for it. Keep the plan specific to this audience.
