I am trying to make sense of a claim in the study of writing systems: that an alphabetic orthography is not merely a code for sounds but something like a phonological theory of the language it writes. Edward Sapir, in *Language* (1921), argued that written forms are a secondary symbolic layer whose elements refer not directly to meanings but to spoken sounds, and that the alphabet therefore reflects an implicit analysis of the speech stream. I want to know whether this supposed theoretical character of a script is a testable claim. Suppose an orthography represents some phonetically distinct realizations by one symbol, while it uses two symbols for a contrast that is no longer audible in ordinary speech. Or suppose a writing system preserves a morphophonemic spelling, such as the shared base in *sign* and *signal*, even though the vowel sound differs. What kind of cross-orthographic or historical evidence would count for the view that such choices encode a phonological analysis, and what evidence would count for the rival view that they are merely frozen conventions, etymological ornaments, or borrowings? Design the strongest test you can: say which writing-system phenomena you would examine, which variables you would control, what specific result would favor the phonological-theory interpretation and what result would favor the historical-convention interpretation. Then state precisely what the test would leave unresolved—for example, whether the theorist is the orthography itself, the scribal community, the user’s learning process, or the modern analyst’s reconstruction.
