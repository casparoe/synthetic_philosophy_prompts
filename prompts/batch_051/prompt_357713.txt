I'm a physics grad student but I've been reading formal epistemology on the side, and there's a parallel I keep coming back to that I can't decide is real or just vocabulary doing the work. In the foundations of statistical mechanics, the standard story about why entropy increases toward the future needs a special low-entropy initial condition (the Past Hypothesis). The dynamics and coarse-graining don't give it to you; its fed in by hand, and it looks arbitrary — there are vastly many ways the early universe could have been, and this one is special. In Bayesian epistemology, the prior seems to be doing nearly the same job: it's the starting distribution over hypotheses, it isn't generated by the update rule itself, conditionalization only tells you how to move once you already have it, and "where did that prior come from?" is one of the standard objections. Both look like "unearned initial conditions" that the formal machinery needs but can't justify from within.

I want to know whether this is a genuine structural parallel — something like the problem of initial conditions showing up in two places — or a superficial analogy. Please act as someone who works on foundations of physics and formal epistemology, and think through this carefully; maybe start by brainstorming the mapping before you stress-test it. Specifically: is the arbitrariness objection to the Past Hypothesis the same kind of objection as the arbitrariness objection to priors, or are the two "arbitrary" in different senses? Do the standard Bayesian replies — priors wash out as evidence accumulates, merging of opinions — have a real counterpart for the Past Hypothesis, or does the analogy predict a counterpart that turns out false? And what would have to be true of both sides for a result to actually transfer? I don't need a survey of the literature; I want to know exactly where the structures match and where they come apart.
