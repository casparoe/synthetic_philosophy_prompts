Okay here’s a thing I keep going back and forth on. A friend of mine who’s very into experimental philosophy insists the only real evidence in philosophy is intuitions about concrete cases — Gettier cases, trolleys, that kind of thing. Principles, he says, are just summaries; when a principle and a case conflict, you drop the principle. But I keep noticing that some of the principles we lean on hardest don’t obviously come from cases. Transitivity is one: people sometimes make intransitive choices in experiments (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A), yet philosophers mostly treat transitivity as a norm to impose on the data, not an empirical generalization a counterexample could shoot down. Simplicity feels even more like a commitment we bring to cases than an intuition we read off them.

So here’s what I want. Treat the friend’s claim as a testable hypothesis about method: that case-level intuitions are more trustworthy evidence than principle-level intuitions. Design the best study you can to test it. Be concrete about what you’d measure — the order in which people see cases vs. principles, framing effects, expert vs. lay agreement, how sticky each kind of judgment is over time, whatever you think is the strongest design — and say what each pattern of results would favor. Then be honest about what even the cleanest result could not settle: it would tell us about the psychology of intuition, not necessarily about which intuitions track truth. Is there any test that can cross that gap, or is that gap a reason to suspect the whole framework of treating intuitions as evidence at all?
