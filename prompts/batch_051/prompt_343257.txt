My school is trying this pilot in freshman physics and I have to be on the student committee for it, mostly because I kept interrupting the teacher's pitch with objections, so now I have to actually think it through. The rule: if you get a conceptual multiple-choice question wrong, you can file a theory defense within a week. In it you write up your wrong answer as if it were a real scientific theory—it needs a rule (like "objects stop unless you keep pushing them"), a prediction about a new case, and an explanation of why it got the original question wrong. Then your lab group builds one cheap demo designed to test it, and you get graded on the defense and the demo, not on whether the theory is true. If you finish the whole thing, your zero gets changed to "contested," which counts as partial credit.

The teacher keeps saying wrong answers aren't random, that students are running these little systematic theories about the world—"heavier things fall faster," "force is something an object carries around"—and the best way to deal with a false theory is to drag it into the open and try to break it scientifically. What I want is someone to actually work out how this plays out as a system, not the usual is-it-good-pedagogy speech. Who ends up filing defenses and about what? How do students game it—me personally, I would start tanking the easy questions on purpose if the defense route is an easier B, and apparently the teacher is already proposing a cap of two defenses per unit. Does it matter whether the kid actually beleives the misconception or just misread the question? And does having your wrong answer recorded as a "contested theory" make you dig in so the misconception sticks around longer instead of getting replaced? If you add rules to stop the gaming, which patches ruin the whole idea and which leave it basically intact?
