I'm a novelist. My next book is set in a near future where formal argumentation has become a televised sport: two-person teams, live scoring by judges, slow-motion fallacy reviews, training academies, corrupt endorsements, the works. The longer I worldbuilt, the more I accidentally talked myself into the actual thesis behind the world — that turning argumentation into a real sport, with all the scoring and spectatorship that implies, would make how ordinary people reason better, not worse. Sharper moves, public accountability for sloppy inferences, audiences who learn to spot a shaky inference because it gets flagged on screen. Ive started defending this at dinner parties and it sounds like I'm pitching a terrible novel, which, fair.

Before the book becomes a long advertisement for something I havent stress-tested, I want you to take the view seriously and hit it where its weak. The position is: competitive, scored, spectated argumentation would improve human reasoning on balance, rather than corrupt it. Tell me what would count against it. I don't just mean the obvious worry that it would select for rhetorical flash over soundness — push one level down. What specific facts about how people argue, about incentives, about what judges can and can't reliably score, or about what audiences actually learn from watching, would each be evidence against the view? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be specific about which empirical developments would matter — for example, if it turned out that live scoring mostly taught spectators to judge confidence rather than validity, would that be fatal or just embarrassing? A defender of the view should be able to say which of these they'd concede as genuinely counting against them, so mark that too. And be honest about whether the position has been rigged to be unfalsifiable — whether every bad outcome gets blamed on bad implementation — and whether that insulation should worry me more than the objections themselves.

For me personally, the whole thing stands or falls on one question: can trained judges, under time pressure and in front of an audience, reliably tell the difference between a move being sound and a move merely being impressive? Evidence that they can't would be the most persuasive thing against the view.

Finally, end by saying what would change your own mind about the verdict you reach — which argument or finding would flip your overall assessment, or where your reasoning here is most likely to fail. I'll use that last bit as a note from the novel's antagonist.
