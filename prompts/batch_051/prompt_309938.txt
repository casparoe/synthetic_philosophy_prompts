I'm taking an epistemology seminar and we spent half a session on Fitch's paradox of knowability. The professor went through it fast, and when I said I couldn't quite follow the last stretch, a friend send me a chapter from a popular book on paradoxes to help. The chapter gives roughly this summary. Start from the principle that every truth is knowable: if a claim is true, then it's at least possible for someone to know it. Now take a perfectly ordinary truth that happens not to be known, say the exact number of coins in someone's pocket. The conjunction saying that there are n coins in the pocket and nobody knows that there are n coins in the pocket is itself a truth, so by the principle it, too, should be knowable. But could anyone know that conjunction? To know it, they'd have to know both conjuncts, and the second conjunct says nobody knows it. So the moment someone knows the whole conjunction, the second conjunct turns out false. Knowing an unknown truth is therefore impossible. Run that for any candidate unknown truth and you get: there can't be any unknown truths. In other words, if every truth is knowable, then every truth is already known. But that's preposterous — of course there are truths nobody currently knows — so the knowability principle collapses into near-omniscience, and that's why Fitch's paradox is usually taken to have refuted the verificationist idea that truth just is what can be known.

Reading it back, something about the final step — from "every truth is known" to "the principle is refuted" — feels rushed to me, and the phrase "truth just is what can be known" sounds stronger than the principle the proof actually starts from.
