My friends and I were arguing about the Fermi paradox, and I realized I have a pretty stable policy for any question about how many minds or observers the universe contains. The policy is: never treat your own existence as evidence for a theory that predicts more observers. If theory A says the galaxy should be full of trillions of civilizations and theory B says we're alone, I don't give A extra credit just because, under A, there are way more people like me and so the odds of my particular existence might seem higher. I treat "I exist" as already given—a precondition for asking the question at all—not as a piece of data that could favor one side over the other.

My friend says this is incoherent. He says if you really conditionalize on everything you know, your own existence is a fact, and facts should shift your probabilities when one theory makes them more likely then the other. He calls my rule a bias toward empty, lonely universes and says it would make me systematically bet against theories that say minds are common, even when other evidence supports them. Is my rule defensible, or am I dressing up a small-world bias as epistemic modesty? If the rule is bad, is there a nearby version—something like "don't treat yourself as special"—that survives?
