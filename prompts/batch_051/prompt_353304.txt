i have a personal rule about disagreements and i honestly can't tell if it's defensible or just stubbornness wearing fancier clothes. the rule: i never move toward someone's view just because they're smart, well-read, and confident. before i'll treat them as an epistemic peer, i need a track record in the relevant domain—predictions made in advance, updates that turned out to track the evidence rather than their mood or vibe. once they clear that bar, i still don't split the difference all the way; i discount their credence by something proportional to how much i trust their process, then go re-derive things from the shared evidence myself. the concrete case is a guy in my reading group, clearly smarter than me, who disagrees with me about a bunch of longtermism and AI timeline stuff. his track record is mixed, some good calls and some loud misses he doesn't bring up anymore. he keeps saying he and i are both roughly rational and exposed to the same arguments, so one of us has to be wrong, and that i'm being immodest by not updating toward him. i refuse because "roughly rational" is exactly the thing under dispute, and i won't hand out peer status without a calibration history. but i can also see the failure mode: i might never hand anyone that status, at which point my rule becomes a machine for dismissing every disagreement forever. so: is the rule defensible as stated? what's the version that keeps me from getting bulldozed by articulate, confident, wrong people but doesn't just turn into full epistemic immunity—and how would i tell, from the inside, whether i'm running the fixed version or the stubborn one?
