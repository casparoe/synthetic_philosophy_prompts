Feels like every conversation I have with my friend Rhea turns into the same fight about consciousness, so last night we decided to stop trying to win and instead write an adversarial-collaboration document we could both actually sign. We're not trying to convert each other; we just want our real agreements and real disagreements made exact. I'll lay out our positions, and you draft the document for us.

Rhea's an illusionist in roughly the Frankish/Dennett vein: she says introspection represents us as having private, ineffable, intrinsic qualia, but that representation is false — there are no such properties, and the mind is fully captured by access, function, representation, and reaction. I'm a minimal phenomenal realist: I think there are real "what it's like" facts that aren't exhausted by those access/function/representational facts, and introspection gives at least prima facie reason to take them seriously. We both agree the phrase "user illusion" does real work as a metaphor but gets stretched past its breaking point, though we can't agree on where the breaking point is.

The document should have two parts and nothing else. First: exactly five numbered agreed claims, each one or two sentences, substantive enough that neither of us would yawn at them. Cover at least what the data are that any theory of consciousness has to handle, what the user-illusion metaphor gets right, and where it overreaches. Then: a disagreements table with exactly three rows. For each crux, give it in neutral wording first, then Rhea's side in wording she'd sign, then my side in wording I'd sign, then a case, experiment, or argument we both agree bears on it (blindsight, change blindness, masking, the rubber hand illusion, the explanatory gap, whatever actually discriminates), and what each of us predicts about it before seeing anything new. Keep each cell to a sentence or two. If one of our usual disagreements turns out to be merely verbal once it's stated carefully, dissolve that row and give us a third genuine crux instead. I suspect two of the real cruxes are (1) whether introspection can be unreliable about the nature of experience while still reliable about its existance, and (2) whether the explanatory-gap/conceivability intuitions are evidence for a further kind of fact or just an epistemic gap — but you don't have to use those if you find better ones. No introduction, no conclusion; just the five numbered claims and the table.
