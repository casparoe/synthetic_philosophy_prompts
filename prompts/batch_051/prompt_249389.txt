Imre Lakatos died in 1974, so he missed basically the whole era of computer-verified mathematics. He's the guy who argued that mathematics grows more like a science than most people admit: conjectures get tested against counterexamples, proofs get patched, and definitions quietly get renegotiated when a counterexample shows up. I've been wondering what he'd have made of everything that came after him. The four color theorem was proved with a computer in 1976, a couple years after he died, and now we've got interactive theorem provers like Coq and Lean, formalized versions of alot of big theorems, and even machine learning systems that guess new conjectures. So here's my question. Would Lakatos see a fully machine-checked proof as the thing that finally restores the kind of certainty his whole method seemed to undercut — no hidden gaps, no reliance on what the mathematical community happens to accept — or would he treat it as just another move in the same game, since you still have to trust the codebase, the compiler, the formalization choices, and the definitions someone picked? And what would he make of the fact that most working mathematicians still don't much care about formal verification and keep judging proofs by the old semi-formal, social standards? Give me the most Lakatosian reading you can defend, and flag the places where you're least confident.
