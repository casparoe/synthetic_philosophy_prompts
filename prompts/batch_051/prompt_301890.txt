I am about to read a number of theories about whether effort, striving, and struggle are valuable in themselves rather than merely as costs paid for results, and I want the specification of what such a theory must get right before I start. Assume the theory is supposed to serve two purposes at once: advising an agent about when, if ever, to choose a harder route over an easier one; and explaining what, if anything, is admirable or valuable about another person's effort when the outcome is mediocre or absent. Write the desiderata.

The list should build on cases like these without being limited to them: Sisyphus, whose labor produces nothing; two people who produce the identical excellent result, one with evident struggle and one with no visible effort; someone who imposes gratuitous difficulty on themselves—adding weight, refusing shortcuts, declining help—when the outcome is unchanged; someone who strives brilliantly in service of a harmful end; someone who pours enormous discipline into an arbitrary activity that connects to nothing else they value; someone who has absorbed a hard practice so completely that it no longer feels effortful; and the fact that people tend to value results more when they cost them more. For each requirement, give the case behind it and say why a theory that fails it has gone wrong.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really just commitments of particular camps (for example, that effort must be a pure cost, or that effort must be partly constitutive of goods like mastery or self-respect). Most importantly, flag any pairs of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied—I want to know whether the list itself forces a choice, for example between endorsing the intrinsic value of effort and refusing to praise gratuitous obstacles. Identify the single requirement that eliminates the most otherwise-live views, with a sentence on what it rules out. If useful, close by briefly running three broad positions—effort is always a pure cost; effort is never good in itself but is a necessary constituent of at least some genuine achievements; effort is in itself a fitting object of esteem—against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard.
