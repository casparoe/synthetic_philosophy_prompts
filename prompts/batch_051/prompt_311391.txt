I teach a year 12 critical thinking class in a fairly ordinary state sixth-form college: about 22 students, 90-minute lessons, mixed ability. Most years we do a unit on the idea that the world has become less violent over the long run — the big sweep from tribal raiding to modern states and so on. Last year I tried to run the class as a debate and it fell apart immediately: two students traded statistics past each other, a third said the whole question depends on what you count as violence, and everyone else waited for me to say who was right. I dont want to front-load the distinctions again, because then it just becomes another lecture.

Please design a 90-minute classroom activity or game (not a lecture, no reading beforehand) that makes the students run into the problem themselves. They should have to construct the comparison between time periods or places, and in doing so make decisions about what counts as violence, whether they're counting events or rates per 100,000 people, and whether one atrocity should count the same as a thousand common assaults. It would be brilliant if two groups end up with defensible but opposite verdicts and neither realises why until the debrief, because they've quietly used different definitions or denominators. I want them to leave able to say exactly which counting choice would flip the answer, rather than just that the answer is contested. I need exact rules or roles, materials I can print or make on a teacher's budget, rough timings within the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps their decisions onto the per capita/absolute distinction, the definitional problem, and the question whether theres a determinate fact of the matter once those are fixed. They shouldn't hear jargon like operationalisation until after.

Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and be honest about which feature of the real debate the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in the debrief. Finally, say which design choice you are least confident in and what would change your mind about it — for instance, a particular way a student might push back that would show the game had been rigged or the debrief was doing too much work.
