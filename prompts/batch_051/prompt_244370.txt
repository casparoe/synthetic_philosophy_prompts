Two people I know got into an argument about whether the real numbers are discovered or defined, and it went further than I expected. I'll reproduce it as best I remember, because I genuinely can't tell who's right.

It started when Maria said the continuum is something we discovered — the fact that there are more reals than naturals isn't a convention, it's forced on you once you grasp the real line. Dave said no, the reals are something we defined, and the fact that we had to construct them is the giveaway: there are multiple constructions (Dedekind cuts, Cauchy sequences) and they aren't literally the same object.

Maria said that's too picky. Dedekind cuts and Cauchy sequences are different set-theoretic objects, sure, but both give you a complete ordered field, and any two complete ordered fields are isomorphic. So they present the same structure; the underlying plumbing doesn't matter. Saying they're different real lines is like saying the number 2 is a different number depending on how you encode the naturals in set theory.

Dave said "up to isomorphism" is doing all the work, and anyway the continuum hypothesis shows the real line isn't a determinate thing — you can consistently add CH or its negation to the standard axioms, so if the continuum were discovered there should be a fact of the matter about its cardinality. Maria said that's a fact about the limits of our axiom systems, not about the real line itself. If the axioms are to weak to settle CH, that doesn't mean the line has no determinate size; it means we picked axioms that can't see it.

Dave replied that this is unfalsifiable — every time the formalism underdetermines a question, she blames the formalism, so nothing could ever count as the continuum being defined rather than discovered. Maria said okay, here's a concession: the concept of a complete ordered field is our definition, but once you fix it, the properties force themselves — uncountability, the Archimedean property, uniqueness up to isomorphism. You can't stipulate those away. Dave said that's just relabeling: "discovered given the definition" is just defined, then explored.

Maria came back: by that standard the prime numbers are invented too, since we fixed the naturals by axioms. But nobody seriously says the infinitude of the primes is a convention. Discovery always happens relative to a fixed notion; the fact that the notion was fixed by a definition doesn't make the resulting facts optional.

That's where they left it. Can you go through this exchange and adjudicate it point by point — which specific moves are right, which are wrong or overstated, and what both of them are missing? Then say who's more right overall, if either.
