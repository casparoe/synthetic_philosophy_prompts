As a philosopher of mind who still reads the old papers, I keep worrying at a particular move in Frank Jackson's "Epiphenomenal Qualia." He sets up Mary in the black-and-white room, and then — I'm paraphrasing here, not quoting from memory — he says that it just seems obvious that when Mary leaves the room she will learn something about the world and our visual experience of it, and that since she already had all the physical information, physicalism must be false. I want you to treat that "just seems obvious" step as a testable claim, not a rhetorical flourish.

So here's the actual question. How would you test whether Mary learns something genuinely new, rather than merely gaining a recognitional ability, or just acquiring a new way of representing facts she already knew? I want you to design the best experimental setup you can — it can be a thought experiment made concrete, or something you could actually run on human subjects or on an AI system trained on all the physical facts about color — and say what result would favor each of the three readings: new factual knowledge, ability-only, or new mode of presentation. Then do the honest part: what would even the best test still not settle? Is there a residue here that no operationalization can reach, and does the real philosophical action live in that residue, or in the testable part?

Think carefully first — brainstorm a few possible operationalizations before settling on the one you'd actually defend.
