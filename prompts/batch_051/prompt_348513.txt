I'm a software engineer, and I spend most of my time working with machine learning, so priors and posteriors are more natural to me than the surrounding philosophical vocabulary. Over the past year I've kept returning to an idea I can neither kill nor endorse, and I'd like a serious assessment of it. I've been calling it the single-instance prior. Treat the question of whether other people have a felt, first-person inside — whether they are subjects and not just behaviorally complete systems — as an inference problem. My dataset contains exactly one instance of a subject I can verify directly: me. Every other person is an unlabeled case. I can observe their behavior, their reports, their physiological responses, but never the property in question itself. With one positive example, no negative examples, and a target variable that is unobservable from the outside, the posterior over how common first-person experience is among human-like systems is almost entirely fixed by the prior. On this picture, the ordinary person who concludes that others are conscious is running a generous prior; the solipsist is running a miserly one. No future evidence can adjudicate, both because the disputed property is private by construction and because the single positive observation is guaranteed by the setup: there is no version of the dataset in which the collector lacks a first-person point of view. So the dispute seems, by my lights, to be a dispute about prior choice plus a norm about how priors may be chosen, and not about evidence at all.

The problem is that this model seems to fail exactly where I wanted it to be useful, namely the main character or NPC intuition. People who say everyone else is an NPC do not, in my experience, live like rare-event solipsists. They still treat their interlocutors as if those interlocutors can be hurt, persuaded, or shamed; they deploy the NPC claim selectively, often as a way of backgrounding people rather than as a standing metaphysical commitment. My model would have to classify them as running the rare prior, but that does not fit the behavior. So I'd like an honest assessment: is the single-instance prior a coherent way of reframing the problem of other minds, or is it a technical-sounding restatement that quietly begs the question? Has this move already been made under a name I wouldn't know? And where exactly does it break — in particular, am I wrong to treat an observation that could not have failed to occur as a normal data point?
