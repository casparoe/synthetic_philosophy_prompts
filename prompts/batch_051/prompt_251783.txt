I write about science and ideas for a living, and for a column on how people decide where to put resources, I keep noticing a parallel between two debates that don't usually talk to each other. On one side, the doing-good crowd argues about whether the best move is to fund marginal, evidence-backed interventions—bed nets, deworming, cash transfers—or whether that's just tinkering inside a broken system, so the real work is systemic: institutions, laws, political economy. On the other side, people who train models deal with a surprisingly similar tension. Gradient descent is basically a commitment to taking the locally best step, and everyone knows it can strand you in a local minimum or on a plateau, oblivious to the much better valley over the ridge. The field even has remedies that sound like the systemic side of the philanthropy debate: random restarts, simulated annealing, exploration bonuses, Bayesian methods that keep track of uncertainty about the whole landscape.

I can't decide whether this is a real structural parallel or just me pattern-matching on the word "local." If it is real, what would have to be true on the doing-good side for the analogy to carry weight—do we need something like a fixed objective function and a stable landscape, and isnt that exactly what social systems lack, since the "loss function" is contested and the terrain shifts when you act on it? More sharply: is there anything from optimization theory that actually transfers and might change someones mind in the marginal-versus-systemic debate, and what does the parallel predict that's probably false? I'd rather not hand my editor a false analogy dressed up as insight.
