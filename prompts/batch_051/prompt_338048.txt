Last week two colleagues in my department argued, in my presence, about whether explaining a historical event requires subsuming it under general regularities — the covering-law form of explanation — or whether a narrative can explain in its own right. They kept returning to the same example: why the July Crisis of 1914 ended in general European war rather than in the negotiated settlements that had resolved earlier pre-war crises. I listened without taking a side and could not referee the dispute. Their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it, went as follows.

(1) The first, a political scientist, said that a narrative, however rich, does not by itself explain. The war began with the assassination at Sarajevo, then the Austrian ultimatum to Serbia, then the Austrian declaration of war on Serbia, then the Russian mobilisation, then the German declaration of war. List these events and you have a chronicle, not an explanation. To explain why they issued in war rather than another retreat from the brink, we must subsume them under general regularities: states tend to honour their alliances, military staffs favour earlier mobilisation when they believe a window of advantage is closing, and crises escalate when the parties cannot credibly signal their red lines. Together with the conditions of 1914 those regularities made war the likely outcome; an account that cites none of them has not said why the event occurred.

(2) The second, a historian, replied that the regularities he cited are too coarse to pick out 1914. The same alliance commitments, the same incentives to mobilise first, and the same signalling difficulties were present in the Moroccan and Balkan crises before the war, yet those crises ended in settlements. What distinguished 1914 was the particular chain: the terms of the ultimatum, the order and timing of mobilisations, the decisions taken by particular statesmen and generals under particular misapprehensions. A narrative that traces how each step closed one option and opened another explains this outcome precisely by showing what was distinctive about this case; it does not pretend that the same chain would recur.

(3) The political scientist answered that the narrative's transitions are either lawful or merely successive. When the historian says the Russian mobilisation pushed the German government from waiting to acting, she is relying on an implicit generalisation about mobilisation timetables and the calculations of general staffs. If that generalisation is true, then it belongs in a covering account; if it is false, the transition is only 'and then', and nothing has been explained. A narrative that will not state the regularities on which its transitions depend is, he said, an explanation sketch rather than an explanation.

(4) The historian answered that the generalisation he demands cannot do the work he assigns to it. Mobilisation does not, as a general rule, push the other side into war — the earlier crises again show otherwise — and any version hedged so that it holds only in July 1914 is no longer general and cannot mark the difference between explaining and describing. The narrative's work, she said, is of another kind: it shows how the war was possible, and how it became actual, by tracing the reasons, misapprehensions, and institutional constraints of the actors as they actually operated. That is an explanation of a particular event, not a law about a class of events, and it ought not to be assessed by standards appropriate only to the latter.

I still cannot decide between them. Please assess the exchange point by point: for each numbered move, say whether it is largely sound, partly sound, or unsound as stated, and justify that judgement; then say which of the two positions, as actually advanced here, stands in better shape overall, and what, if anything, the exchange overlooks.
