People defending legal personhood for AI often use the corporate-person analogy: corporations aren’t natural people, the law just treats them as persons because it’s useful, and we could do the same for highly capable AI agents. Hobbes tends to get cited as the source of the idea of an artificial person. In the Introduction to Leviathan (1651) he says:

Nature (the art whereby God hath made and governes the world) is by the art of man, as in many other things, so in this also imitated, that it can make an Artificial Animal. For seeing life is but a motion of Limbs, the begining whereof is in some principall part within; why may we not say, that all Automata (Engines that move themselves by springs and wheeles as doth a watch) have an artificiall life? For what is the Heart, but a Spring; and the Nerves, but so many Strings; and the Joynts, but so many Wheeles, giving motion to the whole Body, such as was intended by the Artificer?

Here’s what I want to know. Hobbes’s automata, and then the “Artificiall Animal” of the Commonwealth, are unified artifacts whose motions come from some “principall part within”—in the state, the sovereign functions as the artificial soul. But the AI systems currently proposed for legal personhood are not like that. They are foundation models that get fine-tuned, used behind agents, steered by system prompts and safety policies, and can be run in thousands of copies at once. There’s no single “artificial will” and no clear sovereign. Does the Hobbesian argument for artificial personhood survive this change, or does it only work when the artificial body is constructed to have one will and one principle of motion? What premise in his argument does the corporate-person analogy rely on, and is that premise actually still true when we move from corporations to AI systems? Try to answer without just saying “it depends.” I want to know which premise would have to be re-engineered—legally or technically—for the analogy to do real work.
