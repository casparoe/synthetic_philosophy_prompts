I host a podcast on philosophy of perception and the arts, and next week's guest defends the strong transparency thesis about photography: the claim that in looking at a photograph we literally see the photographed object, much as we see through a telescope or in a mirror, and that this is what fundamentally distinguishes photographs from handmade pictures. She is prepared to accept the counterintuitive consequences, including that one can literally see a person who died decades ago when looking at an old photograph. Draft eight to ten interview questions that move beyond restating the thesis. Include at least one question on each of the following pressures, and for each provide a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give: (1) the disanalogy with telescopes and mirrors, since photographs break the continuous optical path and do not carry information about the object's location relative to the viewer in the way those devices do; (2) the photograph of a long-dead person, and whether the thesis is genuinely committed to seeing into the past, and whether that commitment is coherent; (3) the role of the mechanical-causal link between scene and image, and whether digital manipulation severs whatever link grounded the transparency claim; and (4) the gap between seeing an object and being positioned to act on it, given that no one would duck a photographed baseball. The questions should press where the philosophical debate actually presses rather than asking for biography or scene-setting.
