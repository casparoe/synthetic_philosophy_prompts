I'm a novelist, so I mostly think about pain through craft choices: a character's interiority, what you can show versus tell, how much the reader is made to flinch with them rather then just watch them flinch. Lately I've been reading about pain asymbolia — people who, after certain brain injuries, can tell you exactly where a cut or a pinprick is, how sharp or hot it feels, but insist it doesn't bother them; they don't wince, they don't want it to stop. That seems to break the view I'd have defended in a bar: that the badness of pain is just the pain itself, not some extra thing added on to the sensation. Restate that philosophical view in my actual working vocabulary — scenes, point of view, a body's responses versus a character's caring, reader identification — as precisely as you can. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the view translate cleanly into craft terms, which parts you had to fudge, and whether the asymbolia challenge has any counterpart in how novels make pain feel real. Is my old assumption better described as being about suffering than about pain, or does the novelist's vocabulary help save the idea that the pain and the badness are one thing?
