So I cover the science-and-ideas beat, and lately I've been doing a bunch of pieces on fringe history — ancient mysteries, "the timeline is wrong," lost civilizations. I've noticed my gut verdicts on what counts as pseudohistory don't line up with any definition I can state cleanly. Here are the cases I keep using, with my actual verdicts.

Atlantis-as-literal-predecessor-civilization: pseudohistory. But I don't think Plato was doing pseudohistory, because the Atlantis story is in a dialogue and is doing political-philosophy work; it becomes pseudohistory when people treat it as a research finding about the actual past.

Ancient aliens, von Däniken style: pseudohistory. I don't care that it's popular or that some of the underlying artifacts are real; the moves are the problem.

Fomenko's New Chronology: pseudohistory, no hesitation.

The "phantom time" claim that several centuries of the early middle ages are invented and Charlemagne didn't exist: pseudohistory.

Velikovsky-style catastrophism where the planets rearranged themselves in historical times: pseudohistory.

The Donation of Constantine being a medieval forgery: not pseudohistory. That's a claim that an important document was faked, and mainstream history accepts it. It feels different from Fomenko even though both say "lots of people were taken in by invented texts."

Vikings in Newfoundland before Columbus: not pseudohistory — including in the decades before L'Anse aux Meadows, when it was mostly a saga-based guess and respectable historians thought it was romantic nonsense.

Polynesian contact with South America before Columbus: not pseudohistory. I think it's still underdetermined (sweet potato, maybe chickens), but it's an ordinary empirical question, not a story that survives by being unfalsifiable.

The one I can't settle: Graham Hancock-type lost advanced civilization, wiped out by a Younger-Dryas comet, with Gobekli Tepe left behind as evidence. On one hand it uses real sites and a real climate event and makes claims that could in principle be checked. On the other hand it feels like it starts from the conclusion and treats every counter-argument as academic gatekeeping.

Is there a principle — or a small set of rules — behind my verdicts so far, and which of them does it force me to drop? Whats the principle say about the Hancock case: is it pseudohistory, heterodox-but-legitimate, or a genuinely different kind of thing? And I don't want just the confident answer: tell me what would change your mind — which evidence, if it came in, would flip the Hancock call or break the principle — and where the line you draw is least secure.
