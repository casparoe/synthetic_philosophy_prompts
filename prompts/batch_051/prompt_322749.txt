I keep getting pulled toward a reductive account of privacy — the view that privacy is just a convenient label for a bundle of other interests: property over your stuff, bodily autonomy, reputation, and the instrumental value of being able to withhold information so it can't be used against you. It appeals to me for the usual tidy reasons: it's simple, it unifies a bunch of different complaints under one test, and it doesnt posit a free-floating privacy interest doing unexplained work. It also hands you a decision procedure. Whenever someone says their privacy was violated, you ask which underlying interest was actually set back; if none was, the complaint is confused. But I have a couple of cases where that procedure returns no real harm and my intuition screams that something was still wrong.

The first is the diary case, which a friend kept pressing me on. Someone reads my private diary without permission, tells no one, never mentions it, never treats me differently, and I later find out. Nothing downstream happens. The reductive view seems forced to say the only wrong was a trivial interference with the physical notebook, which points at the wrong object: the exposure itself, not the atoms of the diary, is what feels violated. The second is the watcher. A stranger sits across from me in a nearly empty train car and overtly studies me for an hour, no notes, no camera, no approach. I'm in public, they learn nothing I hadn't already exposed, and they do nothing with it, so the reductive test says no underlying interest was set back. But being made into an object of someone's attention without my consent feels like the clearest privacy violation I can imagine. Then theres the trickier case: someone infers a deeply personal fact about me, say a health condition or something I have never disclosed, purely from public data, my tweets, my purchase patterns, the way I walk. They accessed nothing nonpublic and they never act on the inference. Have they violated my privacy? The reductive view seems to say no, since every input was something I voluntarily put out there, but I can't shake the sense that the assembly is itself the intrusion.

I don't want someone to just tell me which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes — is it just politeness-marker confusion or leftover boundary-disgust with no moral content? — and what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, for instance by adding a distinct interest in not being made an object of attention or in controlling access to your inner life, and whether that still counts as reductive. Is there a reading on which the reductive account survives by swallowing one of these as a real underlying interest, and does that save the tidiness or just relocate it? And please write the whole thing as continuous prose, like an email: no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
