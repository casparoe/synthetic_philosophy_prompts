I was appointed to a trial court of general jurisdiction ten months ago, and the question I still cannot answer in a disciplined way is not how to find the law, but whether to apply what a statute plainly says when the result, in the case before me, looks absurd. I meet this question several times a month, in small civil matters and low-level criminal matters alike. I sit alone, with one staff attorney and no law clerks; I must decide in writing, usually within two weeks; and the appellate court reviews my statutory rulings on a fresh basis, so I need a rationale I can defend.

Please write me a decision procedure — prose or a numbered sequence — that I can run whenever a party argues that the literal text should not control under what the treatises call the absurdity doctrine. Do not give me a research memorandum or citations; give me the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a sufficient showing at each step, and where the sequence terminates in one of three ways: apply the text anyway; adopt the narrower, non-absurd construction; or hand the decision back to me because the step calls for a value judgment the procedure cannot make for me.

The procedure must discipline my own impatience with texts I dislike. It should separate (a) an application the drafters would have rejected had they foreseen it from (b) an application that is merely harsh, unwise, or unfair; and it should separate both of those from (c) a drafting slip that no one in the legislative process actually chose. It should also say honestly where it will misfire — where a judge who wants to reach a result can march the checklist to it.

Then run the procedure on two provisions I have actually faced this year, and show exactly where the two cases come apart inside the sequence:

1. A state statute reads: "Any person who knowingly transports a person under sixteen years of age across a state line without the consent of the person's custodial parent or guardian commits a felony." An ambulance crew, answering a car crash, transported an unconscious eleven-year-old across the state line to the nearest trauma center because the closest in-state hospital was forty minutes farther; the child's mother lay unconscious at the scene and could not consent. The prosecution has charged the crew under the statute.

2. A probate provision reads: "A claim against a decedent's estate is barred unless it is filed with the clerk of probate within six months after the death." A creditor received timely personal notice of the death and of the six-month deadline, then missed the deadline by a month because its bookkeeper was on leave and the file was mislaid in its own office. The executor moves to bar the claim. The text is clear, the deadline serves the estate's interest in finality, but the result is that a genuine debt goes unpaid.

Keep the entire response — the procedure and both applications — to no more than 900 words.
