I serve on a standing panel that rules on proposals for new psychiatric classifications, and I am trying to determine what our own precedents commit us to in a case now before us. I have been thinking about Ian Hacking's looping effect: when people become aware of a classification that applies to them, they can change how they understand themselves and behave, so that the classification begins to reshape the very phenomenon it names. Our past rulings are these. We accepted a chronic bereavement condition with clear functional impairment after the same symptom course had been documented in several culturally distinct populations, though no biological marker was ever identified; naming the condition reduced family blame and opened access to care. We accepted a movement disorder with a distinctive neurophysiological marker, despite its symptoms having been reported by only one research group, because patients were seriously impaired and the diagnosis directed them to effective treatment. We rejected a proposal to classify a widespread but usually mild disposition to suspiciousness, in part because an online community had already organized around the proposed label and was making it into an identity. We rejected a proposed category of recurrent volitional collapse episodes, known only from self-report and never replicated across sites, with no evidence that the label would unlock effective care and early signs that publicity might produce an imitative wave of cases. And we provisionally accepted a tic-like vocal disorder with no confirmed physiology but a broadly consistent, functionally impairing presentation across three countries, because use of the label in two clinics reduced family blame and improved engagement with behavioral treatment.

The new proposal is for a category provisionally named relational fixation disorder: a pattern, lasting at least six months after the end of a romantic relationship, of being unable to reorganize one's life and everyday functioning, with marked distress and measurable impairment in work and family roles. No biological marker has been established, and the presentation has so far been described in only two clinics in a single country. A patient advocacy organization has already formed around the proposed label; some consultants predict that adopting the name will consolidate a self-conception that worsens the rumination, while others argue that recognition is the only way to unlock a structured psychotherapy program and would reduce self-blame.

To be consistent with our past rulings, should the panel accept, reject, or accept this category with conditions? Identify the most plausible rules that fit our earlier rulings, state what each rule would say about this new proposal, say which earlier ruling is the closest analog, and give your best judgment about which decision the panel should reach. If the record underdetermines that judgment, specify a single future proposal whose disposition would most cleanly settle which rule the panel has really been following.
