I'm in charge of the fundraising side of a student history society, and after someone mentioned the classic dollar auction in a committee meeting (we were arguing about whether it counted as gambling), we got stuck on a counterfactual version we're half-considering running. I'd like you to reason through how it would actually play out.

The standard game, as I understand it, has the two highest bidders both paying their final bids while only the winner gets the prize, which is what creates the horrible 'if I stop now I lose everything' incentive. Suppose instead we ran it like this: the prize is a £50 bar tab, bidding starts at £1 and rises by at least £1 with each new bid beating the current highest, the winner pays their full final bid, but the runner-up gets 90% of their final bid back on the spot, so their effective loss is only a tenth of it. The bidders are forty-odd tipsy undergraduates, mostly first-years, who all know each other. Walk me through what happens over the evening: does the familiar escalation spiral still appear, does it run faster or slower than the standard version, and roughly how much does the society make relative to the £50 face value of the tab? I'm less interested in the final yes-or-no than in the reasoning. I want to know which feature of the standard auction is doing the real causal work — the push of 'if I stop now I lose the lot', the sunk-cost rationalising, public face-saving, plain competitiveness — and whether the 90% refund removes that feature or just weakens it. For each link in the prediction, say how confident you are and what fact about the crowd or the rules would have to change for it to flip. Could you write the answer in British English?
