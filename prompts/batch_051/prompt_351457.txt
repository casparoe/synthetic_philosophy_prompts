Two friends of mine got into a long argument about a made-up country's constitution, and I ended up not knowing weather I agreed with either of them. Imagine a country where the constitution says the president is elected to a fixed four-year term, and that the term shall end at midnight on a certain date, when the certified winner takes office. It says nothing about what happens if no winner has been certified by then. Now suppose the election is razor thin, both candidates — the incumbent and the challenger — claim they won, there are credible reports of fraud, and the election commission refuses to certify anyone. The date arrives. At 12:01 AM, who is legitimately in charge?

One of them, Dana, said the outgoing president stays on as acting president. The constitution, she said, can't have as its whole point leaving the country with nobody who can direct the police during a crisis. There has to be an implied power to fill gaps, and the least disruptive way to fill this one is continuity: keep the incumbent in place until the election dispute is resolved.

The other, Sam, said that gets it backwards. A fixed term is a fixed term. If the outgoing president can extend their own term whenever the handoff fails, the term limit means nothing, and a corrupt incumbent now has a reason to manufacture exactly this kind of crisis. When the clock hits midnight, the incumbent is a private citizen.

Dana came back: the rule of law isn't a suicide pact. No written rules can foresee every case, so every legal system needs some emergency doctrine. If you don't read an implied continuity power into the constitution, you're saying the whole legal order is supposed to self-destruct — and that can't have been the point.

Sam said: fine, maybe there's an emergency power. But whose is it? You don't give the emergency power to the one person with the strongest interest in using it to stay in office. Maybe the legislature has it, or the courts can order the commission to certify, or the top judges can appoint a caretaker.

Dana said the legislature and the courts are in the same boat. The legislature passes laws and the courts decide cases; neither commands the military or the police. The constitution creates offices, and those offices are where the loyalties of the staff attach. At some point the "is" matters — if every cop and soldier keeps saluting the outgoing president, that's the de facto government, and calling it illegal doesn't make it disappear.

Sam said that's a bait and switch. We started by asking who has the right to govern, and she answered with who will in fact be obeyed. Those are different questions, and the whole problem is that the rules don't answer the first one. If we just go with whoever has the guns, we've given up on constitutional government, and that's a conclusion, not an argument.

Dana said no — the conclusion is that the constitution contains an unstated principle: in a gap, existing authority continues as a caretaker only. The president can keep the lights on but can't pass laws, make appointments, or use the emergency to tilt the recount. That's not the end of constitutionalism; it's the constitution's own survival instinct.

Sam answered that "caretaker only" is doing all the work and none of the deciding. Who says which acts count as keeping the lights on? The same person the whole dispute is about. That's just continuity with extra steps.

Go through this exchange and say who is right about which specific point, where the argument actually moves, and what either one of them is missing.
