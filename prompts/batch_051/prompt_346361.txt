I've been trying to get clear on when I think an omission is a cause, and my gut gives me a weird pattern. Here are my verdicts. If a nurse assigned to check on a patient overnight doesn't check and the patient dies of something the check would have caught, I say the nurse's failure caused the death. If a neighbor who said he'd water my plants while I was away forgets, and they die, I say his not watering them caused them to die. If a stranger walks past a kid drowning in a shallow pond and does nothing, I don't think the stranger's not jumping in caused the kid's death, even though the kid would have lived if he had jumped. If a passerby notices that a step outside a café is loose and doesn't warn anyone, and someone later falls, I don't think the non-warning caused the fall. But then here's the one that bothers me: if a parent doesn't feed their infant for a day and the infant dies, I definitely say the parent's omission caused the death; but if a person who could easily donate to a famine-relief charity doesn't, and a child dies in another country, I don't say their not donating caused that child's death. I can't see what makes the second pair different from the first except maybe that one feels more like a special duty, but I don't want it to just be vibes.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close, and what does it say about a case I keep going back and forth on: a doctor who decides not to put a patient on a ventilator that would very likely save them, where the patient had previously said they don't want aggressive treatment? The doctor's failure to start the ventilator seems like it causes death, but the refusal of treatment confuses me. Please state the principle precisely and tell me which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to drop.
