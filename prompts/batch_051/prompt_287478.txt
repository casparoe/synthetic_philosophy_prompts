I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about the "your little bit doesn't matter" line, so I went through some cases and wrote down my gut verdicts. Here they are, roughly. Skipping a national election: not okay, you should vote, even though your one vote basically never decides anything. Eating a factory-farmed burger: I'm a little grossed out, but on the whole I think it's fine. Buying a cheap shirt I strongly suspect was made in a sweatshop: not fine. Taking a one-hour budget flight for a long weekend: not fine, though if you push me I can't say exactly why. Driving five minutes to the shop instead of walking: fine. Crossing a picket line to buy somehing small during a strike: not fine, even though my purchase won't settle the strike. Not donating the twenty pounds in my pocket to a malaria charity: fine.

I can see the pattern is unstable: with voting, the tiny chance of being decisive feels like it counts; with the burger and the drive, the tiny contribution feels like it doesn't; with the flight it counts again. Is there a principle that captures all or most of these verdicts, or is it just my mood? What would that principle say about the cases I keep flip-flopping on—like someone who flies a couple times a year but still eats meat every day, or joining a boycott of a company you would never have bought from anyway? And which of my verdicts does it force me to drop?
