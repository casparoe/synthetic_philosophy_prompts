Two people in my reading group got into it last week over what thought experiments actually do, and they asked me to referee, and I honestly couldnt tell who was winning. I'll reconstruct the exchange as best I remember it.

Mina said thought experiments are mostly just stories, and the fact that you have a strong reaction to a story is not evidence about how knowledge or consciousness or morality actually works. She brought up Gettier-style cases, where the whole thing is meant to show that justified true belief isn't enough for knowledge. She said the case only "works" if you already have the intuition that the person doesn't know, so it can't prove anything to someone who doesn't share that intuition.

Cal said that's a misconception. A Gettier case is a counterexample to a proposed definition, like a counterexample in math. The intuition isn't an extra bit of evidence on top of the case; it's the data the definition has to fit. If a definition delivers the wrong verdict in a clear case, the definition is the thing that's wrong.

Mina replied that in math a counterexample can be checked by proof, but in a Gettier case all you're checking is whether people in a certain tradition say "he doesn't know." That tells us something about our concept, not about knowledge itself. Cal said fine, that's still something, and anyway the concept is what a definition of knowledge is answerable to. Then Mina moved to the Mary case — the color scientist who knows all the physical facts before she ever sees red. She said there the thought experiment is supposed to tell us about the world, not just our concept: people move from "I can imagine Mary learning something new" to "there are non-physical facts," and that's exactly the intuition-pump trick, where imagination gets quietly promoted to possibility.

Cal said the Mary case isn't resting on raw imagination; it's an argument. If Mary knows every physical fact, and then genuinely learns something on first seeing red, it follows that not all facts are physical. The story just makes the premise vivid. You can deny the premise, but you can deny a premise in any argument, so that's not a special problem for thought experiments.

Mina said the premise that she learns something new isn't established by anything independent of the reader's feeling that she would. A physicalist can simply deny she learns anything new, or deny the scenario is coherent. The story doesn't provide evidence one way or the other; it just recruits whichever side you already find intuitive.

Cal said by that standard no argument can establish anything, since any premise can be denied. He added that the Mary case is actually like a controlled experiment: it holds the physical facts fixed and varies only Mary's history of exposure to color, then asks what follows. That's isolating a variable, not trickery.

Mina said it isn't an experiment because nothing is measured and nothing could fail. If someone reports a different reaction to the Mary case, there's no further fact that settles who's right, so it's rhetoric with better stage lighting.

Cal said she was using thought experiments herself — inventing a hypothetical person with a different reaction to test a theory about thought experiments. He said the real distinction isn't between thought experiments and arguments; it's between thought experiments that function as arguments from explicit premises and ones that merely pump intuitions, and the hard question is just how to tell which is which.

Mina said there's still a difference between using a hypothetical to illustrate a point you could make without it, and using one as your evidence. She said her imagined person with a different reaction was just illustration. Cal said that's exactly the distinction he has been drawing, so she owes him a criterion for when a scenario is evidence and when it's illustration — and she can't just decide that her own scenarios are illustrations while his are intuition pumps.

So that's where they left it.
