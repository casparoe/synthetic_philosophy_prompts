I keep coming back to a passage in Hegel's Philosophy of Right, in the international law part, where he argues something like: a state is not yet actual as a state until other states recognize it; through recognition it first becomes a state for others and gains its objective validity. I'm paraphrasing, but that's the core.

Here's what bugs me. We now have these places—Somaliland, Abkhazia, maybe others—that have controlled territory, collected taxes, run courts, and kept a monopoly on force for decades, but almost nobody formally recognizes them as states. Some even sit in technical international bodies and get treated like states for airport codes and postal routes. If Hegel's argument is right, are these not states at all? Or are they states in a deficient mode? More importantly, does the argument survive once formal recognition becomes partial and fragmented, spread across different kinds of actors and registers, rather than the all-or-nothing acknowledgement he seems to have in mind? Maybe it still works, or maybe it turns into a claim about a ceremony that has lost its original function.

Please assess whether Hegel's argument goes through, needs repair, or just doesn't fit these cases. Also, separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who study recognition in international relations, and don't present one side as the consensus. American English, please.
