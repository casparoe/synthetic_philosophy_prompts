Take Pascal's wager, but strip out the infinity and the God stuff and make the bet about signing up for cryonics. If revival works, you get (say) 50,000 extra years of decent life; if it doesn't, you've payed dues and taken a mild hit to how seriously people take you. The probability that cryonics will ever work isn't just low, it's contested in a way that looks unmeasurable from the inside: reasonable-ish people put it anywhere from 0.1% to 10%, and the disagreement traces back to different premises about scanning/upload tech, institutional continuity, and whether the revived person is really you. Run the expected-utility argument at both ends of that range and say whether it still compels the way Pascal's did. Then take the extra step Pascal didn't need: if you're ambiguity-averse (say maximin, or Hurwicz with a pessimism coefficient of 0.8), does the bet still go through? I'm trying to figure out whether the force of Pascal's wager comes from the infinite payoff or from the structure of betting under unquantifiable uncertainty, and cryonics is basically the cleanest finite-payoff version of it.
