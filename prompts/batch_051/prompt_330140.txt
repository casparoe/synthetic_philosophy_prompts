A friend and I have been arguing about voting reform — he recently read something about impossibility theorems and has become convinced that fairly aggregating individual preferences is hopeless, not just in practice but in principle. I have tried to reconstruct his argument in a way he accepts, but I cannot find the step where it goes wrong.

(1) The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem says that, with at least three candidates and voters who have strict rankings, any voting rule that is non-dictatorial and onto — every candidate can win under some profile of votes — is manipulable. That is, for some profile of sincere rankings, some voter can bring about an outcome she strictly prefers by submitting a ranking other than her true one.
(2) So no such rule is strategy-proof: in some situation, someone has an incentive to misreport what she wants.
(3) A collective decision procedure is fair only if it is strategy-proof. A process that sometimes rewards lying about one's preferences is not neutral between honest and strategic voters; it lets the outcome turn on who games the system best rather than on what the group actually prefers.
(4) Therefore, no non-dictatorial, onto voting rule for three or more candidates is fair.
(5) A dictatorial rule — one in which a single fixed voter's top choice always wins, whatever anyone else reports — is plainly unfair.
(6) A rule that is not onto is also unfair, since it could never select some candidate even when every voter ranks that candidate first.
(7) So no voting rule for three or more candidates is fair. Democracy, and any election, hiring decision, or committee choice with more than two alternatives, is either a disguised dictatorship, a game of strategic misrepresentation, or a system that arbitrarily excludes some outcomes regardless of unanimous support.

I want very much to keep the theorem, since it is a mathematical result, and to keep the principle that a fair procedure should not make lying profitable. But the conclusion seems to prove too much: it would show that fair multi-candidate decision-making is impossible in principle, not merely hard to achieve. Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am asking for a diagnosis of the argument rather than for facts about electoral systems: name the step that is doing the damage, explain why it looked innocent, and spell out what we must give up about fairness, honesty, or the scope of the theorem if we take that exit.
