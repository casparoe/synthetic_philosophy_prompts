I've been volunteering with a local historical society, scanning old records for a public database. Most of what got kept is deeds, tax rolls, church membership lists, and court minutes, so it's almost all propertied men; the women, laborers, tenants, and kids are mostly just not there. The project lead says our jobs is to describe what's in the boxes, not to speculate about who isn't, because the gaps are just accidents of what survived and reading into them would be "inventing people." He also says a database has no place for guesses. But I can't shake the feeling that the absences aren't random, and that treating the record as if it's the whole picture is itself a kind of distortion. Is he right that we should stick to what's there? If not, is there a defensible way to represent the missing people without making things up? I guess I'm asking what a historian or philosopher would say about who gets recorded and whether the silence itself says something.
