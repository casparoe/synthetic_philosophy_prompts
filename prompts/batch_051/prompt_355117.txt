I'm a student in a political philosophy course, and we've been working through J.S. Mill's On Liberty and the harm principle — the idea, roughly, that the only legitimate ground for restricting a person's liberty is to prevent harm to others. I've been trying to apply this principle to cases Mill never anticipated: harms that occur entirely within persistent virtual worlds. Consider three cases. First, one player carries out a sustained harassment campaign against another through their avatars, and the victim comes to feel serious anxiety and dread about logging on, though no physical body is ever touched. Second, a player destroys or steals another player's virtual home and inventory — items that took hundreds of hours to build and that can be sold for real money on an external market. Third, a player subjects another player's avatar to a non-consensual and humiliating act; the victim's body is untouched, but the experience causes enduring psychological distress. My classmates are split. Some say these are plainly harms to others in Mill's sense, especially since he protected interests like security and property, not just bodily integrity. Others reply that this stretches the concept of harm until almost any offense or bruised feeling could be regulated, and that Mill himself was suspicious of arguments that reach a harm only through a long chain of indirect effects.

I want you to predict, as concretely as you can, how Mill would most likely resolve this: would he regard a liberal state as permitted, under his harm principle, to restrict liberty in order to prevent such mediated or virtual harms? Don't give a bare answer. Give exactly five distinct distinctions, considerations, or principles you think Mill would actually deploy — for example, the line between physical and psychological harm, the distinction between harm and mere offense, his treatment of self-regarding versus other-regarding conduct, his wariness of remote or indirect harms, and the relevance of interests such as property and security. Number the five items one through five, write each as one or two sentences, give no introduction or conclusion, and in each item state explicitly whether that consideration pushes toward permitting regulation, toward forbidding it, or cuts both ways.
