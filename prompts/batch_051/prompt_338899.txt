I'm a novelist, a third of the way into a near-future book where a grief counselor starts to suspect that her city—maybe her entire remembered life—is running as an ancestor simulation on hardware built by some far-future descendant of our's. I've been working through the simulation argument itself (Bostrom's trilemma, roughly: either civilizations almost never reach the point of running ancestor simulations, or they almost never bother to, or we are probably in one). But whenever I talk to people about it, or read the pop-science explainers, everyone reaches for the same analogy to make it vivid: we are like characters in a video game. The world is the level, physics is the engine, each of us is an NPC running mostly scripted behavior, and the simulators are the players—or the designers, depending on who's talking. The analogy gets used to license two different attitudes, and I want to know how much weight either one can bear: some people use it to say we're probably simulated and that's fine, because the game is real enough to the characters in it; others use it to say nothing much matters here, and our choices are as scripted as an NPC's side quest.

Can you pull this apart for me? I'd like an explicit correspondence table—what maps onto what between a video game and the simulation argument—and then the places where the mapping breaks. In particular, I want to know which features of the game side get quietly imported into the argument without being justified (the player, the scripted NPC behavior, rendering only what's on screen, the whole "it's just a game" emotional register), and which features of the simulation argument would need to be in place but have no real analogue in a video game (for instance, actually conscious simulated minds rather than cleverly animated ones). Then tell me whether the two conclusions people draw from the analogy survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and sketch a better analogy if there is one.

Also, since I'll probably bend this material into dialogue later: can you seperate the parts that are basically settled among people who work on this from the parts that are genuinely contested—especially the steps I should not have a character state as if they were the consensus—rather than presenting one camp as the obvious view?
