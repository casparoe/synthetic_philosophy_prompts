Last week my reading group was working through the Zhuangzi—the story about Cook Ding carving up an ox without his blade ever going dull, and the passage where Confucius tells Yan Hui to fast the mind—and two of the members, Jen and Marcus, got into a long argument I couldn't referee. You're an expert on classical Chinese thought and the philosophy of action. Please think carefully, brainstorm the strongest version of each side before you commit, be charitable to each move before criticizing it, and then adjudicate the whole thing move by move.

Here's roughly how it went, reconstructed from my notes.

(1) Jen said the point of those stories is that effortless action is real but it's not magic: it's the end product of training. Cook Ding didn't start out effortless; he learned the joints over years until the skill sank below conscious thought. So wu-wei is something you can aim at the way you aim at any ability—drill the components until they become automatic. Modern psychology just calls this automaticity. No paradox, just practice.

(2) Marcus objected that this flattens the whole idea. If wu-wei is just automatized skill, then touch-typing or doomscrolling on your phone would count as wu-wei, which is ridiculous. The text's whole thrust, he said, is against deliberate self-improvement: fasting the mind is literally a teacher telling a student to stop working so hard at being virtuous. The trap is that the more you aim at being effortless, the more you monitor yourself—and self-monitoring is the opposite of the state.

(3) Jen shot back that this is still an instruction from a teacher to a student who came for instruction. What is fasting the mind if not a technique? The passage doesn't say do nothing; it tells him to stop listening with the ears and listen with qi, or something like that—which is a prescription. Even Marcus's own objection, she said, smuggles in a goal: he wants her to stop aiming so that she'll fall into the state. That's just trying not to try. She hasn't escaped the structure of trying; she's moved it one level up.

(4) Marcus answered that there's a difference between a first-order action and managing the conditions for one. He wasn't saying try to enter wu-wei. He was saying: rearrange you're life—who you spend time with, what you practice, where you put your attention—so the meddling self has less to do. That's like setting up circumstances in which a realization can occur, not manufacturing the realization directly. The state can be caused without being aimed at, because causes run below the level of intention.

(5) Jen said that if rearranging your conditions reliably produces effortlessness, then effortlessness is produced by a deliberate plan—and so it is, after all, an effect of a strategy. Calling the strategy indirect doesn't dodge anything; it just hides the aiming inside the word conditions. And if the rearrangement isn't reliable, then the advice is just do some stuff and hope, which is luck, not guidance.

(6) Marcus replied that the paradox only shows up if you assume intentional production is the only kind of production. It isn't. You can deliberately walk into a dark room knowing you'll bump your shin, without aiming at the bump. A teacher can say something knowing it will get a student to stop straining, even though the stopping isn't what the student is aiming at in that moment. Wu-wei, he said, is like the stopping: a real result, deliberatly usable in practice, without having to be the content of anyone's first-order aim.

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the six moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is right about which specific points, what (if anything) both of them are missing, and who is more right overall.
