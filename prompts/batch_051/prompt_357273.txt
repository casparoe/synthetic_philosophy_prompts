You're a political philosopher who works on the concept of corruption, and I want you to build me a thought experiment rather than answer a straight conceptual question. The target is a case where two standard-ish accounts of corruption come apart in one particular direction. Account A is the private-gain account: roughly, corruption is when an official abuses their office for personal enrichment — it's something like a quid pro quo. Account B is the impartiality account: roughly, corruption is when an official departs from treating like cases alike and hands out public goods, permissions or attention on the basis of allegiance, personal ties or expected future support rather than public rules. I want a case where Account A says not corrupt, and Account B says corrupt (clientelism, basically) — but the A-verdict has to be genuinely plausible, not just the result of the official being naive.

Constraints: one paragraph for the scenario; realistic and specific (a local council, a small-town mayoralty, a housing office — something like that, nothing grand); set in a wealthy Western country, not a caricature of somewhere 'developing'; nothing the official does can be illegal; and the two accounts must not disagree about the facts — everyone in the scenario agrees on exactly what happened. Make the conduct a standing practice rather than a one-off gift, something like a 'favour bank' the official keeps running for supporters.

Then do the walkthrough. State precisely what the practice is and which feature of the relationship is doing the work. Show, step by step, what Account A says and why, and what Account B says and why. Tell me which single feature of the case is carrying all the weight, and what happens if you remove it — for instance, if a small personal cash kickback or a holiday gets added, does the case collapse back into ordinary bribery where both accounts agree? Then play the devil's advocate: how would a defender of Account A attack your case and try to keep the 'not corrupt' verdict stable — maybe by saying it's just constituency service or democratic responsiveness?

Before you settle on a final case, brainstorm two or three candidates and say briefly wich you rejected and why. Don't just stipulate that one account calls it corrupt — the verdicts have to fall out of the facts and the two accounts. Think carefully about wether your official is gaining something personal after all (re-election? status? a future favour returned?) and whether that should matter to Account A.
