I recently began a research post in cosmology, and a colleague's offhand question — do you actually believe in dark matter? — exposed a policy I had apparently been following for years without noticing it. I use dark matter, dark energy, and the whole apparatus of halos and inflaton fields in my work: I run simulations, fit curves, and write papers that treat these things as real, unobservable machinery. But I never allow them into what I genuinely believe. My standing rule is that I withhold belief in the unobservable posits of even the most empirically successful scientific theories, because the history of science looks like a long lesson in exactly this: successful theories are repeatedly overturned, and their central unobservable machinery is usually the part that gets discarded. I use the theories instrumentally, and suspend judgement about whatever lies behind the predictions. A philosopher friend recently named the rule for me — the pessimistic meta-induction — which is how I came to notice I had been living by it.

Is this policy defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as epistemic caution? I can already see two worries. The rule seems self-undermining, since my confidence that science has a reliably poor record on unobservables rests on present science and testimony, which the rule itself would tell me to mistrust. It also seems too blunt: some posits survive theory change, and the boundary between the observable and the unobservable shifts as instruments and indirect detection improve. What I want is an assessment of the policy as a policy: when it is reliable, when it fails, and whether it can be restated so as to keep whatever protection it offers against overconfidence without producing a blanket refusal to believe anything I cannot literally see. And how would I tell, from the inside, whether I draw the line where the evidence of history genuinely supports it, or merely where it lets me avoid the discomfort of holding strong beliefs?
