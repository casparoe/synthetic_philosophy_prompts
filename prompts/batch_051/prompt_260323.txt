Write a dialogue of at most 900 words between two equally competent characters who disagree over whether an agent can assign a probability to their own choice while still genuinely deliberating about it. The first defends the Bayesian view that refusing to assign such probabilities leaves you vulnerable to Dutch books and that you already hold degrees of belief about your own future behavior. The second defends the view that deliberation crowds out prediction: attaching a probability to an option that is still open either treats the option as an already-settled fact or smuggles a plan in under the guise of evidence. Set the scene somewhere mundane — a bookmaker offering odds on the first character's next move, for instance — and do not stage it around Newcomb's problem. Each character should answer the other's actual last point at least twice, and each should make one partial concession that sharpens rather than resolves the disagreement. End with the first-personal question left genuinely open: what, exactly, the probability is supposed to track. Do not cite any literature.
