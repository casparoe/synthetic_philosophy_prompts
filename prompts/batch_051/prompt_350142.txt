The part that won't die in my head is the circularity worry: the account says X causes Y iff there's some possible intervention on X that changes Y (holding background fixed), but an intervention is then defined in blatantly causal terms — the intervention has to cause X, it can't affect Y except through X, it has to be independent of the other causes of Y, and so on. Woodward's line is that this circularity is harmless, because he isn't offering a reduction of causal talk to non-causal talk, just a way of regimenting it. I think the circularity might be doing more work than that, and I want a case that shows it.

Build me a concrete, realistic thought experiment — a field study, a lab experiment, a public-health rollout, nothing science-fictional — where a researcher follows Woodward's instructions as closely as anyone actually could: they find a manipulable variable, randomize it if possible, hold things fixed, and observe the effect. The catch: whether their manipulation counts as a genuine Woodward intervention should depend on exactly the causal structure they're trying to test, so two reasonable researchers with all the same data could disagree about whether an intervention even happened, and the disagreement should trace to a specific clause in the definition of an intervention, not to sloppiness on the scientist's part.

Then walk me through it: state the target cause and effect, the putative intervention variable, which clauses of the intervention definition it satisfies and which clause it does or might violate, and why that clause is the one doing the causal work rather than anything empirical. Tell me which structural feature of the case makes the circularity actually bite, what happens to the case if you remove that feature (does it collapse back into an ordinary confounding story where everyone agrees no intervention occurred?), and what a Woodward-style defender would say to defuse the case.
