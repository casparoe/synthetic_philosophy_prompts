I have been working through Pascal's remarks on justice and force, and I would like help turning my scattered reactions into a single principle. As I understand him, Pascal's thought runs something like this. Justice by itself is inert: a perfectly just rule that no one can or will enforce protects nobody and changes nothing. Force by itself is mere domination: coercion that is not answerable to justice is tyranny, no matter how orderly or efficient it looks. The proper arrangement is therefore a union of the two — force placed in the service of justice, and justice equipped with the force it needs to be effective. He adds, more cynically, that since societies find it hard to make the just strong, they generally settle for making the strong look just, dressing established force in the language of right. The slogan attracts me, but my gut reactions to particular cases keep pulling against it.

Here are my verdicts. (1) A judge applies a sentencing statute she privately believes is unjust — an excessive mandatory minimum — and I regard her act as permissible, though I blame the legislature for the law. (2) Police enforce that same statute with needless brutality against a nonviolent offender, and I think the brutality is a further wrong, worse than the statute itself. (3) A just workplace-safety law is never enforced because the regulator has been starved of funds, and workers continue to be injured; I want to say the situation is still unjust even though the law on the books is just. (4) A vigilante beats a man he knows to be guilty of a violent crime whom the state failed to convict on a technicality; I think the beating is wrong even though the man is guilty and the outcome is, in a crude sense, deserved. (5) Protesters blockade a government building to stop the enforcement of a law I agree is unjust; I think their use of force can be justified. (6) Officers use the minimum force necessary to arrest someone violently resisting a plainly just public-health order, and I take this to be the clearest case of legitimate coercion.

The pair that troubles me is (1) and (4). In the first I accept official force in the service of an unjust law, while in the fourth I condemn private force in the service of a just outcome. That looks like the reverse of what Pascal's maxim would predict, unless some further principle about authority, role, or standing is doing the real work. I want to know whether one principle can capture all six verdicts — or, failing that, which verdicts I should revise. Please state the principle precisely, say what it predicts about a case I have not listed, and say whether Pascal's maxim should be repaired, restricted, or abandoned in light of it. If your principle relies on a distinction between public and private force, or on role-based permission, defend it against the objection that this merely blesses whoever already holds power rather than tracking a genuine moral difference.
