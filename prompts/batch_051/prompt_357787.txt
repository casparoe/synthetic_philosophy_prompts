I have been trying to get clear on iterated amplification and distillation (IDA) in the AI alignment literature, and there is one issue I keep losing my grip on. Let me state the setup as I understand it. A human H, together with a model A, forms an amplified system H[A]: H can break a hard task into subtasks, check A's attempts at each subtask, ask follow-up questions, and assemble the checked pieces into a final answer. A new model A' is then trained to imitate H[A] on a distribution of tasks; A' replaces A, and the cycle repeats. The stated hope is that competence goes up while alignment is maintained, because every piece of the amplified answer was, at some point, checked by a human.

The point I want nailed down is the worry that decomposition does not preserve alignment or competence, even when each subtask looks good to a careful human. I can repeat the usual one-liners — the sum of checked parts can be an unchecked whole; distillation captures the outputs without the checking; the human's choice of how to decompose the task is itself a place where misalignment can enter; iteration can deepen a subtle error rather than cancel it — but I cannot tell which of these are vague analogies and which are precise failure cases.

Please explain the conceptual core of this worry, in a way that does not depend on the details of any one proposed scheme. In particular: (a) give the strongest precise case you can for how a composed solution made from human-approved subtasks could still be misaligned or less competent than the pieces suggest; (b) say what exactly is lost in distillation, given that distillation imitates the amplified system's outputs rather than its checking, backtracking, and questions; and (c) say why repeating the cycle might make the problem worse rather than better, and under what assumptions that conclusion holds. If a small toy example makes the failure mode sharp, use one; if the genuine worry resists clean toy cases, say so.

Please keep the explanation self-contained: no reading lists or citations, just the reasoning. And at the end, explicitly distinguish what is settled or nearly settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on the topic, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
