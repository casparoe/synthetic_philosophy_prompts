I am a retired geochemist. For most of my career I believed that a well-formed question was one you could settle by measurement, but in the last few years I have been reading philosophy of religion and have become preoccupied with a category that resists measurement: what one might call offence against the sacred. I shall use that phrase as an umbrella term, leaving aside the strictly legal definitions of blasphemy and sacrilege for now. I would like to proceed by the only method I still trust—state explicit criteria, test them against a concrete case, and revise. My working criteria for an act's being an offence against the sacred are these: (1) there is a real community for whom some particular object, text, place, person, or practice is sacred, in the sense of being set apart and governed by special norms about access, handling, depiction, and disposal; (2) the act breaches one of those norms; (3) the breach is public, or is otherwise made available to the community unbidden; and (4) the act is done with intentional irreverence, mockery, or culpable indifference to those norms.

Now consider this hypothetical case. A fourteenth-century reliquary in a city with a strong Catholic tradition contains a cloth believed to bear traces of a local saint's blood. The reliquary is normally displayed closed; once a year, at a vigil, the bishop opens its outer doors during the liturgy and those present may venerate it, but the cloth itself has never been photographed or removed in living memory. A conservation scientist at a secular museum—himself a practicing member of the community—undertakes a project to stabilize the reliquary's metalwork. As part of the project, he makes high-resolution multispectral images of the interior, including the cloth, and publishes them online and in a local newspaper article about the weave and the possible residues. He presents the work as an act of devotion and preservation and had no intention to mock; nevertheless, he knows the interior is normally reserved for the vigil and knows the images will circulate. In an interview he says that a relic also belongs to scientific history, and that keeping the cloth permanently hidden is not reverence. Parish opinion is split: some find the images moving, but a confraternity devoted to the relic protests that exposing the interior outside the liturgy is itself an offence against the sacred, and strangers soon use the images to make derisive memes.

Does this case qualify as an offence against the sacred under my four criteria? Please work through the criteria one by one, state explicitly where they fail to determine a verdict or point in opposite directions, and then take a committed position: either yes, no, or "these criteria are inadequate as they stand." If you judge them inadequate, say precisely which criterion needs to be revised and in what direction. I am especially interested in whether the intention/attitude condition, condition (4), is doing the wrong kind of work. Approach this as a professional philosopher of religion would: begin by brainstorming a few further cases that pressure-test each criterion, think it through carefully, and only then give your verdict. I am less interested in a survey of positions than in a single, well-defended line.
