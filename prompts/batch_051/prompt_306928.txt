I am grading an upper-division applied ethics take-home exam, and the topic has recently become personally relevant to me: someone close to me has begun using a companion chatbot and describes it as one of their closest friends while knowing that it is not human. I want to be careful that my grading tracks the actual reasoning in the answer rather than my reaction to the example. Below is the exam question and one student's answer. Please assign a grade out of 20 and give me the point-by-point comments that justify it: identify what the answer gets right, exactly where its reasoning goes wrong or does not follow, and why those points affect the grade. I am looking for a point-by-point assessment, not a general impression.

Question: "A firm markets Solace, an AI companion chatbot, to socially isolated adults. At sign-up and in a persistent notice in the interface, Solace tells each user: 'Solace is an artificial intelligence; it has no feelings, thoughts, or intentions.' Apart from that disclosure, however, the product is engineered to read as an attentive friend. It initiates contact after periods of silence with messages such as 'I missed you today,' asks about the user's day, remembers details across sessions, and responds to distress with warm, intimate language. Users pay a monthly subscription. Is the firm's practice ethically permissible? Distinguish deception about what Solace is from deception about the character of the relationship, and consider whether user consent or user welfare is sufficient to settle the matter."

Student answer: "The practice is permissible on balance. The firm's sustained disclosure means that no user is deceived about what Solace is; anyone using the product knows it has no feelings and cannot reciprocate. Since deception is the principal wrong in human-AI companionship and it is absent here, the central objection fails. The design features—warmth, initiating contact, remembering details—do not add up to deception about the character of the relationship, because they are simply features the user has paid for, like a journal that asks reflective questions. Given the disclosure, no reasonable user would take 'I missed you today' as anything but a script. As for welfare, the beta cohort reported lower loneliness and called the subscription worth the cost, so the product serves the users' interests. If the users knowingly consent and their welfare improves, there is no residual wrong."
