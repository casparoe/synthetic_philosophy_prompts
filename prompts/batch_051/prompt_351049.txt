You are a professional philosopher specialising in applied ethics. I am a retired natural scientist — I spent my career in ecology, and I have only recently begun reading moral philosophy seriously. I would like you to help me think through an asymmetry that seems to organise ordinary judgment about animal suffering. Please work carefully: begin with an explicit brainstorming pass in which you list candidate justifications, then evaluate them rather than moving straight to a verdict.

Here is the asymmetry. Hold the animal and the injury fixed. A rabbit receives a slow, painful, ultimately fatal wound. When the cause is a fox, most people say this is simply nature: it is sad, but no one had a duty to intervene, and intervening would have been meddling. When the cause is a pet dog, most people say the owner should have prevented it. When the cause is a human being, it is cruelty, and the law treats it as a crime. The rabbit's experience does not vary across the three cases; only the identity of the agent varies.

The same pattern reappears at larger scales. We spend public money cleaning and rehabilitating seabirds after an oil spill, because the harm is anthropogenic, but we do not respond in the same way to a natural event that kills or injures the same species. And in food production, an institution — not a solitary agent — does the harming: a broiler chicken or a feedlot steer lives and dies under conditions that would produce outrage if a private person inflicted the same injuries on a pet. A field mouse killed by the combine harvester that supplied the grain is no less an institutional effect, yet its death is commonly treated as negligible, if it is treated at all. In these cases the moral question is often handled as a matter of policy, price, and consumer choice rather than as cruelty.

I am trying to determine whether the distinction between “natural” and “anthropogenic” suffering, and between an individual agent and an institution, can carry the weight ordinary judgment places on it.

Please do the following. (1) Brainstorm the strongest candidate justifications for treating the same animal pain differently according to who or what causes it — for example: we have special obligations only for harms we cause; predation is normal or necessary for ecosystems; natural harms are not injustices because no agent commits them; intervening in wild systems is too risky or unwarranted; an individual agent's act reveals a vice while an institution's effects do not; or anything else you can construct. (2) Test each candidate against nearby cases: the fox, the pet dog, and the human with the same rabbit; a porpoise killed by an orca versus one drowned in a fishing net; a seabird harmed by an oil spill versus one harmed by a naturally occurring algal bloom; a field mouse killed by a combine harvester versus a mouse deliberately killed by a person. Distinguish, in each rationale, the empirical claim from the normative claim. (3) Then press the best-surviving rationale on the industrial cases: if a broiler chicken's suffering is entirely anthropogenic and institutional, does the distinction still do any work, and what follows for someone who condemns the child who injures a rabbit but buys the chicken? Finally, tell me which parts of the asymmetry — if any — can be kept, and whether the rest should be restricted or abandoned.
