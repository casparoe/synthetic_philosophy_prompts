I am a novelist, and a question that began as a problem of characterization has hardened into a philosophical one. The book I am drafting concerns a small city that, a generation after a massacre, quietly stops teaching the massacre in its schools, votes down a memorial, and lets the annual commemoration lapse. My protagonist, a local teacher, regards this as a profound moral failure. Yet this same character once told her sister, who had been badly wronged in her twenties, that she admired her for refusing to live in the past; she meant that the sister's willingness to let an old injury go was a sign of health and generosity, not a betrayal. The novel thus appears to assume that a person who forgets a wrong done to her is, if anything, doing something admirable, while a community that forgets a wrong done to some of its members is compounding the wrong.

I want to know whether that asymmetry in ordinary judgment can be defended, or whether it is an inconsistency we have simply learned to live with. In daily life we are inclined to treat an individual who keeps an old wound open — who revisits the anniversary year after year, refuses reconciliation, insists that others know the details — as stuck, perhaps even resentful, and we urge her to move on. In public life we treat a polity that forgets an old atrocity — that ends the commemoration, drops the history from the curriculum, leaves the site unmarked — as adding a second injury to the first. Is there a single principle that yields both verdicts, or only a psychological difference in how we picture individual memory and collective memory? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the case of the sole surviving victim, who is also the last member of the wronged community; if you would give it up, say which side of the asymmetry should be surrendered and why. Keep your answer to no more than 500 words.
