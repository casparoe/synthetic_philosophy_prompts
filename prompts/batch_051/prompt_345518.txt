I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've been dragged into a running argument with a faction on my team that wants our internal documentation rewritten in what they call "etymologically correct" English. They maintain that each word has one true meaning, fixed by its root, and that later usage is a kind of defect. As near as I can tell, this is the etymological fallacy, though I couldn't defend that label in a seminar. Their actual examples: "decimate" may only mean reduce by one-tenth; "nice" properly means foolish or ignorant, so calling a code review "nice" is false; "awful" properly means worthy of awe. I've tried pointing out that a word's history is not a specification to which present use must conform, and I've made no headway. I'd rather make the point with humor than with another Slack thread.

Please write a 500–650 word satirical piece in the form of release notes for a fictional open-source command-line tool called `etymon-lint`, announced as "a linter for semantic drift." The voice should be deadpan developer documentation; the page can include version notes, rule definitions, a deprecation notice, and a short FAQ. The tool should scan English prose (READMEs, commit messages, code review comments, incident reports) and fail any word that is not being used in its earliest known or root-derived sense, treating historical change as a build error. Use the examples above as a starting point and add a few of your own. The target is the practice of appealing to a word's origin to settle its current meaning, not any particular person; exaggerate that practice rather than inventing a straw man. The humor should get the actual fallacy right: a word's origin is a fact about its history, not a binding rule on its current meaning. Let the consequences show themselves — for instance, the tool's own prose should quietly trip its own rules. Don't break character, and don't include citations or a reading list.
