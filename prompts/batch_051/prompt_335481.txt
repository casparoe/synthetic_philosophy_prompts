I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I have been reading about the frame problem in AI and I would like an honest assessment of a way of thinking about it that I have been developing. I call it the Cache-Invalidation Account of the Frame Problem. In software, when some underlying inputs change, you do not recompute every derived value; you track dependencies and invalidate only the values whose dependency paths touch the changed inputs, treating everything else as valid by default. I want to claim that a solution to the frame problem is exactly this: an agent's beliefs are cached inferences over a world model, an action mutates a small set of base facts, and the agent invalidates precisely the cached beliefs that transitively depend on those facts. On this account, frame axioms are not a list of non-changes that must be explicitly stated; they are cache entries that survived invalidation, and the persistence default is justified by the dependency graph rather than stipulated. The account also predicts that frame-problem failures should look like stale-cache bugs: either a belief whose dependency was touched but whose invalidation was missed, or over-invalidation that thrashes the system recomputing what never needed recomputation.

I am aware this could be a restatement of the problem rather than a solution, or something worked out decades ago under a name I do not know. Give me the three strongest objections to this account, say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable, and tell me whether it is materially different from things like the common-sense law of inertia or nonmonotonic reasoning.
