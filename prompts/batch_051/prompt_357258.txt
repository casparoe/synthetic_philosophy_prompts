I teach a graduate seminar in biomedical ethics, and this term I have been drawing on my own service on a university research ethics board as material for our sessions on moral bioenhancement, by which I mean pharmacological interventions meant to make people more self-controlled, fair, generous, or compassionate. Reviewing the board's minutes, I am struck by something: we have never stated a single governing principle, yet our decisions across several years display a recognizable shape. Three cases stand out. In the first, we approved a trial of a drug that strengthens inhibitory control and long-range planning in people with repeated convictions for violent assault, to be administered as a condition of early release from prison. In the second, we approved a voluntary program offering surgical residents a mild vigilance- and conscientiousness-promoting agent intended to reduce perioperative errors. In the third, we rejected a proposal to give healthy volunteers a nasal spray that directly induces a short-lived feeling of warm benevolence and gratitude, for a study of its effects on self-reported well-being.

Next month the board must decide a different kind of case. A police department proposes to require officers with a documented record of escalation in use-of-force incidents to take, as a condition of continuing in street patrol, a drug that does not improve deliberation or self-control at all, but directly elevates empathy and dampens the fear and anger that flare during ambiguous encounters. The intervention would act straight on the officers' sentiments rather than on their own capacities for moral reasoning, yet it is defended precisely as a way to protect citizens from serious harm.

I would like you to work through how a board that made our three earlier decisions ought, for the sake of consistency, to rule on this new proposal. What are the candidate principles that would fit the three past verdicts equally well, and which of them pull apart on the police case? For each leading principle, say what it predicts and why. Then offer your best prediction about the board's decision, identify the closest analog among the earlier cases, state candidly what principle the board would be committing itself to either way, and propose one further case of your own invention whose outcome would most cleanly settle which principle the board is actually following.
