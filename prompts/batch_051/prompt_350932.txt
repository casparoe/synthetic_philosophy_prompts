Here is an asymmetry in ordinary moral judgment that I want you to subject to pressure. Two housemates with different musical tastes alternate control of the kitchen speaker, each giving up some listening time in exchange for more of the music they prefer; two countries with different comparative advantages lower tariffs on each other's goods; an employee swaps hours for wages. In each case, the parties realize what an economist would call a Pareto improvement: each gives up something they value less for something they value more, and both are better off by their own standards. Ordinary judgment finds these transactions unremarkable, often cooperative and desirable.

Now apply the same structure to moral values rather than ordinary preferences. Consider two advocacy groups with different value systems. One works to reduce farmed animal suffering and ranks that as the gravest issue; the other works for humane prison conditions and ranks that as the gravest issue. Each genuinely regards the other's cause as a serious good, though lower on its own ranking. They learn that, because of their different contacts and credibility with different legislators, each could produce more progress on its own cause if it spent some of its effort advancing the other's cause in exchange for reciprocal effort. By their own estimates, both groups would end up with more of what they all-things-considered value. Yet many people would hesitate to call this a clean exchange; they might call it a compromise of conviction or a sell-out, and many would rather forgo the gains than have their side spend real effort on another cause under such a deal.

The two cases seem formally identical: parties with different rankings exchange contribution across those rankings, and each expects to gain by its own lights. So I want to know whether anything justifies treating the ordinary preference trade as innocent and the moral trade as something to resist. Is there a defensible principle that permits the first and condemns the second—one based on integrity, on a difference between values and preferences, on the expressive character of action, on incommensurability, or on something else? Or is moral trade simply ordinary trade with a taboo attached, leaving moral gains on the table?

Give me the strongest candidate justification for the asymmetry, test it against at least one nearby case of your own construction (for example, a version in which each side supports the other's cause through donations rather than advocacy, or a version in which the two causes conflict directly rather than merely through the allocation of effort), and then deliver a clear verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or dropped. In doing so, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
