In John Maynard Keynes's 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," he writes:

> My purpose in this essay, however, is not to examine the present or the near future, but to disembarrass myself of short views and take wings into the future. What can we reasonably expect the level of our economic life to be a hundred years hence? What are the economic possibilities for our grandchildren?

I have adopted an epistemic policy modeled on this passage, and I want to know whether it is defensible or merely a way of protecting my beliefs about the good life. I now refuse to engage with any argument about how we should live, or about whether current economic institutions are serving human flourishing, unless that argument is framed as a claim about the next hundred years. I treat any objection that appeals to present conditions—stagnant wages, precarity, inequality, the texture of work today—as too "short view" to count. My reasoning is that the present is always noisy and crisis-laden, and a conviction about the good life that cannot survive projection far into the future is not a serious one. But I can see that I may have built a filter that conveniently shields my preferred social vision from uncomfortable short-term counter-evidence. Is this a defensible way to allocate my attention, or a bias dressed up as a rule? How would I tell the difference from the inside, and how should the policy be restated, if at all, to keep whatever insight Keynes is pointing at without the evasion?
