I'm putting together a handout for an undergraduate metaphysics seminar on the special composition question — roughly, the question of when some things, the xs, compose a further thing. The students have read van Inwagen's treatment of it, so they can produce the usual labels (nihilism, universalism, organicism, and the moderate answers he considers and rejects, such as contact, fastening, cohesion, and fusion), but I've found that their labels don't reliably track what they actually think about cases. I'd like you to write a decision tree, presented as continuous prose, that sorts someone into a position purely by what they say about particular cases, with no question that simply asks which label they accept.

Where possible, use diagnostic cases of the kind van Inwagen himself presses on: two people shaking hands, a severed head, a fresh corpse, a heap of loose sand, a chair that is gradually disassembled, a committee, a flock of birds, an atom and its parts. Each node should be labeled by the claim or case judgment that splits the remaining views, and each leaf should carry the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody actually defends it) along with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from the list of names, and where a combination of answers is unoccupied, briefly diagnose whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any positions the tree cannot separate — for example, if two moderate answers differ only over which relation suffices for composition — rather than pretending the tree pulls them apart.

Please keep the whole instrument to roughly ten nodes, suitable for a forty-minute discussion section, and write it in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write an email or a letter.
