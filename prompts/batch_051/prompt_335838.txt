I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about when its okay to use mental words — believes, wants, expects, tries — for nonhuman systems, going by my gutt on cases. I have no problem saying a chess engine "wants" to win when it sacrifices a piece, but I balk at saying a thermostat "thinks" the room is cold; that just feels like shorthand. I'll happily say an LLM "understood" my question, but I won't say it "was glad to help" even when it outputs those exact words — and yet I do say the chess engine "wants" to win even though it has no more feelings than the LLM. I say my phone's autocorrect "tries" to fix my typos, but I don't think it tries anything at all. A self-driving car that slows for a pedestrian: I'm fine saying it "expects" the pedestrian to step out, but I'd never say it "cares" about them. And when an LLM confidently gives a wrong answer, I don't think it "believes" the wrong answer — but then I realize I also don't think it "believes" the right ones, so I'm not sure why I was ever tempted to say it "knows" things. Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? Would Dennett's intentional stance — roughly, attribute beliefs and desires when doing so best predicts behavior — deliver my verdicts, or would it force me to drop some? And what does the principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a robot that tells me "I don't have beliefs," then corrects itself and says "actually, I believe you're asking a philosophy question."
