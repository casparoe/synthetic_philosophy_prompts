I spent thirty-odd years on chemical equilibria and catalysis and only recently started reading philosophy and the "doing good" literature, so forgive me if this is old hat. There's a move I keep running into on the systems-thinking side of that world: a small marginal fix in a stable but broken system gets absorbed — build a clinic and it sits unstaffed because the ministry won't allocate nurses, ship in food and local grain prices fall and farmers plant less next season. The claim is that if you don't shift the underlying structure, the system just adjusts around your intervention and undoes most of it.

That sounds, almost point for point, like Le Chatelier's principle. A system at equilibrium, when you perturb it, shifts in whichever direction partly cancels the perturbation: heat it and the endothermic direction is favored; dump in product and the reverse reaction picks up. The systems people seem to be treating a stable social system as a kind of equilibrium that buffers external pushes.

I can't decide whether this is a deep structural analogy or just pattern-matching. In chemistry the response is blind, automatic, and only partial — you reach a new equilibrium, but you don't get full cancellation of the perturbation. Human systems are full of agents who can notice the intervention and choose to sabotage it, amplify it, or route around it; and sometimes a small push genuinely tips the whole thing, which Le Chatelier would never predict. So maybe the analogy breaks exactly at the point that matters. But then why does it keep feeling apt? I'd like someone to actually work through what would have to be true of the social case for the transfer to go through, and to name a consequence this analogy predicts that ends up false — rather than just telling me "social systems aren't chemical systems."
