Please act as an expert on Kierkegaard's social criticism and the history of media, and think carefully before you answer. I recently left social media, and the friends who persuaded me kept describing what they thought it had done to me in Kierkegaardian terms: they said platforms dissolve concrete communities into an anonymous, abstract public that forms opinions about everything and takes responsibility for nothing. I want to know how far that comparison genuinely holds. Audit it as an analogy between, on one side, Kierkegaard's attack on the daily press and his category of the public in mid-nineteenth-century Copenhagen and, on the other, contemporary social media platforms and their users. Start by brainstorming the correspondences: what maps onto the daily press, the editor, the reader, the anonymous public, leveling, and the chatter of the present age? Then give the disanalogies in an organized way — the advertising-based business model, algorithmic curation, readers who are also producers, pseudonymous rather than anonymous authorship, speed and scale, and anything else you find. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the analogy's intended conclusion from the ones that do not. End by judging whether that conclusion — roughly, that social media erodes committed individual existence and levels values — survives the audit, or whether the analogy has been doing the real work. If the analogy fails, propose a better one.
