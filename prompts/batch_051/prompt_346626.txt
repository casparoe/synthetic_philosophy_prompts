In T. H. Huxley’s essay On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata, Huxley argues on comparative-anatomical grounds that the behaviour of non-human animals is produced by nervous machinery, with consciousness accompanying that machinery but not contributing to it; he then suggests that the same reasoning cannot plausibly be prevented from applying to human beings. This extension produces a difficulty that is now often described as the paradox of phenomenal judgment. For if a person’s assertion that consciousness is epiphenomenal is itself caused entirely by neural states, while the conscious experience of understanding the argument is causally idle, it is unclear how the conscious experience is supposed to justify or explain the assertion. Please give an intuitive explanation of why this difficulty is thought to be genuinely damaging to epiphenomenalism, rather than a merely verbal puzzle. Step through the reasoning carefully, and then say which premise the objection really targets: is it the causal claim, the account of knowledge, or the assumption that justification must be causally transmitted? Avoid surveying the literature; I want the underlying structure of the problem made transparent.
