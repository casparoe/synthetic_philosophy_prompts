You are an experienced designer of economics and political philosophy teaching simulations, and you know the early- to mid-twentieth-century socialist calculation debate well: Mises's challenge that rational economic calculation is impossible without market prices for capital goods, Hayek's restatement of the problem as one of dispersed and often tacit local knowledge, and the market-socialist reply associated with Oskar Lange, in which a planning board adjusts posted prices by trial and error in response to shortages and surpluses. Think carefully before committing to a design, and do not settle for the first idea.

Design a runnable classroom activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the history of economic thought and political philosophy: 16 students, 90 minutes. The topic is whether comprehensive central planning can in principle allocate resources rationally. There should be no lecture up front, and the names Mises, Hayek, and Lange should not be used until the debrief. The idea must show up in what participants do and in what goes wrong for them, before it is named. By the end, participants should come away seeing, from the inside, why Hayek thought the decisive problem is not computational difficulty but the fact that much of the knowledge needed for production is local, dispersed, and hard to transmit in aggregate form, and why a Lange-style price board can appear to repair things for a while without touching the deeper local-knowledge and incentive problems.

The activity should put participants in a small simulated economy with distinct roles, such as a planning authority, enterprise managers, suppliers, and households, using paper, cards, tokens, and a whiteboard rather than software. The rules and payoffs should be arranged so that participants run into the real obstacles themselves: first, the near-impossibility of comparing production methods when no prices exist for intermediate goods; then the possibility of repairing this with a Lange-style scheme in which the planner posts and adjusts prices in response to reported shortages and surpluses; and finally a Hayekian round in which local shocks, such as a broken machine, a delayed delivery, or a shift in local demand, are plainly visible to participants on the ground but cannot be fully transmitted to the planner through aggregate reports, even though everyone is honest and well-motivated. Specify roles and rules, payoffs, the exact materials, and a precise timeline for the 90 minutes, including setup and debrief.

Before presenting the final activity, briefly brainstorm at least two candidate mechanics, for example a card-driven production-chain game, a local-knowledge role-play with hidden information, or an auction-style mechanism, and explain which you chose and why it forces the target insight rather than a generic coordination failure. Then give the polished, facilitator-ready instructions.

The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the positions in the debate: the initial Mises problem, the Lange price-adjustment reply, and Hayek's displaced objection. It should say what predictably goes wrong when the activity is run and how to handle it, and it should end with an honest note on which features of the idea the activity cannot reproduce, such as the scale of a real economy, genuinely tacit knowledge, long-run innovation and investment under uncertainty, and the incentive problems of actual socialist economies, and what the facilitator must therefore supply in discussion. Throughout the debrief and facilitator notes, keep what is settled about the historical debate clearly separated from what remains genuinely contested among specialists: for example, do not present the claim that Hayek simply won or that Lange refuted Mises as the consensus; flag as contested whether trial-and-error prices fully answer Mises's original challenge, whether modern computational planning would defuse Hayek's knowledge argument, and whether the calculation problem can be cleanly separated from incentives. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, citations, or verbatim quotations; the text should allow a facilitator to run the activity from it alone.
