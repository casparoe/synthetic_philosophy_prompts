Okay, I'm a novelist and I've been going in circles on this, so I need a philosopher to give me something I can actually use. I keep coming back to a passage in Augustine's Confessions, Book XI, where he says:

At Your nod the moments flee by. Grant thereof space for our meditations among the hidden things of Your law, nor close it against us who knock.

I know he's praying, but the reason I can't let it go is that it feels like a description of what I try to do when I edit a scene. Moments flee by—that's just plot, event, the clock running. But the second half, "grant space for our meditations," that's what I want my prose to do: not pause the story, but open room inside it for the reader's mind to move around. I've been calling this "temporal drag" in my notes, but honestly I dont know if I'm using Augustine or just using him as a mood board.

What I need is a practical editing procedure. I write literary fiction, so I can't just slow the plot or add description—the syntax itself has to create that space Augustine is asking for. Could you turn his distinction between fleeing moments and granted space into a checklist I can run on a draft of a scene? Something like: read the scene once for hurry, once for room, then apply a small set of questions in order, with examples of the kind of sentence that passes and the kind that fails. Maybe use a sentence like "She remembered the hospital, the long hallway, the clock" and show me how it would have to change if it were going to have temporal drag rather than just running away from the reader. And then tell me where the checklist would misfire, because I know Augustine wasn't writing an editing manual and I dont want to pretend he was.

Also, please keep the whole answer in American English.
