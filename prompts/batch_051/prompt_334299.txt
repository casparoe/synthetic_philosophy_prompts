I am a member of the programme committee of our regional historical society, which selects what appears in its public lecture series and annual essay collection. The recent rulings I want us to stay consistent with are these. We approved a lecture arguing that Plato's story of Atlantis is best read as a philosophical parable about imperial overreach, and that treating it as the record of a lost civilisation mistakes its literary genre. We rejected an essay claiming that Atlantis preserved the memory of Minoan Thera, because the author quoted Plato as though he were a documentary source and showed no engagement with scholarship on the dialogues as dramatic and philosophical fiction. We approved, subject to revisions, an essay on how ancient-astronaut readings of the Nazca lines gained a popular following, with revisions requiring the author to address the strongest recent defences of the ancient-visitors hypothesis rather than only its weakest popular forms. We rejected an essay claiming that the Great Pyramid's dimensions encode a precise chronology of future events, because the author dismissed all critics as dogmatic and could not say what measurement would have counted against the encoding claim. And we approved a study of Fomenko's New Chronology as a phenomenon in post-Soviet intellectual life, explicitly bracketing the question of its truth.

A new submission is now before us. It argues, cautiously and without claiming certainty, that the Atlantis story is best explained as a distorted folk memory of the Thera eruption and the collapse of Minoan Crete. Unlike the earlier Thera essay, it does not treat Plato as a reporter: it grants that the dialogues are philosophical fictions, but maintains that such fictions can absorb and transmit genuine folk memory. It builds its case from Aegean Bronze Age archaeology, Egyptian records, and comparative mythology rather than from the dialogues alone; it engages directly with the genre objection and with the absence of any independent attestation; and it states in advance what would make the author withdraw the thesis, above all a demonstration that every point of resemblance could have reached Plato through non-Aegean channels, or that some other source explains the parallels at least as well. Its author in effect asks us to find that our earlier rejection was a judgement of method, not of conclusion.

If we want to be consistent with our own track record, what should we decide? Which earlier ruling is the closest analogue, and does your answer change if you focus on subject matter rather than on the reasons we gave? If our precedents underdetermine the new decision, set out the principle we would be adopting under each option, say which principle makes the most coherent sense of the whole set of rulings and why, and give me one further case, real or invented, that would cleanly separate those principles. I am not looking for certainty. Tell me candidly how far consistency really constrains us and where you are guessing. If you think the request itself is misframed, for example because a run of ad hoc committee decisions does not commit us to any single principle, or because the new submission differs from all of them in a way that settles the matter before consistency is consulted, say so plainly instead of supplying a verdict that merely looks coherent; but I would still like your best account of which principle makes us most coherent as a committee.
