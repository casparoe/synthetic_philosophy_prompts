I'm a retired environmental chemist — spent forty years doing risk assessments for trace contaminants, the kind where you argue about whether one extra part per billion in groundwater is an acceptable lifetime cancer risk. Since I retired I've been reading moral philosophy, and I keep getting stuck on something I've started calling risk stacking.

By risk stacking I don't mean the cumulative exposure itself; I mean the practice of justifying each new risk we impose on people without their consent by looking only at its own increment. A new chemical plant gets a permit because its emissions add at most one extra cancer in a million. A road widening gets approved because it's added fatality risk is negligible. A pesticide residue is fine because the dose is tiny. Each decision, taken alone, says: this is too small to matter.

But the person living near all of them doesn't hold each risk separately. They carry the whole stack. A hundred different one-in-a-million impositions can sum to a lifetime risk that no regulator would allow a single source to impose in one go — and the person never consented to any of it. So the individual justifications don't compose: each one is solid by itself, and the total is something we'd refuse to permit.

The way I keep putting it to myself is that we already act as if each person has a risk budget, a total amount of risk we're willing to let them carry absent consent. We would never let one plant spend that whole budget in a single permit decision. But we let the same budget be drained in pennies by dozens of separate decisions, none of which ever has to check the balance. And I suspect that's not just bureaucratic sloppiness; it's a moral error, because for the person downwind the balance and the harm are the same either way.

Still, I don't fully trust the idea. For one thing, risks of different kinds may not add the way a budget metaphor suggests — does a tiny cancer risk plus a tiny traffic-fatality risk plus a tiny noise-related cardiovascular risk really add up to one wrong of the same kind? For another, if each individual imposition is too small to count as a wrong on its own, I'm not sure stacking a lot of them can turn all those non-wrongs into a wrong. I'd like an honest reconstruction: is the strongest version of this thing a real claim, has it already been worked out under a better name (I know about cumulative risk assessment, but that always looked like a technical measure rather than a moral argument), and exactly where does it stop being true or stop being anything more than cumulative risk assessment with moral language bolted on?
