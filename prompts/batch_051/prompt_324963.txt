Last night a friend and I spent hours circling the same disagreement about Kuhn and Popper, and I came away unable to locate where his argument goes wrong. He was pressing a dilemma for anyone who wants to keep Popper’s conviction that science is rational in a strong sense while also accepting Kuhn’s account of what actually happens during a scientific revolution. Each step seemed forced on me as he said it, but the conclusion strikes me as unacceptable. He put it like this.

(1) A choice between two theories can be rational only if there is a body of reasons that can be specified independently of either theory and that favors one over the other; otherwise the theory is, in effect, choosing itself. (2) In normal science, the reigning paradigm supplies exactly such reasons by fixing what counts as a genuine problem, an anomaly, an acceptable solution, and an acceptable margin of error. (3) Across a scientific revolution, the competing paradigms disagree about precisely those things — which problems matter, what would count as solving one, and which of the familiar scientific values (accuracy, simplicity, consistency, scope, fruitfulness) deserve the most weight in the contested cases — so no paradigm-neutral body of reasons is available to both parties. (4) If no paradigm-neutral body of reasons is available to both parties, then the decision to abandon one paradigm for another cannot be a matter of rational assessment; it can only be persuasion, conversion, or the exercise of standards internal to one of the competing paradigms. (5) Therefore theory choice during a scientific revolution is not rational. (6) But a practice whose most consequential episodes — the replacement of its entire conceptual framework — are not rational cannot itself be called rational in the way Popper and his successors wanted. So either the Kuhnian picture makes science irrational at the moments that matter most, or the argument fails somewhere.

He admitted the conclusion was stronger than he would have liked; I admitted that every premise felt true as he stated it. I would most like to keep (6), and I am least attached to (1), but I cannot see how to reject (1) without collapsing the distinction between being persuaded and being shown. Which step, if any, should be rejected, and what exactly does one pay for rejecting it?
