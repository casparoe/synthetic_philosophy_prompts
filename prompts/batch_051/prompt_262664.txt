I'm a grad student in formal epistemology, and I've been turning over van Fraassen's Reflection principle — roughly, the idea that your current credence should defer to the credence you rationally expect to have later — and I can't settle a scenario. Imagine a world where a cheap oracle becomes available that is terrifyingly accurate at predicting people's future credences: give it a proposition and a person, and it tells you what credence they'll assign one year from now. It gives no reasons, just the forcast, and it's right about almost everyone. The world is otherwise ours.

Anya, a cell biologist, currently has credence 0.3 in her own hypothesis H. The oracle tells her that in one year she will assign H credence 0.8. It also tells her the shift will be about half real evidence and about half the influence of a charismatic senior colleague whose reasoning she currently considers epistemically sloppy; she won't remember that influence by then, so future-Anya will be fully convinced she got to 0.8 by evidence alone. Anya has no reason to doubt the oracle's reliability.

What should Anya do right now — set her credence to 0.8, stay at 0.3, or land somewhere else — and through what chain of reasoning? Then zoom out and predict what a research community where everyone consults such an oracle looks like after a decade. I want the reasoning shown: which conditions behind Reflection are doing the work, which links in your prediction are robust, which are guesses, and exactly what would have to be different for the verdict to flip.
