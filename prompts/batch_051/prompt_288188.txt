I'm a backend engineer, so I think about everything in terms of processes, state, coupling, failover, deployment, rollback, consensus protocols, that kind of thing. I got into an argument with a coworker about presidential vs. parliamentary systems (both of us are political junkies with zero formal training), and I kept reaching for system-design analogies and wanted to check whether they actually hold up. From where I sit, a presidential system looks like two independently elected long-running processes that each own part of the state and can't terminate or replace each other, so when they disagree the whole thing deadlocks — gridlock is basically deadlock, and impeachment is a super heavyweight kill switch with a very high threshold. A parliamentary system looks more like a process tree where the executive is spawned by the legislature and can be killed by a no-confidence vote, which gives you failover but also couples the two branches so tightly that you can't really say they're separate services. The weird thing is that a lot of what political scientists seem to care about — legitimacy, mandate, party discipline, informal norms — doesn't map cleanly onto any of that vocab, or maps in ways that feel forced.

So here's the task. Translate the presidentialism-vs-parliamentarism debate into the vocabulary of distributed systems / software architecture as faithfully as you can, for someone like me whose actually fluent in that vocabulary and only dabbles in the politics side. Cover the main claims on both sides (gridlock and instability, accountability vs. separation of powers, the majoritarian vs. consensus democracy stuff if it's relevant). For each claim, tell me what translates cleanly, what gets distorted, and what has no counterpart at all. Then do the residue report: is the stuff that doesn't translate just a limitation of the systems vocabulary, or is it a hint that those parts of the debate are less precise than they look? If I model a polity as a running system, what do I learn and what do I lose by doing it?
