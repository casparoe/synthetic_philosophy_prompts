I am a doctoral student in political theory, and I sit as the graduate representative on my university's Estates and Communities Committee. At its next meeting the committee must decide whether to approve a proposal to demolish an older postgraduate residential block and replace it with new self-contained studio accommodation. The proposal has a strong material case: the present block has cramped rooms, shared kitchens and bathrooms, poor insulation, and higher maintenance costs per resident than the university's newer stock. But the block is also a dense and unusually durable community. A residents' association runs shared meals, language exchanges, and mutual childcare; some students have lived there well beyond the ordinary tenancy cycle; and the building has become an informal anchor for international students and working-class students, who describe it as markedly less atomised than the newer halls. As drafted, the proposal would decant current residents into scattered temporary rooms, grants no enforceable right of return, and makes no provision for the common spaces on which the association depends.

The committee is divided. One side holds that the university's duty is to provide the best housing it can afford and to respect adult students' own preferences; on this view, attachment to a particular building is a taste to be captured in a satisfaction survey, priced, and weighed against better insulation and private bathrooms. The other side holds that the building sustains a form of belonging that is not an aggregate of individual preferences. What is at risk, they argue, is a shared form of life with non-instrumental value: residents relate to one another as participants in a common practice rather than as consumers of a housing service, and dispersing them would destroy a good that improved studios cannot replace. They add that survey methods will record the preferences of current members while remaining blind to the fact that the good they value exists only because a pattern of common life was allowed to persist over time.

I have been asked to circulate, before the meeting, a written opinion recommending what the committee should decide. Please draft it as the committee's decision memo. It should recommend either approval, rejection, or approval subject to conditions; any conditions should be concrete enough to be written into the proposal, with the body responsible for enforcing them and a timetable. The memo should state and honestly answer the strongest argument on the losing side rather than caricature it, and it should not simply assume either that community value is incommensurable with housing quality or that it reduces cleanly to individual preferences. The final text should be in British English.
