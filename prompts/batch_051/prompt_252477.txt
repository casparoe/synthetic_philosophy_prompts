I overheard two of my grad students going at it about Moore's paradox the other day, and I want you to sort out who's right about what. The dispute started when one of them wrote the sentence It's raining but I don't believe it on the board and asked whether the problem with it is semantic or merely pragmatic.

Mara argued its pragmatic. She said the sentence is perfectly consistent: the rain doesn't care what I believe, so there are possible worlds—this one right now, if I'm stuck in a windowless office—where it's true. What's odd is only the act of asserting it, because assertion normally expresses belief. By asserting the first conjunct you hold yourself out as believing that it's raining, and then the second conjunct explicitly cancels that. So it's like saying It's raining, but I don't mean it—not contradictory, just self-undermining as a speech act.

Dev pushed back. He said that if it were just about assertion, then it should be perfectly fine to believe the Moore sentence or to judge it true. But you can't. Try to believe that it's raining and that you don't believe it's raining: if you believe the conjunction, you believe the first conjunct, and then the second conjunct is false of you. So the sentence is a blindspot—it can be true, but it can't be believed by the very person it's about. Dev said that's not a Gricean point about conversation; it's a structural fact about first-person present-tense belief ascription. The weirdness is in the content's relation to belief, not in the norms of saying.

Mara came back: sure, you can't believe it, but that's just because belief distributes over conjunction and the second conjunct gets made false by the believing. That's a fact about the logic of belief, not about the meaning of the sentence. The sentence is still truth-conditionally consistent—it has a truth table with a T row—so calling the defect semantic is too strong. If it were semantic, it'd be false in every model, and it isn't. And the fact that asserting it is defective follows from the general norm that you shouldn't assert what you don't believe, plus the fact that you can't believe it. So the pragmatic story still does all the work at the level of assertion.

Dev replied that Mara was now smuggling his point into her own account: she needed the unbelievability claim to explain why the assertion norm is violated, and that unbelievability is exactly the non-pragmatic core. He also added that the first-person present tense is doing special work. It was raining, but I didn't believe it and It's raining, but she doesn't believe it are completely fine. If the problem were just a conversational norm about asserting p only if you believe p, the tense and person shouldn't matter that much. The asymmetry points to something about self-ascription of belief, not about speech acts in general.

Then they started arguing about whether a robot that had no beliefs but could assert sentences could say the Moore sentence without the oddness, which is where I left.
