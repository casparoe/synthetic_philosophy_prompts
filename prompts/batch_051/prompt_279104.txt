I spent the better part of last night arguing with a friend about the repugnant conclusion. His preferred escape route was a person-restricted axiology: outcomes are ranked only by the welfare of the people who already exist, or would exist anyway independently of the choice; people whom a policy merely brings into existence do not count on the positive side. He argued that this blocks the repugnant conclusion, because the enormous population of lives barely worth living never counts as an improvement — those extra lives have no positive claim on us, and their modest positive welfare does not enter the ranking. By the end of the argument I had become convinced that the very restriction that does the blocking also has a disturbing reverse implication, and I want a thought experiment that brings it out.

Construct a case in which this person-restricted axiology — count only the baseline-existing population, and within that population maximize total welfare — is forced to recommend a policy that creates a large additional population whose lives are, in expectation, somewhat below the neutral level: not worth living, though not lives of extreme torment. In the same case, classical total utilitarianism and average utilitarianism must both oppose the policy. Constraints: the policy should be a realistic piece of public administration or institutional design (aging-society care, labor migration, infrastructure, or something similar); it must not rely on coercion, deception, or mistaken beliefs among the existing population; and the benefit to existing people must be real and positive but not so large that a total utilitarian would approve on balance. The creation of the new people should come about through ordinary voluntary choices, not through anything like manufacturing persons, and the case should hold fixed their rights once they exist.

After presenting the scenario, supply an explicit numerical welfare profile for the existing population before and after the policy and for the new population under the policy, and show the calculation for each of the three views: person-restricted, total, and average. Then state which structural feature of the case does the work — I suspect it is the baseline restriction together with the absence of a filter against creating lives below neutral, but show rather than assume this — and spell out what happens if that feature is removed by re-describing the new lives as among the people whose welfare already counts. Finish by writing the strongest reply a defender of the person-restricted view could give to your case, and say whether that reply can avoid the conclusion without quietly abandoning the restriction that was meant to block the repugnant conclusion.
