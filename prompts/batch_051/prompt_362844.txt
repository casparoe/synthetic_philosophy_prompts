I'm a PhD student in cognitive science working on conceptual change, and I keep running into the same claim in different forms: students' systematic errors in science are not gaps to be filled but theories to be replaced. The claim is usually illustrated with cases like the belief that plants get their mass from the soil, that heavier objects fall faster, that the seasons are caused by the Earth's distance from the Sun, or that heat is a fluid. I find the word "theory" doing a great deal of work in these discussions. Sometimes it appears to be an empirical claim: the wrong answers are supposed to hang together, transfer to novel questions, resist piecemeal correction, and be revised as a whole rather than one item at a time. Other times it seems to be little more than a flattering description of the fact that several wrong answers feel related. I would like to push on that distinction rather than continue using the term impressionistically.

Could you help me design the strongest feasible test of the claim that a particular misconception is organized like a theory, rather than being a patchwork of local, context-triggered rules or a mere absence of the correct knowledge? I would like the test spelled out concretely: the kind of subjects and materials, the conditions or measurements, which results would count as evidence for the theory-like reading, and which for the patchwork reading. I would also like you to be explicit about what even a clean positive result would not settle — for example, whether it licenses any conclusion about instruction, whether the gap between "behaves like a theory" and "is a theory" remains, and how far the result would generalize beyond the single misconception tested. Please make the final line of your response a one-sentence summary of your answer.
