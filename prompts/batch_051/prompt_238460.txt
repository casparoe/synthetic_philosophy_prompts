I chair the contested-resource committee at a large hospital. The resources in question are the genuinely scarce ones — an ICU bed, a ventilator, the last batch of a drug — and in a bad week there are more patients with some claim on them than there are resources. The committee sits weekly during surges. The decisions are life-altering, and each decision produces a one-page written justification signed by the chief medical officer and potentially reviewed later by a court, an insurer, or a regulator. The committee has about an hour per decision, and its members are clinicians and administrators with no formal training in moral philosophy.

I want the committee to work from Scanlonian contractualism, which I understand roughly as follows. A decision is acceptable only if it can be justified to each person affected, individually, rather than to the group considered as a lump. The test is whether any affected person could reasonably reject the principle behind the decision, given the burden that principle would impose on them personally. On this picture, adding up many small benefits or losses across different people in order to justify what happens to some other person looks suspect, because justification is owed to each person one at a time, and no particular person's small stake simply joins together with anyone else's. But I also know this is the so-called aggregation problem: the view seems to imply that numbers do not matter, and yet it plainly matters whether we save one person or five.

Please convert this into a decision procedure the committee can actually run: a checklist or decision tree in words that tells us, in order, which questions to ask, when we may count heads, when we may not add smaller individual harms together to outweigh a larger individual harm, and what to do in between. It must be usable in the room without philosophical vocabulary, and it must make explicit which steps are mechanical and which steps are irreducibly matters of judgment.

Please structure your answer in exactly these four sections, with roughly the lengths given:

1. The procedure (about 600 words). A numbered sequence of steps, written as a decision tree in words. Each step should be a concrete question the committee can answer from the information in front of it, followed by what to do next. Label each step either RULE or JUDGMENT, and state what the written record must contain at every JUDGMENT step if the justification is later to survive review.

2. Why this is still contractualism, in plain terms (about 350 words). Explain, without philosophical jargon, why the anti-aggregation rule follows from the requirement to justify the decision to each person one at a time, and why the procedure nevertheless lets the numbers count in some cases. A committee member should be able to read this section and understand what the procedure is protecting them from.

3. Worked applications (about 600 words). Run the procedure on these three cases: (a) one patient needs the entire remaining batch of a drug to survive an otherwise fatal condition, while the same batch could be divided among one hundred patients to prevent severe but non-fatal and time-limited illness; (b) one ventilator and two destination units where one patient will survive if sent to unit A and five will survive if the ventilator is used at unit B, with all six claims equal in gravity; (c) one patient needs the entire resource to avoid permanent paralysis, while twenty patients need fractions of it to avoid losing a hand. At least one of these must be a case where the procedure returns an answer the committee will resist — in fact, deliberately make one of them the case where the procedure tells us to pass over the hundred to save the one — and say plainly that this is what the procedure requires and why the committee cannot simply override it and still claim to be using the procedure.

4. Where judgment cannot be eliminated, and what the procedure cannot do (about 350 words). Identify the exact steps at which the procedure stops being mechanical, including how to compare the size of different kinds of complaints in the intermediate range, and say what the procedure is not competent to decide — for instance, whether setting budgets across a whole population with not-yet-identified patients requires a different justificatory frame than a decision about identified patients in front of us, and which parts of the procedure even sympathetic contractualists would dispute.
