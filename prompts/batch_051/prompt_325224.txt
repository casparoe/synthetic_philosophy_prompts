Two people in my philosophy of science group got into it about the indispensability argument -- not the general version, the one that says math is genuinely explanatory in science. It started with periodical cicadas. (1) Mara said the explanation for why Magicicada broods have 13- and 17-year life cycles is partly numerical: prime periods minimize how often the brood lines up with predator or parasite cycles whose lengths divide the cicada period, so you can't say why 17 and not 18 without the number-theoretic fact. (2) Joel said that's not the explanation -- the actual work is a causal, natural-selection story where broods with non-prime periods got eaten more, so the primes just summarize the outcome; numbers aren't out there causing survival. (3) Mara came back that explanation doesn't only cite causes, it also cites constraints: a bridge stays up partly because of geometry, not because geometry pushes on it. The arithmetic is a constraint the biological story needs, so it's explanatory even if it isn't causal. (4) Joel said that just shows the math is indispensable to our way of stating things, not to the explanation in the world. He brought up honeycombs: bees make near-hexagonal cells because hexagons minimize wax per area, but bees don't solve variational problems -- physics or selection does the causing, and the optimization fact just describes the outcome. (5) Mara said 'bees don't compute it' misses the point: the optimizing fact explains why hexagons rather than squares are the stable or selected outcome, which is an explanation, and that's all the indispensability argument needs. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Walk through the exchange move by move, say which claims hold up and which don't, and say who's more right overall.
