Over the past few months I have been helping a small charitable fund weigh a reliable poverty-alleviation programme, whose benefit per pound is modest but unusually well evidenced, against a far more speculative proposal that claims to reduce a low-probability, extremely large-scale future risk. The disagreement keeps collapsing into expected value fanaticism: on the standard view, a sufficiently enormous payoff seems able to outweigh a vanishingly small probability, so the speculative proposal wins no matter how thin its evidence looks, provided the claimed stakes are large enough. I have read around the debate, but I still lack a settled view about which proposed repair to expected value theory is doing the most legitimate work, and which ones are merely ad hoc ways of avoiding an uncomfortable conclusion.

As a professional decision theorist, please help me work through this systematically. Below are seven candidate repairs to expected value maximisation: (1) bounded utility, insisting that utilities have a defensible upper bound so that no payoff can outrun an arbitrarily small probability; (2) probability underweighting, treating extremely small probabilities as if they were even smaller, or effectively zero, unless the evidence meets an independent corroboration test; (3) normative uncertainty, holding that because we are unsure which theory of choice is correct, and fanatical conclusions are fragile across such theories, we should discount decisions that only a narrow range of theories would endorse; (4) evidence thresholds, requiring that a probability estimate pass a minimal epistemic reliability standard before it may enter expected value calculations at all; (5) background uncertainty, arguing that real choices always contain enough unquantified surrounding uncertainty to swamp the fanatical term, so the fanatical option is never in practice the expected value maximiser; (6) a two-tier view, treating expected value maximisation as a criterion of rightness under idealised information but not as a complete decision procedure for bounded agents in ordinary contexts; and (7) quarantined fanaticism, accepting that the fanatical option really is favoured by expected value theory but maintaining that this conclusion is largely confined to a narrow class of choices.

Please begin with a short note on the criteria you are using and any initial brainstorming about whether two or more of these repairs are best combined. Then rank the seven proposals from most to least defensible as responses to the charity-style case above, give each a score out of 10 for how well it defuses the case while preserving as much of expected value reasoning as possible, and add a one-sentence justification for each score. Please write the entire answer in British English.
