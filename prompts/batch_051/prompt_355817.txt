I chair the acquisitions and exhibitions committee at a university art museum. Last spring the estate of the painter Soren Hale offered the museum a major bequest: fifty-one canvases from Hale's final twelve years, together with drawings, letters, and an endowment sufficient to maintain a dedicated gallery in perpetuity. Hale is regarded as a significant and difficult figure in postwar figurative painting, and the bequest would be the largest single addition to our holdings in decades. But the offer arrived alongside the estate's own release of its archives. Newly transcribed minutes, inventories, and correspondence show that during the occupation Hale served on the government's "Commission for the Inventory of Degenerate Art," the body that classified artists, compiled the lists used to seize their works, and heard the appeals that were almost never granted. Hale was not a peripheral member. He annotated the inventories in detail, pressed for particular collections to be seized quickly, and visited the confiscated works in storage. His late canvases—including several we would receive—borrow heavily from compositions and techniques recorded in those rooms, and at least nine of the late portraits depict sitters whose families Hale had himself listed. The proposed gift agreement includes the donor's standard condition that the works be displayed together, prominently, and without interpretive material that, in the estate's words, "diminishes the artistic standing of the works or of their maker."

The committee is divided. One faction says the museum's question is only whether the paintings are good art, and by the standards of the discipline several of them plainly are; a gift condition requiring us to show the work as art is not sinister, and refusing on grounds of biography is moralism rather than curatorial judgment. Another faction says the question cannot be restricted to biography, because the moral conditions under which the work was produced can enter the judgment that the work is good, and a museum that displays these canvases without context would present as unqualified goods objects whose creation was entangled with the very seizures that made them possible; on their view we should decline unless the estate permits explicit wall labels—which the estate has said is non-negotiable. A third group would accept the works but restrict them to the study room, on the view that the museum's scholarly obligations survive the artist's wrongs even where its celebratory obligations do not. The board has asked me, as chair, to draft the decision memo for its next meeting. Please write that memo: recommend a single course of action—accept as proposed, decline, or accept subject to revised terms—and defend it, while giving an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side. The memo should also say directly what work, if any, the moral character of an artist or the moral conditions of production do in judging whether art is good, and whether that judgment settles what a university museum should do.
