At work I reason from base rates all the time and nobody thinks twice about it. If our dashboards say 80% of failed requests trace to one service, I'll restart that service or pull it out of rotation without waiting for a stack trace that proves this particular timeout came from that particular process. A probability over which member of a class is responsible is plenty of evidence when I'm deciding what to do. But the same kind of reasoning gets treated as obviously insufficient in a courtroom: if 80% of the buses on a route are owned by one company, a jury is not supposed to conclude beyond reasonable doubt that a bus from that company caused the accident. I find this a weird asymmetry, because in both cases I'm moving from 'most of category X has property P' to 'this particular member of category X probably has property P.'

That said, I can feel the difference too. In the courtroom it seems to matter that the evidence doesn't single out this defendant rather than another, even if the odds are high it was one of them. At work I'd be mocked for refusing to act on an 80% base rate; in court I'd be horrified if someone were convicted on it. Is that just about stakes, and the fact that in the legal case the cost of being wrong lands on a specific person being punished, while in engineering the cost of being wrong is some avoidable downtime? Or is the law tracking something more principled about evidence needing to tie the verdict to the defendant, not merely to a category they belong to — and if so, should I be more reluctant to use bare base rates in my own decisions too? Or is the legal standard just irrational, and the courtroom should be treated as one more place to update probabilities and act on them? Id like to know whether the asymmetry is real and justified, which side should give way, and what changes if the base rate is 99.9% instead of 80%.
