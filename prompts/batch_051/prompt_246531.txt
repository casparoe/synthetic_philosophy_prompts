At a conference on AI governance that I was covering, two researchers continued an animated argument after a panel and left me their positions because they wanted an outsider to referee. The question was whether Fearon's rationalist bargaining framework — the claim that war occurs only when private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, or indivisibility prevents a mutually preferable bargain — carries over to the current race toward advanced AI.

Miriam argued for the carryover. Labs, she said, repeatedly fail to reach arrangements that would leave them all better off: they cannot credibly reveal how far along they are, because laggards have reason to understate progress to avoid scrutiny and leaders have reason to overstate or conceal depending on the audience. That is private information with incentives to misrepresent. Likewise, a pause or shared-safety agreement cannot be enforced because a lab's incentives shift once its models gain capability or market share, which is a commitment problem. So a race can be fully rational while still reproducing the causal structure of a bargain that should have been possible but was not.

David resisted. In Fearon's setup, he said, the puzzle is how two actors end up fighting when both would prefer some feasible peaceful settlement; the mechanisms explain why that settlement cannot be struck. But labs are not negotiating one settlement over a divisible prize — they choose investment levels over time, like firms in a patent race or standards contest. If a frontrunner would prefer racing to any feasible cooperation agreement, then there is no bargain to fail, merely ordinary profit-seeking competition. And if racing is individually rational but collectively ruinous, that is a collective action problem and an externality, not one of the three mechanisms. He added that disclosure channels may aid regulation and safety, so the incentives to misrepresent capabilities are not uniformly adverse in the way crisis bargaining assumes.

Miriam replied that a merger, a shared safety protocol, or a credible compute cap is the analog of a negotiated settlement, and that the difficulty labs have reaching one is exactly what needs explaining rather than evidence that the puzzle is ill-posed. David answered that she had now loosened the framework until any competition with trade secrets and weak enforcement qualified as war-like; the genuinely distinctive feature of the AI case, he said, is that racing produces a risk externality none of Fearon's mechanisms was built to explain. Miriam closed that the externality is the damage side while the commitment problem is the strategic side, and a proper model of an AI race needs both.

Please go through this exchange claim by claim and adjudicate it: where each person is right, where the analogy to Fearon's framework breaks, what both are missing, and who is more right overall about whether the rationalist framework illuminates the AI race.
