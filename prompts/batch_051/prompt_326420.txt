I am a novelist rather than a linguist, but my present book — narrated in part by a character who speaks a rural dialect in the middle of losing its older verb forms — has left me with a small theory of grammar, and I should like it assessed honestly rather than politely. I noticed that the forms which survived longest were the commonest, while the rarer verbs had been made regular, and I have built a larger claim on that observation. I call it the sediment account of grammatical rules. Its claim is that a grammatical rule is never a separate instrument that a speaker consults; it is only the visible pattern in a mass of individually stored word-forms. What linguists call productivity is, on this view, a statistical property of the stored collection — how many similar forms there are and how evenly they are distributed — not evidence of a rule. A speaker producing the past tense of an unfamiliar verb is not applying an -ed rule but matching the new item to the nearest and most numerous stored examples. Suppletion (go/went, or the forms of to be) then becomes exactly what one should expect: a form so frequent that it is stored too firmly to be levelled into the general pattern. Regular sound change seems to me to be the same process operating over pronunciations, which is why its exceptions should cluster among high-frequency words and special contexts rather than appear as random breaches. I am prepared to be told that the theory is false, trivial, or both. Please give me an honest assessment. Is it coherent, or does it merely redefine 'rule' as 'pattern' and thereby miss whatever a rule actually is? Does it already exist under another name, and if so, what position does it most resemble? And where does it break — which facts about regularity, suppletion, and sound change fit it worst? Please write your answer in American English.
