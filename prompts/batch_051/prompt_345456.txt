A city with a large and constant tourist population creates a Local Conventions Registry. Any resident may submit an unwritten local convention—how queues actually form at bakeries and bus stops, the genuinely expected tip at a bar, which side of the sidewalk people keep to, whether pedestrians cross against the light once the street is clear—and a convention becomes certified as local practice after two hundred residents attest to it. Certified conventions are printed on street signs and pushed to visitors through mapping and booking apps. Residents who witness a visitor violating a certified convention may file a report through a municipal app; the visitor receives a courtesy notice explaining the convention, and after three reported violations of the same convention within a year, a small fine is levied. Treat the registry as a mechanism to be analyzed rather than a proposal to be endorsed. Predict how it would actually behave: what it would incentivize among residents, visitors, and local businesses; how each party would game or capture it; what second-order effects officially codifying informal norms would have on the norms themselves; where the scheme would break down; and which of those failure modes are fixable without giving up the registry.
