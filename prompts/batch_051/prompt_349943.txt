I'm on the hardship fund and accomodations comittee at a mid-size public university, and we're trying to sort out how to make decisions less all over the place. We get maybe ten to fifteen requests a week from students asking for things like note-takers, extended deadlines, money for specialist equipment, emergency bursaries after a financial shock, a single room because of sleep problems, or help with childcare. We have a fixed pot of money and limited staff time, so we can't say yes to everything, and our decisions can be appealed to the university ombudsman, which means we have to be able to justify them in writing. Most of us have zero philosophy background.

One committee member keeps saying we should follow luck egalitarianism: roughly, that people should be compensated for bad luck they didn't choose (what she calls brute luck) but not for bad outcomes that flow from choices they could reasonably be held responsible for (option luck). I sort of get the principle, but we need it turned into something we can actually run in a 30-minute case review.

Please write us a decision procedure or checklist we can use. It should state the questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, identify where the procedure stops with a no, and mark clearly the steps where a person has to make a values call rather than apply a rule. Make sure the procedure handles the classic traps for this view: the expensive-tastes problem (we shouldn't end up compensating someone just because their preferences are costly to satisfy, but where's the line?), the objection that this amounts to abandoning people to their foolish choices, and the mess of telling option luck from brute luck when a person's situation is a mix of both.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical cases: (1) a student born with a visual impairment requests funding for screen-reader software; (2) a student who ignored repeated advice not to rent from a particular landlord, and who now lost their deposit and is asking for an emergency bursary to cover rent; (3) a student whose family cut them off financially after they came out, asking for the same kind of emergency support. I specifically want to see at least one case where your procedure tells us to say no to a student we'd probably rather help, and I want that flagged clearly.

Finally, tell us what the procedure can't do: the cases it can't settle, the places where luck egalitarianism goes silent or gives what feels like the wrong answer, and how we should talk about those limits in our written decisions.
