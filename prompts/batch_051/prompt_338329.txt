I keep going back and forth on Kavka's toxin puzzle. On one hand, I'm pretty convinced by the coherence argument: a rational agent can't form a present intention to drink the toxin tomorrow while also knowing full well they won't drink it once the money is already paid. Intentions are supposed to be the kind of mental state that settle what you'll do, so intending to do something you're sure you won't do looks like a flat inconsistency, not just an oddity.

On the other hand, I can't shake the feeling that the rational agent this coherence story gives us is a chump. Tonight, before midnight, forming the intention strictly dominates not forming it: if you manage to mean it, you get the million; if you don't, you get nothing. Tomorrow's choice not to drink is a separate decision, and by then not drinking is clearly the better move since the payout is already settled. So the coherence requirement is telling you to leave a free million on the table. A theory of rationality that systematically pays worse than self-deception feels like it's lost the plot somewhere.

Don't just recite the standard resolution. I want to know what it would take for the coherence theory to yield, what it would take for the intuition to yield, and whether there's a reading on which both survive — maybe by distinguishing "intending to drink" from "having whatever state satisfies the payout condition," or by saying the million-dollar state is real but not an intention, or by allowing belief–intention consistency to lapse only in cases like this. If you had to offer the best negotiated verdict, which side would you give up first, and what would it cost?
