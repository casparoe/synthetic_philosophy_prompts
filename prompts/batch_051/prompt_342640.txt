Metaethics is my field, and after years of going back and forth I've settled — maybe better to say I've backed into — a fairly robust moral realism, and I want you to stress-test it as a peer rather than as an undergrad-survey summarizer. Put on the hat of a professional philosopher who actually works in the realism/anti-realism debate, and think carefully.

Here's the position. I'm a moral realist, and the part I'm actually commited to is this: moral assertions express propositions, and at least some of those propositions — e.g., that torturing a child purely for fun is wrong — are true. Their truth doesn't depend on anyone's attitudes: not ours, not those of someone we'd all converge on, not an idealized observer's. I'm attracted to non-naturalism because I don't see how to reduce those truths to natural facts, but I treat that as a downstream commitment, not the load-bearing one.

I don't want the standard list of objections. I want a map of what would count against this position. What would have to be true for it to be false? Which intuitions, possible cases, and results in neighboring areas — evolutionary psychology, experimental philosophy, the linguistics of moral language, the literature on peer disagreement and higher-order evidence — would genuinely push against it, and which are just noise? Distinguish the defeaters that would be fatal from the ones that are merely awkward, and tell me which ones a committed realist should herself concede count against the view (not just which ones critics lob at it). If the position turns out to be well insulated, say so, and tell me whether you think that's a virtue or a problem. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Before you write the final answer, take some time to brainstorm candidate defeaters and then sift them.

The two things I'd personally find most persuasive against my own view are the evolutionary debunking arguments and the fact that moral disagreement seems to survive full information sometimes — so don't let those two off the hook easily.
