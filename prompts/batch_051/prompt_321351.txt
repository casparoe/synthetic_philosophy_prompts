Put on your philosopher-of-language hat, think carefully, and start by brainstorming the different ways one could read the rule before you settle on an answer.

Our university Conduct Code has a clause that reads: "No member of the University community shall, in the course of University activities, make a statement that demeans or expresses contempt for a person or group on the basis of race, color, or national origin." When it was drafted in the late 1990s, the committee's minutes show they were thinking mainly of slurs, threats, and face-to-face bigotry.

Here's a case they never anticipated. A student running for student government releases a campaign video filmed in front of a public housing complex. She never mentions race. She says, "This city has lost its sense of order, and this campus has too. If you elect me, I'm going to restore safety and decency — time to clean things up." In our area, "restoring order" and "cleaning things up" have a well-documented history as coded language for getting Black residents out of public life; the local news archive shows candidates using those exact phrases right before pushing policies that displaced Black families. A Black student organization files a complaint saying the video demeans and expresses contempt for Black students. The candidate's supporters say that's absurd — nothing in the words mentions race, and the candidate says she meant urban safety, nothing more.

I'm less interested in the university politics than in the interpretive question. I'm on the conduct board and we genuinely don't know weather the clause reaches this. Walk me through what each of these approaches would say: (1) the plain wording, (2) the clause's purpose, (3) what the drafters would have said if you could ask them now, and (4) what a reasonable hearer with local knowledge would take the video to be doing. Then tell me which approach should govern and why. Along the way, draw out what this shows about whether the racism of a statement lives in the speaker's intention, the literal meaning, the effect on the targeted group, or the community's decoding of it.
