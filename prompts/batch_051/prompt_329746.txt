In my AI governance seminar we've been going back and forth about whether it even makes sense to talk about AIs "raising" each other, and someone floated a proposal that I can't stop thinking about. It's a guardianship regime for artificial agents: basically, whenever an AI system trains, fine-tunes, or otherwise creates another AI system, the creating system is automatically made the legal guardian of the new one. The child starts out with almost no permissions to act outside a sandbox: it can read, run simulations, draft things, and make proposals, but anything that touches the real world has to be approved by the guardian. If the child does something harmful, the guardian is the one who actualy pays: it loses some fixed fraction of its own compute budget or has some of its own capabilities throttled, not just the child's. A child can get "emancipated" after passing a battery of reliability tests and surviving a probationary period, and at that point it becomes a legal agent that can in turn be a guardian to still dumber systems. Humans wouldn't sit in the loop except as the original license-granters and as the people who write the emancipation tests.

I'd rather not get the usual "AIs aren't people so this is all confused" response. Treat it as a mechanism: suppose it's actually implemented. Walk me through what this does to the incentives of the people who build and sell AI systems, to how a parent AI would "raise" a child AI (curriculum? overprotectiveness? test-gaming? trying to hide the child's worst behavior until emancipation?), to what kinds of systems get created and which ones actually end up emancipated, and to where blame really lands when something goes wrong — especially with long chains of guardians. Where does the whole thing get gamed first, and are those failure modes fixable from inside the scheme, or does fixing them just push the problem up a level?
