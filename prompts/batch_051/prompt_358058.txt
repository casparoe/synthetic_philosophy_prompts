I'm drafting an essay in decision theory with the working title Money Pumps Target Choice over Time, Not Preference at a Time, and I'd like your comments and criticisms on the skeleton below before I expand it. The essay gives a deflationary reading of the money-pump argument against intransitive preferences.

Notes. (1) The standard pump takes an agent with pairwise preferences A over B, B over C, and C over A, then cycles the agent through trades licensed by those preferences—say, A for C, C for B, B for A—charging a small fee at each step, so that the agent returns to the original item minus the fees. The usual conclusion is that synchronic intransitive preferences are irrational. (2) I argue that the argument slides from a synchronic notion of preference, a ranking held at a time, to a diachronic notion, a sequence of choices made over time. The pump gets its force only if the agent assesses each offer as an isolated pairwise improvement rather than as part of a plan. An agent who knows the full sequence can refuse it by adopting a policy: never enter a known finite sequence of pairwise trades that returns to the status quo minus fees, even when each trade would otherwise be attractive on its own. That policy is compatible with retaining cyclical pairwise rankings. (3) So the money pump shows, at most, that an agent's choices over time should not form a pattern a patient counterparty could turn into a guaranteed loss. The rational requirement is diachronic: adopt choice dispositions that are not exploitable by a known sequence of offers. Transitivity is then required only derivatively, as one convenient way—not the only possible way—of satisfying this norm. (4) If correct, the money-pump argument does not establish transitivity as an intrinsic requirement of rationality at a time; it establishes it as a constraint on the management of one's temporally extended self.

I'd especially appreciate comments on: (a) whether the synchronic/diachronic distinction carries the weight I place on it, or whether an intransitive agent who adopts my proposed policy thereby reveals transitive preferences in some relevant sense; (b) whether there is a non-myopic version of the pump—using simultaneous offers, bundled options, or something else—that reestablishes the direct conclusion against synchronic intransitivity; and (c) whether my proposed diachronic norm is extensionally equivalent to transitivity over suitably redescribed outcomes, and if not, a concrete case where they come apart. Please also flag any place where I'm being unfair to the argument as it is standardly presented.
