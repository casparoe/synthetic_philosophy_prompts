I've spent years arguing with a guy I know from a rationalist meetup, Eli, about moral worth—specifically, when a right action is done for the right reasons and therefore "counts" in the way that matters, rather than just accidentally conforming to duty. Eli's calls on particular cases are weirdly stable, so I've kept a list. The ones I'm confident about:

- A woman returns a lost wallet because she pictures the owner panicking about having to cancel all their cards and replace their ID. Eli: the action has moral worth.
- A guy returns a lost wallet only because the woman he's trying to impress is watching and he wants to look honest. Eli: no moral worth.
- A guy returns a lost wallet because he thinks "it's not mine to keep; the owner has a claim to it." Eli: moral worth.
- A woman jumps into a pond to save a drowning child only because a news crew is filming and she realizes a rescue would make great footage. Eli: no moral worth.
- A woman jumps in because she can't stand the child's screaming and wants the screaming to stop. Eli: moral worth.

New case: a man finds a wallet, checks the address, and realizes the owner probably lives in a much poorer neighborhood than the wallet's fancy leather suggests. He returns it because he hates the thought of someone who's already struggling losing their rent money. But he'd happily keep an identical wallet if he thought the owner were rich, since he figures a rich person can absorb the loss. What should I expect Eli to say about this new case, assuming he's applying the same underlying principle as in the list? Give me a definite prediction rather than just laying out readings.
