Two members of my study group got stuck on a puzzle our instructor called the Judy Benjamin problem, and neither would budge. The setup is this. A paratrooper is dropped at night into a territory with two sectors, Red and Blue, each divided into two subsectors: Red-First (RF), Red-Second (RS), Blue-First (BF), and Blue-Second (BS). Her prior is uniform: 1/4 for each cell. Then a radio message tells her that if she is in Red territory, the probability she is in RS is 3/4. The message imposes only the constraint P(RS | Red) = 3/4, equivalently P(RS) = 3 · P(RF). The question is what her full posterior should be.

Ana argued that we should keep everything the message does not force to change fixed. The message is a constraint on the distribution inside Red, and it says nothing about whether she is in Red or Blue. So Red's total should stay at 1/2 and the Blue cells at 1/4 each; reallocate within Red to get RF = 1/8 and RS = 3/8.

Dev replied that this assumes the very thing at issue. The principled way to update under a partial constraint is to choose, among all distributions satisfying P(RS) = 3 · P(RF), the one closest to the prior, with closeness measured by relative entropy, KL(new ‖ old) = Σ p_i log(p_i / q_i). Solving that minimization gives BF and BS ≈ 0.266 each, RS ≈ 0.351, and RF ≈ 0.117, so Red's total falls to about 0.467 while Blue's rises to about 0.533. On that rule, a message about the inside of Red makes her less confident she is in Red at all. If that seems perverse, he said, the burden is on Ana to say what in the message rules it out.

Ana came back that the burden runs the other way. Relative entropy treats the four cells as interchangeable and lets a message about Red's internal split leak into the Red–Blue margin. The message is about a conditional, so the similarity metric should respect conditional structure. Keeping Red's total fixed is not an extra assumption; it is just recognizing that a conditional message changes only what it mentions.

Dev answered that "about" is doing all the work. Any answer must preserve something, and relative entropy at least preserves a single, well-motivated thing — information-theoretic closeness to the prior — uniformly across problems of this form. Ana's rule is one of indefinitely many possible invariance choices with no general rationale beyond intuition. He added that relative entropy is the standard treatment of exactly this kind of constrained update, so the onus is on her to justify a rival rule rather than rejecting a result because it looks odd.

We could not settle it.
