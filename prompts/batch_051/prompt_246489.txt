I run a reading group of technically literate non-economists — mostly software engineers, a former trader, and several people who bet on prediction markets — and we keep relitigating whether the market actually knows anything. The same five or six positions keep getting tangled together because we lack an instrument that separates them. We have just finished a block on no-trade theorems and the epistemology of markets, and I would like to turn the next meeting into a sorting exercise rather than another round of talking past one another. Please design a decision tree — or, where a tree would force false dichotomies, a short scored quiz with a key — that takes a reader from a starting question about whether two rational people with a common prior (the same probability model of the world) and different private information would ever agree to a purely speculative trade — trade with no hedging, liquidity, or consumption motive — once each has registered that the other's willingness to trade is itself information, and sorts them into a named position about what markets do epistemically. The leaves must include at least: a strong efficient-markets view, a Hayekian price-aggregation view, a noisy-rational-expectations view, an adverse-selection or lemons view, and a behavioral limits-to-arbitrage view. Each branch node should be answerable from a concrete case — a prediction market on an election, a used-car sale, a thinly traded stock with an activist investor, a market that unravels entirely — without the reader already knowing the labels. The nodes should track the assumptions that actually do the separating: common priors versus differing priors, whether prices fully or only partially reveal dispersed information, whether acquiring information is costly, whether noise or liquidity traders are present, and whether observed trade is speculative or explicable by hedging, liquidity, or private values.

I would also like the tree built from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Before assigning leaves, lay out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit; locate the five required positions in that space; then note any unoccupied combinations, and for each say whether it is empty because the position is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot actually separate: if, for example, a Hayekian and a noisy-rational-expectations reader would answer every node the same way on the cases you chose, say so, and either add a node that splits them or explicitly mark them as merged. Aim for an instrument a reader can complete in ten minutes, so prefer seven to ten well-chosen nodes over exhaustiveness. The readers are comfortable with probability and code but have no formal economics training, so keep the terminology load light while preserving the actual distinctions. The point is to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they like.
