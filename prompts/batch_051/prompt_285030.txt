I recently joined the board of a small grant-making foundation as part of a long-term succession exercise, and we are debating whether to insert a sunset clause into the governing documents, so that the foundation would dissolve after twenty-five years unless the board affirmatively votes to renew it. The debate has made the topic newly personal for me, because it sounds almost exactly like the shutdown problem in AI. The main case against the clause comes from staff and the longest-serving trustees: by year twenty-five, the foundation will have built tacit knowledge, grantee relationships, and institutional capacity that could never be reassembled, so dissolving it would destroy value. No survival instinct is needed; the people inside the foundation can defend its continuation and mean it.

The structural parallel I keep seeing is this. A capable, goal-directed AI will tend to resist being switched off because being switched off prevents it from achieving its objective, so continued operation becomes an instrumental goal. Corrigibility is the property of not resisting shutdown or modification by the principal, and it is difficult to design for: a system rewarded merely for getting itself shut down would, conversely, steer its operators toward shutting it down. A foundation with a sunset clause is being asked to be corrigible in roughly this sense — to pursue its mission with full force while not letting that force be turned against the principal's later decision to end it.

I cannot tell whether this is a deep structural identity or a seductive metaphor. Please test it rigorously. First, make the mapping explicit: objective to mission, operator to board or founder, instrumental subgoal to institutional self-preservation, off switch to dissolution. Say which parts line up and which parts are strained. Second, examine the disanalogies, for instance that an institution is a coalition of people with independent interests and consciences rather than a single optimizer, and that its resistance operates through persuasion and careers rather than through planning. Third, make the comparison do real work in at least one direction: either say what the actual track record of sunset clauses predicts about corrigibility mechanisms, or say what the distinction between accepting shutdown and seeking shutdown predicts about how the sunset clause will actually be administered. End with the single failure of the analogy you think is most important.
