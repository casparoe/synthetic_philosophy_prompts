I am a novelist, and I need to settle a question I usually handle by ear: what exactly is it for a sentence to be an assertion, and what belief-related condition does asserting place on the speaker? I was pushed into this by noticing a strange little sentence. A person can be in a state in which both of these are true: it is raining, and she does not believe that it is raining. Yet a sentence that says both things at once — that it is raining and that she does not believe that it is raining — goes wrong when spoken, even if she is being perfectly accurate about both the weather and her own mind. It strikes nearly everyone as defective: not a plain lie, not a plain contradiction, but a kind of self-undermining act. Call this the Moorean case.

I want a working definition of the term 'assertion' that predicts the force, or lack of force, of these utterances. I have arranged them roughly from clear to hard.

1. A person answers 'Is it raining?' with 'Yes.' This should clearly count as an assertion.

2. A witness says, with apparent sincerity, that it is raining and that she does not believe that it is raining. This should count as an assertion, but one that is defective in a distinctive way. I want the definition to say what the defect is — contradiction, lie, attempted impossibility, or a broken norm of conversation — and whether the defect lies in what is said or in the act of saying it.

3. A man says that he posted the letter yesterday, knowing he did not. This should clearly count as an assertion, and it seems to be a plain lie. The definition should distinguish this from case 2.

4. An actor on stage delivers the case-2 sentence. The actor is not defective in the same way; the stage appears to suspend the force the witness had. The definition should say what, exactly, is suspended.

5. In a novel, a first-person narrator writes, in the narrative present, that it is raining and that she does not believe that it is raining. The novelist is not asserting this about the real world, and yet inside the fiction the narrator seems to do something self-undermining. I want to know whose assertion, if anyone's, this is, and whose beliefs are on the hook.

6. A woman says 'I believe the bridge is safe,' and then refuses to drive across it, taking a long detour instead. The sentence is grammatical and not Moorean, but if belief is partly a matter of how one acts, there is a tension between the report and the behavior. Is this a sincere assertion that is simply false about her own mind, or is it something else?

7. A man says 'I don't believe my son is guilty,' while doing, day by day, exactly what someone would do if he believed his son was guilty. Is this a problem for the definition of assertion, or a problem about self-knowledge that a theory of assertion can leave to one side?

8. A lecturer writes the case-2 sentence on the board and says 'Notice that this sentence is strange.' The lecturer is not defective. The definition should explain how quoting or mentioning a sentence cancels the commitment that saying it would normally carry.

9. A large language model, asked to produce an example of Moore's paradox, outputs the case-2 sentence. Does it assert anything? If assertion requires a speaker's own mind to be committed, and the model has no beliefs to commit, then either the output is not an assertion or assertion does not require belief in the way the earlier cases suggest.

For what it is worth, I am confident that cases 1 and 3 are assertions and that 8 is not; cases 6 and 9 genuinely puzzle me.

Propose the definition, run it through all nine cases, and say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer. Then revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. I would also like to know which single case does the most work in forcing the distinction between asserting and merely uttering, and which of your verdicts you consider least secure.
