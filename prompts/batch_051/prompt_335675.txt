So I recently got asked to help run a small community grants pot where I live, and the thing I keep tripping over is recusal. My sister sits on the board of one of the charities that applied for money, so I excused myself from that bit because it felt like the proper thing to do. But then I watched two other panel members cheerfully steer cash towards outfits run by their neighbours, and nobody batted an eyelid. It's left me genuinely unsure whether my recusal was principled or just a reflex — and whether loyalty to the people you know is always a form of bias, or sometimes exactly the sort of local knowledge a small fund ought to be leaning on. I've read a few quick summaries, so I know there is a real split between people who think special obligations are genuine reasons and people who think impartiality is the only thing that makes an act defensible, but none of it helps me work out which position I'm actually in, case by case.

So here's what I'd like: an eight-to-ten-question "which position do you actually hold?" quiz on loyalty to your own people versus impartiality, with a scoring key. The audience is my panellists and people like them — normal adults making real decisions about favouring friends, family, neighbours, church groups, that sort of thing — not philosophy students. So no question should let someone pick a label, and no question should say "do you believe in special obligations?"; they should be concrete cases (hiring a friend, whistleblowing on a colleague, who you pull out of a burning building, awarding a grant, defending a friend who's in the wrong, covering for someone) with two or three answer options each. Build it from the distinctions underneath rather than from the names: whether relationships create reasons you wouldn't otherwise have; whether only chosen relationships count or unchosen ones like family and neighbourhood do too; whether partiality is ultimately just a useful device for producing the most impartial good, or part of what makes a life worth living; and which side gives way when loyalty and impartiality genuinely conflict. I'd like the scoring key to map answer patterns onto at least these positions: no-special-obligations impartialism, an instrumental/rule-of-thumb view where partiality is justified by its good consequences, a voluntarist view where only relationships you've taken on generate the duty, an associative-duties view where unchosen memberships bind you too, and a loyalty-as-virtue view where standing by your own can be a way of being a good person rather than a bias to be corrected.

Please make the key smart about this: if two views differ only in one hard-to-test spot, either write a question that catches the difference or say plainly that your questions can't separate them. If some combination of answers has no position living in it, say so and hazard a guess about why — maybe it's incoherent, maybe just deeply unattractive, maybe nobody has thought to occupy it. And write the whole thing in British English, since I'll be adapting it for our parish magazine.
