I am a novelist drafting a novel whose pivotal episode is a Molyneux case: a woman blind from birth has her sight restored in her thirties, and before the bandages are removed she handles a wooden cube and a wooden sphere of similar size; when the two solids are first placed before her, before she may touch them, she is asked to say which is the one she held. The novel does not settle the episode, and the narrator remains genuinely uncertain how to interpret what she reports. Two philosophers have attached themselves to the manuscript: a visual empiricist, who holds that the connection between felt shape and seen shape is learned through prior association and that correct matching on first sight should not be expected; and a cross-modal nativist, who holds that at least some representation of spatial form is amodal or available to sight independently of visual experience, so that under adequate viewing conditions the woman should succeed at an above-chance rate. They have argued past each other for a year. I want you to write the document their good-faith adversarial collaboration should have produced.

The document will serve as an appendix to the novel and should have the discipline of a legal memorandum. It is to contain exactly two parts, with no preamble or closing matter. First, exactly five numbered agreed claims, each of one or two sentences; these should be substantive but jointly signable, and they should make clear what the original question presupposes, what observations would not settle it, and which neighbouring questions the original question is not. Second, immediately after the fifth claim, a disagreements table. Each row is to set out the crux in neutral wording, each side's position phrased so that its own side would accept the wording, the concrete case or evidence both sides agree would bear on the crux, and each side's prediction about it recorded in advance. Where part of the dispute seems to be verbal, for instance over what is meant by seeing, recognising, immediate, or available to sight, isolate that part and restate the substantive remainder in wording both could accept. Do not cite studies or named experiments; describe empirical considerations only as predictions about what a specified test would find, not as established results.
