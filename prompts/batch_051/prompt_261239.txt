I'm secretary of a small amateur astronomy association, and we've run into a genuinely wierd situation that I think is really about legal fictions and what a "deemed" fact actually is. Our Regulations have an interpretation clause saying: "For the purposes of these Regulations, the Association shall be taken to be a natural person of full age and sound mind, ordinarily resident at its registered office." The clause was written in 1978, when the club had a tiny clubhouse with volunteers around most evenings. Two years ago we sold the clubhouse and moved to a virtual office: our registered office is now a mail-forwarding address in a business park, nobody from the Association has ever set foot there, and the address is basically a locked mailroom with our name on a plaque.

The case: our rules also say that if a member has been expelled they can appeal, but the appeal "must be handed to the Association at its residence within 14 days of notice." Paul, a member we expelled, drove to the business park on day 13 and pushed his handwritten appeal through the letter slot. The rest of the comitee says his appeal wasn't validly delivered, because the Association doesn't "reside" there in any ordinary sense — nobody saw him, nobody signed, and the slot is only emptied every week or so by a forwarding service. So I have a practical question and a philosophical one. Practically: does the deeming clause make the business park our residence for delivery purposes, or does a fiction drafted for a physical clubhouse stop being usable when the registered office hollows out into a mailbox? Philosophically: what is that "taken to be" clause doing ontologically — is the Association a person resident at the address only as long as some human is around to play the part, or does the fiction create a legal reality that doesn't depend on the facts on the ground? Act as an expert on statutory interpretation and the philosophy of law. Start by brainstorming the plausible readings of the deeming clause, then work through what the plain words, the purpose of the clause, what the 1978 drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader would take them to mean each give for Paul's appeal. Then say which approach should win and what the whole mess tells us about how legal fictions behave when the world changes out from under them.
