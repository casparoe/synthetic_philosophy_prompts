One of the ideas I keep failing to get across in my reproductive ethics teaching is how quickly the therapy/enhancement distinction comes apart once you accept embryo screening, and how that pushes you towards something like procreative beneficence. I run a third-year bioethics seminar — about eighteen students, one 90-minute session — and I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of that, rather then just watching me draw the usual slippery slope on the board.

The standard move in the literature is to say it's fine to select against a serious childhood disease but not to select for being tall or musical or clever, and I want the students to discover for themselves how hard it is to hold that boundary when they have to place case after case on one side or the other. The aim isn't to convert them to procreative beneficence; it's to make them see why the standard line is less stable than it looks. So please design a runnable structured deliberation or sorting game: a sequence of embryo profiles — a severe childhood condition, a late-onset condition, a merely elevated risk, a non-disease trait like height, a cognitive trait, a personality disposition — where pairs or small groups have to say 'screen this one out' or 'select this one in', and then have to defend where they drew the line when the neighbouring case is slid in front of them. I'm open to other formats if you think they'd work better, but it has to work in a seminar room with no money and nothing beyond printed cards or a handout.

Please give me the full plan: the exact rounds or stages, what goes on the cards, timings, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the therapy/enhancement distinction, procreative beneficence (roughly, the view that if you're already selecting you should pick the child with the best expected life), and the non-identity point (roughly, that the embryo you didn't choose isn't harmed by never being born, so the wrong can't be straightforwardly a harm to that child). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students refusing the premise, treating the whole thing as eugenics apologetics, or everyone agreeing too quickly — and how to handle each. And be frank about which bit of the idea the activity can't reproduce and I'll need to just supply in the debrief.
