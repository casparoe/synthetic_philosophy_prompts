People in my corner of the rationalist internet lean hard on a logic analogy whenever self-trust in AI comes up, and I want to know how much weight it can actually bear. The analogy, as I understand it: an agent that trusts its own conclusions is doing the same thing as a formal system that asserts its own soundness, i.e., derives for every sentence P that if P is provable in this system then P. And Löb's theorem (the less famous cousin of Gödel's) says a strong enough consistent system can't do that across the board: if you can derive that if-P-is-provable-then-P for a particular P, Löb already gives you P itself; so deriving it for all P proves everything, which only an inconsistent system does. So the story goes that a self-improving AI can't bootstrap real self-trust from the inside, and reflection has a hard ceiling. I want it audited as an analogy, not as a proof. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the formal system, the provability operator, the reflection schema, and the actual act of trusting one's conclusions in a real agent — and then the spots where it breaks: probablistic rather than proof-theoretic reasoning, checking a particualr derivation versus asserting global soundness, trust coming in degrees or being earned empirically, adopting a policy rather than proving a theorem, and so on. Then tell me whether the no-principled-self-trust conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the work the whole time. And keep the parts that are mathematically settled clearly separate from the parts people who know this material genuinely fight about; don't pass a contested reading off as consensus.
