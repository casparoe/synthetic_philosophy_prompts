Notice an asymmetry in how adaptive explanations are received. People who nod along with the claim that zebras have stripes because stripes deter biting flies, or that a peacock's tail is elaborate because it advertises fitness, will often dismiss as an unfalsifiable just-so story the claim that men are more prone to sexual jealousy because uncertain paternity reduced ancestral fitness, or that depression persisted because low mood helped ancestors disengage from unreachable goals. The two kinds of claim have the same form: start from a trait, posit a fitness benefit, and infer that selection favored the trait for that benefit, typically without measuring heritability, reconstructing the ancestral environment, or testing the fitness claim. Is there a principled difference that justifies holding adaptive stories about human psychology to a stricter standard than adaptive stories about other animals, or should the standards be made symmetric—either both treated as hypotheses awaiting evidence, or both treated as stories? If you think the human case is special, name the exact property that makes it special, and test that property against a nonhuman case such as reconciliation behavior after conflict in chimpanzees.
