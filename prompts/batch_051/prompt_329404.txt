A few friends and I keep rehashing the same argument about the pandemic-era emergency measures — lockdowns, border closures, curfews — and I've noticed we use "legitimate" in a way that's all over the place. Sometimes we mean "legal," sometimes "morally okay," sometimes "the kind of thing a democracy can do without becoming something else." So I'm trying to pin down a working definition of legitimacy (or of a legitimate emergency measure, if you think that's the better unit) that actually does some sorting work. The thing I can't tell is whether I'm smuggling in moral justification, democratic authorization, or sheer necessity under the word "legitimate."

Here are the cases I want it to sort:

1. A health minister imposes a stay-at-home order under a decades-old public-health statute, with no new parliamentary vote, during a genuine pandemic. Courts later uphold it.
2. A hospital administrator breaks procurement rules during a ventilator shortage to buy from an unapproved supplier, and it plainly saves lives.
3. A country closes its borders to all foreign nationals during a pandemic, over public-health objections from international bodies that say the evidence for such closures is weak and the economic damage is severe.
4. A government, having declared an emergency, uses the emergency decree powers to pass a tax reform that has nothing to do with the emergency.
5. A mayor declares a curfew without the city council's approval because a disaster has made it impossible to convene the council, even though the law says only the council may declare one.
6. A state of emergency is declared to break a port strike, on the grounds that the strike is strangling the economy, rather than on public-health or safety grounds.
7. During an outbreak centered in a particular immigrant neighborhood, the authorities restrict movement only in that neighborhood, citing public health, while outbreaks of similar size elsewhere face no restrictions.

My intutions are pretty firm that 1 and 2 are legitimate and that 4 and 7 are not; 3, 5, and 6 I go back and forth on. Once you have a candidate definition, apply it case by case. Where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, either revise the definition or defend the verdict it gives — including trying to move me off my intuitions if you think I've got a case wrong. Then tell me which of the seven verdicts you're least secure about.
