Question (3 points): "Some philosophers deny bivalence: they hold that a declarative sentence can be neither true nor false. Compare two alleged sources of truth-value gaps: presupposition failure (for example, a subject term with no referent) and vagueness (a borderline case of a vague predicate). (i) For each source, briefly explain why a gap is thought to arise. (ii) Does abandoning bivalence force one to abandon the law of excluded middle? Explain. (iii) How does supervaluationism treat a borderline case of a vague predicate?"

Student's answer:
"Bivalence is the principle that every meaningful declarative sentence is either true or false. Presupposition failure and vagueness are two ways it can fail. In presupposition failure, a sentence like the present king of France is bald presupposes that there is a present king of France. Since that presupposition is false, the sentence cannot be true or false. In vagueness, a sentence like John is tall said of a borderline-tall John is neither true nor false because tall has no sharp cutoff, so there is no fact of the matter whether John falls under it. Strawson and Russell both thought the king of France sentence was false; they just disagreed about whether the problem was grammar or semantics. On (ii): excluded middle says every instance of p or not-p is true. If p is neither true nor false, then not-p is also neither true nor false, and a disjunction of two sentences without a truth value has no truth value. So yes, wherever bivalence fails, excluded middle fails too. Supervaluationism is a three-valued logic that assigns borderline cases a third value, indeterminacy. Therefore, for a borderline p, p or not-p gets the third value too, and supervaluationism gives up excluded middle as well. It is not a way of keeping classical logic while rejecting bivalence; such a position would be incoherent."
