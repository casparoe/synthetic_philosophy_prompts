I'm producing a philosophy podcast and will soon be interviewing a philosopher whose work has been defined by the conviction that Levinas has the order of explanation right: the face-to-face encounter with the Other imposes an asymmetrical, infinite responsibility, and this ethical relation precedes both epistemology and ontology, so that ethics — not metaphysics and not the theory of knowledge — is first philosophy. Her new book extends this claim into political territory, arguing that the coming of the third party and the need for comparison, equality, and justice can be thought from within the ethical relation itself, rather than being bolted on from outside.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that force her beyond a mere restatement of the position, and for each question write the follow-up you would ask if she gives the reply her commitments most naturally push her toward. The questions should include at least: one pressing her on the normative content of the face — if the face issues a prohibition on killing but delivers no determinate moral rules, how is it supposed to guide concrete choice; one on the passage from the face-to-face relation to the third party and the impersonal demands of justice, and whether that passage preserves or quietly supersedes the primacy of ethics; one on whether ethics as first philosophy can genuinely dispense with Heideggerian ontology or merely postpones the ontological questions it claims to outrank; one on the distinction between the saying and the said — whether the very act of writing a book that formulates the saying turns it into a said and thereby betrays it; one on the scope of the face — whether it must be a human face and what that implies for moral considerability beyond the human; and one on the objection that an infinite, asymmetrical responsibility is so hyperbolic as to be practically unusable or even self-undermining. Where the literature has a standard reply — for example, that the third is always already present in the face, or that the said is a necessary betrayal rather than a refutation — anticipate that reply and design the follow-up to push past it. After the questions, append a brief note indicating how confident you are in the interpretation of Levinas the questions presuppose, and identify the single interpretive point you are least sure about.
