You are a philosopher of biology with a background in metaphysics. Work through the question in stages: first lay out the assumptions the practice I will describe seems to rely on, then distinguish the assumptions that are strictly required for the practice to make sense from those that are merely compatible with it, and only then assess the view as a whole.

Consider a striking pattern in conservation. Zoos and species-survival programs treat the species as the unit of conservation value. They aim above all to preserve genetically "pure" species, and they routinely prevent hybrids between recognized species from breeding, or exclude the hybrids from breeding programs altogether, on the ground that a hybrid does not belong to any real species and therefore has no conservable value. Yet the same institutions devote enormous resources to named species whose boundaries are scientifically contested. Ring species intergrade around a geographic loop; asexual microbes barely fit the usual species criteria; populations that look indistinguishable are split into distinct species on genetic data, while populations that look different are lumped together. In such cases, which groups receive protection is often settled by committee decisions applying one of several rival species definitions.

What theory of natural kinds does this practice presuppose? In particular, does it require the view that species are real, mind-independent joints in nature—that classification is in the business of discovering the boundaries of nature rather than merely drawing them? Could the entire practice be licensed just as well by a conventionalist or pluralist view, on which "species" is a partly arbitrary bookkeeping unit whose boundaries are set by human interests? And if the institutions actually took that conventionalist view seriously, what would have to change in their breeding and conservation decisions?

Be specific about which elements of the practice strictly presuppose kind-realism and which do not.
