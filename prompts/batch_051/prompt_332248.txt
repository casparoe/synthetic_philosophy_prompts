I'm a software engineer who spends most of my time on observability and incident analysis for ML systems, and I have a fairly precise working knowledge of programming-language semantics and formal methods: observational and contextual equivalence, interface versus implementation, behavior-preserving refactoring, invariants, specifications versus implementations, and mutation testing. I have no formal background in philosophy.

There is a dispute in the philosophy of biology and medicine that sounds to me like a more abstract version of a problem I face constantly. When an anomaly detector flags that some biological system has stopped producing its normal phenomenon, investigators ask whether this is the same mechanism operating in a degraded regime, the same mechanism with a broken part, or a different mechanism producing the same surface output. Philosophers, as I understand it, disagree about what fixes the identity of a mechanism across descriptions and instances. Some say identity is fixed by the entities, activities, and their organization. Others say it is fixed by the phenomenon the mechanism produces, so a mechanism remains the same as long as the phenomenon is the same, whatever the lower-level parts. Others use a manipulation criterion, counting something as part of the mechanism only if you can intervene on it and change the phenomenon, or intervene on the phenomenon and change it. There is also a view that mechanism identity is partly an interest-relative choice of grain, boundary, and level, and a further debate about whether saying that two systems share the same mechanism is what licenses extrapolating a finding from one organism or context to another.

Restate this debate in my vocabulary. Map the positions one by one onto distinctions I actually reason with: observational equivalence of programs, contextual equivalence, the same control/data-flow structure up to renaming, a shared implementation, satisfying the same specification, passing the same regression suite, and a refactoring being behavior-preserving. Map entities-activities-organization onto components, operations, and the call/data-flow graph. Map the manipulation criterion onto something like fault injection, mutation testing, or dependency tracing. Map the same-phenomenon view onto the required behavior or SLO the system is held to. And map the normal-versus-pathological question onto the difference between the same code path receiving different inputs or parameters and a genuinely different code path being exercised.

Then give me the residue report. For each philosophical position, say what carries over cleanly, what had to be distorted, and what cannot be expressed in the software vocabulary at all. Flag the places where my vocabulary smuggles in its own assumptions, such as the fact that programs have explicit interfaces, finite descriptions, and designed purposes, while a biological mechanism has none of these exactly. Conclude with your verdict: is the untranslatable residue mainly a deficiency of my framework, or evidence that the philosophical phrase same mechanism was doing several different jobs that come apart once translated? Keep the answer to no more than 1,200 words.
