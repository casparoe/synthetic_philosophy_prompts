I direct the grants program at a mid-sized family foundation whose stated mission is the relief of poverty and the expansion of human capability. Over the past two years our board has split on a question that now surfaces in nearly every review cycle: whether material prosperity itself has moral worth, or only the goods it buys — health, education, security, agency, leisure. One faction argues we should be willing to fund proposals that raise incomes or expand local productive capacity even when we cannot trace the money to specific welfare gains in the near term, because an enlarged stock of material possibility is itself a constituent of a good life. The other faction calls this growth for its own sake smuggled into the mission. The result is that staff decisions keep getting overturned at the committee stage, and applicants cannot tell what we actually value.

I need you to turn the underlying philosophical issue into a screening procedure my program officers can use before anything reaches the committee. We screen roughly forty proposals per cycle; each officer has about twenty-five minutes per proposal, no formal training in philosophy, and authority to decline on their own, while anything advanced goes to the full committee and then to me. Ground the procedure in the distinction between final value and instrumental value — where final value means worth for its own sake and instrumental value means worth because of what it brings about — but write it in plain operational language. It should be a checklist or flow of questions run in a fixed order, with clear criteria for advance, decline, or send to committee. It must distinguish proposals that treat prosperity as a mere proxy for other benefits, proposals that assert prosperity as an end, and proposals that mix the two. The hard cases it must handle include: wealth that creates options no one currently uses; wealth valued by a community chiefly as collective achievement or status; and wealth whose near-term beneficiaries are not poor but whose benefits are claimed to diffuse later. Mark each step where the underlying question is one of values the foundation must settle for itself, not something the procedure can decide, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot do.

Write the procedure, then run it on three proposals: (1) a cash grant to very poor households that raises consumption but does not materially change productive assets; (2) a community-owned solar and battery cooperative that raises local incomes and also expands what local firms can do; and (3) a proposal I am personally enthusiastic about from a town I know, to build a small industrial park that would raise gross domestic product in the region but mostly by relocating businesses from the next town. I want the procedure to be honest about the step at which my enthusiasm for proposal (3) should be overruled.
