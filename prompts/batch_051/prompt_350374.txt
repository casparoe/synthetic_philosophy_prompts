After a long argument with a friend about sex-work policy — we went through the Nordic model, German and Dutch legalization, and New Zealand-style decriminalization — I came away with a clear record of her positions on particular cases but no statement of the principle behind them, and I do not think she could state one either. Her settled verdicts, as she repeated them, were these:

1. An independent adult escort who advertises her own services and sees clients in her own flat ought to face no criminal penalty.
2. The adult client who buys from her, with no deception or coercion on either side, ought to face no criminal penalty.
3. An ordinary cab driver paid his regular fare to take a sex worker to an outcall, knowing the purpose but otherwise uninvolved, ought to face no criminal penalty.
4. A partner or spouse who shares a household whose pooled income is partly earned in sex work, without applying any pressure, ought to face no criminal penalty.
5. A manager who sets a worker's prices, screens her clients, controls the money, and pays her an allowance ought to be prosecuted.

Our argument broke down before we could settle what she thinks about a further case: a website that lets independent escorts post advertisements for a flat monthly listing fee, takes no percentage of any booking, and imposes no conditions on pricing, screening, or terms of work.

What I want to know is not which familiar policy package she belongs under. I want to know: which candidate principles fit all five of her earlier verdicts; what each candidate principle predicts about the website; which of the five earlier cases is the closest analog to the website under each candidate principle; your best guess as to her verdict on the website, with your reasons; whether her five verdicts genuinely underdetermine the website case; and, if they do, what further case would most cleanly separate the remaining candidate principles, and which way you expect her to go on that further case.
