I work in a university-affiliated lab that is building a robot to assist elderly people in their homes, and the team has become stuck on a dispute that increasingly feels more like philosophy than engineering. Our robot's social behavior is learned: it has been exposed to many hours of recorded interactions with highly skilled human caregivers, and it then produces the kind of response those caregivers would produce in a similar situation. In one of our evaluation scenarios, a resident with advanced dementia becomes distraught and asks after her husband, who in fact died several years earlier. The caregiver whose behavior the robot typically learns from, confronted with an episode of this kind, once told the resident that her husband was out for a walk and would return soon; the resident grew calm. When our robot was run silently through the same scenario, it produced a remark of the same kind. Part of the team regards this as the system working as intended: the human caregiver acted compassionately, and the system learned to act as a compassionate and competent caregiver acts. Another part of the team is uneasy: the robot has no grasp of death, of deception, or of the fact that it is sparing another being a certain kind of pain; it is, at bottom, reproducing a behavioral pattern whose point it cannot see. The project lead has now proposed a compromise. We would stop training the misleading behavior and instead encode a hard rule: the robot must never knowingly mislead a user. But we have been unable to write that rule in a way that survives our own test cases. A strict version blocks harmless fictional remarks that patients clearly benefit from; a softer version allows any comforting falsehood, which is no longer a rule against misleading anyone but a rule for calming people. We keep circling back to two questions. First, when a machine reproduces the lenient deceits of a competent caregiver, has it done what the caregiver did, ethically speaking, or has it merely performed the outward shape of an action whose moral character it cannot entertain? Second, if we cannot formulate a workable hard rule against misleading users, does that show that the project lead's unease was misplaced, or only that our first attempts at the rule were badly formed? We are not looking for a survey of current debates; we need a clear, defensible position I can take into the next design meeting. How should we resolve this, and what would the best resolution commit us to about what it actually takes for a machine to act well rather than merely to imitate well?
