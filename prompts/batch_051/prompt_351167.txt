im TAing an intro philosophy of language course and i need to grade a short answer, but i want a second pair of eyes before i put a number on it. The question on the problem set was:

"What are generic statements (e.g., ducks lay eggs, mosquitoes carry malaria), and why do they pose a problem for a truth-conditional semantics that explains truth in terms of how things stand with particular members of the kind? Discuss, using those two examples, how generics manage to tolerate exceptions."

Heres the student's answer:

Generics are generalizations like ducks lay eggs or mosquitoes carry malaria that don't wear their quantifier on their sleeve. All ducks lay eggs is false, because male ducks and ducklings don't lay eggs, and some ducks lay eggs is too weak, but ducks lay eggs still comes out true. So generics can't just be universal or existential quantifications over individual ducks.

The obvious fix is that a generic quantifies over the normal members of the kind: ducks lay eggs really means all normal ducks lay eggs, where a normal duck is a mature female. This also explains why exceptions don't refute the generic. The ducks that fail to lay eggs are simply abnormal in the relevant respect, so the exception just shows they fall outside the domain of the universal.

This also shows that the truth of a generic is at bottom a matter of statistical prevalence. Ducks lay eggs is true because egg-laying is what the overwhelming majority of ducks (the adult females) do, and mosquitoes carry malaria is true for the same reason: even though only a tiny percentage of mosquitoes actually carry the parasite, it is the disease mosquitoes are famous for, so it is prevalent in whatever sense matters.

So semantics bottoms out in numbers: a generic is true when enough members of the kind have the property, context decides how much is enough, and the only real complication is that context moves the threshold around.
