I've been noticing lately that when people argue about whether a bigger family, a bigger city, or a bigger species is better, they slide between two quantities that aren't the same: total welfare and average welfare. A bigger family can look better by the total even if each member ends up slightly worse off, and a denser city can look worse by the average even if its total utility is higher. The moment I saw this I couldn't unsee a parallel with thermodynamics, where the same split shows up as extensive quantities (energy scales with the size of the system) and intensive quantities (temperature stays fixed when you double the system). Is that a real structural identity, or am I just pattern-matching? If its genuine, does anything actually transfer—for instance, the thermodynamic point that intensive quantities are not less real or less explanatory than extensive ones, or the point that even in thermodynamics the mean isn't the whole story because the distribution matters too? And where does the analogy break first? I suspect it would predict that total and average are the only two candidates in population ethics, when ordinary moral thought seems to track distributional facts as well; I'd like that checked. Answers in American English please.
