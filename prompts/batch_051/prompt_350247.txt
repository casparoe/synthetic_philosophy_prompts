I'm a PhD student in political philosophy and I've been going in circles on something my advisor treats as obvious: what actually counts as a moral compromise. The standard story I keep hearing is that compromise is a loss — you give up part of what you value to reach an agreement, and the interesting question is when that loss is justified. But I've been thinking about compromises between value systems rather than between interests, and there's a picture that doesn't fit the loss story at all. If two people have different moral priorities, there can be gains from trade: suppose I care much more about climate than about animal welfare, and you're the reverse. We could each make a concession on our low-priority issue in exchange for the other's support on our high-priority issue, and by our own seperate lights we both end up in a morally better place than if we'd stuck to our guns. Nobody betrayed a core commitment; it feels more like a transaction in value-space than the usual sad story of compromise. I'm not sure whether that should count as compromise, logrolling, or something else.

So I'd like a working definition of "moral compromise" that draws the line where I can't. Run it through these cases and tell me where it surprises you or needs revision: (1) a climate activist and an animal-welfare activist publicly back each other's bill, each thinking the other's issue is comparatively minor, because the package helps both; (2) a pacifist takes a desk job at a defense contractor because it's the only way to support a disabled sibling, and their convictions haven't changed; (3) a legislator votes for a bill containing a provision she thinks is mildly unjust because the same bill funds a health program she's fought for for years; (4) a vegan eats a small portion of meat at a host's table to avoid humiliating them; (5) two friends with different dietary ethics order a vegetarian pizza and the omnivore genuinely doesn't mind; (6) a couple with different religions agree to raise their child in one faith while teaching the other's holidays; (7) someone stays silent through a relative's bigoted remark at dinner to keep the peace; (8) after a long argument, one friend genuinely concludes the other is right about immigration and says so; (9) a coalition government drops a core manifesto promise from each party to form a cabinet; (10) a doctor and a Jehovah's Witness family settle on a bloodless surgical approach the doctor thinks is medically inferior but still acceptable.

I think (1), (3), and (6) are compromise; (2), (5), and (8) aren't; and the rest I keep fliping on. Give me the definition, apply it to all ten, and flag any place where your definition's verdicts come apart from mine — I'm open to being wrong about my own cases.
