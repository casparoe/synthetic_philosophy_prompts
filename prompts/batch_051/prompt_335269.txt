I teach an upper-level undergraduate moral theory seminar with sixteen students, and I have one ninety-minute session to spend on what is usually called the paradox of deontology. The idea I want them to feel before anyone names it is this: an agent-relative constraint can present itself as binding in the first person — for instance, I may not frame an innocent person to stop a riot, or authorize one unjust detention to prevent several — while at the same time, when those same choices are viewed cumulatively from the outside, everyone's honoring the constraint can leave the world containing more of the very treatment the constraint was supposed to prevent. By the end, they should see this as a genuine structural puzzle internal to deontological commitments rather than a point that automatically favors consequentialism. I would like the students to arrive at that tension through their own decisions, not through a lecture, and I would prefer a scenario fresh enough that their stock intuitions about familiar trolley-type cases are not doing the work.

Please design a runnable ninety-minute session: a role-play, staged deliberation, or small-stakes game with explicit roles or rules, materials that can be assembled from ordinary classroom supplies, a realistic minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps particular moments of the exercise onto the structure of the paradox — especially the gap between deciding as the agent and evaluating the aggregate outcome as an outside observer, and the fact that both verdicts can still feel rationally binding. The students should not hear the label before the debrief, and the point should show up in what they do rather than in what I say at the end. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run the activity and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by me afterwards.
