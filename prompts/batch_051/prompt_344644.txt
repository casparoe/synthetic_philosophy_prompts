I keep running into this argument in debates about wild animal suffering — last time it was my friend who's into the whole effective altruism thing — that an ecosystem is basically a giant legacy codebase. Millions of interacting componants, no tests, zero documentation, and it's been running in production forever. The argument goes: you don't refactor a critical legacy system you don't fully understand, because changes cascade into outages nobody saw coming, and intervening in nature is the same kind of risk. So vaccinating animals, population control, predator management, that sort of thing — the conclusion people draw is just leave nature alone.

I'm a backend engineer, so the analogy defintely lands with me, but I suspect it's carrying more weight than it looks like. Can you audit it for me? Like, first give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the code, the modules, the coupling, the tests, the refactor, the outage, the rollback, the maintainer, the spec. Then tell me where the analogy actually breaks in ways that matter for the don't-intervene conclusion. The thing I keep tripping on is that in software there's a spec, or at least users who file bugs when something breaks. What's the equivalent in an ecosystem — and if there isn't one, does the whole argument fall over? And if the analogy is busted in important ways, is there a better one for what intervening in wild nature is like?
