ok so in my philosophy of mind class we did the causal exclusion argument and I came away with a version of it that seems to prove way to much, and now i cant find the exit. I want a diagnosis: which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what do I have to pay to reject it. here's the argument as I've got it:

(1) every physical event that has a cause has a complete physical cause. (causal closure)
(2) no event has more than one complete cause, unless it's a genuine case of overdetermination.
(3) when I decide to raise my hand, the decision is a mental event and the hand rising is a physical event.
(4) my decision is distinct from the neural event that is the complete physical cause of the hand rising.
(5) so if my decision caused the hand rising, the hand rising would have two complete causes: the decision and the neural event.
(6) that's not a genuine case of overdetermination (no firing squad).
(7) so my decision doesn't cause my hand to rise. mental causation is an illusion.

but the argument never uses anything special about the mental. run it on a drought causing a famine, a rock breaking a window, a recession causing unemployment: the drought is distinct from the complete physical cause of the famine, so by the same reasoning the drought doesn't cause the famine. you end up with: nothing above fundamental physics ever causes anything. and then i noticed something worse. take the neural event N, the complete physical cause of the hand rising. N is a physical event, so it has a complete physical cause of it's own at the molecular level, and N is distinct from that molecular realizer. so by the same reasoning N doesn't cause the hand rising either. and the molecular cause is distinct from its atomic realizer, and so on down. it looks like the argument drains causation out of every level, including the physical level it was supposed to protect.

i don't believe the conclusion. i'd most like to keep (1) and the claim that mental states cause behavior. my hunch is that (2) is doing something sneaky — maybe "complete cause" is ambiguous between "a sufficient cause" and "the whole causal history" — but i get tangled when i try to say exactly what. where does this actually go wrong, if it does? and if it doesn't, which premise is the least costly to give up, and what do i have to accept instead? name the step, why it looked fine, and the bill.
