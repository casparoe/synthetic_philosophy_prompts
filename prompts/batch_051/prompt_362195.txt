I'm in a course called "The Enlightenment on Trial," and we're doing a unit on Kant's essay "An Answer to the Question: What is Enlightenment?" (1784). I got a bit lost on this paragraph, and I'd rather actually figure it out than fake it in discussion. We're reading the Gregor translation:

"But what sort of restriction hinders enlightenment, and what sort does not hinder but instead promotes it? – I reply: The public use of one’s reason must always be free, and it alone can bring about enlightenment among human beings; the private use of one’s reason may, however, often be very narrowly restricted without this particularly hindering the progress of enlightenment. But by the public use of one’s own reason I understand that use which someone makes of it as a scholar before the entire public of the world of readers. What I call the private use of reason is that which one may make of it in a certain civil post or office with which he is entrusted. Now, for many affairs conducted in the interest of a commonwealth a certain mechanism is necessary, by means of which some members of the commonwealth must behave merely passively, so as to be directed by the government, through an artful unanimity, to public ends (or at least prevented from destroying such ends). Here it is, certainly, impermissible to argue; instead, one must obey."

The thing tripping me up is that the labels feel backwards. If a pastor is preaching or an officer is drilling troops, I'd call that public, but Kant puts that on the private side; writing for a reading public, which sounds private to me (you sit alone and write, people read it alone), he calls public. So what contrast is he actually trying to draw? And does the distinction do the work he wants? He seems to be saying we can have unrestricted freedom of criticism in one role while being required to obey in another, and that the obedience part won't slow down enlightenment. But is that a stable line, or just a convenient one for someone living under Frederick's censorship? My prof keeps pushing the question of whether the Enlightenment is best seen as a source of emancipation or of new forms of discipline, and I can't tell wether this passage cuts one way or the other—it honestly sounds like both at once.

Could you clarify what Kant means by "public" and "private" use of reason here, why he thinks restricting private use is compatible with the progress of enlightenment, and whether the distinction actually holds up? Also, since I know this passage gets read in very different ways, tell me where your reading is weakest and what argument or textual evidence would make you change your mind about it.
