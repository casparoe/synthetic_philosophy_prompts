Here is an argument I have been unable to assess on my own, and I would like you to check it step by step. Its conclusion is that the dispute between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness is merely verbal. I do not know whether the argument is valid, and I cannot find the weak step if there is one. My hunch is that it slides between two senses of the word difference, but I need you to show whether that is right.

Background. Consider a sorites series of color patches running from a clearly red patch at one end to a clearly orange patch at the other. Epistemicism says there is a single sharp boundary between the red and the non-red patches; every patch in the series is either red or not red; and our inability to say where the boundary is amounts to ignorance, not indeterminacy. Supervaluationism says that red has many admissible sharpenings, each drawing the boundary somewhere in the middle; a sentence about a patch is true simpliciter when it is true on every admissible sharpening, false simpliciter when it is false on every admissible sharpening, and neither true nor false otherwise.

The argument runs as follows:

(1) The two views agree on every ordinary-language behavior a speaker can produce or respond to. They agree on the clear cases; they agree that competent speakers will neither flatly assert nor flatly deny that a middle patch is red; they agree on when such claims should be retracted under pressure; and they agree that excluded middle holds for every patch in the series — each patch is either red or not red.

(2) The only remaining difference is over an evidence-transcendent boundary. The epistemicist posits one sharp boundary that no speaker can locate; the supervaluationist posits a range of admissible sharp boundaries with the same inaccessibility. The two views do not differ about any fact a speaker could ever detect.

(3) If two theories differ in no possible observation, assertion, retraction, or inferential behavior, and differ only over an evidence-transcendent boundary, then the difference between them is merely verbal: choosing one rather than the other is choosing a notation, not a substantive doctrine.

(4) Therefore, epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness are notational variants, and the debate between them is merely verbal.

Please do three things. First, assess the argument's validity: does (4) follow from (1) through (3)? If the argument is invalid, identify the exact inference step that fails. Second, for each premise, give the strongest reading on which it is true, and say whether the premise is true on that reading. Third, give the diagnosis in full: say which step is doing the illicit work, whether the argument can be repaired by weakening or strengthening one of the premises, and whether any such repair would beg the question against one of the two views. In particular, examine the possibility that the argument conflates two senses of difference — one first-order and one semantic — and consider whether premise (3), on its strongest true reading, supports only empirical equivalence rather than notational variance. Say what an epistemicist and a supervaluationist should each say about the argument's conclusion.
