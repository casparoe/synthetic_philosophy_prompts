Over on a forum thread about the paradox of deontology, I kept running into a move that feels more like a spell than an argument. The argument was whether a deontologist who refuses to kill one innocent person even to stop five other innocent people from being killed is implicitly weighting the one against the five; whenever someone pressed that point, one poster would just reply "let justice be done though the heavens fall" — sometimes in Latin, fiat iustitia, ruat caelum — and the thread would move on as though the five deaths were now beside the point and the person asking was just being a naive consequentialist. But when I try to pin down what the one-liner is actually claiming, it keeps coming apart in my hands. It could be, for instance, (a) a claim that doing the right thing is lexically prior to all outcome reasoning; (b) a refusal to do consequentialist bookkeeping at all, rather than any claim within it; (c) a kind of trust that if everyone respected the constraint the aggregate would somehow work out; (d) pure rhetoric about what kind of agent to be, with no propositional content at all; probably others.

So could you lay out the distinct precise claims that "let justice be done though the heavens fall" might be making, running from the ones nobody denies to the ones that are basically eschatological, with a verdict on each reading (trivial, substantive but in need of defense, probably false, or just rhetoric)? And then, seperately, say which of those readings would actually do the work the poster was using it for — namely, defusing the objection that a deontologist who won't kill one to save five is, by her own lights, treating the one as worth more than the five. If none of the readings carries that load, I want to know that too.
