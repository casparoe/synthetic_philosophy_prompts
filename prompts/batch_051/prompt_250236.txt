I've spent way too many years on LessWrong and the adjacent blogs, and one move I keep seeing is Dennett's line that the self is a center of narrative gravity. A friend has been using it lately to settle several debates at once: the self isn't a real thing in the head, it's an abstraction like a center of gravity, so questions about whether you survive uploading or whether you have free will are category errors, and there's nothing precious to defend. I'm broadly sympathetic, but I've got a nagging feeling the analogy is doing more work than its being inspected.

You are a philosopher of mind who has actually read Dennett carefully and can also spot where rationalists over-extend him. Start by brainstorming the correspondences and the disanalogies before you commit to anything, and think carefully about which disanalogies actually matter for the deflationary conclusion and which are just cosmetic.

Then audit the analogy for me. What maps onto what between a center of gravity and a self? A center of gravity doesn't cause anything, isn't located in any one part of the object, and moves around as the object flexes; a self feels like it's doing causal work and has some continuity over time. Is that a disanalogy that matters, or is the feeling of causal work just part of the story the brain tells? Does the narrative part do different work than the gravity part, and are we actually smuggling in two analogies at once? Where does the analogy start loosing its grip? And once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that the self is a useful fiction actually follow, or is it being imported by the choice of source domain? If the center-of-gravity picture breaks exactly where it's needed, what would a better analogy be?
