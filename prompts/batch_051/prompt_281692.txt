Eihei Dōgen's fascicle Uji ("Being-Time") in the Shōbōgenzō contains the following passage, in Reiho Masunaga's translation:

"So the pine tree is time; the bamboo is time. Do not think that time merely flies by. Do not learn that flying by is the only function of time. For if you recognize time as flying by, there is an interval (between going and coming). The truth of uji is not truly grasped because time is understood as only passing. Ultimately all existences are linked and become time."

Dōgen wrote in the thirteenth century, well before McTaggart's argument that the A-series is contradictory and before twentieth-century philosophy of time split into A-theorists (time genuinely passes; only the present is fully real; tensed facts are fundamental) and B-theorists (passage is an illusion of perspective; all times are equally real in a block universe; tenseless relations are fundamental). I want to work out, as rigorously as the text allows, where a view like Dōgen's should be placed in — or against — that later debate.

First unpack the passage: what exactly is Dōgen denying when he says not to think that time "merely flies by," and what positive account of moments is implied by saying that pine and bamboo are time and that "all existences are linked and become time"? Then argue for the most textually warranted extrapolation: would Dōgen be an A-theorist, a B-theorist, or is uji a third position that the A/B opposition cannot represent? If you take the third option, identify the specific assumption shared by both modern camps that Dōgen would reject. Finally, tell me which one modern thesis — the privileged present, the block universe, the unreality of tense, or the unreality of change — Dōgen would regard as most mistaken, and show how the quoted passage supports that judgment. I am less interested in a "this is ineffable" resolution than in a definite, argued reconstruction.
