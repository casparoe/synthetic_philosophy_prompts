I am drafting a novel in which two former collaborators, both game theorists, fell out years ago over a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma that emerged from an absurd dispute about a shared prize, and whose lingering question is whether one of them should have cooperated. Researching their positions pulled me into Hofstadter's superrationality, and I have now reconstructed an argument that seems to me to prove far too much — yet I cannot find the exact step at which it overreaches. Here is the argument as I understand it. (1) If two agents face exactly the same decision-relevant situation — identical payoffs, information, and reasoning capacities, with common knowledge of all of that — and they reason correctly from the same principles, then any consideration that would justify one in cooperating would justify the other in cooperating, and likewise for defecting; so their decisions must coincide. (2) Therefore, in a symmetric one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between such agents, the only outcomes consistent with their common reasoning are mutual cooperation and mutual defection; the mixed outcomes cannot arise. (3) Since mutual cooperation gives each a higher payoff than mutual defection, each should cooperate. (4) This, as I have pieced it together, is the nerve of superrationality. (5) But the argument appears to use no fact about the two agents except that they are rational and in the same game; nothing in it requires that they know each other's characters, share a history, or have any particular reason to expect their reasoning to be alike. (6) So if superrationality is correct, I ought to cooperate in any one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with any rational stranger — a counterpart at an antiques fair, a rival I will never meet again, even a stranger I know to be a strict expected-utility maximizer who will defect because defection strictly dominates. (7) Yet to cooperate against a known defector is knowingly to accept the sucker's payoff. A principle of rational choice that instructs a person to walk knowingly into exploitation has proved too much.

I would most like to keep step (1), since it seems to express only the determinacy and universality of correct reasoning, and I also want to keep the thought behind (7), since surrendering it seems to give up on prudential rationality in favor of something like martyrdom. Which step or inference in this argument do you reject, why did it look legitimate, and what does rejecting it cost for the rest of the view?
