So our lab has been fighting about stats ever since a big replication project fell apart, and I've been asked to write up "my actual position" for a methods discussion next week. I'd like a consistency check before I present, because I have a feeling I'm holding a few things that don't quite fit. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(1) I'm Bayesian about evidence in principle. Priors are unavoidable, data shift degrees of belief, and once the data are collected, what they support should depend only on what was actually observed, not on the stopping rule or on what other analyses I would have run if the results had come out differently. (2) But in practice I think optional stopping is a real problem and that peeking at the data inflates false positives, so I think we should preregister stopping rules or use sequential corrections. (3) I think the replication crisis is mostly not a contest between frequentism and Bayesianism; it's p-hacking, low power, and publication bias, so preregistration and data sharing matter more than whether a paper reports a p-value or a Bayes factor. (4) I also think the field wildly understimates how many published p < .05 findings are false because base rates are low, and that we should use prior information much more. But I don't trust anyone's prior enough to put a number on it in print, so I'd rather not see subjective Bayes factors replace p-values. (5) I think the .05 cutoff is arbitrary and that .049 versus .051 shouldn't flip anyone's conclusion, but I'll still defend error control as the thing that gives a hypothesis test its meaning.

Which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they're in tension, and what is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and tell me which single commitment I should give up if the set can't be made coherent, along with what giving it up would cost.
