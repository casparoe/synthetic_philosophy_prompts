Consider a small residential cooperative that keeps a written code of standing duties: specific kinds of work and mutual aid that every member must perform. The cooperative adopts the following rule, called the ratchet. A member who has done something not required by the code at the time—something costly, useful, and optional—may later petition the assembly to add that kind of act to the code as a standing duty binding on all members. Only the person who performed the act may bring the petition. Approval takes a two-thirds vote; the new duty cannot be amended or repealed for twelve months; and the petitioner is recorded in the minutes as the duty's originator but receives no material reward.

Treat the ratchet as a mechanism, not as a moral proposal. Walk through what it does to who performs costly optional acts, which of those acts get proposed, and how the assembly is likely to vote. Where does the system get gamed first, what second-order effects does it have on the community's boundary between duty and supererogation, and can its failure modes be fixed without abandoning the ratchet's purpose?
