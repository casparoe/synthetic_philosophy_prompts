In a discussion about whether the "lessons of history" help us think about present conflicts, I argued that analogies like Munich, Vietnam, or the Thucydides trap usually mislead, because the differences between the old case and the current one overwhelm the parallels. A colleague conceded that no two events are identical, but insisted the value of such analogies is that large patterns recur. When I asked which pattern recurs, he quoted the saying: history doesn't repeat itself, but it rhymes. I think this is doing more argumentative work than a bare admission that details vary.

Treat that saying as a slogan in need of disambiguation. Lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making about how historical cases bear on present decisions — about exact repetition, recurring structures, law-like regularities, human nature, the defeasibility of analogy, or any further readings you find. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and whether it actually supports drawing a warning for U.S.–China relations from ancient or twentieth-century precedents. Then say which reading his argument most likely needs, how confident you are in that assessment, and which point you are least sure of.
