I keep finding myself attracted to a fully social, fallibilist picture of evidence. On this view nothing is self-authenticating: a photo or video only gets its evidential weight from background assumptions about how it was produced and who's vouching for it, so my response to any recording should really be a kind of channel-checking rather than taking it as given. It's an elegant theory because it handles forgeries, staged photos, and testimony all with one story. But I also have a firm intuition I can't shake that this can't be the whole story, because we seem to need an epistemic backstop. When a clear video of a politician taking a bribe drops, with metadata from three independent sources and his own voice plain as day on it, it doesn't land on me as merely one more fallible trace. It settles things in a way the social picture makes mysterious—and if deepfakes mean any such video could be synthesized, then the theory seems forced to say the backstop is an illusion, while my intuition says its what makes public disputes end at all.

So here's the clash I want you to adjudicate in a negotiated way. Should I debunk the intuition as nostalgia for a pre-deepfake world, amend the theory to give recorded evidence a special default-trusted role, or is there a reading of the social picture that already contains the backstop? Give me the strongest version of each option, tell me which you'd give up, and say what would justify giving it up rather than just picking a side.
