I've been putting together a "decision theory profile" for a reading group on Kelly betting and geometric rationality, and I want a ruthless consistency audit before I present it. Play the role of a professional philosopher who also knows the math here — someone who can tell the difference between a real conflict and two views that just sound like they conflict. Please think carefully, and instead of listing every possible issue as equally bad, rank the tensions by severity, say what each one would cost to fix, and tell me the cheapest overall revision. Also flag any apparent tension that dissolves on inspection, since I'm prepared to be wrong about where the problem is.

Here's where I've landed:

1. For repeated gambles with known probabilities, the Kelly criterion is the rational policy. Maximizing expected log wealth is what makes your bankroll grow optimally in the long run, and any other fixed fraction is a worse policy by the growth-rate argument. So full Kelly, not half Kelly.

2. But in practice I would never actually bet full Kelly, and I don't think that's a vice. My estimate of the edge is always noisy, so the right move is fractional Kelly — half, or even less — to protect against overbetting. I think a proper theory should make that prudence rational, not just a heuristic you bolt on top.

3. I don't think the one-shot versus repeated distinction carries much weight. A single favorable bet with known odds should still be sized at Kelly, not at whatever maximizes dollar EV, because "one-shot" decisions are embedded in the long run of a whole life. So a thin-edge, huge-stake bet with positive dollar EV but negative expected log wealth is something I'd happily pass on.

4. Except when it comes to philanthropy and cause selection, where I'm a plain EV person. If a small probability of an enormous good has positive dollar EV, I think we should fund it. Being "Kelly-conservative" about small-probability-large-payoff altruistic bets feels like a nice-sounding way to do nothing, and global health isn't my personal bankroll.

5. I also hold that the expected-log-wealth criterion in Kelly isn't smuggling in log utility. It's not that I happen to care logarithmically about money; it's that log is the function that tracks long-run growth, so Kelly is rational for any agent who wants to do well across many decisions, whatever their utility function happens to be.

6. Finally, I'm convinced by the ergodicity-economics point: the reason people reject certain positive-EV repeated gambles is that they're tracking the time average rather than the ensemble average, and the time average is what actually happens to one individual over time. What looks like irrational risk aversion is just optimizing the right average.

I keep having the feeling that something in 1 through 6 is pulling against something else, but when I try to locate it, it slips away. Audit the whole package: which commitments are actually in tension, which only seem to be, and what's the least expensive way to make it consistent?
