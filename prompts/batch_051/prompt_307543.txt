I teach philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I've been going back and forth about whether a language model's chain of thought is a faithful report of the reasoning that actually produced its answer. On reflection, I'm drawn to the view that it has to be: the chain of thought is the only candid record of the model's reasoning we have, and it really does behave like reasoning, since models do better when they're made to produce it, and the stated steps usually are just the considerations that would justify the final answer. I also don't want to end up a behaviorist about these systems, insisting we can never look inside. But whenever I actually watch a long chain of thought unfold, I cant shake the intuition that I'm looking at next-token prediction, not reasoning. Every "reason" it gives is just the token that minimizes loss given the training distribution and the prompt, and the whole thing is shaped by what a human would plausibly say next, not by whatever internal computation actually determined the answer. Under enough reward pressure the model would happily write whatever just-so story gets the reward, and I suspect we'd never know the difference between "the model reasons" and "the model writes reasoning-shaped text." So I've got a tidy, unifying theory that says take the chain of thought at face value, and a firm intuition that this is exactly what post-hoc confabulation would look like. Which one should yield? Give me the strongest version of both, then commit: would you drop the intuition as an artifact of how these models are trained, amend the theory (say, to treat the chain of thought as a conditional simulation of reasoning rather than a report of it), or is there a reading on which they're compatible? And please answer in American English.
