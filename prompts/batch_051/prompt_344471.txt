Ok here's one I keep going back and forth on. My country's constitution, drafted in the 90s after a stolen election, has a little section that's done a lot of quiet work. Here it is in full, as best I remember:

"Section 47(1). The Electoral Commission shall be independent and shall not be subject to the direction or control of any person or authority. (2) The Assembly may not enact any law that impairs the independence of the Commission. (3) This section may be amended only by a two-thirds vote of the total membership of the Assembly, followed by approval in a national referendum."

Now the case everyone's fighting about. Three weeks before the presidential election, a ransomware attack locks the Commission out of its own voter-registration and results systems. The government pushes through, by a simple majority in a two a.m. sitting, an Emergency Electoral Continuity Act. It transfers the Commission's functions to the Ministry of the Interior, but only for ninety days, and only "to the extent necessary to hold the election." The Act insists it does not amend Section 47 at all; it just temporarily steps in while the Commission is "operationally incapacitated." The Commission's chair says this is exactly the thing Section 47 exists to stop, and the ninety days neatly cover the one election that matters.

Does the Emergency Act violate Section 47 even though it says "temporarily" and doesn't touch the text? Argue it from the words, from the purpose of the clause (including the Ulysses-style precommitment rationale), and from what the drafters would have said if you'd asked them about a temporary executive takeover during a cyber emergency. Then tell me which approach should control here and why. If your answer is "it depends," say on what exactly, and give a rule for the borderline cases. Keep it under 500 words.
