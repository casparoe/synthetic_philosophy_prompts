At a reading group I attend, two people got into an argument about Dutch book arguments, and none of us could settle it. Since the dispute turned on fine points inside the argument, let me set out the background the way they did. In the standard version, a person's degrees of belief are represented as fair betting prices: a price p on a ticket that pays $1 if a proposition A is true and nothing otherwise. If the person's prices violate the axioms of probability, a bookie can sell or buy a finite set of tickets, each at a price the person regards as fair, such that the person is guaranteed to lose money no matter what happens. The conclusion usually drawn is that rational degrees of belief must obey the axioms of probability.

Dana defended the argument. It is not really about money, she said, and not really about a cunning bookie. Consider someone who sets a fair price of 60 cents on a ticket paying $1 if this coin comes up heads, and 60 cents on a ticket paying $1 if it comes up tails. The two tickets together return exactly $1 no matter what. Yet by the person's own lights, reading each price as the ticket's value to them, the pair is valued at $1.20. That is an inconsistency in their evaluations themselves, visible without any bookie; the bookie merely makes it vivid. An agent vulnerable to a Dutch book has not simply met a clever exploiter. Their system of degrees of belief conflicts with the logical structure of the propositions those beliefs are about. Pricing two exhaustive possibilities at 60 cents each is a way of effectively pricing the tautology that one of them occurs at $1.20 instead of $1, and that sort of incoherence is exactly what "irrational" should name.

Emil pushed back. The argument only seems to work, he said, because it treats the two bets as a package and assumes the agent must value the package at the sum of its parts. But accepting the package is a further step. The agent can say: I will buy the heads ticket for 60 cents if offered on its own, and I will buy the tails ticket for 60 cents if offered on its own, but I will not buy both, since the pair costs $1.20 and returns $1. Stating two fair prices does not commit me to a fair price for the bundle; the value of a bundle need not be additive when the items interact, and two state-contingent tickets interact precisely by sharing a state space. So the argument is circular. It needs a composition principle — that individually fair bets remain jointly acceptable — and that principle is itself part of what the probability axioms were supposed to deliver.

Dana answered that this was a retreat rather than a rebuttal. If Emil refuses the pair, he does so on the ground that heads and tails exhaust the outcomes. But that fact was available when he priced each ticket individually. If exhaustiveness makes the bundle unattractive at $1.20, it should have made one or both tickets unattractive at 60 cents. His stated prices therefore understate his real reservations. The refusal to combine bets does not make the original evaluations coherent; it just declines to display the incoherence in a single transaction.

Emil replied that this overstates what a fair price reports. A fair price need not be a verdict on what a ticket is worth in every context; it can be a local disposition to trade that ticket at that price, sensitive to context. There is no requirement that a collection of local dispositions be embeddable in one consistent global valuation, and requiring embeddability is a substantial idealization, not a datum about rationality. And, he added, even granting the global-valuation picture, the argument's real work is done by the identification of degrees of belief with fair prices in the first place. If someone's degrees of belief are inner estimates of truth rather than prices at which they would trade, vulnerability to a Dutch book shows something about their betting dispositions, not about their beliefs.

At that point I tried to get them to separate the strands. Dana said she was arguing for two claims: a weak one, that a person whose fair prices violate the axioms thereby holds an incoherent set of evaluative commitments whether or not they ever meet a bookie; and a strong one, that rational credences constrain rational evaluations in that way, so rational credences must be probabilistically coherent. She thought both stood. Emil thought the weak claim is only as strong as the disputed composition principle allows, and the strong claim collapses unless credences have already been identified with fair prices — and neither identification comes free.

The group never resolved it.
