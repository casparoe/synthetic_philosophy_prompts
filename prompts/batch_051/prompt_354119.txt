Hey—I've been arguing with myself about three values I keep treating as though they matter in themselves but suspect only matter because they do something else: truth-seeking, tradition, and purity. You're a professional philosopher who's good at reflective equilibrium and value theory; could you think carefully about this, maybe brainstorm a few candidate principles first, and then tell me what principle captures my gut reactions, or which of them have to go.

Here are the verdicts I find myself giving, with no system behind them yet.

**Truth-seeking.**

1. My uncle's oncologist gives him a straight answer about his odds, uncertainties included. I think that's right: the truth helps him decide how to spend whatever time he has.

2. A friend has built a happy life around a religious picture I'm fairly sure is false. I dont try to talk her out of it, and I don't think I'm doing anything wrong by leaving it alone. But I also catch myself thinking truth is worth having for its own sake, and I can't square the two.

3. When someone at a party says something trivially false (like messing up a country's capital), I feel an itch to correct it, but I honestly think the itch is mostly about showing I know things, not about truth mattering.

4. A scientist who fabricates data strikes me as having done something much worse than someone who tells an ordinary lie with the same practical consequences. Corrupting the shared record feels like a whole different kind of wrong.

**Tradition.**

5. A family keeps a holiday they no longer believe in, just because they've always done it. I find that good, not irrational.

6. A suburban town keeps a ceremony that used to mark the spring planting even though nobody farms anymore. I find it touching; I'd be sad if it stopped.

7. An all-male initiation tradition that's central to a community's identity but exclusionary. I think it should change or end, even if that kills the tradition.

8. A few people work to keep a dying language alive even though its last speakers could switch to a majority language with no practical loss. I find this obviously worthwhile.

**Purity.**

9. Someone keeps kosher years after losing the religious beliefs behind it. I think that's fine, maybe even admirable.

10. Someone feels disgust at a consensual sexual practice that harms no one. I don't think that feeling of impurity gives anyone a reason at all.

11. Someone won't wear a coat that belonged to a person they hate because it feels contaminated. I think that's a compulsion, not a real norm, but I also don't blame them for it.

12. Someone does a ritual hand-washing before meals even though there's no hygiene issue. That seems fine to me, even meaningful.

My working theory is something like: these three values matter only when they sustain a person's or community's agency, identity, or coherence over time, and they go wrong when they pretend to track objective features of the world—like real contamination, or a duty to have only true beliefs, or a reason that's just because the tradition is ours. But that theory seems to make scientific fraud only instrumentally bad, which is not how I feel about it, and it makes the dying-language case only about identity, which doesn't feel like the whole story either.

Whats the principle that captures these, if there is one? Or do I have to drop some of these verdicts? Please state the principle precisely, tie each part of it to which of my cases motivates it, tell me what it says about cases I haven't considered, and be honest about which of my gut calls it can't accommodate. And please apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on: someone who deliberately doesn't check whether a nasty story they heard about a friend is true, because they'd rather not know.
