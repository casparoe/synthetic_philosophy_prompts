Take the unilateralist's curse as it's usually set up: a bunch of well-meaning agents each has to decide on there own whether to take some irreversible action—leaking a dataset, say—and each has a noisy private estimate of whether the action is net good or net harmful, so if any one of them acts, the thing happens. The standard point is that even if each person would be wrong only 10% of the time, with enough people the probabality that at least one of them wrongly acts approaches 1, so "anyone can act" is a terrible decision rule.

Now change one assumption: the agents' estimates aren't independent. They've all read the same half-baked briefing, so every private estimate shares a common bias, with only a little independent noise layered on top of it. Work through how the chance of at least one mistaken act grows with the number of agents in this correlated case, and compare it to the independent version. Then say which of the standard fixes—requiring consensus, making everyone pool information before deciding, or letting a small designated group decide—still helps, which one stops helping, and whether any of them becomes actively counterproductive. If the correlation is strong but not perfect, is there a crossover point where the group starts looking reckless again, and what sets it?
