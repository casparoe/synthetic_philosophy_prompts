I spent three decades doing physical chemistry, where a model that does not pin down its domain of applicability is a liability, and I have only recently fallen into decision theory. I want your help turning a half-formed worry into a term-paper proposal (about 6,000 words, one semester), and I would like you to treat the worry itself as open to revision.

As I understand the coherence argument for expected utility maximization, it runs as follows. If an agent's preferences satisfy the usual axioms — transitivity, completeness, independence, continuity, in whichever packaging — then there exists an assignment of probabilities and utilities such that the agent's choices maximize expected utility relative to them; if the agent violates the axioms, no such assignment is possible. The normative punch is that the axioms are requirements of coherence internal to the agent's own preferences, and the Dutch book and money-pump constructions are meant to establish this without appeal to any external standard of good outcomes.

The difficulty I keep hitting is temporal. The representation theorem says something about preferences at a time, over acts specified once and for all. Yet expected utility maximization is normally asked to do diachronic work: it is supposed to tell an agent what to do at each later node of a sequential problem, which plan to stick to, and why a successor self that repudiates the plan has gone wrong. An agent could, it seems, be a flawless static expected-utility maximizer at every moment while the utility function drifts: Monday's coherent preferences and Tuesday's coherent preferences are each representable, but perhaps only by different functions, and no axiom is violated within either day. If that is right, the coherence argument, taken at full strength, justifies only a time-sliced or local version of the principle, and the move from local coherence to the diachronic rule — hold one utility function fixed and maximize with respect to it across time — requires a further argument that may not be a coherence argument at all; it may be an argument about stability, self-command, or the authority of one's earlier self.

Write me the proposal for that question. I would like it to state the question so that a determinate answer is possible and so that someone who rejects my framing can say exactly where it goes wrong. Then explain why the existing arguments, as I have reconstructed them, leave the diachronic step open. Then lay out an approach: what would need to be shown, why the obvious replies — for instance, that we can always assign the agent a time-indexed utility function, or that dynamic consistency by itself forces a single utility function — either close the gap or fail to, and what a positive and a negative result would each look like. Include an honest assessment of scope: whether the full question is too large for a semester, what would count as failure rather than a merely negative finding, and what a defensible smaller version of the project would be. I will supply citations myself, so refer to positions and arguments without assembling the literature.

One further instruction about the kind of answer I want. I am not after a polished essay that picks the stronger-sounding side. If the premise is confused — if the representation theorems are not static in the way I have assumed, or if the local/diachronic distinction collapses on a standard reading, or if the question is otherwise badly posed — say so plainly and tell me what question a clearer version of my worry should have asked. I would rather be corrected at the level of the question than receive a competent proposal for a project built on sand.
