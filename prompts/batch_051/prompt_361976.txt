In my AI ethics seminar I keep tripping over an asymmetry in how people treat contracts made before a power shift, and I'd like you to either justify it or show it's just a prejudice. Everyday practice is full of bargains struck before one party gets strong, where we still expect the later, stronger party to be bound. A nineteen-year-old signs a deal that still binds the thirty-five-year-old who now has all the leverage; a startup founder takes early money on terms that still bind the company after it becomes huge. We don't treat those as oppressive or meaningless; we treat the later party who tries to wriggle out as the one behaving badly. But many of the same people get uneasy about what gets called pre-AGI bargains: agreements made now, before anyone has AGI, about how a post-AGI system's power may be used or distributed, meant to hold later when some party to the arrangement — or the system itself — is vastly more capable than whoever signed. The same person who'd enforce a lopsided record deal against a now-famous musician will often say a pre-AGI bargain can't bind anything, because you can't contract with something that doesn't exist yet or that will outclass you.

What I want is a diagnosis of whether the asymmetry is justified, not a survey of AI governance work. Give me exactly five numbered items, no opening or closing sentence. Each item should be one or two sentences: state a candidate principle that might separate ordinary pre-power-shift contracts from pre-AGI bargains, then say whether it actually survives the obvious countercase. Make the fifth item the one you'd really defend.
