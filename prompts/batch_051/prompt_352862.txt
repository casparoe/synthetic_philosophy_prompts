I've come round to the Dennettian view that the distinction between original and derived intentionality is ultimately a mirage — that there's no difference in kind between the aboutness of my thought that its raining and the aboutness of a traffic light or a chess engine's representation of the board. Searleans want there to be some intrinsic aboutness that minds have and word and symbols only borrow, but I no longer see what that intrinsic ingredient could even be. There's no two-bitser: no extra little component you could bolt onto a robot or a philosophical zombie to flip it from merely derived to genuinely original intentionality. All intentionality is derived, in one way or another; "original" is just a status we hand to ourselves.

What I'd like is a map of what would count against this position. Not the familiar objections — I know the Chinese Room, the guy in the room, all that — but the stuff that would actually put pressure on it: which thought experiments, empirical findings, or commitments elsewhere in philosophy of mind does the view stand or fall with? Is there anything it predicts that the Searle-style view doesn't (or vice versa), so the world could turn out in a way that embarrasses me? I'm especially curious whether the view is falsifiable at all, or whether it has become one of those positions that can redescribe any apparent counterexample; and if so, whether that's a feature or a bug. What would a committed Dennettian themselves concede as counting against the view, and what would they dismiss as question-begging or beside the point? Finally, what would you personally find most damaging if you were trying to talk me out of it — and how would you rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward?
