In my reading group we're doing The Second Sex and one friend (M) read this out from the introduction, in the Parshley translation we read: "A man never begins by presenting himself as an individual of a certain sex; it goes without saying that he is a man." Then the fight started.

L said de Beauvoir is making a mostly methodological point: the question "what is a woman?" already treats maleness as neutral, so philosophy has to start by noticing that the questioner marks only one sex. M said no, she's making a stronger ontological point--man is the subject, woman is the Other, and the fact that men don't have to announce themselves as sexed is evidence for that. L pushed back: the sentence only shows an asymmetry in self-presentation, and you can't jump from "men don't have to announce their sex" to "man is the subject and woman is the Other"; that needs a lot of argument she hasn't given yet at that point. M said the jump isn't a jump, because not having to announce your sex is exactly what subjecthood looks like in discourse--if woman has to begin by declaring herself a woman, then her position is already marked as relative to man.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? Who wins each step, and what are both of them missing? Also say whether the ontological reading really does need more argument, or whether the methodological and ontological readings are even in competition.
