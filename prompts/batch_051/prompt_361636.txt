George Berkeley's *The Querist*, a sequence of questions about money, trade, and public policy, contains a passage in which he asks, in substance, whether money is not in reality a ticket or counter, entitling the bearer to so much of the necessaries of life as he has occasion for, and whether the true idea of money is not that of a ticket or counter rather than that of a material commodity. The passage is a query, not a finished doctrine, and I am paraphrasing it rather than quoting it.

Suppose Berkeley were alive today and asked to assess five contemporary monetary arrangements through the lens of that passage. Your task is to predict his verdict on each, giving the reasons he would most plausibly give, and then to locate the two points where modern practice would put the greatest strain on the passage's account. The arrangements are:

1. A central-bank digital currency: an electronic liability of the central bank, legal tender, usable by households but not necessarily interest-bearing, with no physical counterpart.
2. A permissionless cryptocurrency with a fixed supply, no issuer, no legal-tender status, and a market exchange rate that is highly volatile against ordinary goods and services.
3. A local mutual-credit system in which members grant one another transferable credits within a bounded commercial network; the credits circulate among members but cannot be used to pay taxes or to buy goods outside the network.
4. A stablecoin issued by a private firm, redeemable one-for-one for a national currency, and backed by short-term government debt.
5. A demand deposit at a fractional-reserve commercial bank, transferable by debit card or electronic instruction, convertible into central-bank cash on request.

For each case, say whether Berkeley, reasoning from the ticket-or-counter passage, would most likely classify it as genuine money, as an incomplete or perverted form of money, or as something else, and explain which specific feature of the passage drives the classification. Then answer this: if Berkeley wanted to preserve the core insight of the passage while accommodating the two most strained cases, would he need to abandon the ticket-or-counter idea, broaden it, or add a supplementary principle? State exactly what change to the account would be forced.
