I am a student in an interdisciplinary philosophy and economics seminar on time, rationality, and our duties to future generations. We have been studying the distinction between pure time preference — discounting a benefit solely because it lies in the future — and discounting that reflects uncertainty about delivery, opportunity costs, or the expectation that the beneficiary will be richer later. One recurring problem in our discussion is that these considerations are easy to keep apart on the page and very easy to collapse in practice, especially when people are asked to justify their own impatience.

As a course requirement, I have to design and run a 60-minute activity for the rest of the seminar (about 16 students, mostly without formal economics training), followed by a 20-minute debrief, and then write up what happened. I do not want to lecture first or introduce the technical vocabulary before the activity. I want participants to make a series of real, small-stakes choices and then notice something uncomfortable about their own pattern of choices before any of the concepts are named.

The activity I have in mind uses tokens exchangeable for snacks, small privileges, or the right to leave early — no real money. Participants face repeated binary choices between a smaller, sooner payout and a larger, later one, under a fixed budget per round so that they cannot simply take both. The payouts would be delivered at genuinely staggered times: some immediately, some at the end of the session, some later in the week, and some the following week. I am open to a better structure if you can justify it.

The key phenomena I hope to produce are, first, a clean preference reversal: choices between two future options flip once the earlier option becomes immediate, even though the interval between the two options is held fixed. Second, I want the contrast between sources of discounting to be forced into the open, so that rational responses to risk or expected growth are not mistaken for pure impatience.

Please give me (1) the complete rules and materials; (2) the exact options, token amounts, and payoff schedule; (3) a timeline for the full 80 minutes; (4) at least one built-in moment where participants compare their own earlier and later choices and will likely find an inconsistency; (5) a control condition that separates discounting for time from discounting for uncertainty about delivery; (6) a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto exponential versus hyperbolic discounting, present bias, pure time preference, and the non-time-based reasons for discounting; (7) an honest note on which part of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must supply explicitly — I suspect this is the irreducibly moral question of whether the well-being of future generations may be discounted at all, rather than the descriptive question of what people actually do; and (8) an account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, such as participants trying to appear consistent, failing to honor delayed payouts and destroying trust, or confusing a shorter delay with a higher probability of receipt, with concrete ways to handle each.
