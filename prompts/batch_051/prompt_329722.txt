I teach a seminar for fiction writers on the philosophy of dialogue, and I recently set the following short-answer question on an exam. I reproduce it here along with one student's submission. Please act as a careful, experienced grader: assign a letter grade on the standard A through F scale, then give point-by-point comments that justify the grade. I want an assessment of what the answer gets right and where, if anywhere, its reasoning slips, rather than a general impression.

Question:

You are revising a scene in which a character says to a friend, "I promise I'll be there." The friend hears and understands the sentence perfectly, but does not believe the speaker and feels no reassurance. Drawing on the speech-act distinction between an illocutionary act and a perlocutionary effect, and on the notion of uptake, explain whether the character has succeeded in promising. Your answer should also clarify what role, if any, the speaker's sincerity plays in determining whether a promise has been made, as opposed to whether it is a happy or felicitous one.

Student's answer:

The character has promised, assuming the standing circumstances are otherwise appropriate. Saying "I promise I'll be there" is a performative utterance: in saying these words the speaker does something, namely undertakes an obligation, rather than merely reporting some prior condition of her mind. In the standard account, an illocutionary act such as promising is successfully performed only if the hearer secures uptake, that is, understands the utterance and its force as a promise. The friend plainly understands, so uptake is present. The fact that the friend remains unconvinced and feels no reassurance is a perlocutionary effect of the utterance, and perlocutionary effects are not required for the illocutionary act to come off. The promise is therefore made even though it leaves the friend cold. Sincerity, however, is different. One of the felicity conditions on promising is that the speaker intend to carry out what she promises, and this condition goes to what it is to promise. If the speaker secretly has no intention of going, the utterance violates that condition, and in that case she has not really promised at all; she has merely gone through the outward form of promising while failing to perform the act. Uptake and sincerity thus function as conditions on the act itself, while perlocutionary effects are only consequences.
