I am putting together a teaching exercise on sovereign immunity and would like you to write the dialogue itself. The setting is an informal evening session after a lecture that surveyed the old doctrine that the king can do no wrong. One discussant, trained in a civil-law tradition, argues that the maxim should be read as a structural feature of legal order: the state, as a unified legal person, is constituted by the same public law that defines the state’s capacities, so wrongfulness can attach only to officials who step outside those authorizations, not to the state as such; the state’s legal personality is not an ordinary psychological agency but a point of imputation for acts of a system. The other discussant, a public-law scholar, replies that this is a fiction with a long history of blocking accountability; modern administrative law already treats the state as a corporate agent that can form intentions through authorized decision procedures, take decisions, and cause injuries, and once that is admitted there is nothing conceptually confused about saying the state did wrong—so immunity is a legal policy choice, not an ontological boundary. Write a dialogue of roughly 1,000 words in which they engage with each other’s strongest versions. Include at least one moment where each character revises or refines their own position in response to the other. The exchange should touch on corporate personality, the difference between impunity and non-wrongness, ultra vires acts, remedies against officials, and whether a legal system that denies a remedy for a wrong is still coherently recognizing a wrong. Do not have them reach a tidy agreement; end with the precise point at which they part company.
