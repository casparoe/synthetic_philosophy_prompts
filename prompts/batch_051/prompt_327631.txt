I have been turning over a question that began with statutes of limitations but quickly became a more general problem about time and responsibility. I find myself attracted to a clean, unified view: the wrongness of an act, and the wrongdoer's liability to be censured for it, are fixed by the facts at the time of the act. On this view, time itself does no moral work. It changes only our epistemic and practical situation — evidence degrades, witnesses die, memories become unreliable, and the social costs of punishing someone long reformed may grow too high. Limitation periods are therefore justified purely instrumentally, as a concession to human fallibility and to the changing costs of enforcement, not because blame actually fades. The view is simple, it unifies wrongdoing across short and long intervals, and it gives a tidy decision procedure: determine what the person deserved at the time of the act, and adjust only for what is epistemically or practically defective about reviving the matter today.

Yet I have an intuition that will not go away. A person who, at nineteen, committed a moderately serious assault and has since lived a blameless, generous life for thirty years should not be made to answer for it now in the way someone who committed the same act yesterday should. I do not mean merely that it would be difficult to try the case, or imprudent to disrupt a settled life and a changed person. I mean that the passage of time has itself altered what is owed — that the wrong has, in some respect, expired or been diluted, and that the middle-aged person now stands in a different moral relation to the act than the nineteen-year-old did. I suspect many people feel the pull of both positions, and I want to know which should yield.

Give me the strongest possible version of each: the timeless-desert theory, and the intuition that time can itself erode blame. Then offer a negotiated verdict. Should the intuition be debunked — accounted for as forgetfulness, self-interested bias, or a manifestation of the fact that the person who now exists is not fully identical with the earlier wrongdoer — and set aside? Should the theory be amended to include temporal discounting in desert, perhaps only for some classes of wrongdoing or some measure of subsequent conduct? Or is there a reading on which both can be kept, for example by separating the wrongness of the act from the agent's present accountability? Tell me what would justify each move rather than merely choosing a side.
