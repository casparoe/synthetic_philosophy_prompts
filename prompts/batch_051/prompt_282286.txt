I keep coming back to the same argument with a friend about whether the brain is a computer. He means it literally: not "the brain is a bit like a computer" but that cognition just is computation, and that a faithful enough simulation of the relevant neural computations would itself be a mind with the same mental life. I want to push back, but every attempt turns into thought-experiment tennis, with each side spinning the result their own way. So I'm curious what would actually settle it, or at least move the needle.

What would a serious test of this literal computationalist claim look like? Try to give me something concrete: a class of experiment or observation — whater you think is best, whether that's simulation benchmarks, neural decoding, lesion or developmental data, or something else — the result pattern that would most favor computationalism, and the pattern that would most strain it. Then be honest about what even a great result would and wouldn't establish. For instance, if we built a simulation that was behaviorally indistinguishable from a mouse and whose internal states predicted mouse neural activity well, what exactly would that show about the mouse's mental life, and what would remain as residue? I especially want you to track a distinction my friend keeps collapsing: a computational model can be predictively useful, can explain the thing, or can literally be the thing. Where possible, flag which parts of this debate are settled among people who actually work on it and which are genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
