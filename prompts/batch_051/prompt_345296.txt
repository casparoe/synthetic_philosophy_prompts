I am working on a novel whose central character becomes convinced that her twenty-year-old self and her forty-year-old self are not, in any strict sense, the same thing, and I have been reading the metaphysics of persistence so that her position does not come out as a caricature. A philosopher friend has urged on me an analogy she says is standard in the area: a person is like a rope. No single strand of a rope runs its entire length; the rope is one thing only because many shorter strands overlap and grip one another. She then says the self works the same way, a bundle of overlapping temporal parts with no thread-like core that persists unchanged from beginning to end, and that endurantists who insist that the whole person is wholly present at every moment are chasing a ghost. In her hands the analogy is meant to do real work: it is supposed to show that asking whether the adult is the same person as the child is a mistake of the same kind as asking which strand is the rope, and that my character's feeling that there is no persisting "I" is not a pathology but an accurate report of what a person is.

Before I let this carry the philosophical subplot, I want the analogy audited. In continuous prose, please, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, written the way one would write a long email, lay out what maps onto what in the image, where the mapping breaks, and which features of a material rope it quietly imports that a self or a life may not have; consider, for instance, simultaneous spatial co-presence, a single kind of fiber, visible overlap, and the absence of any first-person perspective, memory, or responsibility holding the strands together. Then tell me whether my friend's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, whether the analogy is actually doing the work she assigns to it or quietly suggesting a different account of a person than the perdurantist one, and what a better analogy would be if there is one.
