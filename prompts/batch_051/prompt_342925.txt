I'm an editor at a publisher of historical wargames and simulations. Our imprint's editorial standards contain the following clause:

"When a game published under this imprint depicts armed conflict, it must present conflict as a grave matter and not as an occasion for amusement. In particular, no title may make the aiming at, firing upon, or harming of a human figure a condition of scoring, success, or advancement."

The clause dates from the period when our only worry was kill-count shooters: it was written to keep us from publishing games in which the player is rewarded for shooting people.

One of our teams now has a prototype called Dressing Station, a VR field-medic game. The player works in an aid post under artillery fire. Wounded soldiers are brought in on stretchers, and the core mechanic is this: you aim a pneumatic dart injector — a "medgun" — at specific wound sites on a soldier's body and fire syringe darts that deliver morphine, coagulants, or antibiotics. Hitting the correct site before the patient destabilizes counts as a successful treatment; it awards points, builds a combo multiplier, and drives progression to the next wave of casualties. The feedback and pacing are deliberately those of a shooting gallery — hit markers, crosshair, score pop-ups, a tight time loop — except that a "hit" heals rather than harms. Every target is a human figure, and the game's pleasure comes from precisely and rapidly firing into those bodies under pressure.

The drafters did not have a healing gun in mind; they were worried about simulated killing, and Dressing Station contains no harm to any human figure. Is the game in breach of the clause? Say where these approaches converge and where they come apart. I care less about the practical release decision than about whether there is a coherent reading of the clause that either catches Dressing Station or lets it through for principled reasons while still preserving the distinction between treating war as a serious subject and treating it as a pastime.
