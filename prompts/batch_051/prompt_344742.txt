I am a novelist writing a novel of ideas in which an evolutionary biologist and a machine-learning researcher argue, over a long correspondence, about whether natural selection is best understood as an optimizer. The biologist's position is that selection climbs fitness gradients in much the way stochastic gradient descent climbs a loss surface, only on a noisier and constantly deforming landscape; its shortsightedness makes it a myopic optimizer, not a non-optimizer. The machine-learning researcher answers that this is a decorative analogy: selection has no objective function, no computed gradient, and no destination, and is better described as a filter that merely retains whatever has not yet gone extinct. Because both characters grant that fitness can behave perversely under epistasis and frequency-dependent selection, the argument cannot be settled by elementary examples.

I want their disagreement to reach an empirical point rather than remain a quarrel over metaphors. Please design the best concrete test you can of the conceptual claim that natural selection is genuinely an optimizing process. The test may be computational, observational, or a hybrid, but it should say precisely what observable pattern or quantity would count as evidence of genuine optimization and what pattern would count as evidence of mere filtering. Then explain what a positive result would establish and what it would not establish, and identify what residue of the question no test could settle, even in principle. Please write the response in American English.
