I work as a software engineer, so I keep wanting to find a single rule underneath a set of cases rather than a story per case, and I notice the issue most sharply in debates about reparations for historical injustice. Here is the asymmetry. If I buy a laptop in good faith from someone who turns out to be selling stolen goods—at a fair price, with no reason for suspicion—I will generally still have to return it to the owner once it is traced. My innocence does not give me title; the only thing protecting me is the practical difficulty of tracing the item, not the quality of my claim. But when the chain of transfers is longer—land seized by conquest, wealth built on slave labor, property confiscated a few generations back—the intuition that an innocent current holder owes something to the original victim seems to lapse. My title and inherited advantages are treated as clean because I personally did nothing wrong, even when the original wrong is not in dispute and I would not hesitate to return a recently stolen laptop. Are these cases running one rule with a parameter I am missing (time elapsed, number of intervening owners, individual versus institutional benefit, ease of tracing), or are they running two rules that contradict each other? I want the strongest principle you can find that would keep the ordinary judgments intact, an honest test of that principle against a case where the time gap is short but the chain is otherwise similar, and a clear verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept or given up.
