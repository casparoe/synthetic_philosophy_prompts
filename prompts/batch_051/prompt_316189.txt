I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and a group of us at work are doing a film discussion of Peter Weir's The Truman Show next month. I'd like a reading of the film through the "main character" intuition — the persistent sense that one's own consciousness is the only one that's fully there and that everyone else is, roughly, an NPC.

The film seems to literalize this intuition: Truman's world really is built around him, and everyone else really is playing a part for his benefit. But I want the reading to hold apart three things that tend to collapse into each other when people talk about this feeling. First, solipsism as a metaphysical claim: only my own mental life exists. Second, an epistemic worry: I can't directly inspect other minds, so I can't rule out that other people are automata. Third, a narrative or affective stance: I treat my own life as the story and other people as background characters even while I fully believe they have minds. My suspicion is that the film is mostly about the third of these, while borrowing the imagery of the first two.

Where in the film do these three come apart? Does Truman ever actually occupy something like solipsism or the automaton worry, or is his situation better described as being, quite literally, the narrative center of a world whose other people do have inner lives but are performing? The ending, where Truman confronts Christof and then leaves, could commit the film to one of these pictures or to none of them. I'd like the reading to be honest about the places where the film resists a clean philosophical message, and I'm not looking for citations or secondary literature, just a close reading of the film itself.
