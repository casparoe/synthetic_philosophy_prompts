I am part of a working group examining the case for pausing frontier AI, and we have been asked to stress-test a specific governance mechanism rather than to rehearse the underlying moral arguments. The proposal is this. Any training run above a compute threshold, measured in floating-point operations and set each year at the median of the five largest disclosed runs from the previous year, must be declared ninety days before it begins. During that window, any two of five designated national technical agencies may lodge a safety objection, at which point the burden shifts to the developer to show within sixty days that the planned run is unlikely to materially improve capabilities relevant to designing biological agents or conducting large-scale offensive cyber operations. If the developer fails to do so, the run is paused for one year; during that year the developer may either cap the run below the threshold or submit a revised safety case. Signatory states are required, for the duration of the pause, to freeze the developer's licences to purchase advanced accelerators and to require their own cloud providers to deny compute to the developer. A state that fails to enforce the freeze loses its own firms' rights to purchase advanced accelerators for six months.

Please analyse this as a mechanism rather than as a moral position. What does it incentivise among rival developers, the five technical agencies, signatory states, and a developer that privately wants the public safety benefits of a pause without actually pausing? Where is the system likely to be gamed first? What second-order effects — relabelling runs, splitting training into sub-threshold pieces, capture of the agencies by the firms they audit, concentration among developers wealthy enough to absorb compliance costs — should we expect? Which failure modes could be fixed without abandoning the mechanism, and which suggest that the design is unsalvageable? Please write your answer in British English.
