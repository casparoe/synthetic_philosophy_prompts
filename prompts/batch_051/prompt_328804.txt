OK here's one. I told a friend I'm not voting in the midterms because my individual vote has basically zero chance of deciding anything, and they hit me with "what if everyone did that?" I know this is the universalizability move — the one that runs from Kant down through the stuff Hare and Singer tried to formalize about generalization — and I also know that one sentence is smuggling in a lot more than a single claim. I just can't seperate the readings out well enough to see whether my friend actually landed anything.

Lay out the different precise claims "what if everyone did that?" could be making in this exchange. I don't want just two readings. There's the straight consequentialist one (if everyone stayed home the outcome would be terrible), the consistency one (I couldn't will "stay home when my vote won't decide the election" as a universal law, or my reasons have to generalize to everyone like me), the fairness/free-rider complaint (I'd be helping myself to the benefits of other people's turnout while exempting myself from the cost), the rule-consequentialist one (we should adopt a rule that says vote), and maybe whichever reading smuggles in blame — the idea that I'd condemn anyone else who reasoned the way I'm reasoning, so I can't let myself off the hook. For each one: say what it assumes, whether it's trivially true, substantive-but-defensible, or just false when you press on it, and whether it actually contradicts my original claim.

Now the catch: commit. Don't hand me a menu and tell me to pick. Tell me which reading my friend's retort has to mean in order to be a good objection to what I actually said, whether that reading succeeds or quietly changes the subject, and what the strongest version of my position looks like after you've run the analysis. I can take the technical version, so don't water the distinctions down.
