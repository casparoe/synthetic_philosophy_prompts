Here are the criteria I was taught for an action to count as rational: (1) it maximizes expected utility relative to the agent's beliefs and preferences at the time of action; (2) the agent would not reverse it from a temporally neutral standpoint that weighs all periods of the agent's life equally; (3) it tracks the agent's reasons, so that a change in the relevant facts would change the choice; and (4) it is produced by an undistorted deliberative process, free of blunder, coercion, and self-deception.

Now consider this case. A traveler stranded in a desert will die unless a passing driver picks him up, and the driver will help only on a credible promise of a very large payment upon reaching safety. The traveler knows that, once safe, he would see payment as against his interests and as something he has no sufficient reason to make; he also knows the driver knows this, so a bare promise will not be believed. He therefore voluntarily takes a drug, known to both parties, whose temporary effect is to make him a rigid rule-follower for the next twenty-four hours: he will perform any promise he made before taking the drug, even when doing so is costly and contrary to his interests and reasons at the time. The drug does not alter his beliefs or his ranking of outcomes; it only makes his actions follow the rule. He takes the drug, promises to pay, is rescued, and, under the drug's influence, pays.

Is the act of taking the drug rational by the four criteria? Is the act of paying rational by the same four criteria? Go criterion by criterion, say where the two verdicts diverge or where the criteria underdetermine the answer, and say whether this case shows that the criteria should be indexed to their object—token act, plan, or disposition—because a rational act can deliberately produce a later act the criteria classify as irrational.
