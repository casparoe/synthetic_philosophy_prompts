I have been reading interpretability work alongside more skeptical arguments that the structures it uncovers are not really in the network but are artifacts of the methods used to find them, and I am attracted to a deflationary view. On this view, saying that a network represents something is shorthand for a claim about us: given a high-dimensional activation space and a sufficiently flexible probe, one can find a direction — indeed, many directions — that correlates with almost any concept the interpreter brings to the data. The view has real systematic virtues. It is parsimonious, since it posits nothing beyond weights, activations, and input–output behavior. It makes sense of why different probing methods yield different and often mutually incompatible features, why features are polysemantic and context-dependent, and why post hoc interpretability can feel like reading faces in clouds. It also handles the awkward fact that much of what gets called a representation is a low-dimensional projection chosen by the interpreter.

Still, I have a firm intuition that this view collapses too far in at least some cases. When a small transformer is trained on modular addition, it does not simply learn an arbitrary lookup table: it appears to convert its inputs into sines and cosines and combine them through trigonometric identities, and the periodic structure is visible in the activations before any probe is trained. Early layers of image models likewise develop edge and curve detectors whose presence one would predict in advance rather than read in after the fact, and ablating or editing these structures changes behavior exactly as one would expect if the model were genuinely computing with them. This feels to me like a difference in kind from a linear probe that finds a direction correlated with the concept of schadenfreude in a language model, and I cannot shake the sense that the first sort of structure is discovered while the second is, at least partly, imposed.

I would like a negotiated verdict rather than a winner. Present the strongest version of the deflationary reply — the one that would show my intuition is a bias toward salient low-dimensional structure, or a confusion of predictiveness with semantic content — and the strongest version of the realist amendment that would argue the deflationary view throws away real explanatory structure. Then say which you would give up, and what would have to be true for the intuition to be the thing that goes, for the theory to be the thing that goes, or for a reading of representation to be available on which both survive.
