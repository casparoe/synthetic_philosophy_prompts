Plutarch relates in his Life of Theseus that the Athenians preserved Theseus's ship by replacing decayed timbers one at a time, and the familiar question is whether the ship at the end is still the ship of Theseus. I want to put pressure on a less examined judgment. In ordinary curatorial and legal practice we treat a continuously maintained object, even one that has lost all its original material through piecemeal restoration, as the same object, while we treat a structure assembled later from the discarded original pieces as a reconstruction or replica. Yet when the old pieces are imagined as being reassembled alongside the continuously repaired ship, many people feel that the reassembled ship has at least as strong a claim. Is there a defensible principle that justifies privileging spatiotemporal continuity over material composition in our ordinary practice, or is this simply an inconsistency we tolerate because the two cases never compete in real life? Please provide the strongest principle you can find, test it against nearby cases such as a restored building and an identical new building, and say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
