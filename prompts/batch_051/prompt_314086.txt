Montaigne’s essay “Of Cannibals” is often read as arguing that we call people barbarous chiefly because their customs differ from our own. Suppose I reconstruct his position as follows: a practice may be called barbarous only if it violates a standard of natural reason that a sufficiently informed observer could recognize across cultures, or if it expresses a vice such as cruelty or treachery; mere strangeness, or eating what one personally finds abhorrent, is not enough. Now consider a contemporary community that practices consensual mortuary cannibalism. When an adult member dies, the immediate kin consume a small prepared portion of the body as part of the funeral rite. The deceased gave prior informed consent, the act involves no violence, and the community treats it with the same solemnity we accord to burial. Evaluate whether this practice qualifies as barbarous under the reconstructed Montaignean criteria. Work through each criterion, say where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and then tell me whether the case shows that Montaigne’s distinction between barbarism and mere difference cannot do all the work his essay wants it to do.
