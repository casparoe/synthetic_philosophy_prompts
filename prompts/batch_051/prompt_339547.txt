I’m a novelist, and I’ve been trying to write a scene where two philosophy grad students—one who’s sympathetic to medieval philosophy, one who’s trained in analytic philosophy—argue about Boethius’s account of divine foreknowledge in *The Consolation of Philosophy*. I can’t get the rythym right, so could you write the dialogue for me?

The bit I keep coming back to is in Book V, where Philosophy distinguishes between two kinds of necessity. One is simple necessity, like the sun’s rising: it has to happen. The other is conditional necessity: if someone is walking and you can see them walking, then it is necessary that they are walking while you see them, but that doesn’t mean the walking itself was necessary. She then says God knows future events not as future but in an eternal present, more like how we know something we’re watching right now. I want the Boethian character to argue that this genuinely resolves the tension between God’s infallible foreknowledge and human free will, and the analytic character to argue that it doesn’t actually solve the problem—because if God infallibly knows at some eternal standpoint that I will do X, then there’s still a fact in place about what I will do, and that fact seems to rule out genuine alternatives, whatever mode we assign to the knowing.

Make both characters equally sharp and equally matched; I don’t want one side just collapsing. Have them respond to each other’s actual moves, especially the conditional necessity move—maybe one tries to turn the walking example back on the other. It should sound like how bright people actually argue in a bar, with interruptions, restatements, and one of them saying something like “that’s not what I meant.” I need the back-and-forth, not a lecture.
