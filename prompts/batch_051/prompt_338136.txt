A non-disclosure agreement signed by officials leaving a national-security agency contains the following clause:

"The signatory shall not disclose, transmit, or otherwise make known any classified information to any unauthorized person by any means, including oral, written, or electronic communication."

The agreement defines "unauthorized person" as "any natural person who lacks the requisite security clearance and has no official need to know the information."

A former official keeps several thousand pages of his own dated classified notes. He subscribes to a commercial large language model service through an encrypted application programming interface. The service is fully automated: customer inputs are processed by a model within the vendor's infrastructure, no employee ever reads them, and the vendor's terms provide that inputs are not used for training and are retained for at most thirty days for automated abuse detection, then deleted. The official pastes these notes into the service in batches, asking the model to organize them and to flag anything that would create legal or reputational risk if the pages were read by an outsider. He does not regard this as a "disclosure," because no natural person other than himself ever sees the notes during the period of processing, storage, or deletion.

Is he in breach of the agreement? Address the question under each of these approaches, and then say which should govern and why: (i) the plain meaning of the words, read in light of the definition; (ii) the purpose of the clause within the agreement as a whole; (iii) what the drafters would have said if this case had been put to them; and (iv) what a reasonable signer would have understood themselves to be promising. In particular, consider whether the fact that no human being but the official ever reads the text—only an automated system that receives, holds, scans, and deletes it—should decide the matter. If the approaches diverge, explain what that divergence shows about what the clause was ever able to protect.
