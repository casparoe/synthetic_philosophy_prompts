Okay, here's my prompt: Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers of science about whether mathematical facts can genuinely explain physical or biological phenomena. One defends the idea; the other thinks all genuine scientific explanation is causal-mechanistic and that math is just a representational tool. They should work through the periodical cicada case from Alan Baker's 2005 Mind paper Are there Genuine Mathematical Explanations of Physical Phenomena? — specifically the passage where Baker lays out the idea that prime life-cycle periods minimize intersection with predator life cycles. Don't have them quote the passage; instead have them reconstruct and press on what Baker actually argues there. Then have them turn to the honeycomb case: bees' hexagonal comb is optimal as a partition of the plane. The anti-realist should push actual objections: that the math doesn't cause the cicadas' period, that primeness is just a redescription of physical period facts, that the honeycomb case can be explained by selection pressures or physical constraints without invoking abstract objects, and that indispensability for scientific practice doesn't by itself establish existence. The defender should respond in kind, not strawman: distinguish causal from mathematical or structural explanation, explain what the mathematical content contributes beyond the physical description, and make the case that if scientific explanations are allowed to cite non-causal dependencies, then mathematical facts are among the best candidates. Make it a real back-and-forth where each responds to the other's last point, maybe six to ten exchanges. Then after the dialogue, add a short note saying how confident you are in how you represented the two views, and single out the point in the dialogue you're least sure about.
