Over the years I have followed the lottery/preface literature on rationalist blogs and in formal epistemology, and I get the sense the debate has been stuck for decades. One side takes the lottery paradox to show that high probability alone is not sufficient for rational belief: you can believe each ticket will lose without believing the conjunction that all tickets will lose, because rational belief requires something like knowledge, acceptance, or a context-sensitive threshold. The other side takes the preface paradox to show that rational belief is not closed under conjunction: an author can rationally believe each sentence in her book while also believing the preface statement that at least one sentence in it is false, and this is not an inconsistency because belief is fallible and aggregative. The anti-closure side is accused of making belief too weak to do any work; the anti-threshold side is accused of quietly identifying belief with certainty. My working hypothesis is that this is not one debate but two: a verbal disagreement about whether belief should name a high-credence state or an all-out commitment, and a substantive disagreement about whether graded credences or all-out commitments are the better model of how agents reason and act. But I am not sure, because there may also be a hidden formal question about whether any threshold-plus-closure package collapses into triviality. Diagnose the impasse using the actual positions in the debate, take a definite position on what kind of disagreement this really is, and say what would actually count as progress: a formal result, a conceptual distinction, or abandoning the assumption that the two paradoxes must have a single lesson.
