I'm a science journalist preparing a long piece on what the no-free-lunch theorems do and do not tell us about induction. The line I keep getting from researchers is that these results give a precise modern statement of Hume's problem: if any two learning algorithms have the same expected performance when averaged over all possible prediction problems, then any actual advantage one algorithm enjoys must be purchased by an inductive bias, a prior assumption that the world has some particular regular structure, made without evidence, because any evidence for that structural assumption would itself have to assume the very regularity at issue. I would like the piece to do more than repeat that line. Treat the claim that all inductive success is bought entirely by prior assumptions rather than by anything like a general method as a testable hypothesis, and design the strongest test of it you can think of. Be concrete about the design: what prediction environments would you sample, under what distribution, which algorithms would you compare, and what measured outcome would you count as evidence for or against the hypothesis? Then be honest about what even a clean positive result would leave untouched, and whether the part no test can reach is the part that was philosophically interesting in the first place. Write in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or an email; please avoid headings, bullet points, bold text, and tables.
