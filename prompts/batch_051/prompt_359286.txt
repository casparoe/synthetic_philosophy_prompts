I'm a journalist working on a piece about the free-will debate for a science-and-ideas outlet, and I want to understand — not just explain — the pull of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. The version I've absorbed goes roughly: people do what they do because of the way they are; to be truly responsible for an action you'd need to be truly responsible for the way you are; but to be responsible for the way you are you'd need to be responsible for how you became that way; and that requirement just reapplies, so the chain either regresses forever, goes in a circle, or bottoms out in luck. In print it can sound like a cute puzzle, but I've seen otherwise hard-headed people find it almost undeniable when its staged right.

Act as an expert philosopher who designs classroom exercises, and think carefully before you write. Brainstorm two or three possible mechanics first, then pick one. I want a structured activity for an adult reading group — around 12 people, one 75-minute session — that makes participants feel the force of the Basic Argument by discovering the regress themselves, rather than having the argument named up front. Give me the roles or rules, materials, a timeline, a debrief that maps what happened to the premises of the argument, an honest note about which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce, and what will predictably go wrong the first time it's run. It should be runnable by me, a non-specialist facilitator.
