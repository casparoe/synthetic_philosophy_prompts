I'm the student rep on our department's safety committee and I need to write a short opinion on one case. Our human-subjects protocol for non-invasive brain stimulation says, in section 7.3: "Any sensory experience induced by a study must be one that the participant can, at the time it occurs, fully describe to the investigator in ordinary words. If the investigator determines that an induced experience cannot be so described, the stimulation must be stopped and the participant withdrawn until such a description is provided." That clause was written years ago for phosphenes and tingling, but the lab now has a visual-cortex array that can evoke colour experiences. A participant reports an experience she insists is colour-like but no colour she has ever seen: not red, not green, not a mixture or anything between them on the hue circle, more like a new primary—"a fourth traffic light." She can say that much, and that it is uniform the way a unique hue is, but she cannot describe its colour in terms she had before the session. The investigator thinks she is in exactly the situation the consent form warns about, and wants to keep stimulating to map the thing. Section 7.3 never anticipated a stimulus-evoked novel colour experience. Is the clause breached? Argue it from the words, from the purpose of the rule, and from what the drafters would have said if asked about genuinely novel colours, and then say which reading should win and whether any rule could distinguish a novel-but-describable experience from an indescribable one.
