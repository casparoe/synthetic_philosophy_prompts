For our school's literary magazine, whose next issue is about arguments for and against reform, I want to submit a poem. The editors have asked for pieces in which the poem's form does some of the arguing rather than simply carrying an opinion. In history class we recently discussed Edmund Burke's warning that long-standing institutions often hold knowledge that no single reformer can see from the outside, and my teacher connected this to the image now called Chesterton's fence: a reformer finds a fence in a field, cannot imagine why it is there, and wants to tear it down, but the prudent rule is not to remove a fence until you have discovered why it was built, since its purpose may be invisible from where you stand. The point is not that fences should never come down; it is that the burden of understanding falls on the person who wants to remove them.

Please write a Shakespearean sonnet, in iambic pentameter, that dramatizes this idea. In the octave, let the speaker be a confident young reformer who describes a crooked, moss-covered fence that seems to do nothing but obstruct, and who lists with mounting impatience the reasons for clearing it away. Make the volta, at the start of the third quatrain, the exact moment the speaker discovers what the fence was actually doing; the poem should quietly plant the clue in the octave so that the discovery is surprising but prepared for, whether the fence marks a hidden ditch, drains a field, or keeps apart two neighbors who would otherwise fall into conflict. The closing couplet should turn this particular discovery into the general principle that one should understand a thing before destroying it. Do not mention Chesterton or Burke in the poem, and do not use the phrase Chesterton's fence; someone who knows the idea should recognize the poem as a dramatization of it, and someone who does not should still find it a coherent poem about a fence and a reformer who moved too fast.
