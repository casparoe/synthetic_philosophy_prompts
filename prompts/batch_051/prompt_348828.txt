I work as a software engineer, and I've been turning over an idea I don't have the vocabulary to evaluate properly. I've been calling it 'conditional self-concealment', or, more informally, advice that goes blind exactly when the lights go out.

The definition: an operational lesson, rule, or warning is conditionally self-concealing when the probability that a person will recall and act on it is much lower in the very situations it was written for than in ordinary situations. The trigger condition and the suppression condition coincide.

The central claim: a meaningful share of repeated failures in mature organizations — and perhaps outside them — are not caused by missing knowledge, poor documentation, or negligence. The knowledge exists, often written down and even rehearsed; it just becomes selectively inaccessible when it matters. The person is not ignoring the rule; the situation that should invoke the rule is the same situation that removes the rule from working memory.

Some examples:
- Security guidance says that a request which is both urgent and asks you to bypass a normal procedure is a red flag. But actual phishing and social-engineering attacks work by manufacturing urgency and invoking authority — the exact conditions that narrow attention and make the warning least likely to surface.
- A runbook for a particular class of outage is usually written after the outage, once the team has recovered and can think clearly. The next time the same outage occurs, the team is back in the tunnel-vision state in which the lesson was written but cannot be retrieved; the runbook goes unread even though it is one click away.
- A team decides, after a bad Friday deployment, to adopt a firm rule against Friday deploys. The rule is most likely to be overridden exactly when Friday deploy pressure is high, which is also when the reasoning behind the rule is least persuasive to the people deciding.

My construct says these are a single phenomenon: the information's availability is inversely correlated with its applicability. It feels related to the broader idea of antimemes — ideas that resist being remembered or spread — but I mean something narrower and more mechanical. The idea spreads and is remembered fine in the abstract; it only goes dark under its own trigger conditions.

I'd like an honest assessment. Is this a real distinction or just a metaphor for ordinary forgetting plus hindsight bias? Does it already exist under another name — in cognitive psychology, safety engineering, or anywhere else — and if so, what is the nearest existing concept, and how does mine differ? Where does the construct break? That is, what does it wrongly count as self-concealment, and what would a counterexample look like? If I've stapled together several known phenomena and the seams are visible, I want to hear that too. Please also say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the specific point you are least sure of.
