A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about whether Gödel's incompleteness theorems have any consequences for the mind, and by the end he had me cornered in the following argument. I have written it out as carefully as I can, because I cannot find the exit and I suspect the argument proves too much.

(1) Let S be any consistent formal system whose axioms can be effectively listed (recursively axiomatizable) and which contains enough arithmetic for Gödel's construction. The first incompleteness theorem yields a sentence G_S in the language of S that says of itself that it is not provable in S.

(2) If S is consistent, then G_S is not provable in S; and since that is exactly what G_S says, G_S is true.

(3) So anyone who understands S and accepts that S is consistent can recognize G_S as true, even though no derivation inside S can reach it. Human mathematical insight thereby recognizes an arithmetical truth that the system's own rules cannot produce.

(4) Nothing in the construction depended on which system S we chose. Human beings therefore have a general capacity — given any consistent formal system, form its Gödel sentence and recognize its truth from the system's consistency — that outruns every consistent formal system.

(5) Any digital computer that produces mathematical theorems, and whose output is consistent, can be exactly simulated by some consistent, recursively axiomatizable formal system.

(6) Therefore no computer can match human mathematical insight: whichever system a machine embodies or simulates, a human can recognize a truth — its Gödel sentence — that the system cannot prove.

(7) Now suppose the human mind is itself a machine of the kind described in (5). Then its mathematical output is captured by some consistent, recursively axiomatizable formal system M. By (4), a human can recognize the truth of M's Gödel sentence, although M cannot prove it. But M was to capture all of the mind's mathematical output, so the mind both can and cannot recognize that sentence. Contradiction; hence the human mind is not a machine.

I find that conclusion far stronger than anything the theorems alone say about formal systems, and I am uneasy that two results of mathematical logic, together with a broad claim about what computers are, could settle a large question in the philosophy of mind. That is why I suspect the argument proves too much. But suspicion is not a diagnosis, and every objection I raised last night collapsed.

I would most like to keep (1) and (2); the mathematics seems beyond reasonable doubt, and I am prepared to reject something downstream. But I want to know exactly what that rejection costs. Please walk through the argument, identify the step or steps that fail if it is unsound, explain why the rejected step looked fine, and price the alternatives, including what I commit myself to if I accept the argument after all. Also tell me which nearby conclusion, if any, the argument could actually support.
