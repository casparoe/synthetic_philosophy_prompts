I am a junior in high school and one of the moderators of a philosophy club that has spent this semester on a single recurring dispute: whether humanity should try to keep the values of future civilizations recognizably close to our own, or should instead expect and even welcome deep change in those values. After months of arguing past each other, two members with sharply opposed views have agreed to write an adversarial collaboration, and I have been asked to produce the document they should have signed. One side, which we will call the Preservation camp, holds that unmanaged value drift poses a serious risk of a far future whose values are not merely unfamiliar but genuinely bad, that such outcomes may be impossible to undo once locked in, and that we therefore have strong reason to try to keep future values close to a carefully considered version of present human values. The other side, which we will call the Revision camp, holds that freezing our current values would entrench moral errors and blind spots we cannot yet see, that much historical value change has been moral progress, and that the right response to the risk of bad future values is to build institutions for deliberate, self-correcting revision rather than to preserve today's particular values.

Please write that document. Use this format: first, a numbered list of claims both camps can sign, precise enough to matter but stated in neutral wording; I expect this to include claims about how values have changed historically, about the possibility that future technological systems could lock in values on very large scales, and about the distinction between drifting and deliberately revising. Then present a disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, state the Preservationist view in wording a Preservationist would accept and the Revisionist view in wording a Revisionist would accept; identify the crux or cruxes that would move at least one side; and give, wherever possible, a concrete case both sides agree bears on the crux, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. We have tentatively fixed on two cases around which the argument keeps circling: the abolition of chattel slavery, which both camps regard as a major and welcome change in values, and a future artificial system given a single maximizing goal such as producing paperclips, which both camps regard as catastrophic value drift. I suspect the deepest cruxes are whether large-scale value change tends to track moral improvement or merely what is easiest to preserve and optimize, whether a civilization whose values had drifted could still reliably see that drift as bad from the inside, and whether preservation is feasible enough for the disagreement to be more than hypothetical. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say so explicitly. Please do not include a bibliography or recommend readings; the document should stand on its own.
