I'm teaching a philosophy of biology seminar this fall and the first unit is on definitions of life. I used to do this by handing out the standard definitions one by one and letting students attack them with a favorite counterexample, but that always devolves into everyone arguing from a single weird case. So this year I want to front-load the criteria: here is what any definition of life has to be able to do, before we even ask whether a particular definition does it.

Act as a philosopher of biology whose whole job is sorting hard categories, and start by brainstorming maybe ten or a dozen candidate cases to stress-test a definition of life. Don't just list the standards—virus, fire, crystal, mule—throw in some ones that do different work, like a desiccated tardigrade, a prion, a minimal synthetic cell, a self-maintaining plasma in a magnetic bottle, a computer virus, a bacterial spore. Then, from that list, extract the desiderata: the requirements any adequate definition or theory of life has to satisfy. For each requirement, give (i) a one-sentence statement of it, (ii) the case or phenomenon that forces it, and (iii) whether it's non-negotiable or just a preference of some camp.

Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfed. And finish by telling me which single requirement does the most work eliminating candidiate definitions—I'll use that as the hinge for the first class discussion.

Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their motivating cases; don't turn it into a scorecard of the classic definitions, I'll do that with the students. The purpose is a notion of life good enough for teaching and for adjudicating borderline cases in general biology, so if you think the list would be different for, say, a legal definition of death or an astrobiology life-detection mission, say where and why.
