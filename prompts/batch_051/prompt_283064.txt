Read A Few Good Men (the film; the play is fine if you know it) through the contrast between two folk moralities: a German-style morality of rule-following and order, and an American-style morality of individual conscience. Treat these as ideal types, not claims about actual Germans or Americans. On the German-style side, the right act is the one required by your role, the code, and the chain of command; obedience and institutional order have a dignity of their own, and conscience is mostly the rules internalized. On the American-style side, rules are rules of thumb, institutions earn authority only insofar as they track what conscience already sees, and a person's first loyalty is to their own moral perception, even at the cost of breaking with their unit. The court-martial plot seems almost built to stage this: Dawson and Downey follow a Code Red because the code and the chain of command are the whole source of their duties, while Kaffee slowly learns to defy the system he works inside. I'm teaching a unit on authority and conscience and want to use the film without turning it into a simple morality play.

Give me the response in this form:

The lens (about 100 words): sharpen the two moralities enough to use them, and say what would count as the film favoring one over the other.

The reading (about 250 words): walk through three or four scenes where the film pits the two moralities against each other, and say what each scene adds; no quotation needed.

Where the deck is stacked (about 100 words): identify the film's rhetorical choices that make conscience look good independent of the actual arguments.

Where the film resists the lens (about 100 words): details that don't fit the clean contrast, or where the rule-and-order side gets the better of it.

The verdict (about 100 words): which of the two moralities the film ultimately favors, whether it has earned that, which one you think actually has the better of it, and what you would tell students to keep them from overreading the film.
