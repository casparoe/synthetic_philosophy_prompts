I cover science and ideas for a living, and the disputes I report on—water rights, gene patents, land enclosures, carbon emissions—have left me with stable, gut-level verdicts about when someone has legitimately acquired something by putting work into it, and when they have not. I want to know what principle, if any, captures these verdicts.

1. A forager picks an apple from a wild tree in a commons where many apples remain. I think the apple becomes theirs.
2. A homesteader clears and farms a plot in a region of abundant unused land. I think the plot becomes theirs.
3. A settler fences off the only spring in a dry basin and excludes the people already drawing from it. I think they acquire nothing, whatever work the fence required.
4. A company isolates a human gene and claims exclusive rights to its diagnostic use. I think the sequence does not become theirs, even though real technical labour went into isolating it.
5. A firm emits carbon into the atmosphere and uses it as a free sink without paying anyone. I think the firm is taking something from everyone else and acquires no right to emit, even though it put no labour into the atmosphere.
6. A farmer drains a wetland, turns it into productive cropland, and in doing so destroys the flood control a downstream town relied on. I don't think the gain in productivity gives the farmer an unqualified title, because others have been made worse off.
7. A writer builds a novel from the plot points of several earlier books, adding only arrangement and style. I think the book is theirs, but the underlying stories are not.

My working suspicion is that something like Locke's labour theory of acquisition and the Lockean proviso are jointly doing the work, but I can't tell whether those are two independent principles or two expressions of one deeper principle about not worsening the position of others. Please propose the best principle—or the smallest set of principles—that captures these verdicts, run it against each case, say which of my verdicts it forces me to revise or reinterpret, and apply it to a case I haven't listed. Tell me how confident you are in the principle and identify the point you are least sure of. Begin your reply with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the list.
