Hey, I'm TAing an intro philosophy of technology class and grading a short-answer question. Heres the question the students got:

"Does information about you — say your location history, browsing habits, or health metrics — belong to you in anything like the way your phone or your house does? Give the best argument you can for treating personal data as property, then the best objection. Finally say, in a sentence or two, whether the property framing is ultimately the right one."

And here is one student's full answer:

"I think the strongest argument for treating personal data as property is Lockean. If I own my labor, I own what I produce by mixing that labor with the world, and my data is produced by my actions and choices. Companies that collect and sell my location history without my consent are taking something I made, which is basically a form of theft. Ownership also gives me two important powers: I can exclude others from using the thing, and I can transfer or sell it on terms I choose. So if I owned my data I could tell a data broker to stop profiling me, or charge them for it.

The strongest objection is that data is non-rivalrous. My location history can be copied without being used up, unlike my laptop or my house, so the usual reason we need property in rival goods—to stop fights over scarce resources—doesn't apply. Treating privacy as a commodity you can sell also worries me: poor people would end up selling their data for pennies just like they sell plasma, which is not really a choice. And the Lockean story is stretched, because walking past a cell tower isn't really labor, and the phone company's sensors create the record, not me.

So I'd defend a middle view: personal data probably isn't property in the strict sense, because non-rivalrous things can't be owned the way physical things are. But the property metaphor is still useful because it correctly captures the intuition that people should have some say over how information about them is used, and that companies shouldn't just take it without consent. Overall I'd say data is 'sort of' property: not full ownership, but enough of a property-like control right to do the moral work."

Please give this a grade out of 10, plus the specific point-by-point comments that justify your grade. I want to see where you gave credit and where you took it off, not just a general impression. And keep the whole thing — grade and comments together — to at most 220 words.
