After tangling with various arguments about what the GPL does and does not cover, I've come to think the genuinely hard question underneath all of them is a gears-level one: where does one program end and another begin? I've been trying to formalize an answer, and the best I've produced is a criterion I call semantic coupling. Two bodies of code form a single derivative work just in case a competent reader trying to understand either one will eventually have to understand the other, because data structures, invariants, or control flow cross the boundary in a way that makes neither side fully intelligible in isolation. On this test, linking a library into an application counts as creating a derivative work when the application's correctness depends on the library's internal representations or the library's behavior depends on assumptions about its caller, but not when the boundary is a clean, documented contract that could be swapped for an independent implementation.

I know copyright law does not actually use this test, and I know a lot of free-software discourse runs on a cruder operational heuristic — same process or shared address space means one program. I am not asking for legal advice or for case law. I want the concept itself stress-tested. Is semantic coupling coherent? Does it roughly track the disputes people actually have, and where does it silently break — plugin architectures where host and plugin are useless apart but independently authored, networked services that encode deep assumptions about each other's internals, generated code, config files that change program semantics? Have I reinvented an existing idea in the philosophy of software or in free-software writing under another name?
