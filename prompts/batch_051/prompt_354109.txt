I spent my career as a research chemist before retiring into philosophy, and in moral psychology I keep running into the same puzzle: a stable evaluative disposition—an aversion to a food, an aversion to an accent, an aversion to a social group, or the conviction that some act is simply wrong—is called a preference by its owner and a bias by an observer, yet the underlying structure seems continuous rather than categorical. I am preparing a handout for an undergraduate philosophy of mind seminar and would like a decision tree that sorts a concrete evaluative disposition into exactly five leaves: mere taste, preference, bias, prejudice, and moral conviction. No branch question may use those leaf words or ask the respondent to self-label; each branch should pose a concrete case or ask about a diagnostic feature—whether the attitude is presented as binding on others, whether it revises in response to evidence the person accepts, whether its object is a social group, and whether the person would avow the attitude once it is made explicit. Flag any two leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate and say why. Present the result as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: four branch questions with their answer routes and leaves, and a fifth item on the inseparable pair.
