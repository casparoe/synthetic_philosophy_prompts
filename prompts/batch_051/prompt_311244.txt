I run a small reading group with two friends who have spent six months arguing about what gives an oath its binding force, and we've narrowed the fight to one test case: the old Greek bit where the gods swear by the river Styx. The version of the myth we've been working with goes roughly like this: a god who swears by the Styx and breaks the oath gets hit with an automatic penalty—laid low for a year and shut out of the other gods' company for a long while after—without anyone having to decide to punish them. That detail is doing a lot of work between the two camps. One of them (the self-binding view) says this just makes explicit what every oath really is: a conditional self-curse, where you stake something you care about as collateral, so the binding force comes from the penalty and the gods are basically the mechanism that collects on it. The other (the relational view) says an oath binds because it calls in a witness you stand in a relationship to; swearing by the Styx matters because breaking the oath would be a betrayal of the thing invoked and of the order of gods that takes the invocation seriously. On that view the automatic penalty is a symptom of the wrong, not the source of the obligation.

We've agreed to stop arguing and instead write the document a proper adversarial collaboration would produce, and I'd like you to draft it for us. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section—things both sides can sign about oaths in general and about the Styx case in particular—followed by a disagreements table where each row is worded so the person whose view it describes would accept the wording of their own side. Then lay out the cruxes: the sub-questions that, if settled one way, would actually move at least one of them. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts about that case. I suspect one crux is a completely private conditional self-curse with no witness at all, and another is whether the oath still binds once the witness has lost all power to enforce it—but if you think the real cruxes are elsewhere, go with those instead.
