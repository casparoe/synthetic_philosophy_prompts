I'm a philosophy PhD student and I'm on my department's undergraduate curriculum committee. We're revising the intro ethics syllabus, and the big fight is about thought experiments. Right now the course leans on them hard—trolley problem, the violinist, the experience machine, that kind of thing—and a group of students have complained that these cases feel less like "what do you actually think?" and more like "watch how the setup steers you toward one answer." One of them wrote that after the violinist lecture she couldnt tell whether she had discovered a moral commitment or been maneuvered into one. Our chair is floating a proposal to cut the thought experiments entirely and teach the same positions (utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics) through plain argument and real-life cases. I think its too drastic, but I can't shake the feeling the students are onto something about how these cases persuade.

Could you draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Wednesday's meeting? It should recommend a concrete course of action with a semester-by-semester timeline—keep the cases, cut them, or modify how we use them—and it should seriously engage the strongest version of the "just cut them" position, not a straw man. In particular I'd like it to work through the idea that a thought experiment can be doing different jobs at once: illustrating a theory, testing a principle, or working as an intuition pump. It can mislead exactly when nobody notices which job it's doing, and I want the memo to say whether teaching students to notice that machinery is a realistic fix or whether the cases should go.
