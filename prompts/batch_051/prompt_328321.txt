I have no academic background in philosophy; I mostly read and moderate discussion forums, so please forgive me if I misuse a term. There is a pattern I keep noticing in arguments about family life, child-rearing, and the roles of men and women, and I would like to know whether it has an established name.

The pattern turns on the word 'natural' and its opposite. In one long discussion about whether fathers can be primary caregivers, a commenter said it was 'unnatural' for an infant to be cared for mainly by its father. When someone objected that fathers do much of the infant care in some societies, the commenter backed up and said: 'I'm not making a value judgment; I only mean it runs against how the species is built.' That sounded like a plain factual claim, and I took it that way. But two posts later that same commenter concluded that a family choosing the arrangement would be 'making a mistake and ignoring reality.' The word had been defended as mere description and then used again as a verdict.

The same rhythm appears in arguments about diets, schooling, and the work women are encouraged to enter. In a discussion about steering young women away from physically demanding trades, a participant called such work 'unnatural for women.' Pressed on it, she said she meant only that women have rarely done that work in most recorded societies — again, a point about frequency. In her next post, however, she wrote that because the work was unnatural, women who wanted it were out of step with reality. Under attack, 'unnatural' meant unusual; in the conclusion, it meant something like defective or unfit.

I have heard people use labels near this point: 'appeal to nature,' 'naturalistic fallacy,' and the old point that a fact about how things are does not by itself settle how things ought to be. None of those seems to name the thing I am watching. An appeal to nature, as I understand it, is the overt claim that what is natural is good, but the people I am describing deny that claim when challenged. The other points are about whether an inference is valid, not about the two-step rhythm of retreating to a safe factual sense and then quietly sliding back to a judging sense for the conclusion. I have also seen the term 'motte-and-bailey' used for a related strong–weak retreat, but there the two positions are usually stated separately; here one word carries both meanings and the switch is never announced.

So my question is this: does this specific pattern have a name of its own? If it does not, please name it and define it precisely enough to separate it from an outright appeal to nature, from ordinary equivocation, from the point about not deriving an ought from an is, and from motte-and-bailey if that distinction is real. The exact boundaries matter more to me than the label. Please answer in American English.
