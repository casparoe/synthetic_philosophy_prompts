I have been working through classical Indian debates about the sources of knowledge (pramāṇas), and I find myself persuaded by a broadly Mīmāṃsā package. On my current view, cognition is innocent until proven guilty: a cognition, including the understanding produced by a linguistic utterance, possesses intrinsic validity (svataḥ prāmāṇya) until some specific defeater appears, so we are not first required to establish the reliability of a source before trusting what it says. I also accept the stronger and more distinctive Mīmāṃsā thesis this principle is meant to protect: the Veda is authorless (apauruṣeya) and beginningless. It was not composed by God, by the seers (ṛṣis), or by any human being; its injunctions are transmitted in a line with no remembered starting point. On my version of the view, this is bound up with a further claim about language itself: the relation between a word and its meaning is natural and beginningless rather than a human convention, so the Veda's injunctions do not acquire their binding force from any speaker. The motivation is that dharma — roughly, what is to be done, as opposed to what already exists — is not the sort of thing perception or inference from observed facts could reveal. A human author of the Veda would therefore have been either reporting a super-sensible matter he could not have known or inventing commands with no ground. The authorless Veda is thus supposed to be the only possible source of knowledge about right action.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher who knows the Mīmāṃsā system well but is not committed to it, and give me an honest map of where this package of views is exposed. I want to know what would count against it: which observations, arguments, formal considerations, or historical facts would be decisive, and which would merely be awkward. For each candidate defeater, say exactly which component of the package it targets — the default validity of ordinary cognition, the extension of that default to testimony, the special authority claimed for Vedic injunction, the authorlessness thesis, the naturalness of the word–meaning relation, or the argument that only an authorless text can ground knowledge of dharma. In particular, distinguish the objections that a well-informed Mīmāṃsāka would themselves concede as a real cost or standing problem from the objections that only outsiders think are damaging. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and assess honestly whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and, if it has, whether that should worry an adherent of a view of this kind.

To make the question less abstract, the defeater I personally find most persuasive is this: many scriptural traditions claim something like a divine or beginningless source for their own text, so a theory of testimony that entitles each tradition to treat its own scripture as intrinsically credible looks as if it licenses mutually incompatible certainties. I want to know whether that is fatal, merely awkward, or restatable as a point a Mīmāṃsāka can already accept. Before you answer, take some time to brainstorm the candidate defeaters and sort them by strength and by the premise they attack; then present the strongest version of each rather than a caricature, and be explicit about what the replies you propose cost the original position.
