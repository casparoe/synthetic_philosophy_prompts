I work on systems where the reasoning trace matters as much as the final result, and I have ended up with a set of case judgments about process-based versus outcome-based supervision that I cannot yet unify. I have been reading the paper by Jonathan Uesato, Nate Kushman, and colleagues, "Solving Math Word Problems with Process- and Outcome-Based Feedback" (2022). Early in the paper they write:

"Process-based approaches emphasize human understanding — in order to demonstrate or select good reasoning steps, human annotators need to understand the task. Human-comprehensibility is of direct interest in many domains. For example, in educational settings, an answer without an (understandable) explanation may often confuse more than it explains. In the longer term, human-comprehensibility may also help with detecting when ML systems may be using deceptive or unethical actions to achieve superficially appealing outcomes, such as by subtly manipulating people or systems to increase various metrics."

That rationale feels right in the abstract, but my actual verdicts pull against it. (a) A model trained on math word problems with outcome-only reward that regularly reaches the right final answer through invalid intermediate steps: I do not accept this, even when its final-answer accuracy is high. (b) A code model supervised only by an exhaustive test suite: I think this is fine, and I do not care whether its internal or natural-language reasoning is faithful, because the tests are the ground truth. (c) An open-ended essay-tutoring model supervised on the quality of its step-by-step feedback by human raters: I think this is not fine — or at least no better than supervising the final essays — because the human step labels are matters of taste dressed up as correctness. (d) An outcome-supervised model whose traces reveal a systematically incorrect shortcut that still gets many final answers right: I think this is actively harmful, not merely unattractive, because the training signal is reinforcing a strategy that will fail on nearby variants. (e) A reward model trained on outcome labels only, but then used to score individual steps, which this paper reports ends up emulating process-based feedback: I accept this for checkable math tasks, yet I cannot say why I should trust it if the whole point of process supervision was that a human understood the steps.

Is there a principle — or a small set of candidates — that captures all five of these verdicts? Please test the quoted rationale against them, say which of my verdicts it would force me to revise if any, and state what your preferred principle predicts about the case I keep going back and forth on: process feedback supplied by a learned judge whose errors I cannot inspect, versus outcome feedback from a formal proof checker that I do understand. As you do this, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
