Since my last great-aunt died, the question whether a language can be pulled back across the line from death has stopped being academic for me. She was the last person in our family to have spoken our heritage language from childhood, and I have since joined a circle of roughly forty descendants and local allies, none of us native speakers, who are trying to revive it. Before our next planning meeting I need help settling a conceptual question we have been avoiding.

Here are the criteria I have been using for calling a speech form a living language. First, it has a community of speakers for whom it is a normal, unrehearsed medium of communication in at least some everyday domains, not only in ceremonies or lessons. Second, it is being transmitted to children as one of their first languages, so that new speakers acquire it at home rather than from coursework. Third, it continues to change through unplanned, everyday use, with new words and constructions arising and spreading by usage. Fourth, its speakers regard themselves as continuing one and the same language rather than staging a reconstruction of one.

Our case looks as follows. The adults study from recordings and a descriptive grammar made in the 1950s, but they now speak the language at home and in weekly meetings as their ordinary medium of communication. Several households have begun raising children with it alongside the majority language, though the children increasingly answer in the majority language. New terms for things like texting and electric kettles have appeared and spread informally, but more substantial grammatical innovations are still corrected against the 1950s grammar as errors. Opinion in the circle is split about whether the children are acquiring the language or merely acquiring a classroom construction dressed in old words.

Go through the criteria one by one and say honestly where our case qualifies, where it fails, and where it falls in between. Then tell me whether the remaining shortfalls are failures in our facts or failures in the criteria, whether our community should count as an instance of linguistic diversity rather than a museum piece, and whether living is even the property we should be trying to restore.
