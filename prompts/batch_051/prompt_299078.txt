Im trying to sort out what I actually think about semantic externalism, so I wrote down my gut calls on a bunch of cases. They all feel right individually, but I cant find one principle behind them. I want you to find the principle, or tell me which of my verdicts I have to give up. Then apply it to a case I havent thought through.

Case A (1750 water). Oscar on Earth and Twin Oscar on Twin Earth are molecule-for-molecule duplicates. The stuff in their lakes looks, tastes, and behaves identically, but on Earth it's H2O and on Twin Earth it's XYZ. Nobody in 1750 knows any chemistry. My call: Oscar's word 'water' already means H2O and Twin Oscar's means XYZ, so the two words mean different things even though the speakers are internally identical.

Case B (arthritis). A man tells his doctor he has arthritis in his thigh. He's linguistically normal; he just has a false belief about the disease. My call: his word 'arthritis' means the same as his doctor's—inflammation of the joints. He hasn't coined a new concept that happens to include thigh pain.

Case C (elm and beech). Someone who cannot tell elms from beeches has sincerely called beeches 'elms' his whole life. My call: his word 'elm' still means elm, not beech, and not elm-or-beech. His sentences about those trees are false, but his meaning hasn't shifted.

Case D (a place name). A name for a stretch of mainland coast gets copied onto maps with errors until it attaches to an offshore island; everyone now uses it for the island. My call: the reference actually shifted to the island, not that we're all still really referring to the old mainland region.

Case E (jade). A community has one word for the green stone they carve. Later mineralogy shows the carvings are two different minerals, jadeite and nephrite, indistinguishable without modern tests. My call: before that discovery, their one word wasn't secretly two meanings and didn't mean jadeite-or-nephrite as a disjunction. It meant something like the green carvable stone—the surface-level kind.

The knot for me is A versus E. In A the hidden chemistry matters and deference to experts isnt doing the work, because no experts exist. In B the community's expertise is exactly what fixes the meaning. In E the hidden mineralogy doesn't matter, and the meaning stays at the surface. I keep wanting to say the difference is whether the term is used as a natural-kind term, but that sounds circular—how would I tell, except by looking at the verdicts I'm trying to explain?

So give me a principle (or a small set of candidate principles) that handles A–E, state it precisely, say which of my verdicts it forces me to revise if any, and tell me what it predicts for this extra case: a community has one word, 'toba', for a leaf they smoke, but the leaf is actually two unrelated plant species always grown and traded together and indistinguishable to them. Are they referring to one superficial kind, two hidden kinds, or something else? Also tell me where my set of verdicts is least secure.
