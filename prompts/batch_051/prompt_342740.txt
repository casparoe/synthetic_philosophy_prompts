You are a political philosopher specializing in democratic theory and institutional design. Think carefully before answering: begin by brainstorming several candidate distinctions, then test each one against nearby cases before committing to a verdict.

Consider the following asymmetry in ordinary democratic practice. Many citizens enthusiastically support term limits for legislators: they regard the career politician as a problem and a cap on tenure as a natural remedy. Many of the very same citizens would regard sortition—selecting a legislature, or a substantial part of it, by lottery from the eligible population, roughly as juries are selected—as bizarre, irresponsible, or plainly undemocratic. And this is so even though sortition appears to attack the career-politician problem more directly than term limits do. Term limits stop particular individuals from continuing in office, but they leave in place the electoral system that produces professional politicians, incumbency advantages, and the campaign-finance machinery attached to them. Sortition would prevent a self-perpetuating political class from forming in the first place, and it has independent democratic resonances, from Athenian practice to contemporary citizens' assemblies. Yet the first proposal is mainstream and constitutionally common, while the second is treated as fringe.

The puzzle is not whether term limits are good on the merits. The puzzle is whether there is any principle that generates this pair of attitudes: supporting the blunt instrument that preserves elections, while rejecting the sharper instrument that replaces them. I want you to find the strongest candidate justification for that combination. Plausible candidates might appeal to accountability (elected officials must face voters in a way lotteried officials do not), competence screening (elections, however imperfect, filter for some aptitude that lotteries do not test), consent or authorization (people have a right to be governed only by those they chose), or the incentive effects of seeking re-election. For each candidate, check whether it really distinguishes term limits from sortition, and whether it would also condemn things we broadly accept, such as jury selection or short-term citizens' assemblies, or things we broadly reject. Then give an honest verdict: is the asymmetry justified, should it be reversed (accept sortition and drop term limits), or should it be given up because the two proposals should stand or fall together?
