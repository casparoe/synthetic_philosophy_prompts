I am a retired physical chemist, and in the last few years I have been reading philosophy with more pleasure than I ever expected. I recently returned to Eugene Wigner’s 1960 essay The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences. I shall paraphrase the passage that has been on my mind, so as not to misquote from memory. Wigner remarks that mathematical ideas developed with no thought of empirical application—he mentions the theory of complex numbers—later turned out to be indispensable for the formulation of quantum mechanics, and he treats this recurrent appropriateness of pure mathematics to the structure of the physical world as a kind of miracle, something we neither fully understand nor have earned.

That passage now bears on a concrete decision I have to make. A former PhD student of mine runs a small company that screens small molecules for biological activity. His team has built a predictive model using persistent homology, a technique from algebraic topology that was developed decades after I left the bench. The model performs very well on retrospective data: it outperforms several older physics-based scoring functions. But none of the team can explain why a molecule’s topological features should track binding affinity in mechanistic terms. The mathematics is deep and beautiful; the biology is not. The student argues that when mathematics this deep shows up and works, that is itself a reason to think something real is being captured. Another member of our advisory group calls it numerology with better notation.

I am not asking for a business recommendation. I want a philosophical assessment of the principle involved. Does Wigner’s observation about fundamental physics license any confidence in a mathematically sophisticated but mechanistically opaque model in a messy, historically contingent domain such as biology? Or does the analogy fail at a precise point? And if it does fail, what is the principle that lets us distinguish cases where the miracle of mathematical applicability is evidential from cases where it is merely aesthetic?
