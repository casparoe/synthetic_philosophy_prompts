The following argument reconstructs, in contemporary terms, the reasoning behind the thesis of one of Kant's antinomies of pure reason: the claim that the world must have a beginning in time.

(1) Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the world had no beginning in time. Then the series of past states is infinite: each past state was preceded by an earlier state, and there was no first member.
(2) A completed series must be completable: it must be possible to produce the series by successive addition, starting from a first member and proceeding through every member to the end.
(3) An infinite series cannot be produced in that way. Because it has no first member, there is nowhere for the successive addition to begin; and however many members one passes through, one is never in a position to have exhausted the series.
(4) Therefore, if the world had no beginning, the series of past states could not have reached its end. [from (2) and (3)]
(5) But on the supposition that the world had no beginning, the series of past states has reached its end, namely the present state.
(6) That is a contradiction, so the supposition is false: the world has a beginning in time.

Does this argument succeed? Assess each inference for validity and each premise for truth, and identify the first step at which the reasoning could be resisted. In particular, distinguish two senses of being 'completed': having a last member, and having been produced by a successive addition that begins at a first member. Which sense does step (2) need, and which sense does step (5) establish? Does the argument survive once this ambiguity is made explicit, or can a repaired version still prove that the world has a beginning in time? If you think some other step is the weak point, defend that instead.
