I keep coming back to a parallel I can't decide about. Dennett's ladder of creatures — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian — is a story about where selection happens: first across generations, then across a creature's own behaviors during its lifetime, then inside the creature as it tries out candidates internally and discards the bad ones before they hit the world, then across a shared environment of words, tools, and culture. And modern AI seems to recapitulate the ladder almost level by level. Neuroevolution and evolution strategies look Darwinian: variation and selection over a population of parameter sets, with no learning inside a single lifetime. Model-free RL looks Skinnerian: the agent acts, gets reward, adjusts, with no internal model of the world. Model-based RL and planning with a learned world model look Popperian: the agent simulates candidate actions and lets bad ones be discarded in the simulation rather than by the real environment. And LLM agents with scratchpads, retrieval, calculators, and tools look Gregorian: the intelligent behavior is partly outside the network, in external artifacts and language.

But I can't tell whether this is a real identity of structure or just me pattern-matching. Is the joint Dennett is carving actually "where the selection loop lives and what gets selected," and does the AI version preserve that joint — or does it scramble the thing that made the ladder a ladder, namely that each level adds a new environment in which options can be tested? What would have to be true of an ML system for it to count as genuinely Popperian rather than just Skinnerian with a world model bolted on? And does the parallel predict something false — for example about what later levels can do that earlier ones can't, or about what you have to add to move a system up a rung? If the whole comparison rests on me misreading Dennett or stretching "selection" until it covers everything, say that plainly instead of politely playing along.
