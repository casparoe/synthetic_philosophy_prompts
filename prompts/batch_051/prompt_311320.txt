I am a professor of moral philosophy serving part-time as an advisor to a government economics unit that reviews and signs off on welfare cost–benefit analyses. Several times a year the unit must rule on whether policies that affect how many people exist—malaria prevention that reduces child mortality, pronatalist tax credits, subsidized fertility treatment—should have those demographic effects counted as benefits, and its rulings bind the departments that use the analyses. The analysts have no philosophical training and each decision is made under deadline pressure. I want a reusable procedure, expressed as a checklist or decision tree in plain language and drawing on philosophical work on population size and well-being, for deciding when a population-size effect may enter the benefit column and how it should be weighted. The procedure should be completable in an afternoon and should mark explicitly which steps are value judgments rather than technical findings. It must handle at least three cases: (1) a policy that adds many people whose lives are just worth living, raising total welfare while lowering average welfare; (2) a policy that raises average welfare but prevents some births; and (3) a pronatalist transfer that mostly shifts births earlier rather than increasing their lifetime number. Apply the procedure to cases (1) and (3), indicate where it would give an answer the committee is likely to resist, and state what the procedure cannot decide.
