"Karma is often described as a theory of moral desert. Explain what that description means, then give the strongest reason to think karma provides a satisfying account of why people suffer, and the strongest reason to think it doesn't."

Here is the student's answer, copied exactly:

"Karma is the eastern idea that your actions come back to you, so good deeds cause good outcomes and bad deeds cause bad outcomes, either later in this life or in a future life. Calling it a theory of moral desert means that what happens to you is what you deserve, since the outcome is proportional to what you did. The biggest strength is that it solves the problem of apparently undeserved suffering: someone who looks like they suffer for no reason actually deserves it because of a wrong they committed in a past life, so there is no genuine injustice. It also explains the unequal circumstances people are born into, which isn't cosmic unfairness but the bill coming due. And karma doesn't require a god or judge, it works more like a natural law of cause and effect. A limitation is that it can encourage victim blaming, since if someone is poor or sick you might conclude they earned it. But I think karma is basically retributivism stretched across lifetimes, with the universe acting as the punisher, so the same objections apply."
