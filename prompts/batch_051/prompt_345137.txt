After a career in physical chemistry, I have lately been reading in the philosophy of mind, and the symbol grounding problem has struck me as one of those issues that is easy to state but very hard to feel from the inside. I help run a continuing-education seminar for retired scientists and engineers — about sixteen people, two hours — and the group has a running joke that our old instruments were "reading" spectra without having any idea what a molecule is. I would like to turn that joke into a session that makes the force of the problem palpable before anyone says the phrase. These are people who are comfortable with codes, lookup tables, and signal processing, so I expect several of them to be drawn to the view that a system understands a language if it manipulates the symbols correctly. I want an activity that challenges that sympathy from the inside, not by telling them they are wrong but by letting them occupy the system and report what they find.

Design a runnable activity for this group under the following constraints. At least one team should spend the entire exercise operating on symbols according to explicit formal rules, with no access to anything the symbols are about, while a second team observes only the written inputs and outputs and must judge, under time pressure, whether the first team understands what it is doing. The exercise should be cooperative rather than deceptive — the rule-following team should not be trying to fool anyone — and it should not simply re-run the Chinese Room as a skit, since several participants will know that thought experiment; the gap between rule-following and meaning should arise from the task itself. By the end of the two hours, participants should feel the pull of two thoughts at once: that the rule-following team did everything a competent language-user would visibly do, and that, from the inside, no meaning was anywhere in the room. Please provide the rules and roles, a two-hour timeline, all self-contained materials (an artificial "language," a rulebook, and a set of query-answer pairs the observing team can evaluate), a scoring scheme that does not require money if scoring helps, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the exercise onto the distinction between syntactic competence and grounded meaning. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, how to handle that, and be frank about which feature of the philosophical problem the exercise cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must supply in the last ten minutes.
