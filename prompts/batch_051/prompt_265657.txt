In my psych class we did a unit on psychopathy and I ended up going down a rabbit hole about how the legal system treats it. From what I can tell, courts almost never let psychopathy count for an insanity defense, because the legal test for insanity in alot of places is whether the person could tell right from wrong, and psychopaths generally can say that what they did was wrong, they just dont feel it or care. But then courts and juries will turn around and treat those exact same traits as reasons to make the punishment worse: lack of remorse, shallowness, manipulativeness, high risk of reoffending. Like, the same person can be described by one side as having a serious disorder that makes him unable to feel guilt, and by the other side as a cold dangerous predator who should be locked up as long as possible. So the same features that get treated as irrelevant to whether someone is responsible get treated as proof that he's extra responsible. That seems kind of inconsistent to me, but maybe I'm missing a distinction.

What I want to know is what theory of moral knowledge or moral responsibility this practice actually presupposes. Does holding psychopaths fully blameworthy presuppose that they do know that what they're doing is wrong in the full sense, even if they don't have normal empathy or guilt? Or is the practice compatible with saying they don't really have moral knowledge but we punish them anyway for other reasons, like public safety? I'd also want to know if the practice only makes sense under one particular view, or if it's loose enough that a bunch of different theories would all license it. And if someone convinced me that moral knowledge works like fluency in a language — you can ace the written test and still not "know" the language in the way that matters — would the practice have to change?
