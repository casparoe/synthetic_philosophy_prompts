I have encountered a line of reasoning, in debates about occupational segregation, that purports to move from a surprising cross-national pattern to strong conclusions about nature, culture, and justice, and I cannot decide whether it is sound or where exactly it slips. The pattern is that countries scoring highest on standard composite measures of gender equality — political representation, legal rights, educational access, labor-force participation — tend to have larger, rather than smaller, average differences between men's and women's occupational fields, with women relatively concentrated in people-oriented occupations and men in thing-oriented occupations, while many lower-scoring countries show the reverse pattern. The argument runs:

(1) If the gender gap in career choice were produced entirely by social conditioning and structural barriers, then the gap ought to be smallest in the countries where those barriers are weakest.
(2) The countries with the highest gender-equality scores are precisely the countries where the barriers to women's choices are weakest.
(3) Yet in precisely those countries the gap is not smaller; it is, if anything, larger.
(4) Therefore, the gap is not produced entirely by social conditioning and structural barriers.
(5) Therefore, a substantial part of the gap is innate or biological.
(6) If a gap is substantially innate, then an aggregate imbalance between the sexes in an occupation is not by itself evidence of ongoing discrimination or injustice.
(7) Hence, at least in high-equality countries, the remaining imbalances across occupations are not unjust, and policies aimed at achieving gender parity within each field lack justification.

I want to know whether the argument is valid as it stands and, if not, the exact step at which each inference fails. On the most charitable reading, are the premises true? If the empirical pattern in the opening is stipulated for the sake of argument, how far do (4) through (7) really go, and what would a corrected version of the weakest step have to say in order to salvage any part of the conclusion? If someone accepted the opening pattern but wanted to reject the conclusion, which of (1) through (3) is the most vulnerable, and under what reading does it give way?
