For some time I have been troubled by a claim in the methodology of science that functions as a conversation-stopper in economics and has more recently been pressed into service in rationalist-adjacent discussion of machine learning models. The claim is Friedman's so-called F-twist: that a theory's lack of realism in its assumptions is no mark against it, and that theories should be judged solely by predictive success. I want to treat this not as a slogan but as a hypothesis, and I want to know how one could actually bring evidence to bear on it.

For the purpose of this exercise, read the F-twist as an empirical regularity claim: across a population of models of the same target system, differences in how realistic the models' assumptions are do not systematically predict differences in out-of-sample predictive accuracy once model complexity has been properly controlled. I am not interested in exegesis of what Friedman literally wrote, nor in a summary of the secondary literature; I want the test, not the historiography.

Design the strongest feasible test of that claim. Specify the class of target systems, the family of models, and how you would grade assumptions as more or less realistic without begging the question. Say how you would measure predictive performance—out-of-sample and, ideally, under controlled shifts of regime—and how you would keep model complexity from acting as a confound. A design that could be run on simulated systems where the true data-generating process is known would be ideal, with a sketch of how the result could be checked against recorded cases from the natural or social sciences.

Then say what each possible result would show. If the less realistic models do no worse once complexity is penalised, exactly what would have been established about the F-twist, and what would remain open? If more realistic assumptions systematically improve prediction or transfer across shifts, would that refute the F-twist or merely show that it holds only as a finite-sample heuristic? Finally, identify the residue no test of this kind can reach: for instance, whether predictive accuracy is the right criterion at all, what matters when a model is used to explain rather than forecast, and whether we should care about the falsity of assumptions we cannot detect from the data we have.
