I have a policy I use in philosophy arguments and I want to know if its defensible. The policy is this: as soon as I notice the two sides are using a word in slightly different ways, or as soon as neither side can tell me what observation would make them give up their claim, I downgrade the whole thing to a "verbal dispute" and stop spending credence on either side. I don't mean I get up and leave the argument — I mean I stop having a view, because I figure theres no fact of the matter left to have a view about.

Here's where I've actually used it. A friend thinks there are composite objects — tables exist — and another friend says only simples exist, and a table is just a useful way of talking about simples arranged tablewise. I couldn't get either of them to name a single observation that seperates the two worldviews, so I called it verbal. Same thing with a debate about whether a statue is identical to the lump of clay its made of: both sides agree about every event in the history of the clay and the statue, and they just disagree about how to count the things. I called that verbal too. And once, arguing about whether the past really exists or is just implied by present records, neither side could say how a world with a real past would look different from a world with only present records, so I stopped caring.

But I'm also a little nervous about this habit. For one thing, I've never applied it to normative questions — I believe poisoning the reservoir is wrong even though I can't name the observation that would disconfirm that — and by my own rule shouldn't I call ethics verbal too? That seems like either a cost or the right answer and I'm just squeamish. For another thing, maybe some substantive disputes are just not decidable by observation, and my rule makes me blind to them. And sometimes I suspect two people can mean genuinely different things by a word and the disagreement still isn't "merely verbal" — the choice between the two meanings has real consequences for what they go on to infer.

So my question: is this a defensible epistemic policy? If not, what's the best defensible version of it — what exactly is the test for a dispute being merely verbal, and how should a person with limited time and no special training in metaontology apply that test? Keep the answer in American English. Tell me straight which of my uses of the rule you'd overturn and why, and what the rule should have said about each case.
