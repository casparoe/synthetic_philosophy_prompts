I've been chewing on Hofstadter's knob-twiddling idea lately — that creativity is less about inventing from nothing and more about taking a pattern you already have and turning conceptual dials on it, with variations on a theme as the core case. It made me dig up the old rulings of a composition teacher I once had, who was very firm about what counted as a real variation. From memory: transposing a theme to a new key, not a variation; slowing it way down or putting it on a different instrument, also not variations, too trivial. Ornamenting the melody with passing notes, yes. Inverting the melody, yes. Playing it backwards (retrograde), yes. Keeping the chord progression but writing a completely new melody on top, yes. Taking just the opening four-note motif and growing a whole new piece from it, yes. Deleting every second note of the theme, no.

The case I'm stuck on is a new piece I sketched: it keeps the theme's exact rhythm and exact up/down contour, but every interval has been replaced with a randomly chosen different interval, so it was produced by turning a couple of knobs quite hard but honestly doesn't sound like the theme. Would she call it a variation? I care less about the yes/no than about the rule she's been running — which two or three plausible rules fit her track record about equally well, what each predicts for my piece, which one you'd bet on and why, and what single further case would cleanly seperate the leading rules.
