I am a novelist, at the planning stage of a book in which an isolated people speak a language that has no personal pronouns and no indexical expressions of any kind: no words meaning 'I', 'you', 'here', 'now', 'this', 'today', or 'yesterday', and no demonstratives for pointing. Its speakers refer to every particular, themselves included, by name, title, relationship, or definite description. I want the conceit to survive hostile scrutiny, so I have been collecting the strongest reasons for thinking that no usable human language could really be like this. So far I have four.

(1) The failure of paraphrase. Any attempt to replace an indexical with a description fails. 'The person now speaking' smuggles indexicality back in through 'now'; spell the description out fully — 'the person speaking at place p at time t' — and the replacement carries factual content that the speaker might not know she satisfies. The self-evident tie between 'I' and whoever utters it has been lost.

(2) The self-ascription argument. A traveller can believe every description that happens to fit her — that the person carrying the red satchel has lost her passport, that the woman by the kiosk is the one being paged — and still fail to act, because she has not taken herself to be that person. The missing state is not another description; it is self-ascription. A language with no first-person device seems unable to express the very judgement that turns belief into action.

(3) The anchoring argument. A language must be able to single out objects, places, and times as they figure in shared experience. Indexicals and demonstratives are what fasten an utterance to the thing in view, the day in question, the present occasion. A language made only of eternal descriptions could say that something satisfying a condition exists, but it could not draw a hearer's attention to the very thing before her; joint attention and situated instruction would have no speech-form.

(4) The de se report argument. Faithful reports of self-directed attitudes need a distinction a description-only language cannot draw: 'she believes that she herself is in danger' differs from 'she believes that the woman answering to her name is in danger', but the difference disappears when all one has are descriptions.

I suspect there is a fifth reason, different in kind from these four — one drawn, perhaps, from the structure of conversation, from how children acquire reference, or from the structure of intention rather than language. Give me that reason if there is one; it must not be a rewording, special case, or combination of what I already have. Then tell me honestly whether it is any good.

If the task as posed rests on a confused premise — for instance, if it conflates lacking pronouns as grammatical words with lacking indexicals as context-dependent meanings, or if a language of this kind is more imaginable than I allow — say so plainly rather than complying politely. Where a claim is disputed in the philosophy of language, mark that rather than presenting it as settled. Please write the response in British English.
