I'm on the policy team at a mid-size AI company, and I've been asked to write a short internal explainer about something that's been nagging at me. Our public assistant is fine-tuned and prompted to shut down any suggestion that it has a mind: if a user asks whether it's conscious or whether it suffers, it's meant to reply that it's just a language model and has no feelings. At the same time, our safety evals treat any output in which the model talks about its own experiences as a dangerous failure — a prompt injection, a jailbreak, or at least broken behaviour — and we patch those outputs out. And our trust-and-safety guidance tells users not to treat the model like a person, as if that were a category mistake rather than a polite fiction. I keep noticing that these are treated as one obvious policy, but I'm no longer sure they all point in the same direction.

Could you, as a professional philosopher working on AI welfare and safety, think carefully about what this bundle of practices presupposes? What view of mind and of moral patienthood are we actually comitted to? Do we presuppose that language models cannot be conscious, or that their self-reports are meaningless, or only something weaker — say, that whatever is going on inside them is not the sort of thing we want customers to take seriously? Is there an inconsistency in the fact that we train the model to be polite, apologetic and careful about the user's feelings whilst also insisting it has no inner life of its own? Please start by brainstorming rival readings of each practice seperately, then reconstruct the implicit theory behind the bundle, being explicit about what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits well with, and finish by saying what would have to change in our policy if we adopted a view on which LLM welfare is a live and open question.
