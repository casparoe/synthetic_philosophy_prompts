Some friends of mine have fouded a little 'Society for Constant Improvement' whose rulebook borrows Benjamin Franklin's method of moral perfection almost whole cloth. One of its rules reads: 'Every Member shall keep a daily Book of Faults, setting down a black Spot for each Offence against the chosen Virtues; at the Month's End he shall count his Spots and strive that they grow fewer; and in all this he shall practice Humility, the thirteenth Virtue and the hardest to keep.' A member has got into a strange loop: he now records as his chief fault 'wasting hours on self-examination.' That entry itself bothers him, so he re-examines whether the entry was right, writes down another spot for scrupulosity, and so on. Honest question: is this what the rule requires, forbids, or just fails to envision? Walk me through what the rule's words give us, what its obvious purpose gives us, what the drafters would likely have said if asked, and whether there is any reading under which a person can keep the book without the book's becoming the main source of what it records. If no such reading exists, what does that show about the deeper assumption behind Franklin's project — that you can count a fault without, by the counting, adding to it?
