I'm a novelist, currently writing a book about a hospital administrator who must decide whether to enforce a public-health directive she privately distrusts. Drafting her deliberations has forced me to articulate something that has long bothered me in ordinary moral judgment. An operating-room nurse who follows a surgeon's disputed instruction is usually praised as a good team member, even when her own hands are telling her the instruction is wrong, and if the patient dies almost all of the blame concentrates on the surgeon. Yet a lay citizen who follows a politician's disputed claim about, say, a vaccine's safety is usually dismissed as gullible, and if someone is harmed we blame the citizen for his own credulity. In both cases the person has less expertise than the authority, privately doubts the authority, and defers on a matter that falls squarely within the authority's role. Why does the first read as professional deference while the second reads as servile conformity?

I want to know whether there is a defensible principle that generates both judgments, or only a pair of social reflexes pointing in opposite directions. Give me the strongest justification you can for the asymmetry, then test it against interfering cases: a nurse who swallows her objection when a surgeon is about to make a dangerous error because her role discourages second-guessing, and a citizen who defers not to a politician but to a public-health physician's recommendation on a ballot measure. Does the principle survive those cases? If it does not, tell me whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned.
