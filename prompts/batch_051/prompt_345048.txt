I am writing a paper for a seminar in constitutional theory, and I need to think through an argument that keeps reappearing in the reading. The argument is that a constitution is best understood as a society-level Ulysses contract. Odysseus, knowing that his future self would be swept away by the sirens, has his crew bind him to the mast; he prevents his later self from acting on a temptation his present self rationally rejects. In the same way, the story continues, a political community at the founding moment foresees that future majorities will be tempted — by panic, by faction, by short-term electoral interests — and binds them in advance with entrenched rights, supermajority rules, and, in the proposal I am currently writing about, a constitutional debt brake. My interlocutor uses the analogy to conclude that when courts strike down legislation, or when a fiscal rule ties the hands of a sitting government, nothing anti-democratic has happened: the community is merely enforcing its own earlier, more sober choice, and the countermajoritarian difficulty largely dissolves.

I want an audit of how far this analogy can carry that conclusion. I am after the structure of the argument, not a literature survey. Please give me an explicit correspondence table: in the constitutional case, what plays the role of Ulysses, the sirens, the ropes, the mast, the crew with wax in their ears, the decision to be bound, and the later self who is released and endorses the binding? Then identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that do real work for or against the legitimacy argument from the ones that are merely decorative. I am especially interested in whether one correspondence table can cover entrenched rights and a constitutional debt brake at the same time, or whether the analogy does better for one than the other. Does it show that the countermajoritarian difficulty dissolves, show only something much weaker, or could the disanalogies end up counting against the use of precommitment as a legitimating story? If there is a better analogy that does the work more honestly, name it; if the search for an analogy is itself part of the problem, say that too.

One further instruction: if my framing already begs the question — for example, if the phrase "self-binding" quietly assumes the very identity between binder and bound that the analogy is supposed to supply, or if the analogy cannot do the same work for rights and for a debt brake without being stretched past usefulness — tell me that plainly and redo the task in the form that makes sense, rather than politely complying with a confused setup.
