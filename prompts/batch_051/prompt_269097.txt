I keep noticing a pattern in arguments about teletransporters and mind uploading, and I cant find a name for it. Suppose a company offers a Parfit-style teletransporter: it scans your brain, destroys your body, and reconstructs a working digital version of you. When someone wants to argue that the digital version is really you, they describe the whole operation as a transfer—"your mind is transferred to a computer." The destruction of the body gets mentioned only in passing, or not at all. When someone wants to argue that it isn't you, they describe the exact same operation as copying—"the machine copies your mind and then kills you." The physical facts are identical whichever way it's described: scan, destroy the original, reconstruct from the scan. But in the first description the destruction is folded inside the main verb, while in the second it's a separate, morally loaded step tacked on after the copying.

I see the same split with "uploading" versus "mind-copying," and with "teleportation" versus "faxing a person." What strikes me is that the two descriptions aren't just making the same facts feel different. Each one quietly asserts the conclusion it's supposed to support: "transfer" presupposes that something continuous was moved, while "copy" presupposes that the result is a new thing. It isn't exactly a framing effect, because the verb isn't just coloring the facts—it's handing you the identity verdict as part of the description. It also isn't exactly equivocation, because the speaker usually sticks with one verb rather than sliding between senses mid-argument.

Does this move have an established name? If it does, tell me what it is and how it differs from a framing effect, an intuition pump, and plain begging the question. If it doesn't, coin a name for it, define it, and say where its edges are—for instance, does it still apply when the destruction step is omitted entirely and the original survives alongside the reconstructed upload?

And please write the answer in American English.
