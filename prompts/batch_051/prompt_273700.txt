I've been going round in circles with a friend about an asymmetry in our own intuitions, and I want someone to tell me whether anything principled explains it or whether one intuition has to go. I've been reading rationalist-adjacent blogs long enough that the standard moves — substrate independence, Turing-test intuitions, the Chinese room — feel stale to me, so I'm after the asymmetry itself rather than a survey of the usual positions.

Take a specific human brain, say mine an hour from now, and imagine two implementations of the same neuronal update rules. One is a silicon simulation: neuron by neuron, every spike and synaptic weight mirrored faithfully enough that, wired into a chat interface, nobody could tell it from the original across any conversation. Most physicalist-leaning people I know, me included, feel pretty strongly that this would be conscious — there'd be something it is like to be it, and deleting it would matter. The other is a book. Not a book describing the brain, but a brute-force lookup table: for every possible input state at each time step, it says what the next state is, and a dumb mechanism follows its entries in order. Behaviourally the book-plus-mechanism is indistinguishable from the simulation — same inputs, same outputs, same state transitions — yet the thought that they're equally conscious feels absurd to me. I'd pulp the book without a second thought and hesitate over deleting the simulation.

I can generate candidate principles, but each seems to collapse when I vary the case. The simulation runs fast and continuously while the book is consulted on demand, but slow the simulation or imagine the book read through in strict real-time sequence and the verdict starts tracking mere speed. Never mind that no actual book could be that large; I want to know whether the difference is one of principle or only of scale. So: give me the strongest principle that keeps the two cases apart, and if there isn't one, an honest verdict on which intuition to give up — that the silicon simulation is conscious, or that the book isn't — and roughly how confident you'd be under cross-examination. If the framing is broken somewhere — if 'same computation' is doing sneaky work, or the lookup table is gerrymandered so it isn't really implementing the brain — say so plainly; I'd rather learn I've asked the wrong question than get a tidy answer to the one I posed.
