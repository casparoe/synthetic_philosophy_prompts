I keep meaning to close my practical rationality seminar's unit on Kavka's toxin puzzle with a poem instead of another précis, and I'd like you to write it. Just so we're aligned: a billionaire offers you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you can genuinely intend to drink a harmless but unpleasant toxin tomorrow afternoon. The money is paid the moment the intention is formed, before you drink, and there's no penalty if you fail to follow through tomorrow. But tomorrow you'll have no reason to drink — the money's already yours — and you know that now, so it seems you cant form the intention, even though forming it would be pure profit.

I want a villanelle from the point of view of someone trying to win the money anyway. Let the first refrain be the lure — something like the thought that the reward attaches to the intention alone, so intending is nothing but gain. Let the second refrain be the flat fact the speaker keeps returning to: that tomorrow there will be no reason to drink, and so he won't. The form should carry the puzzle rather than decorate it. Across the tercets the two refrains keep taking turns and undercutting each other, and when they finally land together in the closing two lines, their meeting should feel like the intention collapsing under its own awareness of the future, not like a resolution. Dont turn it into a lesson about commitment devices or weakness of will; the bind has to stay genuinely open. And it should be precise enough that a philosopher of action would recognize the gap between intending to drink and merely deciding you'll drink, and the asymmetry between reasons for intending and reasons for acting, without the poem ever using that vocabulary as jargon.
