I write about science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, so I'm the one who ends up covering the meditation app, the Confucian revival group, the nontheist congregation, the UFO religion, the "is fandom a religion?" thinkpiece. A few times a month I have to decide, in print, whether to call something a religion, a spiritual practice, a secular ritual, or just a community with good branding. I've read enough to know the respectable move is that "religion" is a Western folk category that doesn't map cleanly onto Buddhism or Confucianism, let alone secular movements with hymns and Sunday meetings. But I can't write "the category is contested" every time and still keep an editor.

Give me a working editorial procedure for when to use the word "religion"—a checklist or words-only flowchart I can run in about 20 minutes before filing, with an editor who has no patience for theory, a fact-checker who wants me to defend every label, and a corrections policy that will turn a mislabel into a public ding. It has to work for someone who hasn't done the reading. The scholarly argument has real distinctions in it (belief vs practice vs community, self-description vs outsider label, supernatural claims vs a program for transforming your life, institutions vs loose networks). Tell me which of those distinctions have to survive in the checklist and which can be dropped when you're on deadline, and mark clearly the places where judgement can't be eliminated and two reasonable writers would still split.

Then run the procedure on three cases: Theravada Buddhism, Confucianism, and something like the Sunday Assembly (explicitly nontheistic but with congregation-style meetings, songs, the works). Make at least one of them come out against my instincts—I'd probably call all three a religion and I want to see where the procedure pushes back. Then be honest about what the procedure can't do: wich groups it'll still misclassify, and when I should drop the word entirely rather than stretch it. I'd rather have a flawed checklist that says where it fails than a clean-sounding one that sneaks a definition in.
