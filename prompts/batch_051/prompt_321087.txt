Last night I got into a long, heated argument with a friend about whether wealthy countries have obligations to admit migrants and to fund international aid, and he kept invoking Garrett Hardin's lifeboat ethics as though it settled the matter. As he tells it, Hardin's argument is this: the affluent nations are lifeboats, the global poor are swimmers in the water around them, and every lifeboat has a fixed carrying capacity; letting everyone aboard simply sinks the boat and drowns everyone. From that, my friend concluded, it follows straightforwardly that rich countries are not just permitted but morally required to refuse entry and to stop food aid, because generosity only postpones the reckoning and increases the sum of human misery by allowing population to grow beyond what resources can support. He added that Hardin's tragedy of the commons shows that any shared resource left open to all, such as a world food bank, will inevitably be destroyed by free-riders, which is why Hardin rejects the Spaceship Earth ideal and settles on an ethic in which each nation looks after itself.

I find this summary suspicious, but I cannot say exactly where it goes wrong. I have a vague memory that Hardin treats the tragedy of the commons as having a remedy involving mutual coercion, and that the lifeboat argument is supposed to describe a tragic choice rather than to offer an enthusiastic moral defense of restrictive border policies. But my friend is a confident debater, and I want to be precise rather than merely gesture at doubts the next time we talk. Begin by brainstorming the distinctions one would need in order to assess such a summary fairly — for instance, descriptive claims about carrying capacity versus normative claims about what a country may or must do, the intended scope of the lifeboat analogy, and how the tragedy-of-the-commons model relates to it. Then go through my friend's summary piece by piece: identify which elements accurately reflect Hardin, which are subtly incorrect or conflate different parts of his view, and whether the strong normative conclusion my friend drew actually follows from the parts that are defensible. End with a fair paraphrase, in your own words, of what Hardin's lifeboat ethics does and does not claim, so that I have something accurate to use in our next conversation.
