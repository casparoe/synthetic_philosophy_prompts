I have a puzzle I would like a second opinion on. I recently moved from pure mathematics into a policy-analysis role, and for the past month I have been building a model that estimates the expected value of a low-probability, long-horizon intervention. The model generates an unbounded list of possible outcomes, some positive and some negative, and I noticed that the bottom-line estimate shifted whenever I reordered the scenarios in the spreadsheet. I eventually recognized the structure: it is the one usually called the Pasadena game, in which a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, an outcome that first lands heads at toss n pays (−1)^(n−1) · (2^n)/n dollars, and the probability-weighted terms reduce to the alternating harmonic series. That series converges in its natural order, but only conditionally, so the very same terms can be made to sum to any real number, or to diverge, simply by listing them in a different order. The game therefore has no order-independent expected value.

Then I noticed what looks like the same phenomenon in our team's deliberations. Given the same set of considerations about a proposal, the group's all-things-considered verdict changes systematically with the order in which those considerations are raised: a cost introduced early dominates the discussion, a benefit introduced late gets discounted, and so on. I have started describing this in the language of convergence. A practical question, I say, is absolutely convergent when any ordering of the same considerations yields the same verdict, and conditionally convergent when reordering the agenda can change the verdict. On this way of thinking, a conditionally convergent question has no determinate all-things-considered answer, just as the Pasadena expectation has no determinate sum; in both cases the result is an artifact of the order in which fixed items are enumerated.

I cannot tell whether this parallel is deep or merely verbal. For it to be a real identity of structure, practical considerations would have to combine like the terms of a series, and the order of consideration would have to be normatively inert rather than itself a reason; my worry is that agenda order is often informative, or changes the weight that a consideration receives, in a way that merely rearranging terms in a sum does not. The parallel also predicts that any path-dependent committee decision should be reclassified as having no determinate answer, rather than as having a correct answer hidden by a bad procedure, and that prediction strikes me as too strong. Is there a genuine structural identity here, or does the comparison collapse exactly where it matters? In particular, where does the mapping between conditional convergence and order-dependent deliberation break?
