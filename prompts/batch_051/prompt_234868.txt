I run the sustainability program at a mid-size construction company, about 800 people, and I keep hitting the same wall. Every week my team makes dozens of small calls: whether to pay 15% more for low-carbon concrete on one pour, whether someone flies across the country for a two-hour client meeting when remote was offered, whether we switch a single supplier for recycled steel, whether we buy offsets to 'neutralize' a job site. Each decision, on its own, changes global emissions by roughly nothing, and the people holding the budget use exactly that as their argument: "one extra truckload won't move the temperature, so why take the expensive option?" I'm pretty sure that's the inefficacy objection, and I know there are replies to it, but I don't know how to turn the replies into something my team can actually run.

From what I've read, the main replies seem to be: the expected marginal impact reply (my action might have a tiny probability of being the one that pushes a supplier or a policy over a threshold, so I should weigh the small chance against the large harm), threshold cases where an extra unit quite literally makes a difference, the universalization move (what if everyone did that?), and the thought that even causally inert choices matter as participation in or endorsement of the polluting practice. I don't know which of these are actually solid, which collapse into each other, or how they should sort the ordinary decisions we make.

Please write me a step-by-step decision procedure my team can run in 10–15 minutes per decision, with yes/no gates, points where it stops and says "this is a values call, not a procedure call," and a flag for recurring decisions so we set a policy once instead of relitigating it every month. Ground it in the actual philosophy on collective harms and the inefficacy objection, but keep the theory labels light since the users are project managers, engineers, and our CFO. Where the procedure depends on an empirical number we won't have (like how close a supplier is to a threshold), tell me what fallback to use rather than stalling. Then run the procedure on three of our cases: (1) the 15% premium for low-carbon concrete on a one-off small pour; (2) the cross-country flight for the client meeting when the client already said remote would be fine; (3) buying cheap offsets so we can keep flying internally and still call the project carbon-neutral. Make at least one of the three come out the way I won't like, and be honest about what the procedure can't settle, so I don't loose credibility by pretending it's more mechanical than it is.
