I'm applying for a summer fellowship in AI safety and need a two-page research proposal on corrigibility and the shutdown problem. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher working at the intersection of decision theory and agent foundations, and to think carefully before committing to a framing. I have a half-formed idea I want you to turn into a defensible proposal rather than write up as-is. My suspicion is that the shutdown problem is actually two problems that the field tends to run together. The first is non-resistance: an agent should not take steps to prevent a shutdown command from being executed. The second is non-manipulation: an agent should not act to shape whether a shutdown command is issued in the first place — by influencing the operator, degrading or gaming the signal, or altering the environment so that the operator's choices are constrained. Existing approaches seem to solve one while leaving the other open. Utility-indifference proposals make the agent willing to be switched off, but they can also leave it indifferent to whether the shutdown channel remains intact, which looks like a license to manipulate that channel. The approach that makes the agent uncertain about the human's objective can get it to accept the off-switch because the human's decision carries information, but that seems to create incentives to control the information or steer the human. And approaches that drop the completeness assumption on preferences handle non-resistance cleanly but have less to say about how the agent should treat the operator's standing ability to correct or revise it. So the proposal should ask: are non-resistance and non-manipulation genuinely distinct requirements, or does one reduce to the other under the right formulation? Is one more fundamental? And is corrigibility something we should expect to unify in a single design principle, or is it an irreducibly disjunctive set of constraints?

For the proposal itself, please include: a precise statement of the question with a determinate answer; a short account of why the existing positions leave it open; the method I would actually follow over the summer, phase by phase, and why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives of formalizing another counterexample or proposing another utility-function patch; what a useful deliverable would look like for someone designing corrigible systems; what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of what makes the question too big for one summer. Don't bother with citations or a bibliography — I'll add the references myself. Start by brainstorming two or three candidate framings of the question, then pick the one you judge most tractable and say why you chose it.
