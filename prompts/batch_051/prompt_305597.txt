Well, here's the thing. I'm putting together a session for my philosophy of language course on identity over time for languages, and I want to use the Latin-to-Italian case as the running example — it has the advantage that students actually find it more intuitive than heaps of sand. The session turns on candidate criteria for when, if ever, Latin became Italian rather than just a changed form of Latin. I've collected seven. I want an eighth that is genuinely different in kind from everything on the list, not a rewording, not a special case (e.g., just swapping in a different grammatical feature), and not a mix of two existing ones.

Here are the seven:

(1) Mutual intelligibility: Latin and Italian are different languages once a speaker of Classical Latin and a speaker of the local vernacular could no longer manage a conversation.
(2) Grammatical threshold: the case system has collapsed, articles have developed, and the old synthetic future and passive have been replaced by periphrastic forms with habere/essere.
(3) Phonological threshold: the loss of distinctive vowel length, palatalisation of /k/ and /g/ before front vowels, and the like.
(4) Self-ascription: the point at which speakers stop classifying what they speak as Latin and start calling it volgare (and later italiano).
(5) Codification: the point when the vernacular acquires a written standard of its own — first the notarial and legal texts in the tenth and eleventh centuries, then the literary canon (Dante, Petrarch, Boccaccio), then the sixteenth-century codification of Tuscan as the literary norm.
(6) Acquisitional continuity: there is an unbroken chain of native learners from a child in late antique Rome to a child in early modern Florence, so "Latin" and "Italian" are stages of one language and "became" is the wrong word.
(7) No-fact conventionalism: there is no principled boundary; any proposed cut point is a stipulation, so the question is ill-posed.

Now give me an eighth criterion. It should fix the boundry in a way none of these do, or refuse to fix it for a reason none of these give. Then be honest: would it earn a place in the session, or is it a clever idea that doesn't do much work once you look at it? And write the answer in British English please.
