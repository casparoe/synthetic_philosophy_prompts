I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, currently reporting on institutions that decide whether a single team may proceed with work that needs no one else's authorization. The review board I follow publishes only its rulings, never its reasoning, and I have been reconstructing the principle behind them from the record.

So far it has ruled as follows.

1. Approved, subject to a modest aggregation condition, a proposal from one lab to publish a city-scale dataset of timestamped GPS traces from ride-hailing and delivery vehicles, for transportation research. The board accepted that the data could not be recalled once posted but judged the expected benefits substantial and the expected harms diffuse and minor.

2. Rejected a proposal from one lab to release detailed, de-identified emergency-department visit records from three states, without patient consent, to study opioid overdoses. The board's own staff analysis had concluded the research value probably exceeded the expected privacy loss; the board nevertheless rejected it, holding that the harm would fall on identifiable patients who could not opt out.

3. Rejected a proposal from one lab to conduct an open-field trial of a gene drive meant to suppress an invasive rodent population on an uninhabited island. The board found the intervention could not be recalled, that the modified organism could spread beyond the site, and that affected parties beyond the island had not been consulted.

4. Approved a proposal from one lab to fly instrumented balloons over a metropolitan area for two weeks to map air pollution, with automatic descent and retrieval and notice to the aviation authority. The board treated the intervention as cheaply reversible and its expected harms as negligible.

5. Rejected a proposal from one lab to publish the weights of a large generative model the lab had trained, arguing that outside researchers could then study its risks. The board found the publication irreversible, the safety benefit speculative, and the likely harms—mass-produced fraud and impersonation—borne by large numbers of non-consenting third parties.

The board now must decide on a new proposal. A single lab with private funding asks to release a small quantity of a reflective aerosol from a high-altitude balloon over a few days, to study plume chemistry as a preliminary step toward understanding stratospheric aerosol injection. The direct intervention is tiny: orders of magnitude less material than a single commercial flight emits, dispersed within days. The lab argues the proposal is reversible, harmless, and scientifically valuable. Others reply that the physical release is not the true object of decision: if one team performs an outdoor solar-geoengineering experiment on its own, that establishes a precedent, and a succession of such precedents could erode the norm against unsanctioned deployment until a state or private actor scales up unilaterally, which is where the severe tail of the risk lies.

I need more than a one-word forecast. Please articulate the candidate principles that fit all five earlier rulings—including one that draws on the unilateralist's curse, the idea that one actor's optimism should not be allowed to open a one-way door whose plausible downside is severe—and explain what each principle implies for the aerosol release. Then give your best prediction of the board's decision, identify which earlier ruling is the closest analog, and say what choice the board would be committing itself to if the precedents underdetermine the outcome. Finally, specify a further case whose ruling would cleanly separate the remaining candidate principles.
