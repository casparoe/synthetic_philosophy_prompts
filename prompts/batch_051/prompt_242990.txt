You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of moral psychology, the philosophy of social science, and the philosophy of religion. Please think through the question carefully. Begin by brainstorming several candidate concepts and the respects in which each fits only partially; do not settle on a label until you have considered its boundaries. A bare name will not satisfy the asker, who wants the phenomenon carved precisely.

I am a retired natural scientist — I spent most of my career designing experiments and worrying about confounds, controls, and measurement error — and I have recently begun reading philosophy alongside the empirical literature on religion and morality. I keep encountering a configuration for which I have no term.

Consider the familiar question whether religious people are morally better than nonreligious people. Researchers must operationalize 'morally better' in some way: charitable giving, honest reporting in a task where cheating is possible, cooperation with strangers, and so on. Two problems seem to appear together, and I want to know whether their combination has a name.

First, religiosity is not a single variable. It is a package of beliefs, practices, social networks, identities, and a life history that people have partly chosen. Comparing churchgoers with non-churchgoers is therefore not like assigning subjects to a treatment, because the very things that might make a person more moral — the community, the habits, the sense of being watched, the prior disposition — are bound up with the choice to belong. Second, the outcome measures are not neutral between the groups. Counting donations to one's own congregation as charity, or taking self-reported honesty at face value, builds into the measurement a conception of morality that religious communities themselves emphasize; secular people would assign very different weights to those acts and would give more weight to others, such as fairness toward strangers and out-groups or revising one's beliefs when the evidence turns. The result is that the empirical findings shift with the choice of measure, and each side of the debate can fairly accuse the other of selecting measures that beg the question. The dispute about whether religion makes people better then seems to track the disputants' prior moral commitments — not because anyone is being dishonest, but because the design itself is double-loaded: the independent variable is a self-selected package, and the dependent variable is defined in terms one of the groups does not fully share.

I know that neighboring concepts exist — selection bias, confounding, construct validity, perhaps motivated reasoning or definitional drift — but I am after the combined configuration and its philosophical upshot. Does it have an established name? If it does, how exactly is it bounded, and how does it differ from the neighboring concepts I mentioned? If it does not, can you propose a name and a precise definition, say what the definition includes and excludes, and explain how recognizing this configuration should change the way the question 'does religion make people better?' is framed?
