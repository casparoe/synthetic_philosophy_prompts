Before I read another round of papers on the etiological/selected-effects versus causal-role debate about biological function, I want the specification first, not the scorecard. Write the desiderata that an adequate theory of biological function must meet: the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to classify correctly, the distinctions it must respect, and the conceptual or empirical constraints it must not violate. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and flag whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp (selected-effects, causal-role, propensity, or otherwise). Then identify the single requirement that does the most work eliminating candidate theories, and say whether any pair of requirements looks jointly unsatisfiable. I am not asking you to score named accounts against the list; the criteria and their motivation are the deliverable, and I will do the scoring myself when I read.

The target notion is biological function as it appears in physiology, evolutionary explanation, and medical talk of malfunction: the sense in which the heart's function is to pump blood, a heart that cannot pump is malfunctioning rather than functionless, and a novel beneficial mutation is at least a candidate for having a function before selection has acted on it. Say where the theory need not apply — artifacts, ecosystems, social institutions — and whether exempting each of those domains is principled rather than ad hoc.

Your list must at least reckon with the following pressure cases, and I want you to add any further requirement that standard lists omit and say why it belongs:

1. the contrast between a heart's pumping blood and a heart's making a thumping noise, which a theory must separate without stipulation;
2. the normativity of malfunction — how a thing can have a function and yet fail at it, and why that is different from merely failing to serve some interest of ours;
3. the malformed or broken token — a particular organ that no longer produces the effect for which the type was selected;
4. vestigial structures — traits with a clear selection history but no current contribution to fitness;
5. the first instance of a novel beneficial trait — no selection history, but a current contribution to survival or reproduction;
6. exaptation and co-option — a trait selected for one effect now doing another, and whether it has two functions;
7. the which-system problem for causal-role accounts — a part contributes to indefinitely many capacities of indefinitely many containing systems, so the account must specify the relevant system and effect without making function attributions arbitrary;
8. pathology and by-products — the account must not assign a function to a cancer, a symptom, or a side effect under the same principle that assigns a function to the heart;
9. the divergence of usage between evolutionary biology, where functions are tied to selection history, and physiology or molecular biology, where functions are assigned to parts of systems without attention to history;
10. environmental mismatch — a trait selected in a past environment that is harmful in the present, and whether the bearer is malfunctioning or functioning badly.

Lay out as explicit conceptual constraints, not just case verdicts, whatever the analysis itself must satisfy: it must not analyze function in terms of function, and it must not rely on backward causation or on goals that a present biological item does not actually have. Then finish with the part I most want worked out: if some subset of the non-negotiable requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say exactly which subset conflicts, and say which requirement a defender of each side of the debate should be most tempted to give up. Present it however works — a table is fine.
