I keep getting pulled into governance arguments—in my department and in the co-op I belong to—and a pattern keeps showing up that I've got a nagging sence is a named result in some corner of social choice or welfare economics, but I can't place it. Let me describe the shape of it. A small group has to rank or choose among a set of options: say, a department deciding among teaching schedules, or a co-op deciding how to use its shared rooms. Two commitments seem individually sensible. First, we carve off a couple of decisions as protected: each of two people gets to be decisive about one narrow question, so that their preference on that question automatically becomes the group's preference, no matter what anyone else thinks. We do this because we want to respect some kind of individual jurisdiction or autonomy; it would feel illiberal to outvote people on there own sphere. Second, we all think that if literally every member prefers option A to option B, then the group should prefer A to B. Then a concrete case arises where both commitments can't be met at once: the group verdict either cycles or has to break one of the two commitments, and this happens even though every individual has perfectly consistent, well-ordered preferences. The specific thing that strikes me is that the protected questions in the cases I see aren't about someone's private conduct, like what they read or do in their own home; they're about bits of common life that we've delegated to individuals to settle unilaterally. Is there an established name for this configuration? I don't just want the name—I want the most standard statement of the problem, which of the conditions are doing the real work, whether the protected-sphere condition in the standard statement has to be read as a right over one's own personal life or can be read as any delegated authority over shared matters, and what the standard escape routes are and what they cost. If I'm wrong that there's a standard result, tell me what the closest thing is and where my description diverges from it.
