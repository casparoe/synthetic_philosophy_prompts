Okay so I keep coming back to the 2006 Pluto demotion as a philosophy-of-language case study, and I want to do it properly Wittgensteiny rather than the usual “Pluto is just a rock” take. Not the astronomy, which I know: the semantics. Take family resemblance as the view that a concept like planet is held together by overlapping similarities with no necessary-and-sufficient core every member has, and that borderline cases are expected rather than solved by finding the right definition. Take language games as the view that a word gets its life from the particular practices its embedded in — textbook astronomy, amateur observing, planetarium shows, NASA press releases, elementary-school mnemonics — not from a sharp essence waiting to be discovered.

The episode, as I remember it: in 2006 the IAU voted in Prague to define planet (for the solar system) as a body that orbits the Sun, is rounded by self-gravity, and has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit. Pluto failed the third and got reclassified as a dwarf planet. People were genuinely upset, teachers had to redo lessons, and the label dwarf planet caused endless confusion because under the new rule a dwarf planet isn’t a planet at all.

Now run it in both directions. Theory-to-case: lay out the pre-2006 concept as a family resemblance cluster — which features were doing the work (orbits the Sun, big enough to be round, grandfathered in by history, culturally salient, not a moon) and where Pluto sat before the vote. I want an honest mapping, feature by feature, not just “Pluto straddles.” Case-to-theory: what does the vote and the aftermath show about whether planet was functioning as a family-resemblance term, and about whether a language game can be rewritten by a commitee? Specifically, does the cleared-the-neighbourhood clause look like an attempt to snap a family-resemblance concept onto a textbook definition, and did the public reaction confirm what family resemblance predicts when you yank a word out of one language game and force it into another? Finally, be direct about where the episode is awkward for the Wittgensteinian picture. People acted like there was a fact about whether Pluto is a planet that the new definition got wrong, not just a rule change; lots of astronomers kept using the old scheme anyway; and the whole dwarf-planet-that-isn’t-a-planet business looks like a deliberately engineered language game rather than a naturally grown one. Does the episode show that some terms have a hidden rigid core after all, or just that language games have authority structures that determine which moves count? End by saying what a Wittgensteinian should say to someone who insists Pluto was a planet all along and the IAU simply got it wrong.
