Hey — I teach an intro philosophy class (about 24 first-years, one 80-minute session) and we're about to hit the Meno problem: why is knowledge better than true belief, if both get you to the same right answer? I don't want to open with the text or even name the issue. I want a game that makes them feel the difference between being right by luck and being right in a way that keeps working when the case shifts, and only see where they've ended up during the debrief.

Here's the rough shape I'm imagining. Two rounds. Round one: teams answer a batch of simple classification questions and can bet points on thier answers. Some teams get the answer plus the reason or rule behind it; other teams get only the answer, but presented so it looks just as confidence-worthy (a note passed from a rival team, a rumor from a reliable-looking source, whatever you can make work) — and it happens to be true. Round one only checks whether you write the right answer, so both kinds of team score about the same and they should feel like they're doing fine. Round two: the questions shift — new cases, transfer tasks, having to defend or teach the answer, or the cost of being wrong goes way up. Now the teams with only the true answer can't tell which of their beliefs will keep working, and the betting should start to fall apart. The teams with the reason can adapt.

Design the concrete activity properly: rules, materials (cards, answer slips, a points system with no real money), and a timeline for the 80 minutes. The two kinds of information should be indistinguishable in round one — nobody should know they're the unlucky kind until round two starts squeezing them. Then write the debrief. It should connect what happened to the Meno move that true belief is fine as long as it stays put, but it runs away when the world moves; knowledge is true belief tied down by a grasp of the reason why. Map specific moments from the game onto that idea, without the activity opening with a lecture.

Include a section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about what the game can't reproduce: it can only show the pragmatic value of stability and transfer, not whether knowledge is valuable in some further sense, and it has to leave Gettier cases aside.

One more thing: in the debrief notes, keep the difference clear between what's standard (the Meno raises the problem, and Socrates' tethering answer is an early serious attempt at it) and what's genuinely contested among people who work on this (whether the tethering answer actually solves the value problem, and whether stability/transfer is even the right thing to emphasize). Don't write the debrief as if the philosophers have settled the matter — they havn't.
