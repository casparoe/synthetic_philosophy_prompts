Consider a tension in ordinary moral judgement. We treat it as a basic principle of justice that punishment must not be transferred: sentencing one person for another's wrongdoing is a paradigm of injustice, and rites in which a community loaded its guilt onto a sacrificial animal or a stand-in now strike us as both superstitious and cruel. Yet our own practices continually do something that looks structurally similar. A bank is fined for systemic misconduct and the cost is borne by shareholders who merely held the shares in a pension fund; a chief executive resigns or is removed for an institutional failure no one has shown she personally directed; a relay team is stripped of a gold medal because one member later failed a drugs test, even though the others ran clean. Is there one principle that both condemns the old cases and permits the new ones, or is the asymmetry only a useful piece of social machinery we prefer not to examine? I want the strongest justification you can find, tested against nearby cases, and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry survives or whether some of these judgements must be revised. Please answer in British English.
