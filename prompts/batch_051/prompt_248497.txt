I'm a grad student in cog sci, and I've caught myself using a rule for years that I've never actually defended. My rule is: I won't call a system (or animal) intelligent unless it could, at least in principle, give some sort of account of why it did what it did. Not a human-style narrative — a chess engine that could point to the tradeoff between king safety and material would qualify; a chess engine that just outputs moves wouldn't, no matter how strong. Same with animals: if a scrub jay recaches food when it knows another bird watched, I feel the pull to call that intelligent; but if it's driven by a hardwired rule about caches and observers, I want to say it's clever but not "intelligent." I also apply this to people: someone who solves a problem but can't say how gets less credit from me, which I know is unfair.

My advisor tells me this is just a bias toward verbalizable knowledge, and I'm confusing "intelligent" with "able to explain intelligence." She points out that lots of skilled performances aren't introspectively accessible, and that asking a system for reasons tests a second capacity, not the first. But I don't think I mean self-report exactly — I mean whether the behavior is governed by something that could, under the right circumstances, surface as a reason, even if the thing itself can't currently talk. Is this a defensible epistemic habit, or am I smuggling in a demand for explicit representation and controllability as a definition of intelligence? Where does it break down first, and what would the policy look like if I kept the useful part and dropped the parochial part?
