I'm a science journalist who covers mostly physics and AI. For years I've taken Wigner's old puzzle as basically sound: mathematics, which sure looks like a product of human minds playing with abstractions, keeps turning out to be the language the physical world speaks — group theory for particle physics, differential geometry for general relativity, complex analysis for quantum mechanics. That really is strange, and worth taking seriously.

But lately I've spent more time around machine-learning people, and they seem to have dissolved the strangeness without quite noticing. Their systems make startlingly good predictions about protein structures, weather, what a material will do under stress, by learning very high-dimensional functions from data. No compact law, no elegant equation, no claim to have found the underlying mathematical structure of the thing. A trained model, not a derived one. And yet it is not that mathematics stopped working — the models are all matrix multiplication and gradient descent. It is more that the working math sits on the model-building side, not on the world-describing side.

So I'm genuinely unsure now: does the unreasonable-effectiveness argument survive deep learning, or was it always really an argument about the world being compressible enough to model well? Which exact premise does deep learning put pressure on? And is there a repaired version that still says something surprising and true, or does the miracle just quietly turn into flexible function aproximators plus a lot of data?
