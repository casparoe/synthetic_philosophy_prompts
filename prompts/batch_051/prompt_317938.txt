I work as the export-compliance lead at a precision components manufacturer — bearings, seals, fasteners, small machined parts — and our products are dual-use: the same part can go into a dialysis machine or a cluster-munition fuze. I review several transactions a month where we can foresee some downstream misuse, but we do not cause it in any but-for sense: the buyer would source the part elsewhere, or our component is a tiny link in a much larger system. I want a reusable decision procedure I can run in under an hour per case, grounded in the philosophical distinctions about complicity as a way of being involved in wrongdoing without being its cause. The procedure must give me: the ordered questions to ask; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where it terminates with "no complicity finding"; where it flags "complicity, regardless of causation"; and where it must escalate to a person — me, or our outside counsel, who signs off on escalated cases — because the question is one of values rather than facts. It should build in the distinctions between foreseeing and endorsing, between fungible goods sold on ordinary terms and contributions tailored to the wrong, between a single transaction and an ongoing relationship that makes the wrong feasible, and between aiding a wrong and merely failing to prevent it, and it should say explicitly what it cannot settle and where it is likely to misfire. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) standard off-the-shelf bearings sold at list price to a buyer who volunteers that they are for a weapons production line whose use we consider unjust; (2) a custom-machined component we know is specified to fit an interrogation device used for torture; (3) a generic industrial pump we sold with no knowledge of end use, which a later news report ties to mass pesticide poisoning. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I will not like. End with \answer{...} containing a one-sentence statement of the core rule the procedure applied.
