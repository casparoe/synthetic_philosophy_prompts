Give me a working definition of what it is for a piece of information to count as a person's data in the sense relevant to the claim that data about a person belongs to that person. By belongs I mean the sense in which someone might legitimately claim to control, sell, license, withhold, or demand deletion of the information — not merely whatever a given jurisdiction happens to grant, but the conceptual basis for such claims. I want a test, or a small set of tests, not a definition copied from a statute.

Run the definition through these cases, give the verdict it yields in each, and tell me where it conflicts with my starting judgments; then revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts:

1. A private diary entry stored only on your own device.
2. A retailer's timestamped record that you, by name, bought a particular book on a particular date.
3. A high-resolution photograph of a public square, taken by a stranger, in which your face is clearly recognizable.
4. A score a lender assigns you estimating whether you will repay a loan, computed entirely from patterns in other people's borrowing records and never collected from you.
5. A store's aggregate count of visitors per day, which your visit helped produce but which does not identify, describe, or single you out.
6. A post you wrote under your own name on a public forum.

My starting judgments, which I want you to test rather than defer to: 1 and 2 clearly count; 5 clearly does not; 3, 4, and 6 I am genuinely unsure about. Also say whether the definition explains why the concept seems to come in degrees, or whether its graded feel is better explained another way.
