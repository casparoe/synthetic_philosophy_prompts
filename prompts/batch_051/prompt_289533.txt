I lead the risk-analysis team at a regional flood-control district in the United States. Four times a year we work through a batch of proposed capital projects — levee raises, pump-station upgrades, buyouts of low-lying neighborhoods — and we generally have four working days per proposal to produce a recommendation for our board, which has final sign-off. The board is made up of lay commissioners who want a yes or no and a dollar figure, though they will accept a clearly explained "we cannot responsibly give you a single number" if we show our work. The stakes per project run from roughly $5M to $80M, and both kinds of error are costly: approving an unjustified project wastes public money; rejecting a justified one can turn into a public-safety failure.

Our recurring problem is that the event we need to assess — say, overtopping of a levee within a 30-year horizon — gets different point probabilities from different respectable sources: our engineering consultants, the federal models we are required to use, and the academic literature. A typical spread might be 0.05, 0.12, 0.22. Historically we either averaged these into one number or quietly used the most pessimistic one. The average pretends the disagreement away; the worst case is an unacknowledged policy of extreme conservatism.

I have been told there is a body of work in formal epistemology and decision theory on imprecise probabilities, which represents a state of opinion not as a single probability function but as a set of probability functions — a "credal set" — or as an interval [lower, upper]. I understand this work also distinguishes several rules for acting under such a set: interval dominance, maximality, E-admissibility, Γ-maximin, and the like, and that these rules can disagree about which act is rationally permitted. I have not read the literature, and I do not expect my analysts to read it; I want the operational upshot.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure my analysts can actually run on each project. The analysts have graduate training in engineering or statistics but no formal philosophy, so define any technical notions in operational terms. The procedure should be a checklist in words: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; which projects the procedure clears, rejects, or leaves undecided; where it stops with a "no"; where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values or risk attitude rather than fact; and how to present the result to the lay board without pretending we know more than we do. Be explicit about which distinctions in the underlying theory must survive into the procedure and which can be dropped for analysts who will never read the literature.

Then run the procedure on two proposed projects:

1. A $40M levee raise. The credible range for the probability of overtopping in 30 years is [0.05, 0.22]. Expected damages if it overtops are about $300M. In the no-project condition, the current models put the probability at [0.06, 0.24]; the project lowers the probability but does not change the damages figure.

2. A $15M pump-station upgrade for a neighborhood where the credible range for a catastrophic flood in 30 years is [0.01, 0.09]. Damages are badly quantified because much of what is at stake is social and cultural; the community's own account of what would be lost conflicts with the county assessor's figures.

Make at least one of these come out in a way the board will not like, and say why the procedure produces that result rather than the analyst. Finally, state plainly what this procedure cannot do: which cases it will not decide, and what it assumes but cannot verify.

As you answer, tell me how confident you are in each major step of the procedure, and single out the one point you are least sure of, so I know where to push back.
