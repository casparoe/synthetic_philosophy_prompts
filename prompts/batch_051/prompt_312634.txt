I am a novelist, currently researching a novel set between Vienna and Berlin, and I keep returning to an argument whose shape interests me more than its content. One of my characters is German and another is Austrian; each insists that his own country is the more liberal. They rarely dispute each other's facts. Instead, whenever one cites a fact — Sunday trading rules, a public-broadcasting levy, a law governing speech, a speed limit — the other grants the fact but denies that it is what "liberal" really points to, and puts forward another example whose relevance the first then denies. Every proposed test of the comparison seems already to presuppose one contested way of weighing the dimensions, so the disagreement never reaches a point where evidence could decide it. I have noticed the same pattern in arguments about which of two cities is more "cosmopolitan," which of two people is more "free," and which of two eras is more "open."

You are a professional political philosopher. Before answering, brainstorm the nearby named ideas this pattern might fall under; then tell me whether there is an established concept that fits it exactly or only imperfectly, and where the edges are. I am not satisfied with a bare label: I want the carving — how the phenomenon differs from a merely verbal dispute, from ordinary vagueness, and from a question that simply has no determinate answer. If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and specify its boundary cases. Then address what, if anything, would settle the Austrian–German comparison: is there a reading of "more liberal" on which it admits of an answer? Please state how confident you are in your characterisation and single out the point you are least sure about.
