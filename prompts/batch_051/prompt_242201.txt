I just got back from a two-hour argument with my flatmate about what names even do, and Im left with a bunch of verdicts I can't fit together. He kept saying a name is basically a compressed description — 'Juno' just means 'the person who used to be called Maya' — and I kept throwing cases at him. My gut came out ahead, but I can't find the one principle behind it. Help me either find it or figure out which verdicts to drop.

- My friend legally changed her name from Maya to Juno last year. If I say 'Maya drove us to the wedding,' I'm definitely talking about Juno; the old name still refers. And if someone insists on calling her Maya because they 'don't agree with the change,' I think they're still referring to her, just being a jerk. So being a bad name to use doesn't stop reference.
- Everyone called me 'Momo' as a kid because my little brother mangled my name once, and nobody calls me that now. If a stranger who doesn't know the story points at me and says 'Momo,' Im genuinely torn about whether that refers to me. My gut says very barely yes, and only if the name somehow traces back to people who knew the story.
- My colleague is Zhang Wei (family name Zhang, given name Wei). Our American boss calls him 'Wei,' thinking Wei is the family name. I think 'Wei' still refers to my colleague; the boss just has the convention backwards. Reference survives the mistake.
- My Icelandic friend is Jón Einarsson. Einarsson is a patronymic — it just marks him as Einar's son, not a family name. If his conference badge says 'Jón Einarsson,' that's him. But if someone just yells 'Einarsson' across the room, I'm not sure it refers to my friend at all, since it could pick out a pile of Einarssons.
- A novelist publishes all their books under one pen name and has managed to keep their legal name secret. When critics say this author is better at novellas than novels, I think the pen name refers to the actual, hidden writer. But when someone says the pen name has become an institution, I half-feel they're talking about the body of work, not the private person. This one embarrasses me.

Is there a single account of what makes a name refer to a person that gives all of these? If not, which one do I have to give up? And whatever the account is, what does it say about a case I didn't try on him: an actor who has used one stage name for thirty years and whose birth name almost no one knows — does the birth name still refer to the actor, and does it refer to the same person as the stage name?
