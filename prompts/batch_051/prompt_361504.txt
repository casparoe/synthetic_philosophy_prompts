You’re a professional philosopher working on animal ethics and the structure of moral status. Think carefully and brainstorm two or three candidate scenarios before settling on the cleanest one.

I want a thought experiment built to a spec. In Bentham’s An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, near the end, there’s a footnote where he makes the move that a full-grown horse or dog is much more rational and much more capable of communicating than a newborn human infant, so if rationality or language were our test for how we can treat a being, we’d have to prefer the horse or dog over the baby. The only line that avoids that, for him, is the capacity to suffer.

Now here’s the request. Take two views that both agree suffering matters. Degreed sentientism says moral considerability comes in degrees: a being that can suffer more richly or more intensely should count for more. Threshhold status says once a being can suffer at all, it gets the same basic protections as any other sufferer. Construct a realistic case where the degreed view comes out less kind—less protective, or more willing to let a barely sentient creature be hurt—than the threshhold view. Constraints: ordinary setting like a farm, lab, warehouse, or household; no exotic science; no perfect predictors; and don’t just stipulate that the views deliver the verdicts. After you give the case, walk through what each view says and why, identify which structural feature makes them come apart, tell me what happens if you remove that feature, and how a defender of the threshhold view would attack the case.
