Ok here's one I keep going back and forth on. In 1893 the US Supreme Court decided Nix v. Hedden: a tomato is a vegetable, not a fruit, for the purposes of a tariff that taxed imported vegetables but not fruit. The importers pointed out that botanically a tomato is a fruit (it develops from the ovary of the flower and has seeds). The court basically said sure, botanically it's a fruit, but in ordinary language, in the kitchen, and in commerce it's a vegetable, and that's the meaning the statute uses. So the legal question wasn't about plant biology, it was about which sense of 'vegetable' mattered.

I want you to take this case and run it through the three main theories of concepts: prototype theory (categories are organized around typical or central features, and membership is a matter of being close enough to the prototype), exemplar theory (you categorize by comparing a thing to stored examples of category members), and the theory theory (concepts are embedded in a folk theory about how the relevant domain works, and different people can have different theories). Be specific about what each theory would say was going on: why the question could be genuinely live, why the court's ruling felt right to basically everyone who isnt a botanist, whether the court was settling what vegetable means or just choosing which concept to apply to a tomato, and what each theory thinks the importers did wrong.

Then commit. Which of the three gives the best account of the episode, and what does the episode show is missing or forced in the other two? I want a definite verdict, not a each-illuminates-part-of-it answer. Also be honest about where the theory you pick has to stretch or goes silent—I'll trust the verdict more if you mark the spots where the fit is awkward.
