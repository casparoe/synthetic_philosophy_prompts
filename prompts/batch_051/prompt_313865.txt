A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — the position that our sense that experience has a qualitative character, that there is something it is like to be us, is an introspective error. I came away defending a variant I assembled on the spot, and I would like an honest assessment of it.

Call the variant the Phenomenal Annotation View. Its central claim is this: the brain appends to some of its own states — sensory states, affective states, perhaps some imagistic ones — a special kind of representational tag. The tag's content is roughly "this state is like something for the subject." The tags are real, causally active representations, and they do genuine downstream work: they modulate attention, guide report, and underwrite the conviction that one is conscious. What the view denies is that any property exists — phenomenal, qualitative, intrinsic — that makes the tag true over and above the fact that the tag is tokened and consumed. On this view, when it seems to me that my pain hurts, that seeming is constituted by the pain-state's being annotated as hurting, not by a hurt that the annotation records.

An example to fix the idea. On the Phenomenal Annotation View, a pain comprises an ordinary nociceptive representation, a disposition to avoid and complain, and a second-order annotation that marks the nociceptive state as "the kind of state it is like something to have." The first two elements are uncontroversial; the third is meant to explain the stubborn sense that there is something left over above and beyond the causal machinery. The view is not standard eliminativism, because the annotation exists and the seeming is real. It is simply that the seeming is not the apprehension of any further felt quality.

My friend pressed two objections. First, if the annotation is just another representation, why would it produce a subjective appearance at all? An unconscious tag reading "this state is like something" is not the same as the state's actually seeming any way to anyone. Second, if the annotation is supposed to constitute the seeming, then I have merely relocated the mystery: I have posited a special-purpose symbol whose job is to create, not record, the appearance, and it is no clearer how a symbol creates subjective appearance than how a neural assembly does.

I want a candid appraisal that reconstructs the view in its strongest form before attacking it. Is the Phenomenal Annotation View a genuine alternative to both standard illusionism and standard realism, or is it standard illusionism with the word "annotation" doing all the work? What would have to be true for the central claim to be more than a verbal redescription? Which of my friend's objections, if any, is fatal, and can the view be repaired?
