I am a novelist, not a lawyer, and I am drafting a scene in which a judge must decide whether to refuse to apply the plain words of a statute. I want the judge's inner argument to be rigorous rather than a plot convenience, so I have been trying to work out for myself when a court may properly ignore what a text plainly says — the absurdity doctrine. My working view is that a court may decline to apply the ordinary meaning of a statute only if four conditions hold. First, the literal result must be one the enacting legislature could not plausibly have intended, not merely one it would now regret. Second, there must be a narrower reading available in the text itself, its title, or its structure — not one supplied from outside by the judge's own sense of fairness. Third, the departure must be minimal and must preserve the act's overall scheme rather than substitute the judge's policy judgment. Fourth, the absurdity must arise from a specific overlooked application or drafting accident, not from a principled disagreement with the statute as written.

The case I have invented for the novel is this. The state's Public Integrity Pension Act provides that a public officer convicted of any felony forfeits all retirement benefits accrued while in public service, and it expressly withholds from courts any power to order partial forfeiture. Its preamble states that the act's object is to ensure that officials who corrupt their offices do not retire on public money. The defendant is a seventy-one-year-old county records clerk with nearly four decades of unblemished service. She has pleaded guilty to a low-level nonviolent felony unconnected with her employment, for which she received probation. The judge accepts that the words are clear: a felony is a felony, and 'any' means 'any.' Applied as written, the statute strips her of her entire pension, several hundred thousand dollars, for an offense that involved no public funds and no public office. The narrower reading the judge considers is to take 'any felony' in light of the act's title and preamble as meaning 'any felony involving corruption or abuse of public office.' That reading would exclude her, but it requires the judge to override, or at least heavily qualify, the word 'any.' The judge is genuinely uncertain whether this is a proper use of the absurdity doctrine or an act of judicial legislation under a sympathetic set of facts.

Go through my four conditions one by one and say whether this case meets each, marking the points at which a condition underdetermines the answer. Then give a verdict: on my own test, may the judge depart from the plain text? Finally, say whether the case reveals that any of my four conditions is redundant, doing all the work, or misstated, and whether the fault in the test's handling of the case lies in the conditions or in the case itself.
