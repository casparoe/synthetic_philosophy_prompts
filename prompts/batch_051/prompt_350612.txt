I've been trying to determine exactly what the standard money-pump argument against intransitive preferences establishes, and I've reconstructed it as a numbered argument. I'd like to know whether the argument is valid as written, and if not, which step fails.

Suppose an agent has strict preferences over three prospects: A ≻ B, B ≻ C, and C ≻ A. Let ε be a fee small enough that, for any two prospects X and Y such that she strictly prefers X to Y, she also strictly prefers X together with ε fewer dollars to Y, at whatever level of wealth she holds.

(1) If an agent strictly prefers X to Y, then in a binary choice she will pay ε to exchange Y for X.
(2) Starting with C, by (1) she pays ε to exchange C for B.
(3) By (1), she then pays ε to exchange B for A.
(4) By (1), she then pays ε to exchange A for C.
(5) She now holds C exactly as at the start, but is 3ε poorer. Since she could foresee the whole sequence, the pattern is a sure loss reached through her own pairwise preferences.
(6) Any preference structure that leads an agent, through her own pairwise preferences and with foresight, into a foreseeable sure loss is rationally defective.
(7) Therefore, intransitive preferences are rationally defective.

The step I keep wanting to challenge is (1), but I can't tell whether the genuine problem is that a principle about one-shot binary choices is being applied to choices embedded in a sequence the agent can foresee, or whether the argument actually succeeds in showing that intransitive preferences are exploitable even under foresight. If the argument fails, I'd like to know the strongest reading of the step where it fails, and whether the argument can be repaired without adding an assumption that the agent is myopic or that her preferences are already irrational.
