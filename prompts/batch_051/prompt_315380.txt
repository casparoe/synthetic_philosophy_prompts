I keep going in circles on futarchy and I want to know what I actually think, case by case, and whether there's a principle underneath or just a pile of vibes. By futarchy I mean the proposal that instead of electing people to choose policies, you set a numerical metric for what you're trying to achieve — commute times, GDP, crime, self-reported wellbeing, whatever — and let people bet on whether a policy will move that metric; the market price becomes the decision. The mechanism seems basically sound to me. But my gut is all over the place about specific uses.

Here are my verdicts, as raw as I can make them.

A. A commodities desk runs a market on whether a hurricane makes landfall before next Tuesday. Fine, obviously, who cares.
B. A startup lets employees trade on whether the new product ships by Q3. I'm fine with it in principle, though I'd expect some sandbagging.
C. A company board decides whether to fire the CFO by running a market paid out in the stock price a year later. Fine; this is just corporate governance with extra steps.
D. A city runs a market on whether to build a light-rail line, paid out by a published index of commute times, air pollution, and sales-tax revenue over ten years. I feel good about this, maybe even enthusiastic. Textbook futarchy.
E. A school district runs a market on whether to close an underperforming school in a poor neighborhood, paid out by district test scores over five years. I hate this, and I cant say why it's different from D.
F. A state runs a market on whether to keep the death penalty, paid out by the violent crime rate over five years. I recoil, even though if I'm being honest the violent crime rate is exactly what I'd want a death-penalty referendum to be about.
G. A national government runs a market on whether same-sex marriage should be legal, paid out by a self-reported life-satisfaction index. I think the market is fine as a mechanism but the use of it here feels like a category error, like putting a right in the wrong currency. Maybe I'm just squeamish.
H. A national government runs a market on whether to invade a neighboring country, paid out by a basket of security and economic indicators. Obscene, full stop.
I. A city runs a pure decision market on whether to raise the minimum wage, where the contract just pays $1 if the ordinance passes and $0 otherwise, not conditioned on any outcome variable. I cant tell what the price is even supposed to mean; it feels like a straw poll with money attached.

I've tried four rules on myself and they all break somewhere:

1. Facts can be bet on, values can't. Breaks on D, which is a value decision I like.
2. It's fine when the price metric captures what the decision is for. Maybe breaks on E and F, where test scores and crime are exactly what those decisions are for and I still hate them.
3. Fine when nobody profits from somebody elses bad outcome. Breaks on G, where I don't see a loser, and probably on A, where a hurricane is somebody's bad outcome.
4. Fine when it's voluntary and the bettors have skin in the game; wrong when the state is deciding over people who didn't opt in. But almost none of D through H are opt-in for everyone affected, and I don't hate D.

Walk me through the best candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could code them. Test each against A through I, and say which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. Handle the D/E pair head-on: both are local governments, both use a published metric, both help some people and hurt others, and I feel totally differently. Then give me verdicts on two cases I keep going back and forth on:

J. A market on whether to legalize psilocybin-assisted therapy, paid out by a depression-and-substance-abuse index over five years.
K. A market on whether to grant asylum, paid out by a composite of employment, crime, and welfare use over five years.

Is K just the refugee version of E? Does the principle that saves my verdicts force me to be fine with K, or overturn E, or what?

After the discussion, give me the summary as a JSON object with these fields:
- candidate_principles: an array of objects. Each object has:
  - statement: a string, the principle stated precisely.
  - gets_right: an array of case labels from A through I that the principle matches.
  - overturns: an array of case labels where the principle rejects my verdict.
  - rail_vs_school: a string explaining how it handles D versus E.
  - new_case_verdicts: an object with fields J and K, each a short string giving the verdict.
- which_of_my_verdicts_i_should_drop: an array of case labels.
- final_recommendation: a string, at most a paragraph, saying which principle I should adopt and which one or two of my verdicts are doing the most work in the wrong direction.
