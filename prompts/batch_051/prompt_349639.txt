I'm a philosophy PhD student working on value theory and moral psychology, and there's this debate about effort and struggle that I've been following for ages, and it really doesn't seem to move. The two camps, roughly, are: (1) effort is only instrumentally valuable — it's a cost we pay, and what matters is the outcome; an identical painting isn't a worse painting just because it was produced effortlessly, and a hard-won success isn't better than the same success got easily. (2) Effort, struggle, or difficulty is itself part of what makes an achievement valuable — a difficult success is worth more than an easy one, and perhaps a failed struggle has some value even when nothing comes of it, because of what the striving does in the agent. Both sides seem to agree on nearly all the concrete cases. They both grant that the effortless genius's painting is as good a painting; they both grant that we admire the striver in a way we don't admire the lucky success; they both grant that people often find effort aversive in the moment and yet report that difficult activities were the most worthwhile. And then, at the last step, one side says 'so effort is itself valuable' and the other says 'no, that's just a heuristic for future outcomes, or a signal of commitment, or something we've been socialised to admire.'

My working hypothesis is that this isn't an empirical disagreement and not straightforwardly a verbal one either. I suspect the two sides are answering different questions: one about what makes a life go well for the person living it (welfare value), and the other about what makes an activity worthwhile or choiceworthy in a broader perfectionist or agential sense — and they're both using the single word 'valuable' to cover both. But I don't trust that diagnosis yet, and I don't think the 'valuable for whom' move dissolves everything.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal, is it a clash of bedrock intuitions, is it a hidden normative-theoretic question (say, about whether value has to be 'for' a subject), or is it an empirical psychological question — whether effort is intrinsically rewarding or just misremembered as such — that philosophers are too quick to wave away? I want a diagnosis, not a survey, and an honest answer about what, if anything, could move it: a distinction, an experiment, a case, a formal point — or should I just expect it to stay stalled? Please write the answer in British English.
