My county just announced it's spending a big chunk of the public works budget on a 200-year flood-protection plan, and at the hearing a friend of mine who's into longtermism kept arguing we're morally obligated to do it even if it means much higher taxes and overriding the people who voted against it. I'm not a philosopher, just a guy who'd pay the tax bill, but I tried to write out his argument to see where I actually disagree. It came out like this:

(1) People in the far future matter morally just as much as people alive now.
(2) If that's true, then when we weigh the interests of everyone a policy affects across time, the interests of the enormous number of future people will swamp the interests of the relatively few people alive now.
(3) If the interests of future people swamp ours in that weighing, then a government is justified in restricting present people's freedom and overriding their democratic objections whenever doing so is a reasonable way to protect those far-future interests.
(4) Therefore, if future people matter just as much as we do, a government is justified in restricting present people's freedom and overriding their democratic objections whenever doing so is a reasonable way to protect far-future interests.

I keep feeling the problem is somewhere around (3), but I can't pin down exactly what fails: if a policy really would be massively better on the whole across time, why shouldn't some present-day freedom give way? Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails, what the strongest true version of that step would be, and whether the conclusion still follows from the corrected version.
