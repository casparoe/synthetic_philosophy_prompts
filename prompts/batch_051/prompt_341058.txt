I've been thinking on my own for a while about why we call music sad when only a person can be sad, and it's left me a bit tangled. I run a small weekly discussion group for people who like thinking about music and the mind, none of us with any formal philisophy background. Each week we set a one-page question, then swap and comment on each other's answers. This week's question was:

"In everyday speech we say a piece of music is sad, but a sound can't be sad in the way a person is sad, because a sound has no mental states. So what could make it correct to call a piece of music sad rather than just a matter of how a listener happens to react, and is the word 'sad' being used literally, metaphorically, or in some other way?"

One member wrote this:

"Obviously an instrument or a recording doesn't have feelings, so 'sad' can't mean exactly the same thing when we apply it to music as when we apply it to a person. The real question is why we mostly agree that a slow piece in a minor key is sad, rather than it just being a private reaction. The answer is that sad music is music that causes sadness in the listener: a piece counts as sad because it reliably produces that feeling in the people who hear it, just as a sad event produces it. That is also why the minor mode matters, since the minor key is the recognised musical sign for sadness. But really the sadness is in the music itself, not in the listener. A slow, drooping melody is heard as sighing or weeping, and we read those contours in sound in the same way we read them in voices and faces. So the word is literal after all: sadness is a real property of the way the sounds are shaped."

I want specific claims checked, not a general impression. Also, please keep seperate what is settled among people who actually work on music and emotion and what is still genuinely disputed, so I don't go repeating a contested view as though it were the consensus.
