Design a runnable classroom game for a 90-minute civic-education workshop with about 24 adults, on the conflict among sortition, term limits, and career politicians. The game must make participants feel the trade-offs rather than hear about them: its rules and incentives should be arranged so that the tension between accumulated expertise, accountability, rotation, and short-term popularity shows up in what players actually do, before the facilitator names it. By the end, participants should be able to state, from their own play, at least one real cost and one real benefit of each device.

Divide the group into four assemblies of six that make a sequence of decisions across several rounds. Assign the assemblies to conditions of your choosing—for instance, crossing selection method (election vs. lottery) with tenure rule (continuation vs. mandatory rotation)—and explain how elections will be run given that everyone in the room is assigned to an assembly. The rules must include at least these elements: (1) a long-term option that yields a large payoff only if chosen across several consecutive rounds, and whose existence and logic players must learn from experience rather than being told; (2) a short-term option that pays a small, immediate, visible dividend; and (3) an incentive for members who face re-selection to favor the short-term option and for their electors to reward it. Provide concrete payoff numbers that require no real money, materials (cards, a board, private briefing slips, or equivalent), and a schedule dividing the 90 minutes between play and debrief.

Then provide a debrief that maps moments in the game onto the conceptual landscape: what sortition is meant to solve, what term limits are meant to solve, and what the case for career politicians preserves. Predict what will go wrong the first time it is run (for example, one assembly's outcome being driven by a single strong personality, players colluding, or the long-term option being ignored because its payoff is too small or too late), and say how to handle each failure. Be frank about which features of the real institutional problem the game cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Keep the whole answer under 1,000 words.
