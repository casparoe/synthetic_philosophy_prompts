I'm not a philosopher, just someone who keeps a notebook of patterns, and I've got one I can't put a name to. Start with the familiar puzzle: "All ravens are black" is equivalent to "all nonblack things are nonravens," so if a generalization is confirmed by its positive instances, then a red sock or a green lawn chair ought to count, weakly, as evidence about ravens. That much I get, including why it feels wrong. Here's the part I actually care about. Take a green scarf. It's nonblack and nonraven, so by the usual move it supports "all nonblack things are nonravens," which is just "all ravens are black." But the same green scarf is also nonwhite and nonraven, so by exactly the same move it supports "all nonwhite things are nonravens," which is equivalent to "all ravens are white." So one ordinary observation comes out supporting both the claim that all ravens are black and the claim that all ravens are white. Does this sharper version have a name of its own, or is it just the old raven puzzle with the contrast turned up? And when people try to block it, which piece do they actually give up—the positive-instance rule, the claim that logically equivalent sentences get the same evidence, or the assumption that gerrymandered negative properties like nonwhite can do the same work as ordinary ones? I'm after the joints here, not a one-word label: what exactly is the phenomenon, what is it not, and where are its edges. Also, since I'd bet this is contested, please tell me plainly what's settled and what's not, and don't present one school's fix as the consensus.
