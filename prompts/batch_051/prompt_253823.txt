I'm teaching an intro class on causation and I want to screen Groundhog Day as a kind of Humean parable, but I'm worried I'll smuggle in exactly the thing Hume spent his life denying. My read so far: Phil Connors lives the same day over and over, so he learns what happens next purely from past regularities — the boy falling out of the tree, the truck, the dropped tray — and he gets this almost magical predictive power without ever glimpsing an underlying necessity. That's a lovely dramatization of Hume's point that all we ever get is constant conjunction and habit. But then the film also treats the loop as something that has a cause, or at least a condition: Phil's moral transformation seems to be what breaks it, and the film nudges us to think there's some real connection between the kind of person he becomes and why the day finally moves on, not just another regularity. So now I don't know wether the film is old-Hume (regularities all the way down, necessity only in Phil's head) or new-Hume (there are real causes or powers behind the scenes, we just can't know them), or whether its sloppy about the difference and I'd be misleading my students by showing it. Could you walk me through a few of the moments you'd actually point to in class, as best you can from memory of the usual version, and tell me where the film genuinely illustrates Hume and where it quietly assumes a necessary connection or a hidden power? I'd also love to know what the film adds to the classroom that the usual billiard-ball examples can't, and where I should stop and say this is the movie's invention, not Hume.
