I'm a novelist outlining a book set in a simulated town, where the characters are computational objects whose state drifts over time; part of the plot turns on whether an edited character is still the same character or a replacement. To avoid writing nonsense I've been reading Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs by Harold Abelson and Gerald Jay Sussman (with Julie Sussman), and this passage near the start of chapter 3 keeps sending me back to the same problem:

"With objects, we must be concerned with how a computational object can change and yet maintain its identity. This will force us to abandon our old substitution model of computation (section 1.1.5) in favor of a more mechanistic but less theoretically tractable environment model of computation. The difficulties of dealing with objects, change, and identity are a fundamental consequence of the need to grapple with time in our computational models. These difficulties become even greater when we allow the possibility of concurrent execution of programs."

Before I read another dozen language-design manifestos, I want the contract all of them are trying to satisfy. Write the adequacy conditions for a theory of program state and identity: the behaviors it must explain or predict, the distinctions it has to respect (identity vs equality, value vs place, logical time vs wall-clock time, and why each can't be collapsed), and the facts about how interpreters and compilers actually evaluate code that it can't wave away. For each condition, say whether its a hard requirement or just a preference of one camp. And because every argument I've had ends up back at that SICP passage: for each requirement, tell me whether it follows from the shift the passage describes—abandoning the substitution model for a time-sensitive, environment-based one—or whether it would survive even in a pure, immutable, time-ordered setting. Where a requirement only appears once you make that shift, say whether the shift itself is forced or just a modeling choice.

I'd like this as a table with columns: Requirement, The case or puzzle that makes it a requirement, Hard or camp-specific, and Does it follow from the environment-model shift, and if so is that shift forced. Then after the table, a short paragraph: which single requirement does the most to sort out the available approaches, and is there any pair of requirements that looks jointly unsatisfiable to you.
