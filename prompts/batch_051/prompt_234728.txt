You are a professional philosopher specializing in physicalism and the philosophy of science. I have been asked to circulate a short "position profile" on what the word 'physical' contributes to the statement of physicalism, and before I show it to others I want a careful consistency audit. Please think through the commitments one at a time, make each precise enough to test against the others, and do not smooth over tensions by saying everything can be refined away. Be specific about what has to give.

Here is the package I currently hold:

1. Physicalism is true, and by it I mean this: every particular—objects, events, properties, whatever—is either identical with or metaphysically necessitated by the physical.

2. I take Hempel's dilemma seriously. If 'physical' is defined by current physics, physicalism is almost certainly false, because current physics is incomplete. If it is defined as whatever the final, completed physics will countenance, the thesis becomes indefinite, or limply elastic.

3. For that reason I prefer a via negativa definition at the fundamental level: physical entities are those that are not fundamentally mental. Physicalism, so understood, says there are no fundamentally mental entities.

4. But I also require physicalism to be substantive—a contingent, empirically grounded claim that our best science could in principle overturn—not a definitional triviality.

5. I want my physicalism to rule out both panpsychism and strong emergentist dualism: the first because it places mentality at the fundamental level, the second because it allows irreducibly mental properties to emerge from a non-mental base.

6. I locate the empirical content of physicalism in piecemeal reductive discoveries—for example, that pain is identical with C-fiber firing or with some future neurobiological kind that plays the same role—where these identifications are empirical discoveries rather than stipulations.

7. Finally, I hold that if a completed science of the mind concluded that some mental property cannot be identified with any non-mental property and cannot be explained in wholly non-mental terms, that would falsify physicalism rather than merely prompting me to loosen what I count as physical.

Please do the following:

(i) Identify which commitments are genuinely in tension with which, and which pairs only appear tensed until they are unpacked.

(ii) For each genuine tension, state what the cheapest coherent revision would be and what philosophical cost it incurs.

(iii) Rank the tensions by severity, with the most destabilizing first, rather than listing them all as equally bad.

(iv) Say which single revision would do the most to stabilize the whole set, and whether that revision can be made without abandoning a commitment the rest of the package depends on.
