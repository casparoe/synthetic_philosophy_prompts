On the safety-review board of our institute's AI foundation, I have been asked—as the philosopher—to give an opinion on the following clause of the founding covenant: "The Foundation shall not fund or approve the deployment of any autonomous system of general capability unless the alignment of that system with human interests has been solved in principle, and not merely demonstrated in a limited set of trials." The clause was drafted at a time when such systems were assumed to be goal-directed in a familiar way, carrying explicit objective functions. The case before us is one the drafters did not foresee. A team seeks approval to deploy a general-capability system that has no objectives of its own. It is trained only to infer, from instruction and behavior, what its operator would, on reflection, want done, and to carry that out; whenever its inference is uncertain, it is built to defer to the operator. The designers argue that alignment is thereby solved in principle: a system whose sole objective is to track the operator's considered aims cannot harbor aims of its own. Opponents reply that the hard part—characterizing human interests—has merely been moved inside the system as an inference problem, and that success has been shown only in trials, not in principle. How should the clause apply to this system? Please work through four readings—(a) the plain words, (b) the drafters' purpose, (c) what the drafters would have said had this case been described to them, and (d) what a reasonable board member today would take the clause to require—then say which reading should control, and whether "in principle" does real work in the clause. If the clause does not reach this system, say what would satisfy it.
