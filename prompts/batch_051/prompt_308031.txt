I'm a journalist who writes about law and decision-making under uncertainty, and I've been trying to think through a reform that keeps surfacing in debates about "naked statistical evidence."

Courts are famously reluctant to impose liability on bare base rates. In the familiar classroom illustrations, an accident victim can prove only that the defendant's bus line ran four of every five buses on the route at the relevant hour, and a rodeo promoter can prove only that most attendees gate-crashed. Either probability clears the civil preponderance standard, yet courts generally refuse to find liability. The usual explanation is that proof must be "individualized": it must bear on the defendant's own conduct, not merely on the class to which the defendant belongs.

Suppose a jurisdiction adopted the following fallback. In a civil case, if the plaintiff cannot satisfy the individualized-evidence standard but can establish that the defendant belongs to a well-defined class of potential harm-causers, and that a certified estimate puts the fraction of that class who actually caused the relevant harm at f, then the court may enter a probability-weighted judgment for f times the plaintiff's damages. The judgment would not be a finding of fault: it would be styled a "statistical contribution," would be inadmissible as evidence of wrongdoing in later proceedings, and would support no punitive damages or collateral consequences grounded in a formal finding of fault. The plaintiff's election of the fallback would be final, and the defendant could still insist on full individualized adjudication under the ordinary standard. Criminal proof, with its higher standard and its stigma, would remain untouched — at least initially.

Analyze how this rule would actually behave, not whether it is good in the abstract. Who would invoke it, and who would try to escape it? What incentives would it create for plaintiffs, defendants, and the experts who certify base rates — especially around the choice of reference class, which each side will try to gerrymander? Does the rule select for actual responsibility, or merely for whichever party controls the evidence needed to steer the case onto the cheaper track? Does the distinction between being ordered to pay because one probably caused a harm and being found to have caused it erode once the two become routine alternatives, and why would that matter? Are the predictable failure modes fixable — say, by limiting the fallback to repeat institutional defendants or to regulated industries with well-measured base rates — or do the fixes quietly reimpose the very individualized-evidence demand the rule was meant to soften?
