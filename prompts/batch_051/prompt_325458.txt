Two people in my reading group got stuck on this after a talk on thought experiments, and they both made points that sounded right to me, so I'm hoping someone can untangle it.

The speaker had spent a while on the violinist-with-a-kidney-disease case, and afterwards Maya said the whole thing was just illustration. Her line: a philosophical thought experiment doesn't provide any evidence on its own; it's a vivid story that already has the conclusion baked in. The real work is done by whatever principle is doing the arguing, and the story is decoration.

Dan pushed back. He said if it were only decoration, you could strip the scenario out and the argument would be exactly as persuasive, but it isn't. What the case does is make you notice that you actually apply the concept one way and not another — and noticing that is a datum, the same way noticing how a piece of lab apparatus behaves is a datum.

Maya said real experiments have a subject matter that can push back. Your reaction to a case is just your pre-existing view about that very subject, so using it to test the view is circular. A thought experiment cant surprise you.

Dan said that's plainly false — thought experiments surprise people all the time, like when someone is sure they're a utilitarian until they spend time with the transplant case and realize they wouldn't actually cut up the healthy bystander. That's new information about there own commitments.

Maya replied that it's only biographical information: it tells you about the person, or the culture they soaked up, not about whether utilitarianism is true. Dan answered that you can gain knowledge from a derivation from premises you already accepted without any new observation — that's how math gives knowledge, and a thought experiment is closer to a proof than to an experiment.

Maya came back that in math you can check each line of the proof, whereas here nothing checks the step from "this outcome looks repugnant" to "this principle is false." And she said the track record of intuition on these weird cases is patchy enough, and the cross-cultural variation wide enough, that the uncheckable step is doing all the work without being reliable. Dan replied that variation doesn't sink intuition as a source any more than visual illusions sink perception; the job is to work out which intuitions are trustworthy, not throw them all out.

Maya said that just moves the problem: saying "intuitions count when they're reliable" gives you back the exact question the thought experiment was supposed to settle, and the scenario by itself gives you no way to tell the reliable intuitions from the merely vivid ones.

Please adjudicate, move by move: who is right about which specific point, where each of them overreaches, and who do you think is more right overall?
