Two philosophers at a research institute—one a committed compatibilist about free will and moral responsibility in the tradition of Daniel Dennett, the other a free-will skeptic and opponent of retributive punishment in the tradition of Gregg Caruso—have spent the past year engaged in an adversarial collaboration on just deserts and punishment. Their institute has asked them for a single document that both can attach their names to. Draft that document: a joint statement the two positions could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, with the remaining disagreement made exact rather than papered over.

Begin with a numbered list of claims both parties accept. These should cover the phenomena to be explained, the relevant folk psychology, the empirical facts both accept, and the places where one side has revised its earlier formulations under pressure from the other; they should be substantive rather than truisms. Then provide a disagreements table. Each row should state the proposition at issue; the compatibilist's preferred formulation of their own view, worded so that a Dennett-style compatibilist would accept it as their own; the skeptic's preferred formulation of theirs, worded so that a Caruso-style skeptic would accept it as their own; the crux—the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; and, where possible, a concrete case, argument, datum, or experimental design both sides agree bears on that crux, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Cover at least the following fault lines: (i) whether the ordinary notion of deserving blame or punishment contains a basic, non-consequentialist component that a naturalistic account of agency cannot vindicate; (ii) whether the compatibilist abilities the Dennett-style position invokes are strong enough to ground the desert the skeptic denies is grounded; (iii) what the Strawsonian reactive attitudes license, and what each side thinks follows for whether we should preserve, revise, or abandon them; (iv) whether punishment can be fully justified by forward-looking considerations such as incapacitation, deterrence, and rehabilitation, or whether these leave an unsatisfied remainder that only desert can fill; and (v) the empirical or social-scientific question each side regards as most diagnostic—for example, whether public belief in basic desert does any justificatory or stabilizing work for a penal system over and above forward-looking rationales. Conclude with a candid discussion of which elements of their disagreement, if any, turn out to be verbal, and offer a common definition of the contested terms—desert, responsibility, blameworthiness, free will—that both sides can accept for the purposes of the statement. The tone throughout should be that of a document both parties would actually co-sign.
