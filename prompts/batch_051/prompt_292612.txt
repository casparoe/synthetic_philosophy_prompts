I want a diagnosis of a dispute that has been running for decades without, as far as I can tell, any real movement: the dispute between Searle and Dennett, and their respective followers, over whether intentionality comes in an original and a derived form. Searle's side holds that human and animal brains possess intrinsic intentionality: mental states that are about things in their own right, not merely about things relative to an outside interpreter, a designer, or a community of users. A computer program, on this view, can enjoy only derived intentionality: its symbols mean something because we assign meanings to them, not in virtue of what they do. The Chinese Room is meant to force exactly this point—running a formal program over syntax, however intricate, never by itself yields semantics. Dennett's side rejects the original/derived split as a real distinction. It takes the intentional stance as basic: something has beliefs or desires when adopting that stance toward it produces real predictive leverage that cannot be had in other terms. On this account, all intentionality is in a sense derived or stance-relative, including our own, and the demand for an extra ingredient of intrinsic aboutness is a residual demand for a Cartesian res cogitans. The recurring dialectic is familiar: Searle says the stance leaves out the system's own point of view; Dennett replies that the point of view can be fully accounted for by the stance a system takes toward itself and need not be added to the ontology; Searle says Dennett's third-person method misses the first-person facts; Dennett says the first-person facts are themselves stance-relative rather than a separate kind of fact.

Do not rehearse the whole debate or survey the arguments. I want a diagnosis of why it has stalled and what kind of disagreement it really is. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under one name: Searle is asking what a state is in itself, while Dennett is asking what justifies and explains our everyday and scientific practice of ascription, and the single word intentionality is being made to carry both jobs. But I can see rival diagnoses: a disagreement about explanatory standards, about what a philosophical theory of content may legitimately take as primitive; a verbal disagreement over the adjective original; a hidden empirical disagreement about what brains do, translated into metaphysics; or a difference of starting intuitions that neither side can argue for.
