I was up till 2am arguing with a friend about the Gould and Lewontin spandrels critique and now I have to grade this short-answer question for the philosophy of biology class I TA, so I'd rather have a careful read to grade against before I write comments I'll have to defend. The question on the assignment was:

In their critique of the adaptationist programme, what do Gould and Lewontin mean by a spandrel? Use the San Marco example, and explain what they take the existence of spandrels to show about the status of adaptationist explanations.

A student wrote:

In Gould and Lewontin's paper, a spandrel is an architectural left-over: the roughly triangular space you get where a dome sits on four arches. In San Marco, the spandrels were not built to hold mosaics; they are a necessary by-product of the dome-on-arches design, even though they later got decorated. The biological point is that many traits might be like this: side effects of the way an organism is put together, not traits that were directly selected for. That's a challenge to adaptationism because adaptationists tend to treat every trait as if it must be an optimal solution to some problem and then invent a story about what it was selected for. Gould and Lewontin say these stories are largely unfalsifiable, since a clever person can come up with a selective rationale for almost anything. So the spandrel example is meant to show that the adaptationist programme should be abandoned as false, not just that it is overused. They call these structures—byproducts that later turn out to be useful—exaptations.
