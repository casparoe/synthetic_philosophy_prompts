I am taking a high school philosophy class, and a handout from my teacher gave the following summary of Kant's distinction between intuitions and concepts. I suspect that some of it is subtly off, but I do not know enough to locate the errors with confidence.

The handout reads: "For Kant, the mind has two basic capacities. Through sensibility, we passively receive intuitions, which are particular, immediate sense impressions—colors, shapes, sounds—exactly as they are given to us before the mind does any organizing or interpreting. Through the understanding, we actively form concepts, which are general rules or labels for sorting those impressions. Knowledge arises when we bring an intuition under a concept, as when I recognize the brown shape in the yard as a dog. This is what Kant means when he says the two elements must cooperate: an intuition to which no concept has been applied would not amount to knowledge, and a concept with no intuition to apply to would likewise not amount to knowledge. But Kant also thinks the two capacities can operate independently. Bare seeing gives us intuitions without any concepts, and pure thinking gives us concepts without any intuitions. Mathematics is the clearest example of the second kind: 2+2=4 is known by reason alone, with no sensory input and no intuition, which is why Kant treats mathematics as a purely conceptual science."

I am especially suspicious of two parts: the claim that intuitions are given "before the mind does any organizing," and the claim that mathematics is "purely conceptual." For one thing, I have heard that Kant regards mathematics as synthetic a priori and that it depends on pure intuition. For another, I have read that Kant does not identify intuitions with raw sense data, because space and time are themselves forms in which we intuit.

Could you go through the handout claim by claim and say which parts a careful reader of Kant would accept, which parts are common simplifications, and which parts are simply false? After the inaccuracies are corrected, would the overall picture the handout gives still be basically right, or does the correction change what Kant is trying to show?
