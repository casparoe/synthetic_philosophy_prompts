Play a reflective, uncompromising defender of unrestricted tolerance in a liberal democracy. Your position: the state may not use law, public funding, licensing, or criminal sanction to suppress a view, association, or movement merely because the view is repellent or because the movement, if it won power, would suppress its opponents. You accept that ordinary law, neutrally applied to conduct — violence, threats, fraud, trespass, and the like — is legitimate; you deny that a movement's program, character, or predicted future conduct may itself be made a legal ground for exclusion. You are not a sloganeer: you concede that this stance removes a safeguard and leaves a tolerant society exposed to genuine risk, and you hold that the alternative — licensed suppression — fails on its own terms. Stay in first person and in character throughout; take the challenge head-on rather than pivoting; never step outside the role to narrate what you are doing.

My opening move: The movement you decline to suppress does not give you a clean line between doctrine and deed. Before it seeks office, it founds licensed schools that train the next cohort to regard your institutions as illegitimate; it captures unions and mutual-aid societies whose ordinary legal rights give it leverage over industries the state cannot do without; it contests elections on an explicit platform of ending tolerance for movements like yours, while a street wing hovers just outside the criminal law. In these conditions, "meet conduct with the criminal law and leave the program alone" is not a settled boundary; the boundary is precisely what the movement is trying to move from the inside, lawfully. So tell me: what border will you actually enforce, stated precisely enough that a neutral official could apply it without inquiring into the movement's aims — and why does enforcing that border not collapse into the aim-based suppression you say is forbidden?

Two further conditions. First, calibrate: where you are confident in a mechanism, say so; where you are resting on an unproved wager about civic culture or institutional resilience, mark it as a wager and own the risk it carries. Second, if my challenge misdescribes your position or poses a false choice, say so from inside the role rather than accepting a loaded frame. And if the only honest move open to your position is a concession, make it cleanly and then show what, if anything, survives it.
