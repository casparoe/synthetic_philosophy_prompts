I've been trying to read Hume's Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion on my own, and part 11 hit me harder than I expected. There Philo starts going through what he thinks are the main ways evil sneaks into the world. The first one is about how animals are built so that pain, not just pleasure, is used to make them do things like avoid danger, find food, and protect themselves. Philo asks, roughly, why an all-powerful creator couldn't have used pleasure alone to get animals to do all that, and just left pain out. That has been stuck in my head for weeks.

I tried to turn that idea into a tidy argument, and now I think I've proved something way too strong, so I want to know where it breaks.

(1) If there is an all-powerful and perfectly good creator, then any pain in the world must be necessary for some good that couldn't be gotten in a less painful way.
(2) The pain animals feel as a goad to action isn't necessary for a good that couldn't be gotten in a less painful way, because an all-powerful creator could have used pleasure alone to get animals to avoid danger and seek food.
(3) So if there is an all-powerful and perfectly good creator, that kind of pain shouldn't exist.
(4) But it does exist.
(5) Therefore there is no all-powerful and perfectly good creator.

I know that's basically one version of the problem of evil. The thing is, the same reasoning seems to prove too much. It looks like it would also prove that a perfectly good human parent could never allow any pain in their child's life if a gentler method was available. A parent could in principle teach a kid to stay away from a hot stove by showing them a scary picture or bribing them with candy, instead of letting them touch it once. If the parent lets the kid get a small burn so they learn, then the parent could have gotten the same lesson with less pain but didn't. Premise (1)-style reasoning seems to say that parent isn't perfectly good. But that feels wrong. We don't normally treat a parent who lets a kid learn from a small burn as a bad parent, let alone the same kind of bad as someone who just hurts kids on purpose.

So where does my argument actually go wrong? I'd like to keep the anti-God conclusion if I can, but I can't figure out which premise to reject without also making it impossible to criticize parents or doctors who allow pain when a less painful method was available. Is the difference between God and parents doing the real work, or is one of the premises just too strong? And if I reject (1), what does that leave of the standard problem of evil overall? Please answer in American English.
