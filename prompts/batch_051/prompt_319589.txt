I'm a cog sci PhD student and I can't stop chewing on something my roommate says when she's half-joking, which is that she's the only real person and everyone else is basically an NPC. She doesn't endorse it when pushed, but she means something specific: she reports a constant main-character phenomenology, that other people's inner lives seem underdetermined to her, and that she catches herself, especially when tired or stressed, treating people like they're running scripts instead of having a full first-person point of view. I want to drag this out of dorm-room territory. Is there any way to bring evidence to bear on it? The thing is, two claims keep getting tangled: (a) an empirical one about how minds actually represent other people—whether we run something like rich mental-state simulation for everyone by default, or whether we mostly run cheap heuristic NPC-scripts and reserve the full model for ourselves and maybe a few close others—and (b) the solipsistic one about whether other people actually have inner lives. I'd want a test or battery of claim (a) that's concrete enough to run. What would you measure, and what pattern of results would look like 'yes, there's a genuine NPC mode' as opposed to 'no, people model others as full agents by default and the main-character thing is just an asymmetry of access'? In particular, how would you tell the difference between someone who really is doing shallow modeling of others and someone who has rich models of them but just doesn't weight them much in decisions? Design the best test you can, say what result would favor each reading, and then be honest about exactly what a clean positive result would and would not settle about claim (b).
