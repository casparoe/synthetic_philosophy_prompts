I am preparing a discussion exercise for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on eighteenth-century moral psychology, and I would like you to construct a single, realistic scenario to a precise specification. The scenario should be ordinary in its setting—a workplace, a family, a neighborhood, or a small voluntary association—with no exotic dangers and nothing that would turn on looking up the law. One paragraph is enough, but it should make the agent's situation, motive, knowledge, and the actual outcome all explicit.

The specification is this. I want a case in which Adam Smith's impartial spectator would approve of the agent's conduct on the whole, while a version of act-consequentialism that judges only actual consequences would condemn the very same conduct. The case must not get this result by making the consequences depend on improbable long-run effects, by giving the observer information the agent lacked, or by making the agent so admirable that any plausible theory would approve. I want the divergence to turn on what is distinctive in Smith's view: whether an unbiased, well-informed spectator can enter into the agent's motive, and whether that spectator can enter into the gratitude or resentment of the people affected. One further constraint: the person who ends up worse off must be someone whose resentment the impartial spectator would not share—for example, because they knowingly and voluntarily accepted the risk, or because what they have lost is only a benefit to which no one ever gave them a claim. This is meant to force the case through the judgment of propriety and the spectator's refusal to endorse resentment, rather than through the easy point that consequentialism ignores motives.

After the scenario, please work through both sides. For Smith's spectator, separate the judgment of propriety from the judgment of merit or demerit: state the agent's motive, why a spectator could sympathize with it, what each affected party feels, and which of those feelings the spectator can go along with; then explain why the overall verdict is approval even though the actual consequences are worse than an available alternative. For the consequentialist, give the deliberation and confirm that even a careful consequentialist of this type would have to condemn the action. Then identify the single structural feature of the case that is doing the real work, remove or alter that feature and show how the two theories realign, and finish with the strongest rebuttal available to a defender of act-consequentialism—for example, that the spectator, properly applied, would not really approve, or that the case smuggles in a favorable assessment of the motive from some other theory.
