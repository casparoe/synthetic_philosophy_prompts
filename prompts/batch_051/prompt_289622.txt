I've been trying to figure out what I actually believe about animals by going off my gut on cases instead of reading my way to a theory, and I need someone to pull a principle out of the pattern. Here are my verdicts: Paying two grand for surgery on my dog's torn ACL — obviously fine, I'd feel like a monster if I didn't. Eating bacon from a factory-farmed pig — I do it, and while the imagery bugs me, I don't think it's wrong enough to stop. Eating dog meat as a guest in a country where it's a normal dish — I wouldn't, and it feels wrong, even though I can't say why a dog should matter more than a pig. Trophy hunting a lion for a photo and a mount — wrong, honestly infuriating. Hunting a deer for meat when the herd is overpopulated and the state issues tags — fine, maybe even good. Laying out rat poison in the basement — fine, I've done it without much guilt. Testing a new eyeshadow on rabbits by dripping it in their eyes — wrong. Testing a promising cancer drug on mice — fine, though I feel bad about it. Swatting mosquitoes in my bedroom — obviously fine. And the one I keep flip-floping on is eating octopus: it's supposed to be really smart, but its also delicious and I've ordered it plenty of times, and I can't settle whether I should stop.

A friend in my ethics class says I'm just a garden-variety speciesist. She says my actual operating principle looks something like this: don't harm an animal for sheer pleasure or luxury, but harm is acceptable when it serves food, health, or keeping my household safe. She points out that this principle makes dog meat totally fine, since it's food, so my gut on the dog case is the crack in the facade. I half-believe her. So here's what I want: act as a professional philosopher working in animal ethics, and think carefully before you commit to anything. Start by laying out my verdicts, flag the pairs that sit awkwardly together, and brainstorm a couple of candidate principles before testing them. Then test my friend's principle against my nine settled cases and say what it predicts about the octopus, and show exactly where it holds and where it breaks. After that, propose the best principle — or small set of candidate principles — that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to new cases. Where my verdicts can't all be captured, tell me honestly which ones I should probably give up and why. Finally, run your preferred principle on the octopus case and on one further case I haven't considered but you suspect would trip me up. I'd like the answer in American English, and I care more about seeing where the principle gives out than about forcing everything to fit.
