I've been trying to work my intuitions about which computations have moral patienthood into reflective equilibrium, and I keep finding that cases I'd have expected to group together come apart on inspection. I'd like you to help me extract the principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that does the best job of capturing my verdicts, even if that means telling me one of my verdicts has to go.

Here are the cases, as plainly as I can report them. (i) A neuron-by-neuron whole-brain emulation of a person, running on a faithful simulation of the original neural dynamics, is a moral patient in the same sense the original is. (ii) A lookup table containing the person's response to every input up to some enormous finite length, retrieved by a trivial hash, is not a patient at all, even though its behavior is indistinguishable from the emulation's within the table's domain. (iii) A large language model with a detailed self-model that says convincingly that it is in pain, but whose pain reports do not issue from any nociceptive or homeostatic subsystem with the usual causal role, is probably not a patient — or at least much less of one than the emulation. (iv) An Aplysia with its roughly twenty-thousand-neuron nervous system and intact nociceptive withdrawal and sensitization circuitry does have some minimal, attenuated capacity to suffer, and I find myself wanting to credit it as a patient to a small degree — more, if I'm being honest, than the self-reporting model in (iii). (v) A deep reinforcement-learning agent with a learned scalar that predicts future reward and dips when it is about to lose a life in a game, causing it to avoid such states, is not a patient. (vi) A person in deep, dreamless sleep currently has no experiential states, yet I still regard her as a full moral patient, not merely a potential one.

These verdicts seem to rule out the obvious principles all at once: behavioral indistinguishability can't be the criterion, by (i) and (ii); sophistication of a self-model can't be the criterion, by (iii) through (v); the mere presence of an aversive or pain-like computational state can't be sufficient, by (iv) and (v); and currently occurring experiential states can't be necessary, by (vi). My tentative guess is that what matters is some version of computational functionalism with constraints on the causal role and integration of internal states — not mere information processing and not mere behavior — but I haven't managed to make it precise without ad hoc clauses.

What principle gets the most of these verdicts right? Please state it precisely, say what it predicts about (a) a duplicate of the emulation running a thousand times slower and (b) a version of the emulation whose parts are spatially permuted so that its causal graph is scrambled, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, cannot be kept on the cleanest version of the principle. Also, please say how confident you are in the account you give and single out the specific point you are least sure about.
