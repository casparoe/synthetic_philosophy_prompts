I've been in a reading group on constructive mathematics, and we have been working through the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation of the logical constants. After one session, a friend sent me the argument below. He claims it shows that the constructive reading of negation is far stronger than most constructivists admit — strong enough that any decidable property failing to hold universally comes with a built-in counterexample. Each step looks to me like something a careful constructivist might say, yet the conclusion seems to erase the very distinction between a negative claim and a positive existence claim that the whole interpretation was designed to mark. I cannot find the point of failure.

Let α be an arbitrary binary sequence of natural numbers: for each n, it is effectively decidable whether α(n) is 0 or 1. (1) Suppose we have a constructive proof of ¬∀n(α(n) = 0): a proof that not every term of α is zero. (2) On the constructive reading of a negated universal claim, this proof is a construction that converts any alleged proof of ∀n(α(n) = 0) into a proof of a contradiction. (3) Such a construction must exploit some particular term where α takes the value 1; if no term of α were 1, the universal claim would simply be true and there would be nothing for the alleged contradiction to contradict. The proof must therefore carry with it a specific natural number n₀ such that α(n₀) = 1. (4) But supplying such an n₀, together with the decision procedure that establishes α(n₀) = 1, is exactly what a constructive proof of ∃n(α(n) = 1) consists in. (5) Consequently, for every binary sequence α, a constructive proof of ¬∀n(α(n) = 0) yields a constructive proof of ∃n(α(n) = 1). (6) So whenever a decidable property has been constructively shown not to hold of all natural numbers, one is entitled to assert that some definite number witnesses its failure, even though no such number and no bound on where it occurs was part of the original proof. That is classical negation with the constructivist caution removed.

I cannot tell whether I should accept the argument as valid and revise my picture of intuitionistic logic, or whether one of its steps does something the BHK reading does not license. If it fails, I want to know exactly which step fails, why it looks legitimate, and what abandoning it commits me to; if it is valid, I want to know what kind of constructivist I become by accepting its conclusion. Please say how confident you are in the diagnosis and single out the point you are least sure of.
