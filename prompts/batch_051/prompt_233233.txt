I sit on the acquisitions committee of a small provincial museum, and we've hit a genuine interpretive puzzle. Our founding deed (1924) contains this clause, quoted exactly: 'No work shall be admitted to the permanent collection unless it is an original work, executed by the hand of the artist to whom it is attributed and by no other hand. No mechanical reproduction, photograph, or copy, however competent, shall be deemed an original work within the meaning of this Deed.'

We've just been offered a painting by a well-known artist who died in 2019. For the last two years of his life his hands were too unsteady to paint, so he trained a custom robotic arm on a large set of his own brushstrokes and used it to produce a series of canvases. The offered canvas was planned and composed by him, he directed its production more or less stroke-by-stroke, and he rejected eleven earlier versions before approving this one; the arm laid the paint. His studio assistant added the signature to the back at his direction, which the artist always treated as the moment of completion. The estate says the painting is 'by his own hand' in every sense the 1924 clause could have cared about, and that the clause was plainly aimed at forgeries and copies, not at mechanical assistance it could not have imagined.

Does the clause admit the painting? I want the argument from the words, from the purpose, and from what the founders would likely have said if they'd been shown a trained robot arm, plus a verdict on which reading should govern. Format-wise: exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no introduction, no conclusion. Use the first four points for the three readings and the verdict (fold in what a reasonable reader today would make of the clause if you need to), and make the fifth the thing that would change you're mind: the argument or consideration that would flip your verdict, or where you're least sure.
