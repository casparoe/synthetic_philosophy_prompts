Arendt's account of the Eichmann trial argues that the unsettling thing about Eichmann was not that he was a monster with deep, demonic motives, but that he was an ordinary, thoughtless careerist whose crimes were made possible by a bureaucratic division of labour. On her reading, Eichmann's clichés and evasions in Jerusalem were not a clever performance; they expressed a genuine inability to think from another person's standpoint. Later scholarship, however, has drawn on the Sassen interviews — recorded conversations Eichmann held in Argentina before his capture, which he expected to remain private — to challenge this picture. Suppose, for the sake of the question, that the interpretation of those interviews favoured by this later scholarship is right: that Eichmann was privately a settled and devoted antisemite, that he took pride in his role in the deportations, and that he regretted that the murder of Europe's Jews had not been completed. Does this development destroy the banality-of-evil thesis, or does it destroy only one strand of it? In your answer, separate the thesis understood as a psychological claim about Eichmann's private motives from the thesis understood as a claim about the institutional shape of modern genocide, or about the shallow, cliché-bound face that even ideologically committed perpetrators can present. Say exactly which premise of Arendt's argument the new evidence bears on, and whether the thesis can be restated in a form that survives the evidence without becoming vacuous.
