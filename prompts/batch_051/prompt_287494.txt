I've been chewing on a short essay about Hofstadter's idea of the inviolate level in tangled hierarchies, and I'd like some comments on the idea before I invest more time in it. The rough thesis is that the inviolate level is not something you discover about a strange loop but something you choose or impose, and that choice does more explanatory work than people usually admit.

Sketch of the argument: in the standard treatment of Escher's Drawing Hands, the apparent paradox (the two hands draw each other into existance) gets dissolved by pointing to an inviolate level underneath the loop—the paper, the artist who drew the lithograph, the static physical image. The hands only seem to create each other because that lower level does the actual creating. My claim is that in messier cases there isn't one obvious inviolate level. A self-hosting compiler, for instance, has at least the original bootstrap binary, the current source, the language specification, and the hardware as candidates. A legal system has the founding text, the amendment procedure, the latest precedents, and the sitting judges. These candidates can disagree about what the system even says, so picking one as inviolate is a modeling or normative decision, not a factual one. I want to push this toward a claim that in AI safety talk, when people treat a model's reward function or its original training objective as the inviolate ground beneath later self-modification, they're often choosing that level for convenience or control and then describing the system as if it had that ground built in.

My worries are, first, that I might be strawmanning the inviolate level, since maybe it was never meant to be a fact about the system but just a heuristic for explaining away paradox; and second, that the AI safety step is an overreach that dilutes the careful conceptual point. What do you think—is the core distinction worth making, where is the argument weakest, and should I cut the AI safety payoff or keep it? Also, if the essay would work better by zooming in on one concrete case, like the self-hosting compiler, rather than ranging over law and AI, say so.
