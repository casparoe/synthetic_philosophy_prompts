I was rewatching some old Harlem Shake compilations on YouTube with my nephew last week, and it struck me how strange the whole event was. In February 2013, within the space of a few weeks, thousands and thousands of groups — offices, sports teams, university clubs, even military units — filmed themselves acting normally for the first fifteen seconds of the same song, and then, exactly at the bass drop, cutting to the same scene with everyone thrashing around in costumes and throwing props. Then, just as suddenly, it was more or less over. It made me think of an idea I read about years ago: that culture evolves somewhat like a population of organisms. Bits of culture get copied, the copies vary, some variants spread more successfully than others, and a variant that spreads is, in effect, one that is good at getting itself copied.

I want to use the Harlem Shake as a case study for that Darwinian picture of culture — but I do not want the loose treatment that stops at saying memes are a bit like genes. I want you to actually do the disciplined mapping that an evolutionary thinker would be forced to do. What was the population of variants, and what did copying fidelity consist in? What were the main selection pressures, and who or what was doing the selecting?
