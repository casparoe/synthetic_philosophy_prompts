Bit of background: I'm a retired chemist, and for the past year or so I've been working through philosophy on my own, mostly social and political thought. I just finished a popular intellectual history of 19th-century ideas, and its chapter on Marx left me unsure how much to trust. It gives something like the following account of historical materialism. Society, it says, is split into an economic base made up of the forces of production (tools, technology, labor power) and the relations of production (who owns what, class relations). Resting on this base is a superstructure—law, politics, religion, art, philosophy—whose main function is to legitimate and stabilize the base. The book's big claim is that for Marx the base determines the superstructure so thoroughly that, once you know a society's mode of production, you could in principle derive its legal code, its dominant religion, and even its artistic styles from it. It then calls all this a form of technological determinism: change is always driven by improvements in the forces of production, and the superstructure changes only after, and because, the base has already changed. The chapter concludes that historical materialism is refuted by cases where legal forms or cultural habits persist for centuries after the economy has transformed, and by cases where ideas seem to change the economy themselves—its example is the way Protestantism fostered capitalism.

I'm suspicious because this feels to neat, like the book sets up a straw man so it can knock it down, but I can't quite locate the missteps, and I also don't know whether defenders of Marx's view have good answers to the book's objections. Could you go through that summary piece by piece? I'd like an audit of which parts a careful historical materialist would happily accept, which parts they'd wince at as overstatement or conflation, and whether the concluding refutation survives once the accurate parts are recovered. It would also help to know how best to understand the word "determines" here, since in chemistry when A determines B we usually mean a tight, exceptionless causal law, and I suspect something different is going on.
