Please argue for the following position: the question "Why did the Industrial Revolution happen in Britain rather than in China?" is not one well-posed question but a family of distinct questions, and a large part of the apparent intractability of the Great Divergence debate comes from running those questions together.

Your argument should proceed in four steps. First, distinguish at least three precisifications of the contrast: (a) a timing question — why was Britain first among all plausible candidates, including China; (b) a location question — why Britain rather than the Yangtze delta, treating the two as among the most commercialized and densely populated core regions of the eighteenth-century world; and (c) a mechanism question — why Britain rather than a counterfactual China that shared Britain's relevant preconditions. Second, for each precisification, specify what must be held fixed, what would count as a difference-maker, and what kind of evidence could discriminate between candidate causes. Third, show that the precisifications are not interchangeable: a factor can explain one of them while failing to explain another, and they naturally invite different timescales and different kinds of evidence. Fourth, take and defend a position on the stronger claim that, once properly specified, the question "why not China?" is answerable rather than merely a prompt for enumerating cultural or institutional differences. If you think it should be dissolved, state precisely what replacement question or questions you would ask instead.
