I've been developing a distinction I call moral closure versus moral bargaining, and I want it stress-tested. By moral closure I mean the use of a moral judgment to foreclose practical disagreement: to treat the matter as settled, the act as wrong full stop, and further negotiation as inappropriate. By moral bargaining I mean the use of a moralized claim to open or manage renegotiation of the terms of a relationship, with wrongness treated as revisable relative to local roles, relationships, and context.

My hypothesis is this. The well-known cross-cultural differences in folk morality are usually reported as differences in what gets moralized — which acts fall under harm, rights, purity, hierarchy, or the sacred. But the more philosophically important difference may be in the function of moralizing: WEIRD samples look especially objectivist because their moral talk tends toward closure, while in many non-WEIRD samples moral talk functions more as bargaining. If that is right, the folk objectivism measured in WEIRD samples is itself a WEIRD-specific moral convention, and cross-cultural variation in folk morality does not obviously undercut the objectivity of morality — it undercuts one culture's way of using moral talk.

Is the closure/bargaining distinction coherent, or does it collapse into existing contrasts such as moral objectivism versus relativism or the autonomy/community/divinity triad in moral psychology? Does the final inference hold, or does it confusingly mix descriptive moral psychology with metaethics? Where does the distinction break? Give me the strongest counterexamples, including non-WEIRD moralizing that plainly functions as closure and WEIRD moral talk that plainly functions as bargaining. Wherever it breaks, tell me how you would refine the distinction to survive the counterexamples.
