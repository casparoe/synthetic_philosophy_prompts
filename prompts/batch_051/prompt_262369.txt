I am the opinions editor of my high school's student newspaper. Each issue I edit roughly a dozen submissions about school controversies, such as surveillance cameras, honor code revisions, and budget cuts; I have about half an hour per piece, no formal background in philosophy or politics, and the faculty adviser must approve every change I request. Please write me a reusable editorial procedure, grounded in Orwell's view that vague or ready-made political language is not merely ugly but protects weak arguments from inspection by hiding who is doing what to whom, and that the habit of writing in such phrases then makes dishonest thought easier. The procedure should tell me when to flag a passage as language doing the writer's thinking for them and require a rewrite, when to pass it, and when to leave a stylistically dubious passage alone because its abstraction is doing legitimate work. Give me an ordered set of questions to run through, a small number of stock phrases a busy editor can learn to spot, and mark clearly the steps where judgment cannot be eliminated and where the procedure is likely to misfire. Then apply the procedure to three short cases: a student government statement announcing that a new policy will be implemented for the benefit of the entire community; an op-ed opposing that same policy as a violation of our fundamental freedoms; and an editorial by a friend advocating a cause I support that never names a single responsible adult or official and leans on phrases like "the entire student body." Please write the whole thing in continuous prose only, as in a letter or email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
