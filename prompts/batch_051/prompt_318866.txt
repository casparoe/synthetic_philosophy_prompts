I am a retired natural scientist, and nuclear strategy is one of the areas that drew me to philosophy late in life. I have been trying to work out whether my considered judgments about a series of cases express one underlying principle or several incompatible ones, and I would like your help extracting and testing the principle. Please engage the arguments directly rather than through the strategic-studies literature, which I have not read systematically.

Here are my verdicts.

A. Automatic retaliation. A state installs a system that, upon detecting an incoming attack, launches its full arsenal without any human decision being possible. I think this is genuinely stabilizing because it makes retaliation certain and removes any incentive to decapitate the leadership. But I also think the false-alarm risk is grave enough that I would hesitate to deploy it, and on balance I suspect I would not.

B. The reckless commitment. A leader publicly commits the state, in a way that would be costly to undo, to use nuclear weapons in response to even a conventional invasion of its territory. Carrying this out would likely invite nuclear retaliation against the homeland. I nonetheless count the threat as rational and credible: what makes it look reckless is exactly what makes an adversary take it seriously.

C. Secure second strikes. Two adversaries each possess forces that would clearly survive a first strike. I regard this condition as the most dependable source of strategic stability, and I think it makes deliberate war between them much less likely.

D. No first use. A state declares that it will never be the first to use nuclear weapons and configures its forces to survive an attack. I think this is the morally correct posture and that it costs little against a direct nuclear attack, but I worry it substantially weakens the extended deterrent by which the state protects allies from conventional aggression.

E. The limited detonation. Following a conventional skirmish, one side detonates a single low-yield weapon over an uninhabited area to signal resolve. I think authorizing this would be reckless to the point of madness: I do not believe escalation can be held to such a rung, and I would condemn anyone who ordered it.

F. Delegated authority. A state gives field commanders predelegated authority to launch nuclear weapons if communications with the capital are lost. I oppose this: however much it guarantees a response, it multiplies the number of people and failure modes that could produce an unauthorized launch.

My question is this. Is there a single principle about credibility, commitment, and the control of risk that captures all six verdicts? If there is, state it precisely, apply it to two further cases I have not settled, and tell me where it is doing real work. The further cases are: (i) a state that announces a human-in-the-loop policy while in practice operating what is launch-on-warning on very short timelines; and (ii) a cyberattack that leaves a state genuinely unsure whether its nuclear forces are being degraded or struck. If no single principle captures all six, tell me which verdicts must be revised and what the simplest defensible position would be.
