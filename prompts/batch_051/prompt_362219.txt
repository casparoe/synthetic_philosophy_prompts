A puzzle has been structuring my thinking lately. A finite mind seems to have finite resources, yet experience appears permanently open-ended: it always makes sense to imagine a genuinely new color, taste, or mood, and not merely another combination of old ones. The thesis I have been defending, which I call the finite-alphabet view of experience, is that this openness is an illusion. For any given finite mind, there is a fixed and finite stock of simple experiential elements—a phenomenal alphabet—and every experience that mind could undergo is a compounding, blending, or recombination of elements already in that alphabet. The alphabet is not fixed by what the mind has so far encountered or learned to discriminate, but by what the mind is, the way a creature's sensory range and basic forms of feeling are fixed. So the number of possible compounds may be astronomically large, but the supply of elements is closed: a new fruit does not add a new taste to the alphabet; it presents an unused compound of qualities the stock already contained. I take this to explain both why a finite mind never exhausts experience and why radically unprecedented experience is impossible in principle. I know the view is near Hume's claim that simple ideas are copies of impressions, and near Leibniz's insistence that perception cannot be found in the machinery of a brain, but it seems to me stronger than either because it says the supply of simples is closed, not merely that complexes are built from them.

I want to know what in this survives scrutiny before I keep using it. Is the principle coherent as stated, or does it quietly equivocate among "element," "type," and "discriminable difference"? Does something close to it already exist under a better name in discussions of qualia, phenomenal character, or the compositional structure of experience? And where exactly does it break? I can already feel one objection—that elements might be relationally individuated, so a finite alphabet could still yield unbounded novelty—but I cannot tell whether that is a real problem or only a restatement of the view from the outside. Please give me a committed verdict: reconstruct the thesis in its strongest defensible form, name the nearest existing positions, and tell me which objections, if any, are fatal rather than merely costly.
