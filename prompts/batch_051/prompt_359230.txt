Something's been bugging me. In ordinary life we all seem to rank being served as obviously better than serving: given the choice, nearly everyone would rather be the one with the personal assistant than the assistant, the diner than the waiter, the boss than the worker. Being the one waited on is coded as winning. But the Hegelian master–slave dialectic basically says this is inverted. The master wants recognition from the bondsman, but he won't recognize the bondsman as a full self, so the recognition he gets can't mean anything to him — it's like being praised by someone you've already dismissed as not really counting. The bondsman, meanwhile, has to work on the world, and in shaping things he sees his own activity reflected back, which is exactly where Hegel locates durable self-certainty. So the dialectic hands the servant the developmental prize and leaves the master with just flattery. That's the exact opposite of our ordinary preference. So here's my question: is there anything that justifies the everyday preference for the master position, or is it just a collective mistake we've never bothered to correct? If you defend the ordinary ranking, tell me what the master actually gets that the dialectic misses — and defend it aginst the version where the master's entire circle is yes-men and hired assistants, so every piece of 'recognition' he receives is the worthless kind by construction.
