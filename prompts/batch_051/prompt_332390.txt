I have a standing rule in discussion: I treat the word 'true' as doing no more than endorsing or reasserting the proposition it is attached to. When someone says a claim is 'true but not useful', I treat them as having endorsed the claim while declining to act on it; when someone says something is 'true for me', I ask which proposition they are endorsing and about whom; when someone says 'science aims at the truth', I replace the remark with the ordinary success conditions of particular theories. A colleague objects that this deflationary habit is not a neutral piece of bookkeeping: it quietly rules out substantive views on which truth is a genuine property that explains the success of inquiry, or on which some propositions are true only relative to a perspective. Is my habit a defensible default for someone who finds 'truth' doing too much unexplained work, or am I settling by paraphrase what should be settled by argument? If the habit is too strong as it stands, is there a weaker version that keeps its clarifying benefits without begging those questions?
