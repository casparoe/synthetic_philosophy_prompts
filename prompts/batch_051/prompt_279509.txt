I'm a PhD student in moral psychology, and my reading group dug into the guilt/shame literature last week and now I've got an argument that seems to prove way too much. (1) Guilt responds to a particular bad action, while shame responds to a failure of the whole self. (2) Whole-self evaluations rest on a larger, fuzzier evidence base than single-action evaluations, so they're harder to overturn with any one clear counterexample. (3) An evaluation that won't shift when a single clear counterexample shows up is epistemically defective — it's not tracking the evidence, it's just self-image. (4) So shame is, in general, epistemically defective compared to guilt. (5) By the same reasoning, any whole-self evaluation — pride in one's character, self-respect that survives one failure — is epistemically defective. (6) But that can't be right; otherwise someone whose latest data point is a failure would have no rational basis for self-respect. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the claim that guilt is the better moral emotion? I'd be gutted to lose (1), but I don't know what that's actually buying me.
