I write novels that borrow shamelessly from the lives around me, and I've ended up stuck between two things I find convincing. On the one hand, the argument that personal data is property looks powerful: you own yourself and what you do, information about you is a byproduct of what you do, so the records companies assemble about your movements, purchases, habits, and moods are yours — taking them without asking or payment is theft, even if the thing taken is intangible. It's a clean theory with a clean remedy: give people property rights in data about them and let them sell or withhold it. I want it to be true because it would make the data economy legible.

On the other hand, I can't apply it to my own work. I'm a novelist; my stock-in-trade is facts about people that I collected without anything like consent. I notice how a friend is cruel to their wife, a stranger's twitch in a waiting room, the exact was my father seemed smaller the year he got sick, and I turn it into fiction. If data about you is your property because it's a record of your life, then my notebooks are stolen goods, and a huge amount of fiction and memoir is just systematized theft. But my intuition is unshakeable here: you can't steal a fact by learning it. The observation is mine; the knowledge is mine; the person it's about keeps their life but not the ownership of every true thing that can be said about it.

I don't just want to be told that my intuition is a writer's self-interest or that the property theory is corporate apologia. What I want to know is how to negotiate between them. Should the property theory give way when it collides with the freedom to notice and tell, or should my facts-are-free intuition be the thing that's jettisoned — and what would justify dropping one rather than the other? Is there a principled distinction between the continuous, machine-generated record a company builds of me and the particular things a novelist picks up about someone, or is any such line just me carving out an exception so I can keep writing?
