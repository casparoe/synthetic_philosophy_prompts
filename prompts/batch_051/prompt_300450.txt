I'm teaching a final-year undergraduate seminar called Rationality and Its Discontents — 16 students, a mix of philosophy and economics — and we have one 75-minute session on the endowment effect and loss aversion. We've read the usual mug-experiment papers and a couple of the trading-experience studies, but the discussion keeps falling into a stale stand-off: half the room says the gap between willingness-to-pay and willingness-to-accept is just an error, the other half says it's a legitimate preference that ownership creates, and nobody's mind moves.

I want an in-class activity or game that makes them feel the ambiguity before we name it. Ideally it produces, live, a moment where the same person can't tell whether they're being irrational or rationally attached to something they now own, and then a second moment where they can see what would seperate the two readings. Small props are fine — mugs, chocolate, stickers, tokens, cards people can customise — but no real money and nothing that matters if people keep it. The tricky part is that both stories predict the same outward pattern: people ask for more to sell a thing than they'd pay to buy it, so the gap alone can't arbitrate. I'd like the activity to make that point felt rather than announced.

By the end, participants should be able to say under what conditions an endowment effect counts as a bias and under what conditions it counts as a legitimate preference, and why a raw WTA–WTP gap can't settle that on its own.

Give me rules, materials, a timeline, a debrief that maps concrete moments in the activity onto the conceptual distinction, an honest note on what will probably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it, and a frank comment on which part of the distinction the activity can't get them to feel, so I'll have to supply it myself. Please write the answer in British English.
