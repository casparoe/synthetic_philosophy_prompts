I have been trying to settle what I call the ownership question about normative reasons: must every genuine reason be a reason for someone—a particular agent, or at least the occupant of some role—or can considerations count in favor of a response while being no one's reason? I have developed a three-way distinction that I keep using to answer it, and I want a candid assessment of the distinction rather than a survey of the surrounding literature.

The first category is the attached reason: a reason owned by a particular agent in virtue of that agent's commitments, projects, or motivational sensitivities. That I promised to water your plants gives me a reason to water them. The second category is the vacant-role reason: a reason that attaches to a social or institutional position even while the position is unfilled. That the floodgates must be inspected before the rainy season is a reason for whoever is the keeper, and it does not lapse into nothing the moment the post falls vacant. The third category is the free-floating rationale: a consideration that genuinely counts in favor of some response even though there is no actual or possible agent, and no role, whose reason it is. My stock example is the consideration that some past catastrophe ought to have been prevented. It points toward a response—preventing the catastrophe—that no one can now perform, and yet it does not feel like nothing. So I conclude there is a free-floating rationale.

My thesis is that free-floating rationales are real and irreducible, and that many persistent moral puzzles arise because we take a free-floating rationale and pin it, somewhat arbitrarily, to a present agent so that it can enter deliberation. When people argue about historical injustice, for instance, the usual move is to say that "we" today have reason to repair wrongs committed by our predecessors. I suspect the underlying truth is that there is a free-floating rationale—that the wrong be repaired—and that assigning it to "us" is a pragmatic decision about who should take up the task, not a discovery about who already owns the reason. I would say something similar about certain claims concerning future generations or distant strangers: an unowned rationale gets converted into a first-person reason so that deliberation can start.

I want your honest assessment. Is the tripartite distinction coherent, or does the third category collapse into the second once I allow roles to be notional or indefinitely specifiable? Have I only redescribed the familiar contrast between agent-neutral and agent-relative reasons, or the contrast between value and reasons? Does the central thesis fail because "counts in favor" is an action-guiding notion, so a rationale that could not guide any possible agent is not a reason at all but something closer to an evaluation? And if the category survives, does it actually do the explanatory work I attribute to it in the historical-injustice and future-generations cases, or does it merely relocate the puzzle? Give me your strongest reconstruction, and then your sharpest objections.
