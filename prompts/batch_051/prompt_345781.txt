I've been arguing with a friend about whether the famous cases of simultaneous discovery — Newton and Leibniz over the calculus, Darwin and Wallace over natural selection — actually show anything, or whether they are just the vivid anecdotes. My friend insists they support a stronger thesis: that major scientific findings are broadly inevitable, in the sense that if the credited discoverer had never lived, someone else would have arrived at roughly the same discovery within a generation. I think that claim is unfalsifiable, or at least far harder to test than he admits: we only ever observe the history that actually happened, and a few famous double discoveries don't tell us how often discoveries would have been duplicated in worlds where the laureate never existed.

Rather than settle this by intuition, I want to know whether the inevitability claim can be brought into contact with evidence at all. Design the strongest feasible test you can, using records or comparisons that could realistically be assembled — for example, but not limited to, patent and priority disputes, lab notebooks, "near miss" manuscripts, citation and co-citation networks around discoveries, or comparisons across fields and periods with different credit structures. Be concrete about what data you would collect, what patterns would count in favor of the inevitability thesis, and what patterns would count against it. Then be honest about the residue: what would the test fail to settle? I'm particularly interested in whether it can separate the inevitability of a problem being solved from the inevitability of the particular solution, and whether any of this has implications for how we assign credit or whether priority disputes matter. Give me a committed answer: pick the strongest version of the test, defend that choice, and take a definite position on how much it could actually establish, rather than just surveying options.
