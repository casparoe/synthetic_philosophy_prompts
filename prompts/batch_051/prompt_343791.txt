I'm a philosophy professor, and I've been going back and forth with a colleague in statistics about Bertrand's paradox. His line is that the principle of indifference is just like cutting a cake: if there's no reason to make any one slice bigger than another, the only non-arbitrary assignment is equal shares. On his telling, picking one parametrization over another is like favoring a particular knife angle, so the paradox is really about our choice of description, not about the principle. I don't trust the cake picture but I'm having trouble saying exactly why. Audit the analogy for me. What corresponds to what between a partition of possible cases and slices of a cake? Where does the mapping break down when the space is continuous, or when the same set of possibilities can be carved up in rival ways? What does the cake quietly smuggle in about there being a privledged way to cut? And once the disanalogies are on the table, is there any version of the principle of indifference that still survives, or was the cake doing all the work the whole time?
