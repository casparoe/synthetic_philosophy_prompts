I've been working on a piece about people who can still feel pain but no longer find it bad, and it's left me in a bind I can't think my way out of. The cases go under the label pain asymbolia. It shows up after certain kinds of brain damage, and the reports keep sounding the same: a person says yes, that's a sharp, burning pain in my left hand, and they can point to exactly where it is and rate how intense it is, but they don't flinch or pull away, and if you ask whether it's unpleasant they say no, not really. Some of them laugh through stimuli that would be excruciating for you or me. They don't ask for painkillers — not from toughness, but because there's nothing that needs relieving; the 'badness' is just gone. I find it almost impossible to look at these patients and say they aren't feeling pain.

But the account of pain I'm otherwise drawn to pushes me very close to saying exactly that. On this view, the felt character of pain isn't a neutral sensory signal that we then evaluate as bad; the badness is built into the feeling itself. To have a pain just is for some part of your body to feel wrong in a way that demands you do something about it. That's why pain hurts, why it compels attention, why a 'pain that doesn't hurt' sounds like a contradiction in terms. It's a simple, unified picture, and it makes the ethical significance of pain automatic instead of something bolted on after the fact. If badness were a separate layer, it starts to seem mysterious why it's glued so reliably to burns and broken bones.

So I've got a theory with real virtues on one side and a firm intuition about clinical cases on the other, and they seem to contradict each other. I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what each move would cost. What would it take to debunk the intuition — to argue, convincingly, that the asymbolia patient isn't really in pain, or that their reports can't be trusted? What would it take to revise the theory instead — say, by splitting pain into an informational channel and a separate 'this is bad' response that normally come fused but can come apart? And is there a reading on which both survive, where everyday 'pain' just names a package that's usually glued together, and the theory was only ever about the package? Tell me which yeild is justified under which conditions — I'm willing to give up either one, but only if I understand what I'm paying for it.
