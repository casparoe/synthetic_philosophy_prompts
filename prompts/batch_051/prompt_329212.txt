Write a dialogue between two philosophers debating whether the absurd—the permanent disproportion between the seriousness with which human beings invest in their projects and the silence of the universe about whether any of it matters—counts against the possibility of a meaningful life. One is an absurdist in roughly Camus's sense: she thinks the demand for cosmic ratification can never be met, and that the honest response is neither despair nor a leap that pretends the demand has been answered, but a clear-eyed revolt that keeps valuing the world without illusion. The other defends a fitting-fulfillment account of meaning in life: a life is meaningful, on her view, not when the universe signs off on it but when a person is actively engaged in projects and relationships that are worthy of engagement and suited to her particular abilities, with objective worth and subjective attraction doing independent work. Have them probe the weakest points in each other's positions rather than trade set-piece speeches. The meaning theorist should press the absurdist on whether a life of clear-eyed revolt is not itself a paradigm of fitting fulfillment—a gripping project that exercises the agent's powers and is, by the absurdist's own lights, worthier than self-deception or resignation—and, if so, whether the absurdist has conceded that meaning in life is possible once cosmic endorsement is abandoned. The absurdist should press the meaning theorist on whether objective worth can be made sense of without the very cosmic backing she has renounced: if worth is settled only by human standards, and those standards evolve, conflict, and have historically been made to ratify cruelty, then fitting fulfillment may be only satisfied engagement described in grander language, unable to explain why someone absorbed in a sophisticated but wicked project lives meaninglessly rather than merely badly. The two should be equally intelligent and charitable, each granting what is strongest in the other's view and responding to the actual arguments the other gives. The dialogue may end in a sharpened disagreement rather than a reconciliation.

Write the dialogue as continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold type, and no tables. Identify the speakers within your sentences, for example by writing that one of them replied in a certain way, rather than presenting isolated lines under names.
