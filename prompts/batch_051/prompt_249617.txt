In a working group on institutional design, someone kept saying a constitution is basically Escher's Drawing Hands: the document creates the offices that later amend the document, so legal authority is a self-drawing loop, and hunting for a basic norm or a sovereign is pointless. They treated the drawing as doing the entire argument. I want an audit of how far that analogy can actually carry that conclusion. Map the correspondences: what plays the two hands, the act of drawing, and the loop; then ask what corresponds to the inviolate level—the paper, the pens, the artist—which in the drawing is not itself drawn by the hands. Where does the mapping break? Is there something in a constitutional order that plays that un-drawn role (social practice, acceptance, coercion, something like a rule of recognition), and does the claim that there's no external ground collapse once you notice the drawing itself sits on exactly that kind of substrate? Tell me which disanalogies are cosmetic and which do the real work for the argument, and whether the drawing is a better analogy once the inviolate level is kept explicit or should just be replaced.
