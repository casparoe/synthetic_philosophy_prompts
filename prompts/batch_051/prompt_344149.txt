I have been thinking about Edwin Abbott's Flatland alongside an old question in the philosophy of mathematics: what makes something a legitimate mathematical object rather than a device, a metaphor, or a useful fiction? The history of geometry puts the question under pressure. The received picture of Greek geometry takes the straightedge and compass as the standard of legitimate construction: whatever could not be produced by a finite sequence of such operations was not really an object of geometry. In the seventeenth century Descartes greatly widened the canon by admitting algebraic curves, which are described by polynomial equations, while still treating 'mechanical' curves such as the spiral and the quadratrix as lying beyond geometry's borders. A little later the calculus made it natural to treat the cycloid, the catenary, and the logarithmic curve as perfectly genuine mathematical objects even though they are transcendental, not captured by any polynomial equation. Each stage of loosening suggests that the boundary of legitimate mathematical existence tracks what a community is willing to count as constructed, written down, or put to work, rather than some fixed criterion.

I would like a critic's reading of Flatland through this lens. The Sphere is not constructible from within the plane, yet it convinces A Square of its existence by passing through Flatland as a circle that grows and then shrinks; and having insisted that the third dimension is real, the Sphere then balks at A Square's suggestion of a fourth dimension on closely similar grounds. The dreams of Lineland and Pointland rehearse the same pattern: a one-dimensional king cannot conceive of sideways motion, and a point cannot conceive of any being besides itself. Read the book as a dramatisation of the question of what epistemic authority entitles one to extend the ontology of mathematics: where does the parallel with the historical loosening of construction standards genuinely illuminate the story; where does it strain, since Abbott's explicit targets are Victorian social hierarchy, religious orthodoxy, and the limits of the knowable rather than mathematical method; and what does the fiction add to the philosophical debate that a list of historical cases would not? Treat the work faithfully in both directions: don't reduce the novel to an illustration of a thesis, and don't stretch the mathematics to make the novel more orderly than it is. The honest critic should say where the reading is imposed on the text rather than found in it.
