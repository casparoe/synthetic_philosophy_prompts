I am a product lead at a midsized consumer-software company. Every two weeks a review group — engineers, designers, product managers, one lawyer, and me — approves, rejects, or forces a redesign on proposed features: default settings, notification cadence, data-sharing defaults, recommendation and ranking logic. These features reach millions of users, and reversal after launch is costly and public. We keep hitting the same unresolved question: when does "would it be a problem if everyone did this?" actually count as an argument against a feature, and when is it a fallacy or irrelevant?

Please write a reusable decision procedure for that question, grounded in the idea of universalizability as it runs from Kant through R. M. Hare and Marcus Singer, and be explicit about the differences among the following rather than merging them: (1) Kant's contradiction-in-conception test, in which a maxim cannot even be conceived as a universal law because the practice it depends on would be undermined if everyone followed it; (2) Kant's contradiction-in-will test, in which the maxim can be universalized coherently but the agent could not rationally will the world that results; (3) Hare's prescriptivist version, in which a moral judgment is a prescription the speaker must be prepared to accept in all relevantly similar cases, so the test is partly about whether the speaker can sincerely prescribe the universalized judgment given their actual preferences, and not only about logical consistency; and (4) Singer's generalization argument, which asks whether the consequences of everyone doing the action would be undesirable, while taking seriously threshold cases where a few people doing it is harmless but many is not, and the objection that one person's doing it makes no difference.

The procedure has to be usable by people who have never read any of these authors. It should run in under thirty minutes for a single proposal in our review meeting, and for each step it should state the question, what counts as a sufficient answer, and which of the four traditions the step is meant to capture. Mark the steps where judgment cannot be eliminated, and say where the procedure should stop with a no, where it should demand a specific redesign, and where it must escalate to our separate ethics working group because the issue is a matter of values rather than analysis. Rejections and forced redesigns have to be signed off by the head of product and legal.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals we have actually faced: (a) making telemetry-on the default with the opt-out buried in settings, to improve reliability and product decisions; (b) a feed-ranking model that maximizes watch time and, as a side effect, surfaces increasingly emotionally intense content for each user; (c) putting account deletion behind a multi-step confirmation with a delay and a closing survey.

Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer the team will not like, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot settle.

Finally, for the response as a whole, say how confident you are in the claims you make and single out the specific point you are least sure of.
