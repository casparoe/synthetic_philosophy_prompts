I run a small reading group on "the mind beyond the brain" for people who have never taken a cognitive science course, and I want to end the unit by giving everyone a take-home sorting quiz: a "Which position on the extended mind do you actually hold?" instrument in the style of those personality quizzes people actually enjoy. The point is to sort readers by how they respond to concrete cases, not by which jargon label sounds good to them, and I want the whole thing organized around one particular passage rather than my own paraphrase. The passage is from Andy Clark and David Chalmers, "The Extended Mind" (1998):

"In these cases, the human organism is linked with an external entity in a two-way interaction, creating a coupled system that can be seen as a cognitive system in its own right. All the components in the system play an active causal role, and they jointly govern behavior in the same sort of way that cognition usually does. If we remove the external component the system's behavioral competence will drop, just as it would if we removed part of its brain. Our thesis is that this sort of coupled process counts equally well as a cognitive process, whether or not it is wholly in the head."

Please act as an expert philosopher of cognitive science and build the quiz. Start by brainstorming candidate diagnostic cases — pen-and-paper long multiplication, Tetris-style rotation with a button, Otto's notebook versus Inga's memory, a smartphone navigation app, a hearing aid tuned to the wearer's auditory profile, a reference book someone consults once a year, a "hire a passerby as a calculator" scenario, plus two or three of your own — and then pick the eight to ten that most sharply force readers to reveal what they accept in that passage. Each question should present a concrete scenario with three answer options that correspond to different underlying commitments; no question should simply ask whether the reader "believes in the extended mind." Then provide a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto at least these leaves, described so a student need not know the literature labels: (i) parity-based active externalism in Clark and Chalmers's sense, where an external item counts if it would count as cognitive were it in the head; (ii) complementary integrationism, where external elements earn their cognitive role by doing work unlike the brain's, often through reciprocal loops of self-regulation; (iii) embedded cognition, which treats tools as potent scaffolds while keeping the cognitive process inside the organism; (iv) intracranialism, the default view that the machinery of cognition is spatially inside the organism; (v) a coupling-constitution skeptic who grants the coupling but denies that a notebook-like item carries original, non-derived content of the sort cognition requires, along the lines Adams and Aizawa press; and (vi) an enactivist or anti-representationalist position that drops the information-processing idiom altogether. Build the instrument from the logical space rather than from the names: the distinctions the questions should independently probe are (a) parity versus complementary role for the external element; (b) whether the subject is the organism-plus-artifact system or the organism that uses an artifact; (c) how much Clark and Chalmers's four criteria for Otto's notebook — constant presence in his life, direct availability, automatic endorsement upon retrieval, past conscious endorsement — each matter, and whether failing one or two of them is disqualifying; and (d) whether any contribution to behavioral competence suffices, or only a contribution to distinctively cognitive processing, since accepting everything with a causal role seems to court wildly bloated cognition. For combinations of answers that no one defends, briefly diagnose whether that corner of the space is empty because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag which leaves the instrument cannot cleanly separate — I suspect embedded cognition and complementary integrationism will blur on several cases, and that parity-based externalism and integrationism may blur wherever a tool is both functionally similar and deeply complementary — and propose one specific repair for each blind spot, such as adding or rewording a question. The finished thing should include a short introduction for the reader, the questions with their options, the scoring rules, and the brief diagnostic note about unoccupied combinations and inseparable leaves. Write it in polished American English, at a level a smart undergraduate who has never read the debate can follow.
