I have no academic background in philosophy or pest management—just someone who has thought about this while dealing with insects in my house every summer. I have come up with a principle I call "the ladder of intrusion," and I want an honest assessment of it: whether it is coherent, whether it is already worked out elsewhere under some other name, and where it fails. I would rather be told it is old or wrong than be flattered.

The ladder is an ordering of responses to unwanted insects:

1. Exclusion: screens, sealing cracks, keeping attractants cleaned up or sealed away.
2. Deterrence: repellents and making the area inhospitable to the insect.
3. Targeted killing: swatting, traps, or destroying a single located nest.
4. Targeted population control: baits or traps built for one species.
5. Broad poison: sprays, foggers, and dusts that kill whatever contacts them.

My claim is that you may move up a rung only after the lower rungs have been tried in good faith or can be shown to be infeasible at reasonable cost, and that each higher rung requires a stronger justification than the one below it. I do not think this is merely a practical rule about avoiding resistance or wasted money, though it is that too. I think it tracks a real moral structure: an insect's life and its suffering are not worth nothing, so methods that kill many to remove one, or that kill bystanders as a side effect, are morally worse than methods that kill only the offending individual. I think the same point applies to the bystander species—spiders, predatory beetles, small parasitic wasps—that are often the only reason the pest has not already taken over, and that broad poison kills as collateral.

An example: for ants in a kitchen, I think you owe them the attempt at finding the entry point and cleaning up the attractant before you go to a spray. But I can already see a tension: if mosquitoes are a disease vector, waiting at the lower rungs means accepting a real risk to human beings, and that seems to justify jumping straight to poison. I also do not know how the ladder handles numbers—does a hundred ants under the sink license a rung that one ant would not, and if so, where is the threshold?

2. What it gets right (about 200 words). Where does it capture something real about the ethics of pest control?
3. Nearest existing ideas (about 200 words). In plain language and without citations, has this basically been worked out already, and how close is my version?
4. Where it breaks (about 300 words). Give me the hardest cases and most serious objections. At least test it against disease vectors, agriculture, structural pests like termites, and the numbers problem above.
5. Verdict (about 150 words). Is the ladder worth keeping in anything like its current form, and if so, what revision saves it? If not, what narrower truth is left?
