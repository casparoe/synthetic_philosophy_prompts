At my school's math club, two members argued about a thought experiment our faculty advisor had posed. Twenty strangers are each alone in a room with a button they may press exactly once; all are ideal reasoners, and this is common knowledge among them. If exactly one of the twenty presses, that person wins $1,000; if none press or more than one press, nobody wins anything. Daniel said he would not press. (1) He argued that pressing pays off only if he happens to be the sole presser, and since he cannot influence the nineteen strangers, he would almost certainly be throwing his press away: either someone else presses, in which case his own press spoils that person's win and gains him nothing, or nobody else presses, in which case his winning is just luck. (2) Priya replied that if all twenty reasoned as Daniel did, nobody would press, and the group would forfeit a prize that at least one of them could have collected; the only way anyone ever wins is if someone actually presses. (3) Daniel said that the observation that somebody should press is not a strategy a single player can carry out, because he acts as one person, not as the group. (4) Priya said Daniel was ignoring the symmetry of the players: if everyone pressed with probability one-twentieth, the chance that exactly one person pressed would be as high as possible, and that is the only strategy all twenty could rationally share. (5) Daniel answered that her proposed rule is not a reason he could act on, since if the other nineteen really pressed at that rate, his best move would be to press for certain, and that would undo the very arrangement she describes. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: in exactly five numbered points, one for each move above, rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and give a one- or two-sentence justification, with no introduction or conclusion.
