Since I retired from a long career in physical chemistry, I've been working through some political philosophy, and there is one tangle I keep coming back to in the literature on federalism and subsidiarity. As I understand it, subsidiarity is not actually a decentralising principle: it says a power should sit at the lowest level that can exercise it effectively, which can point upwards when lower levels cannot manage the problem on their own. By contrast, the old laboratories-of-democracy idea in American federalism often seems to want to keep authority down even when lower levels aren't fully solving the problem, because variety itself generates information about what works. Those are two different logics — one about effective achievement of a goal, the other about the epistemic payoff of variation — and I keep failing to build a case where they visibly come apart.

Here is what I'd like you to produce. Construct a thought experiment in which subsidiarity and the laboratories-of-democracy rationale recommend opposite levels of government, with subsidiarity favouring the higher level and laboratories favouring the lower level. Constraints: an ordinary policy area (something like a pollutant or a product that crosses borders, not a constitutional crisis); no longer than one paragraph; and the upward push must come from a genuine coordination problem rather than from the lower units being corrupt or incompetent, because then everone would agree and the case would prove nothing. Make both sides look reasonable rather than straw-manning one of them. After the scenario, walk through what each principle says about it and why, identify which feature of the case is doing the work, and say what happens if that feature is removed — do the two principles collapse back into agreement, or does a deeper disagreement remain? Finish by saying how a defender of whichever principle comes out looking worse would attack the case.

This is for a short piece I'm writing on the difference between the two ideas, so please don't just stipulate the verdicts; they have to fall out of the principles themselves applied to the sceenario.
