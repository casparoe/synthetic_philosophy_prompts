Everyone in my department has taken to saying that Frege's political diary bears on his philosophy the way Newton's alchemy bears on his mechanics: not at all, except to biographers. The thought is that the diary is private prejudice and his logic is public proof, and the two are adjacent at best. We've been using this to justify teaching Begriffsschrift and Grundlagen without ever mentioning the diary, and to treat the question whether Frege's politics infects his logic as settled: it doesn't.

I want you to audit the analogy before I put it in print. The diary is from 1924, the last year of his life, and wasn't published until long after. It's ugly: anti-Semitic, anti-democratic, contemptuous of parliament, and full of longing for a strong leader and a national rebirth. The philosophy people actually use, though, is from decades earlier and is all about objective thoughts, anti-psychologism, the third realm, and the strict separation of logic from psychology. So the analogy has real pull: a private notebook full of awful politics on one side, a public body of work about timeless logical truths on the other.

Map it out properly. What plays the role of the alchemy, the mechanics, the private notebook, the public proof, and the fact that Newton kept them in separate rooms? Then tell me where it breaks. In particular, does the alchemy analogy silently import the idea that the private pursuit was a different topic, that it didn't share the public work's conceptual vocabulary, and that the mature physics shows no trace of it? Is any of that true in Frege's case, given that both his logic and his politics seem to orbit a horror of subjectivism and disorder? And in the end does the conclusion survive — that we can teach and use Frege's logic without asking about the diary — or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? If there's a better analogy, give it.

Also tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would get you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
