I've got a friend, let's call her Priya, who's a philosopher working on conceptual ethics — the part of philosophy that asks when we should fix or engineer our concepts and when we should just analyse the ones we've got. She's got a very consistent-looking track record when I talk to her about cases, and I want to know whether I can predict her on a new one. I'm less interested in the bare answer than in the rule she's running.

Here are her verdicts, roughly as she gives them:

- On woman (the social category): she says obviously we should fix it. The ordinary concept was built around a sexist picture, and the job is to build a better concept for feminist purposes, not just catalog what people already mean.
- On race: same direction. The folk categories don't correspond to any biological reality and their history is awful, so the task is to design the concept we should want, not analyse the one we have.
- On marriage: fix it too. It's a legal-social invention, and once we saw the old definition was doing an injustice we should have rewritten it. Treating it as if it had a hidden essence was the mistake.
- On electron: she says don't be silly. That's physics' term for a real kind, and we should let physicists discover what electrons are like. Stipulating a friendlier definition would be ridiculous.
- On species: analyse it, leave it to systematic biology. It's messy and there are several species concepts knocking around, but there's a real phenomenon under the mess that biologists are trying to get right, so we shouldn't gerrymander it to make biodiversity policy easier.
- On knowledge: analyse it. Even though the post-Gettier tangle shows our everyday category is a mess, epistemologists are tracking a real thing — knowledge is to be discovered, not legislated.

The new case: she's writing a piece on whether we should revise the concept of obesity, the one used in medicine, public health, and insurance, and I want to guess her view before I read it. Background: obesity is used for practical decisions all over the place (who gets treatment, whether it counts as a disease, what insurance covers, public-health guidance), but it's also the name of a real metabolic/physiological condition, even if the BMI cutoffs are pretty arbitrary. Current usage also has real stigma effects.

You're a professional philosopher who works on conceptual engineering and ameliorative analysis. Before answering, brainstorm the candidate rules that fit Priya's earlier verdicts. Then say: what each candidate rule would predict about the obesity case, which rule you think is actually driving her, what your best guess is for her eventual verdict, and wich further case would cleanly tell the candidate rules apart.
