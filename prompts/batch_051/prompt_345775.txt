Milton Friedman’s so-called F-twist holds that the realism of a theory’s assumptions is irrelevant to its acceptance: what matters is predictive success, and adding realistic detail can be a vice if it brings complication without improving predictions. Assess whether that position survives the spread of large-scale computational modeling—for example, agent-based macroeconomic models and high-resolution climate models—where researchers now routinely replace patently false simplifications with highly realistic assumptions and run simulations at scale. Does the F-twist still offer a useful principle for choosing among models, or has the changed cost of realistic assumptions turned it into an ex post rationalization for tractability? If it fails, state what methodological maxim should replace it, and whether Friedman’s underlying point about testing by predictions can be saved in a weaker form.
