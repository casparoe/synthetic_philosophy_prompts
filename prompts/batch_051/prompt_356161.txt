Here's a mechanism I've been kicking around, maybe you can stress-test it. A company that farms crickets for protein, under pressure from its retailers about insect welfare, sets up a "sentience escrow." For every cricket it slaughters, it pays one hundredth of a cent into an escrow account (so a billion crickets a year means $100,000 sitting there). Every three years, an independant panel of entomologists, philosophers, and welfare scientists is convened; each member privately writes down their subjective probability that a cricket is sentient, and the median is p. The company then pays p times the escrow to an insect-welfare charity chosen at the start of the cycle, and it gets the remaining (1 − p) back. Don't tell me whether this is a good idea in the abstract—walk me through how it actually behaves. What does it incentivize for the company, the charity, and the panel? Where does it get gamed first, and are those failure modes fixable? I'm especially interested in how this interacts with the scale argument about insect sentience: the per-cricket fee is tiny but the numbers are huge, so a very small p over a very large N can still move real money, and I want to know whether the expected-value structure is doing the philosophical work or just hiding it. And if you think the whole setup is confused—say, that sentience isn't the kind of thing a panel can put a subjective probability on, or that picking a median and multiplying is just a way of making up numbers without earning the right to use them—say so plainly instead of humoring me.
