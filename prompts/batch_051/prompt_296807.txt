Here's an asymmetry I can't get to sit still. A landlord who refuses to rent to someone because their credit score is 580, or because they smoke, gets called prudent; a landlord who refuses to rent to someone because they are Black, Muslim, or a wheelchair user gets called a bigot and, under American fair-housing law, a lawbreaker. Most people hold both verdicts firmly at once. Both sets of traits are, for any given applicant, only loosly connected to what the landlord actually cares about (rent gets paid, no damage, no noise), and both could be turned into actuarial arguments if you went looking. So what makes one set of traits 'suspect' and the other set a normal part of screening? The standard candidates all look shaky. Immutability: religion is changeable and protected, while smoking and a bad credit score are changeable and unprotected. Relevance: a credit score is often genuinely predictive, but the law's ban on suspect grounds is not 'discriminate only on irrelevant traits' — it rules some grounds out even where a case-specific rationale exists. Group subordination: that explains how the list got started, but not why it should have the shape it does, and it offers no verdict on newly important traits. I want the strongest principled justification you can build for the protected/unprotected split, tested against three cases: refusing to hire someone for a very low IQ score in a warehouse job; refusing to rent to someone with an eviction record; and charging someone higher insurance premiums because of a genetic marker that raises disease risk. At the end, tell me whether this is a split we should keep, redraw, or give up.
