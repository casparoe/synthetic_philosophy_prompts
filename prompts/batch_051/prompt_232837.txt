Here's my working definition of a genuine scientific explanation, with four conditions:

1. Derivation: the explanation lets you derive or predict the phenomenon it explains.
2. Compression: the explanation is shorter than a full description of the phenomenon; it saves you from just listing the data.
3. Mechanism/cause: it picks out an underlying mechanism, cause, or structure, rather than just restating what happened.
4. Why-answering: it answers a why-question, not just a what-will-happen-next-question.

Now the borderline case. Consider a cellular automaton on a grid, specified by a one-line update rule (think Rule-30-ish, but the exact rule doesn't matter). Empirically it produces a complicated, seemingly random pattern. Here's the kicker: all the usual shortcut tools (closed forms, statistical summarys, coarse-graining) fail, and to know the state at step 10^9 the only method anyone knows is to run the rule for a billion steps. The going term for this in the Wolfram-sphere is computational irreducibility. On the face of it, the one-line rule nails condition 1 (it generates the state) and maybe condition 3 (the rule is the mechanism), but it fails condition 2 as badly as anything can: the simulation is exactly as long as the phenomenon, so there's no compression.

So here's what I want to know. Is the one-line rule an explanation of the state at step 10^9? Go through my four conditions one by one and say exactly where the case lands. Then tell me whether this case shows that condition 2 is too strict, or that conditions 3 and 4 are the ones doing the real work. Is there a sensible distinction between explaining a system (the rule) and explaining a particular fact about that system (the state at step 10^9)? Or should I just bite the bullet and say some truths are generatable but not explainable, and that's fine?
