Analyze Willy Brandt's gesture at the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising monument on December 7, 1970 as a case study in Karl Jaspers's account of German guilt. Brandt, who had spent the Nazi years in exile and actively resisted the regime, came to Warsaw as West German Chancellor to sign a treaty accepting the Oder–Neisse border. After laying a wreath at the monument, he unexpectedly knelt and remained silent for about thirty seconds. He later said: "At the abyss of German history and under the weight of millions of murdered people, I did what humans do when speech fails them." Read this episode through Jaspers's distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt from *The Question of German Guilt*. Which of these forms, if any, does Brandt's gesture acknowledge or perform, and which does it leave untouched? Can the act be coherently interpreted as an acknowledgment of collective guilt without attributing to Brandt a guilt he personally did not bear? Does the episode support Jaspers's claim that recognizing guilt is a precondition for moral and political rebirth, or does it instead expose tensions in his categories—between state, people, and individual; between responsibility and solidarity; between guilt one can expiate and guilt one can only bear? In your analysis, run the case in both directions: what Jaspers's framework explains about the gesture and its reception, and what the gesture reveals about the limits or ambiguities of the framework itself.
