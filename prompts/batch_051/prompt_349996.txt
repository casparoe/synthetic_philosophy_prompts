Consider a national system of universities caught in a grade-inflation race: each knows that holding grades down will disadvantage its own graduates in the job market, yet all claim they would prefer lower, more informative grades. The universities adopt the following mechanism. Each deposits an annual bond with a jointly run clearinghouse. A university may award grades above a negotiated baseline distribution only if it declares an "inflation allowance" for the year and pays a fee, proportional to the grade points above baseline it awards per graduating class, into a fund distributed among the universities that did not declare. A university that inflates without declaring forfeits its bond to the same fund. The clearinghouse publishes who declared, but not the fee payments. Walk through what this does to grading behaviour, to which institutions declare, to who ends up receiving the fund, and to the informational value of grades. Then identify where it is gamed first—renegotiating the baseline, relabeling high grades as honours, outsourcing grading, recruiting weaker cohorts so inflated grades look honest—and what happens if most institutions declare at once. Commit to a definite verdict on whether the scheme ends up as a coordination device, a cartel, or a tax on honesty, and say whether its worst failure can be fixed without giving the clearinghouse control of the entire grade distribution.
