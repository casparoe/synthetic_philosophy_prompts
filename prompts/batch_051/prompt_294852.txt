Something's been bugging me about how we treat photos and videos now, and I want you to pull apart the assumptions underneath it. Here's the thing. My phone's camera now quietly tags pictures in the metadata as AI-edited, and social platforms put labels on at least some photorealistic AI videos in the feed. But everyone involved admits these labels mostly catch the stuff that's self-disclosed or that a detector happens to flag — a competent fake with no telltale artifacts just sails through unlabeled. At the same time, a ton of ordinary institutions still treat photos and videos as a default backstop: the insurance adjuster takes a dashcam clip and a timestamped photo as basically showing what happened unless someone raises a fuss, and in small-claims disputes a screenshot or a security camera still gets handled as good-enough proof on its face. No provenance check, no cryptographic chain, nobody asking what app the file passed through. So one part of the world is acting like images are untrustworthy until they're vouched for, and another part is acting like they're trustworthy until they're challenged. What picture of the evidential status of images — or of testimony, or memory — does each practice presuppose? Are they strictly inconsistent, or is one practice the genuine rule and the other a stopgap that hasn't caught up? And if we really updated the backstop practices to match the labeling world's implicit epistemology, what would have to change in evidence law, insurance, or journalism? Reconstruct the implicit theory and say where it's philosophically underdetermined.
