I have been trying to decide how much of my career and savings to direct at the far future rather than the present, and the simulation argument keeps unsettling the long-termist expected-value reasoning I am otherwise drawn to. The correction I keep reaching for is something I call the simulation discount. The rule: for any action whose expected value comes mostly from consequences more than a few centuries out, first estimate that value as if we inhabit the one base reality, then multiply it by your credence that we are not living in an ancestor simulation. The rationale is that if we are simulated, the far future is not robustly ours to shape: the simulators may halt, rewind, or intervene; the morally decisive stakes may lie with the simulating civilization rather than with our simulated history; and an agent trying to steer its own simulated far future may be acting inside a process whose continuation is decided by purposes set elsewhere. If the discount is right, it caps the astronomically-wasteful style of reasoning that would sacrifice present goods for a huge downstream future, because the size of the downstream stakes does nothing to raise the probability that we are in base reality.
