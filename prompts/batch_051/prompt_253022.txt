I'm on the board of a small co-op — a community kitchen — that runs itself through monthly membership meetings. Over the past year the chair has pushed through a bunch of changes, each one passed by a clean majority vote and each one justified as just making things run better: agenda-setting now goes through a three-person committee he appoints, debate time is capped, the chair can call snap votes, two oversight roles got merged, minutes are less detailed than they used to be. None of it violates the bylaws, nobody's been barred from running or speaking, and honestly the meetings are faster and less shouty. But a few of us feel like the character of the place has changed: less real contestation, fewer proposals from the floor, more people showing up to vote yes and go home.

One friend who studies politics says this is the exact pattern you see in places like Hungary and Poland — a leader using legal, incremental, boringly procedural moves to hollow out the opposition, with critics told they're just sore losers. Another friend says that comparison is ridiculous and a little offensive: we're a voluntary club with real votes and no way to coerce anyone, and slapping a big scary label on ordinary majority rule is precisely why these words stop meaning anything. I can see both points, and its making me not know what to do with our actual situation. Are these two people describing the same phenomenon or two different ones? Is there a principled difference between ordinary majoritarian decision-making and the slow-motion erosion my first friend is worried about, and does that whole idea even apply to a private association rather than a state? And practically: what would we have to see next for the worry to be justified rather than just a mood?
