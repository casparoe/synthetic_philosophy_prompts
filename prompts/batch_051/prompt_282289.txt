In Alan Turing’s “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” (1950) he writes: “The conditions of our game make these disabilities irrelevant. The ‘witnesses’ can brag, if they consider it advisable, as much as they please about their charms, strength or heroism, but the interrogator cannot demand practical demonstrations.”

I have been struck by a possible parallel between Turing’s move here and the current dispute over whether AI has already become transformative. Turing insists that the imitation game should not penalise a machine for lacking human-like physical or aesthetic capacities: the test deliberately screens off corporeal accomplishments because they do not bear on the question he is replacing. The analogous move in the transformative-AI debate would be to say that whether AI has already happened is a question about a certain kind of underlying capability, and that its failure to produce, say, fully automated firms, visible macroeconomic discontinuities, or obvious scientific breakthroughs is like a machine’s inability to shine in a beauty contest—significant in many ways, but not the criterion for the thing in question.

But I am uncertain whether this is a deep structural parallel or a misleading one. Turing was dealing with a capacity term, “thinking,” which he suspected could not be defined by survey or anatomy. “Transformative,” by contrast, seems already to be an effect term: AI is transformative because it transforms something—production, labour, research, institutions, the distribution of power. If transformative AI is defined partly by its macro-level effects, then bracketing those effects may not be an application of Turing’s method; it may be changing the subject. I am also unsure how a Turing-style test for transformative AI would work. Could one design an interrogator-like protocol that reliably asks for the right kind of capabilities while screening off real-world impact, and would passing it be enough to say that transformative AI has arrived?

I would like you to assess this parallel: is it genuinely illuminating about how the concept of transformative AI is being used, or does it confuse a capacity question with a historical-impact question? Say what a defender of the analogy would need to show, and whether such a test has an analogue to Turing’s edge condition—where the interrogator cannot demand that the machine do things that are irrelevant to the target concept.
