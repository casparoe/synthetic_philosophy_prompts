I keep having the same argument about weakness of will and I want to get clearer on what people are even saying. Case in point: my friend's roommate has "quit" smoking about six times this year and always relapses, and when I said the pattern shows she doesn't actually want to quit, my friend shrugged and hit me with "no one does wrong willingly," as if that closed the case. I'm pretty sure that one-liner could be any of about five different claims, from the boring to the insane, and that he only needed one of them to do his argumentative work.

Can you unpack "no one does wrong willingly" for me in the context of self-control and akrasia? I'd like a menu of the distinct precise theses someone might actually mean by it. Things I'm especially curious about: whether it says the smoker never really judged quitting to be best (so there's no conflict, just ignorance or a miscalculation); whether it's compatible with her present self genuinely wanting the cigarette more at the moment she lights it thanks to hyperbolic discounting or whatever; whether "willingly" should be read as intentionally, as "against her better judgment," or as reflectively endorsed; and whether the whole thing might be a claim about the self being divided rather than one unified preference ordering. For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, substantive but defensible, or just false. And for each, tell me whether it actually gets my friend from "she keeps relapsing" to whatever he wanted it to prove — that she does want to quit, or isn't really blameworthy, or something like that.

A menu rather than a single settled answer, please — and don't worry about polish, I'd rather have the readings cleanly seperated than smooth.
