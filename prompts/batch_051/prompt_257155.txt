I am a retired chemist who came late to political philosophy, and one image keeps recurring in the Confucian and conservative writings I have been reading: the polity as a family writ large, with the ruler as parent, citizens as children, social order as household harmony, and dissent as a kind of filial disobedience. The analogy is not decorative; it is used to support the conclusion that a well-ordered state prizes harmony under shared authority over adversarial pluralism, and that open political conflict is a symptom of disorder rather than a condition of freedom. I would like a systematic audit of this analogy. Please set out the correspondence explicitly, identifying what maps onto the parent, the children, the household, the discipline, the possibility of leaving home, and the quarrels among siblings. Then show where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that are merely incidental. Finally, say whether the conclusion about the priority of harmony over pluralism survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some other analogy would carry whatever is sound in the family picture without importing what is unsound. Please answer in American English.
