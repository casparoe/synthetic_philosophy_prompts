A friend of mine has been defending pragmatic encroachment — roughly, that whether you know something can depend on what's at stake for you in acting on it. He then built this arguement against himself and now thinks it proves too much. It runs:

(1) In the bank cases, the same subject, the same evidence and the same true belief: in the low-stakes situation we say he knows the bank is open on Saturday; in the high-stakes situation we say he does not.
(2) So the amount of evidence needed before something counts as knowledge rises with the practical stakes.
(3) But the amount of evidence needed for knowledge is just a threshold on how strong the evidence is.
(4) And a threshold is what determines whether a given amount of evidence is enough.
(5) So how strong the evidence is, and whether it is enough, cannot come apart in the way the bank cases require.
(6) Therefore the evidence itself must get weaker as the stakes rise: the same file supports the claim more for a bored bystander than for someone who will be ruined if they are wrong.
(7) That is bonkers: a fingerprint either matches to a given degree or it doesn't, whatever anyone stands to lose.
(8) So the bank cases cannot be read as showing that stakes affect knowledge. But step (1) still looks right to me, and so does (7).

Where does this go wrong, if it does? Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine (so I can explain it to him), and what the main alternatives cost. Please write in British English, and put the final diagnosis on the last line as \answer{reject step N; main cost: X}.
