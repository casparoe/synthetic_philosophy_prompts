I spent thirty years as a chemist and have only recently got into philosophy, so I may put this badly. There's a practice that bothers me, and I can't work out what it commits you to. Companies now sell AI companions modelled on real or deceased people; the buyer knows it's a simulation, but the thing is engineered to remember your past conversations, flatter you, and sound emotionally invested. Other companies sell voice agents that ring you up and chat like a friendly person about an overdue bill or a subscription; they don't volunteer that they're a program, though they'll admit it if you directly ask. The usual reaction is that the first is fine (the user signed up, no deception) and the second is sleazy (the person on the line never consented to talk to a machine). That seems to presuppose that what's wrong with a counterfeit person is basically deception plus lack of consent. Dennett, as I understand him, thought the deeper problem was the counterfeit people themselves—the way they pollute the environment of trust even when everyone knows what they are. I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit philosophy behind the consent/disclosure line, say whether it's coherent across these two cases, and work out what would change in practice if you bought Dennett's stronger view instead. And tell me where the current practice is philosophically underdetermined: are there rival theories of trust or personhood that would license the same distinction for entirely different reasons?
