I run an after-school philosophy of biology society at my sixth-form college, with between fourteen and eighteen members aged sixteen to eighteen, and I have a single ninety-minute session available. I would like a runnable activity — a game or structured simulation — that makes the members feel the force of the handicap principle before it is named: the idea that a signal can reliably advertise an underlying quality only because it is costly to produce, and that when the same signal becomes cheap for everyone it stops carrying information. I want the game to push students towards a subtler point as well: the relevant cost is differential. A signal is informative when it costs a low-quality signaller more to produce than a high-quality one, not merely when it is wasteful in absolute terms.

The students should arrive at these points through the incentives of the game, not through any opening talk, and the phrase handicap principle should not appear until the debrief. Please provide the complete design: the rules, any materials a student facilitator could realistically obtain, a payoff scheme that uses points rather than real money, and a schedule that fits ninety minutes including setup, play, and debrief. The game should involve several rounds so that the group can watch cheap signals get ignored or faked before some players switch to costlier, more credible ones, and it should be runnable by me rather than by a teacher.

Then supply a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the relevant distinctions: what quality is being advertised, why a cheap signal would be faked, what makes a signal costlier for a low-quality signaller than for a high-quality one, and why the same display becomes meaningless when its cost falls for everyone. Also tell me what is most likely to go wrong the first time I run the activity — for example, students treating it as pure bargaining, colluding, or refusing to pay the cost even when the incentives say they should — and how I should respond without turning the session into a lecture.

Finally, please state plainly which feature of the handicap principle an artificial classroom game cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator in the debrief. Please write the answer in British English.
