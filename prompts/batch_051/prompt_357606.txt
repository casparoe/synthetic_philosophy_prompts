Consider a proposed rent-stabilization ordinance whose only unusual feature is a succession rule. A landlord may increase the rent for a continuing tenant at most once a year, by no more than the prior twelve-month increase in the city's consumer price index. If the unit becomes vacant, the landlord may reset the rent to the market level. Under the succession rule, however, a sitting tenant may transfer the tenancy to any adult who will occupy the unit as a primary residence—no family or household relationship to the departing tenant is required. The transferee steps into the tenancy with the existing rent, the same CPI cap on future increases, and the same power to transfer the tenancy in turn. The landlord may refuse a proposed transfer only on grounds that would justify evicting the sitting tenant at that moment. Payments between departing tenant and transferee are neither required nor forbidden, and there is no cap on them. Landlords remain bound by the city's ordinary habitability and maintenance standards.

Treat this as an exercise in institutional design rather than a debate about the merits of rent control in general. Spell out the incentives the succession rule creates for sitting tenants, prospective transferees, landlords, and developers of new rental housing. In particular, explain who captures the value of the gap between the controlled rent and the market rent, how that value comes to be priced, what happens to tenant mobility and to the composition of people who hold stabilized units over time, how landlords will try to recover the ability to reset rents, and what the rule does to maintenance, tenant screening, and the construction and pricing of new rental units. Identify where the system will be gamed first, and say which failure modes, if any, could be fixed by amendment without destroying the transfer right itself.
