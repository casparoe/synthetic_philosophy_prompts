Suppose that in 1815, while the Congress of Vienna is still sitting, a small institute in Geneva publishes a method for predicting large-scale historical developments — wars, revolutions, the rise and fall of economic systems — over century-long horizons, and the method actually works. It's not prophecy: more like a compact law of history, and over the next decades its predictions keep coming true. Give me your best argued prediction for how 1815–1914 plays out differently from the world we know. I want a chain of mechanisms, not a story: what do states, revolutionary movements, and investors do once they know they have a working forecast, and under what constraints? Which of the law's predictions turn out self-fulfilling, which self-defeating, and which hold regardless of what anyone does with them? Then use all of that to take a side on Popper's claim in The Poverty of Historicism that laws of history are impossible — partly becase you can't predict the growth of knowledge (if you could predict today what you'll know tomorrow, you'd know it today), and partly because a forecast is itself an event that feeds back into the thing forecast. Does my imagined institute refute him, or did the mechanisms you just gave show that the scenario is internally unstable? Mark each link in your reasoning as high confidence, medium, or a guess, and say what would have to be different for your overall verdict to flip.
