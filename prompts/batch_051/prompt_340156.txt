I've noticed that I have a standing rule for sorting anything to do with numbers into one of two mental boxes. In the first box I put what I think of as real mathematics: results that come with a proof, a derivation, or at least an explicit chain of reasoning. In the second box I put counting, measuring land, keeping accounts, tax tables, and other numerical craft, which I treat as technique rather than mathematics. The rule feels prudent to me: proof is what keeps a numerical claim from being only a lucky claim. But the rule also does quiet work, because anything I drop into the second box loses a kind of dignity in my eyes, and I stop asking what its makers actually understood.

Recently I ran into a case that strains the rule. I was reading about a Babylonian clay tablet from around 1800 BCE, apparently called Plimpton 322, that contains a long list of Pythagorean triples—whole numbers satisfying a² + b² = c²—arranged in a way that suggests the writer had a systematic method for producing them rather than just recording measurements. There is no proof on the tablet, and as far as I can tell that culture did not produce the axiomatic demonstrations the Greeks later did. By my rule the tablet should go straight into the not-mathematics box. But that bothers me, and I cannot tell whether the rule is protecting me from romanticizing old number tables or whether it is just a Greek-shaped bias about what gets to count.

I am not a mathematician or historian, just someone trying to hold this private filter honestly, and I do not want a reading list; I want to know whether the filter itself is defensible. Am I using proof as a reasonable epistemic shortcut, or as a shield for one tradition? Is there a repaired version of the rule—for example, requiring not proof but evidence of a general method, or distinguishing practical arithmetic from systematic numerical knowledge—that would keep the benefits without dismissing what the Babylonians were doing?
