So I build community features for a living and I've been stuck speccing a karma system for an internal developer forum. Every design decision I look at seems to be quietly committing to a whole philosophy of what reputation even is, and I don't have the vocabulary to check that. Could you act as a professional philosopher working on reputation, trust, credibility — whatever the right subfield is — and think through this carefully? Maybe start by brainstorming a few candidate theories before you settle.

The cases bugging me:

Reddit karma accumulates forever, can't be spent, and apart from subreddit-specific filters an account with 500k comment karma has the same site-wide powers as one with 50. Hacker News karma is similar, except it gates downvoting, so past approval buys you a vote you only get after building up enough karma. Stack Overflow is different again: you earn reputation through peer approval of your answers, you can spend it on bounties, and high rep unlocks actual moderation tools. Then there's Uber/Lyft, where a driver below roughly 4.6 stars gets deactivated, so a 4-star ride is effectively a failure. And eBay feedback used to be two-way, but since 2008 sellers can't leave negative or neutral feedback for buyers.

On paper all of these are reputation systems, but they seem to be making different bets about wether reputation is a currency, a permanent ledger of past contribution, an approval signal, or just a binary acceptable/not-acceptable filter. What theory of reputation does each one presuppose? Are they genuinely inconsistent or just optimized for different problems? Where does a design strictly require its theory, and where would it run just as well under a different one? If you swapped the underlying theory between two of these systems, what would suprisingly have to change? I want the implicit philosophy, not product advice.
