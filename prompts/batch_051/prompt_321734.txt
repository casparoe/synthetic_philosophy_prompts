So I've been trying to explain al-Ghazālī's occasionalism to a poetry friend and I keep tripping over the same thing. Write me a ghazal about it—the actual verse form, not just a poem about the man, though the pun on his name is hard to ignore and you can let it hover. The doctrine the poem should nail is this: when cotton touches fire, the fire doesn't cause the burning; God creates the burning at that moment, and the fact that the two always show up together is just God's habit, one He could break. Let the form do the philosophical work too: a ghazal's couplets are supposed to be self-contained, each able to stand alone, and that should mirror the idea that each event exists only because God creates it directly, seperately, right now; the recurring refrain at the end of each couplet should be the one thing that persists across them, like the only genuine cause in the whole picture. No more than ten couplets. Someone who knows al-Ghazālī should recognize the view and not wince, and someone who doesn't should still end up with a real poem rather than a lecture.
