I'm a philosophy grad student, and for a methodology seminar I have to write up the working commitments I actually rely on when I do philosophy — what I treat as evidence, what I allow to override what, etc. I've typed them up below, but before I present them I want a consistency audit, because I honestly can't tell whether this is a stable position or five things that each sounds right in isolation and quietly fight each other.

You're a professional philosopher working on the epistemology of intuitions and reflective equilibrium. Spend a bit of time brainstorming the possible fault lines first, then tell me which ones survive scrutiny. And please write your answer in American English.

Here's where I've landed:

(1) Intuitive verdicts about concrete cases are the basic evidence in philosophy, the closest analogue to observation reports. If a view misclassifies a clear-cut case, that's a cost, full stop.

(2) But I don't give every case intuition a veto. I think a strikingly simple principle can be worth keeping even when it gets two or three exotic cases wrong; I'd rather pay that price then patch the principle with epicycles. So simplicity can override a small amount of case-based evidence.

(3) I also think case intuitions are contaminated in a deep way — by framing effects, order effects, and the theoretical and moral baggage the person already carries. "I just see that this case has property P" strikes me as much less like a meter reading than people pretend. But I don't think this gives us anywhere cleaner to stand.

(4) I think rationality requires transitivity of all-things-considered verdicts: if you judge A better than B and B better than C, you're committed to judging A better than C. A method on which pairwise case judgments can form a cycle isn't an interesting discovery; it's a broken method. But I also don't have a principled rule for which pairwise judgment to throw out when a series of clear-cut cases cycles.

(5) I don't think simplicity is a reliable guide to truth. It seems to track familiarity, aesthetic taste, and what our training has made salient; I see no reason the normative order (or the world) should be simple. Yet I notice that in actual practice I reach for simplicity constantly, not just as a tiebreaker.

So: which pairs of these are genuinely in tension, and which only look that way? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost. If some apparent tension dissolves, say why. I'm not looking for the lecture version of "all of these are fine with enough nuance" — I want to know what has to give.
