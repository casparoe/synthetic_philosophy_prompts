I work in regulatory compliance for a mid-sized chemical manufacturer. Every week or two we have to classify a new or reformulated material under dangerous-goods rules whose legal triggers are written in the language of dispositions: a substance is 'liable to' ignite, 'capable of' detonation, 'tending to' corrode steel, 'may' polymerize. Getting it wrong in one direction risks a fire in a container or a prosecution; in the other, we pay to ship as fully regulated a material that could have moved under a lighter class, with costs and delays that matter. The official tests are single, fixed stimulations — one flash test, one shock test, one packed-sample heating test — but our materials contain stabilizers, inhibitors, water of crystallization, deliberate particle-size grades, and packaging atmospheres that can make the same chemistry pass or fail depending on how the stimulus is applied. So I regularly face two kinds of mismatch: a material can fail the test only because something present is blocking what it would otherwise do, and a material can pass the test only because the official test itself keeps the dangerous conditions from arising. I want a reusable decision procedure my team can run in about half a day per material, before the dangerous goods safety adviser signs off the classification, that decides when a dispositional predicate in the rules applies despite a misleading test result, and when it does not. The procedure should be grounded in the conceptual distinctions philosophers of dispositions have worked out — in particular, that a disposition can be present but masked, its manifestation blocked by a protective condition; and that a disposition can be absent because something acts as a fink, removing the disposition at the moment its usual trigger occurs — but the analysts running it will not read the metaphysics and should not need to. Test results count as evidence, but the decision cannot be 'run the regulation's test and follow the number,' because the fixed test conditions are exactly what the masks and finks exploit. Write the procedure as an ordered set of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a 'no,' and where it must hand the decision to the safety adviser because the question left is one of engineering judgment or risk tolerance rather than fact. Flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a monomer that is violently 'liable to polymerize' when unstabilized, which we ship with an inhibitor that is slowly consumed and not guaranteed to survive a long container voyage; (2) a metal powder that shows no self-heating in the official packed-sample test but self-heats when sieved to the fine dust that ordinary transport vibration produces; and (3) a hydrated oxidizing solid that tests non-oxidizing, though warehouse roof temperatures can drive off the water of crystallization and leave the anhydrous form that burns on contact. At least one of these should return an answer the company will not like; say which one and why. End by saying explicitly what the procedure cannot settle and where it will systematically misfire, given the gap between a rule written as a dispositional predicate and the underlying structure of dispositions, finks, and masks.
