I'm a retired chemist who got into philosophy a couple years back, and the word I keep failing to pin down is exploitation. My lab instinct says a term doing this much work ought to have sharp edges, like a decent instrument, but the way philosophers use it keeps slipping out of my hands. So: I want you to propose a working definition, run it against every case below, and where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, either revise it or defend the surprising verdict.

Cases it should count as exploitation (or tell me why one of them shouldn't count):

- A Dhaka garment factory making sneakers for a big Western brand. Seventy-hour weeks, fire doors locked, pay around twice what comparable local factory jobs pay. Workers take the job because it beats rice farming or the alternatives; if the brand pulled its orders, they'd be substantially worse off.
- A pharma company runs a Phase III trial for a hypertension drug in rural Uganda. Participants get the drug free during the trial, then lose access because they can't afford the market price. The company wins FDA approval partly on that data.
- An undocumented woman does full-time home care for an elderly stroke patient under the table at well below minimum wage. The patient knows her status and knows she can't report the pay without risking deportation.

Cases it should count as not exploitation:

- Your neighbor watches your dog while you're at a conference; you bring her back a nice gift, though she'd have done it for free.
- A local astronomy club fixes a school's telescope at no charge because they want kids to get into science.
- A worker-owned bakery pays its manager three times what it pays its bakers, under a pay structure everyone voted on.

Now the ones I genuinely don't know how to sort, and where I'd like the definition to force a verdict rather than just list factors:

- After a hurricane, a gas station triples the price of bottled water from $2 to $6. Customers are glad to get it; some poorer families now can't afford as much.
- A firm is the only significant employer in a rural county. It pays below what identical workers earn in the nearest city, but well above what those workers earned farming before the plant opened, and moving is costly enough that most people wont.
- A broke college student sells plasma twice a week for $30 per visit to a company that resells it for much more.
- A landlord raises a long-time tenant's rent 40 percent in a year to match what new tenants would pay. The tenant is a retired mail carrier and can't really afford either the new rent or the cost of moving.
- A popular streamer pays a clip editor $200 a month for what is clearly many hours of work. The editor takes it because the job has already landed him two better-paying gigs.

I have a hunch that the ordinary word is trying to do two jobs at once: one descriptive (someone is being used as a resource, or there's a power difference being used) and one moral (the stronger party is getting something they shouldn't). If you think that's right, give me the two definitions and tell me which of my cases change columns between them. If you think it's wrong, give me the single definition doing all the work. What I'm after is the definition itself, sharp enough to settle the borderline cases.
