You are a philosopher of physics who works on the history of atomism and on ways of operationalizing apparently metaphysical concepts. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming several possible operationalizations before settling on the best one.

I was rereading Lucretius, *De Rerum Natura*, in William Ellery Leonard’s translation, and this passage stuck with me:

“This ultimate stock we have devised to name  
Procreant atoms, matter, seeds of things,  
Or primal bodies, as primal to the world.”

Around this point Lucretius treats solidity, or impenetrability, as the mark of the primal bodies: bodies resist because they contain no void, while void is that which offers no resistance. But I cannot tell whether “solid” is doing empirical work or is being partly stipulated into the concept of an atom. I want to know how one would test the claim that the ultimate constituents of matter are impenetrable in a way that macroscopic objects are not. I do not mean the modern chemistry result that atoms resist compression in a limited sense; I mean the conceptual claim that impenetrability is the right primitive distinction between body and void.

Please design the strongest concrete test or set of observations that could bear on this claim. For each possible result, say which reading it would favor—a substantive physical hypothesis about the world’s basic constituents, or a partly stipulated conceptual framework for modeling matter. Then be explicit about what a positive result would and would not show. Could any test distinguish “we have discovered that the ultimate things are impenetrable” from “we have chosen to model matter by treating impenetrability as an axiom”? If not, what would that residual gap tell us about the status of ancient atomism as a physical theory rather than a conceptual scheme?
