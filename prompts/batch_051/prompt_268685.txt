Ive been going back and forth with a friend about whole-brain emulation, and I keep using a distinction I invented that I cant quite get rid of. I call it benchmark fidelity vs biographical fidelity. Benchmark fidelity is what most arguments seem to actually be about: the emulation, running in a simulated body or a robot, passes the same external tests the original would pass — conversation, memory recall, problem solving, the usual stuff — and once it passes all of those, people move on to asking whether thats enough. Biographical fidelity is stronger and, I think, weirder: it means the emulation, if you ran it forward from the instant of scanning, would make the same choices the original would have made, including the private choices no benchmark could ever probe — whether it would have lied to its partner that Thursday, finally finished the book it kept claiming it would finish, flinched at exactly the same offhand joke.

I think these two can come apart sharply: an emulation could ace every benchmark while diverging on exactly the choices that made the person who they were. And I think the identity question really comes down to biographical fidelity, not benchmark fidelity. But then Im stuck, because biographical fidelity is only checkable by someone who already knows the originals future choices, which makes it circular or practically empty. Worse, after the scan the original and the emulation have diverging lives anyway, so "the choices the original would have made" has to mean something counterfactual, and I'm not sure it does.

So my question: is this a genuine distinction, or am I just relabeling something standard? Does the is-it-me question reduce to it? And where exactly does it break?
