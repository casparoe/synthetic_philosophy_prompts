I run an undergraduate epistemology reading group—not mostly philosophy majors, about 14 people, an hour and twenty minutes. I want a classroom activity that makes them feel the pull of knowing something for its own sake and then makes them suspicious of that feeling, without me lecturing up front. By the end they should see why it is genuinely hard to tell whether that pull is curiosity, a compulsion to close an open question, or the appeal of looking intellectually curious.

Design a game with real small stakes. Each student starts with, say, ten tokens they can exchange at the end for snacks or a small amount of extra credit. Over four or five rounds they can spend tokens to learn facts that are genuinely useless: how many beans are in a sealed jar that will be destroyed after class, which of two titles an author considered for a novel she abandoned, how a coin landed in another room and will never be checked by anyone. The facts should have no practical, social, or emotional payoff. The choice has to be real, not a survey about hypothetical scenarios.

Lay out the rounds, rules, materials, payoffs, exact wording of the choices, and a timeline. Partway through, include a twist: some choices are public (hands up) and some are private (on slips), so we can see whether people spend more when they are being watched, without telling them in advance what that condition is for.

Then write the debrief. It should map what actually happened to the live philosophical possibilities: knowledge is valuable for its own sake; curiosity is just an itch to close an open question and buying the answer is scratching it; people are partly signaling that they are intellectually virtuous. Please seperate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present one interpretation of what the game shows as the consensus. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (for example, people not caring about the tokens, or trying to reverse-engineer the game) and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in discussion.
