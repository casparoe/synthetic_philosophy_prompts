Last semester I taught our department's transition-to-proof course for the first time, and I could not stop noticing the split between students who produced formally sound proofs but seemed untouched by any sense of why the material mattered, and students whose conceptual grasp and applications were strong but who could not sustain a rigorous argument. I now sit on the curriculum committee, where a senior colleague has circulated a proposal aimed squarely at that split. Her mechanism is this: in the next iteration of the course, assessable work would be divided equally into two streams — 'rigor tasks' (formal definitions, proofs, and proof-critique exercises) and 'motivation tasks' (conceptual explanations, worked applications, and short reflective pieces on why a definition succeeds or fails) — and a student's final grade would be the lower of the two stream marks, not their average. Her argument is that averaging lets a deficiency in one dimension be masked by strength in the other, while taking the minimum forces students to keep both alive.

The committee is intrigued but wary, and I have been asked to circulate a written analysis of how the rule would actually behave before we discuss it further. Please treat it as a mechanism rather than as a judgment about teaching philosophy: what would it incentivize, for students, instructors, and graders, across the semester? Where would it be gamed or quietly distorted — I already suspect the motivation tasks could degrade into box-ticking, or that graders could start inflating whichever stream becomes the effective bottleneck — and are those failure modes fixable without turning the rule back into plain averaging?
