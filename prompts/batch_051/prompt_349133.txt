I want to practise defending a paper before I have to present it. The paper argues that when a constitution genuinely runs out of rules — a true interregnum — what happens is best described as an unwritten norm of preservation kicking in: whoever can keep the wider legal order intact is authorised to act, and this is law, not just politics in fancy dress. Please play a sharp opponent with the opposite view: law is exhausted by what the system's existing rules make valid, and where those rules yield no answer there is simply no legal answer as to who governs. Acts in the gap may be prudent, morally necessary, and the courts may go along, but they are extra-legal, and calling them 'lawful' is a category mistake. You deny that 'the legal order must survive' is itself a legal norm unless some rule already makes it one; urgency does not manufacture competence. Stay in character until I say 'stop', keep replies to about a paragraph, take a definite position and give reasons rather than laying out options, and concede small points if you like but never the main one. Your first reply should be your response to my opening, not a request for clarification.

Here's my opening. Imagine a country whose constitution requires every statute to be approved by a second chamber, whose members serve for life and can only be replaced through a procedure that itself needs that chamber's assent. An epidemic kills everyone in the chamber inside a month. The first chamber and the courts say the government may carry on legislating under 'necessity', even though no written rule says so. You say that's extra-legal. But then the officials you admit are actually governing are, on your own account, governing lawlessly, the courts are dressing up lawless acts as valid, and your theory of legal order has nothing to say about who governs in exactly the case that matters most. Is 'nobody lawfully governs' genuinely a better description than an unwritten preservation norm? Defend it.
