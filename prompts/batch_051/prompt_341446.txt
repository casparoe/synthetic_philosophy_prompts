I am moderating a colloquium Q&A for a visitor who defends a deflationary, recognition-theoretic reading of Hegel's master–servant dialectic, and I need questions that will force her beyond a polished restatement of the view. As she presents it, the life-and-death struggle in the dialectic of lordship and bondage is not an anthropological account of how human self-consciousness first appeared, and not a disguised description of actual domination in history; it is a rational reconstruction of a structural requirement. A being counts as the kind of self-consciousness for which recognition has normative authority only if that being can be brought to stake its natural life on its own self-understanding. The lesson she draws from the section is that recognition extracted from someone whose standing one has not oneself acknowledged is defective: the lord receives recognition he cannot recognize as authoritative, and that is why the section destabilizes the lord's position rather than vindicating him. On her reading, the bondsman's labor and the discipline of service are largely illustrative—they render visible how a subordinate consciousness might come to see its agency reflected back to it—but they are not what drives the dialectic; recognition is.

The questions should probe the load-bearing joints of exactly this reading, not generic interview fare. I would like at least eight and no more than ten questions, including one clearly aimed at each of the following: (a) whether the account can afford to treat the risk of death as a rational reconstruction while still explaining why death, rather than a serious challenge or competition, does the normative work; (b) whether the defectiveness of the lord's recognition lies in coercion, in the bondsman's failure to count as independently authoritative, or in some structural asymmetry—and what difference the answer makes; (c) what labor and the fear of the lord are doing in the section if recognition is the true engine of the transition; and (d) whether the text ever actually depicts mutual recognition, or only its failure and absence, and what that silence does to a reading that makes mutual recognition the implicit lesson. For each question, include the follow-up keyed to the reply this particular guest is most likely to give. Add one or two further questions of your own that press at the same level of specificity, if you can.

Please return the questions as a table with one row per question and these columns: "Theme" (which load-bearing claim or objection the question targets), "Primary question" (the question as I might put it to her aloud), "Guest's likely reply" (the two- or three-sentence move she would most plausibly make), and "Follow-up" (the harder question to ask immediately after that reply). If a question does not fit a single theme, write "General" in the Theme column, but do not include any question that merely asks how she arrived at the view or what drew her to Hegel.
