I am a novelist, and I want to settle a disagreement with another writer about whether a published work of fiction can itself perform a speech act, rather than merely depict or imitate one. My working criteria for a genuine apology are these four: (1) the act is done through a publicly recognizable conventional form, such as saying "I apologize"; (2) it is addressed by the right party to the person wronged, in circumstances where uptake is possible; (3) the speaker is sincere in the regret the form expresses; and (4) the speaker accepts what the act commits him to — acknowledging responsibility and offering to make amends.

Consider this case. A novelist publishes a book consisting of a single sentence on an otherwise blank page: "I am sorry, Mother." The book is labeled and sold as a novel. The author is genuinely remorseful about a particular wrong, intends the sentence to reach his mother as an apology, and she reads it and understands that he means it as one; but she also knows the sentence has been published as fiction, and the author has said in interviews that the book is a novel and not a letter.

Does the novelist apologize to his mother? Work through the four criteria one by one. Where the verdict is no or indeterminate, say whether that is because of the criteria or because of the case, and whether the case shows that one of my criteria should be revised or dropped.

Answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: one item per criterion, and a final item giving your overall verdict, how confident you are in it, and the single point you are least sure of.
