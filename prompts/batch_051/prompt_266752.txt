I’m trying to figure out when it’s acceptable to take someone else’s word for a moral conclusion instead of working through the considerations yourself. I have a rough sense that the answer has something to do with whether the other person has better access to the non-moral facts, or to a kind of moral perception I lack, but I can’t make that precise. Here are some cases I’ve collected, with my gut reactions. (a) A colleague from an underrepresented group tells me a remark I made at a meeting was patronizing. I don’t yet see why, but I accept that it was wrong and apologize. Fine. (b) A friend who is much better than I am at math tells me that a proposed cost-benefit analysis of a school funding formula is morally flawed because it treats a child’s educational gains as having diminishing marginal value. I accept his conclusion, though I can’t explain it afterward. Fine. (c) A neighbor tells me I have a moral obligation to donate a specific portion of my income to an overseas deworming charity, and I do it after only hearing his five-minute argument, which I don’t really follow. Fine, but I feel uneasy. (d) A priest tells a nonreligious friend of mine that in vitro fertilization is wrong because of the destruction of embryos; my friend adopts the conclusion because the priest is a good person, not because he shares the theology. Not fine. (e) A juror in a self-defense case thinks the defendant should be acquitted, but another juror says the moral thing to do is vote guilty anyway because the defendant, while technically justified, acted viciously. The first juror votes guilty on the other juror’s say-so. Not fine—it seems like a failure to own one’s decision, even if the other juror might be right.

Is there a single principle that captures exactly these verdicts, or close? I’m especially puzzled by (a) and (c): both involve trusting someone who claims superior moral or factual insight, but one feels clearly fine and the other only half-fine. If you have to choose between a complex principle that saves all five verdicts and a simpler one that overturns one, which should win and why? Also apply your favored principle to a sixth case: a hospital ethics committee member accepts the committee’s consensus about withdrawing treatment even though she hasn’t studied the bioethics literature and cannot identify which of two arguments was decisive. Please respond in American English as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) your best candidate principle; (2) how it handles the five cases; (3) what it says about the hard pair (a) and (c); (4) whether sacrificing one of my verdicts gives a much simpler principle; and (5) its verdict on the sixth case.
