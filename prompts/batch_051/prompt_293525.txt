I'm working on a longread about why people who are obviously well-informed still seem to rationalize away uncomfortable evidence, and a philosopher friend pointed me to Peirce's "The Fixation of Belief." This line keeps rattling around in my head: "The object of reasoning is to find out, from the consideration of what we already know, something else which we do not know." But here is where I get stuck, because I also think some pretty standard epistemology stuff. I think knowledge is closed under entailment: if I know that the local plant is hemlock, and I know that hemlock is poisonous, then I can come to know the plant is poisonous by deduction. And I think that if I really know something, then I know that any evidence against it would be misleading — so, e.g., if I know my friend is in London because I just talked to her from a London landline, then a text saying "I'm not in London" is evidence I should disregard, since it's more likely spoofed than that my knowledge is wrong. But I also think rational inquiry can't just be a defense mechanism: you have to be open to evidence, and that's what Peirce's line seems to say. And yet there are clearly cases where a scientist who knows a result should not take seriously a fringe counterexample. So I want a consistency check: do (a) closure, (b) knowledge makes counterevidence misleading, (c) the duty to take evidence seriously, and (d) Peirce's view of reasoning as discovering something new all hang together? Or is the whole dogmatism paradox just a collision between (b) and (c)? Which commitment should I revise, and what's the cheapest fix? I'd like the tensions ordered by severity, not just listed.
