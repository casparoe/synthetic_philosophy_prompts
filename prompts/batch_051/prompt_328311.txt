Since I've been rethinking whether famines are natural disasters or political crimes — Sen's entitlement stuff, the Irish famine, the Great Leap Forward, all that — I've hit a more basic wall: I'm not sure I have a definition of "famine" that doesn't already smuggle in the answer. So give me your working definition of a famine, and run it through the following cases.

(i) A drought destroys an entire harvest in a region where people have stored food and functioning markets, and nobody goes hungry. Famine or not? I say no, but I want to know why not.

(ii) The Irish famine of the 1840s: a blight killed the potato crop that the poor actually lived on, while the country continued to export grain and livestock during the worst years. I say obviously a famine.

(iii) The Great Leap Forward famine in China, late 1950s to early 60s: harvests collapsed, but the state kept procuring grain from the countryside to feed the cities and meet export targets. I say clearly a famine, even though bad weather did part of the work.

(iv) The Bengal famine of 1943. If Sen is right about the facts, total rice availability per person wasn't dramatically worse than in earlier non-famine years; what happened was wartime inflation, speculation, and hoarding put food out of reach of landless rural labourers, and millions died. I say yes, a famine — but "not enough food" would be the wrong diagnosis.

(v) A city under siege whose population is deliberately starved by the surrounding army. Is that a famine, or is it a different thing, like a war crime or an act of war that causes starvation?

(vi) A rich country in a record harvest year where the poorest few percent routinely can't afford enough calories. That's called food insecurity, not famine — but why not?

(vii) A prolonged drought that wipes out the wild foods a small hunter-gatherer band depends on, with no markets, no state, and no agriculture anywhere in the picture. Is that a famine, or is the word out of place there?

What I really want is a definition that says whether famine is partly a social or political category — about distribution, entitlements, markets, institutions — or purely a matter of aggregate food shortage, and whether there's a non-arbitrary line between famine and ordinary hunger or chronic undernutrition. Give me the definition, test it against each case, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and revise it or bite the bullet. Then tell me which of your verdicts your least sure about.
