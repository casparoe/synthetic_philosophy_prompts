I'm presenting a working paper next month to a joint policy school–computer science workshop, and I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a polished defense. My thesis is this: the Future of Life Institute's open letter "Pause Giant AI Experiments" (2023) is unenforceable as written, but its fallback clause — that governments should institute a moratorium if the labs won't pause — silently commits its signatories to compute governance. The letter's capability threshold, something like "more powerful than GPT-4," cannot be verified by a regulator; what a regulator can see, count, and cap is the compute behind a training run. A signatory who wants the letter's moratorium but rejects compute governance is therefore left with no mechanism, only a preference.

The talk turns on this passage from the letter:

"This pause should be public and verifiable, and include all key actors. If such a pause cannot be enacted quickly, governments should step in and institute a moratorium."

The audience is about twenty-five people: half policy staffers from a regulatory agency and two large labs' policy teams, who work on transparency, incident reporting, and procurement; half applied ML researchers whose work is on bias, robustness, and privacy in deployed systems. Almost none are existential-risk people, and several already think the "pause" conversation is a distraction from present harms. They care about compliance cost, about not handing incumbents a moat, and about not losing ground to labs or states that stay outside the moratorium.

My argument runs: (1) a capability threshold depends on the lab's own evaluations, so it is self-certified; (2) frontier training compute is physically concentrated and in principle reportable; (3) therefore the only operational reading of the moratorium clause is compute governance — tracking, reporting thresholds, and caps on large training runs.

Write me the prep sheet as five to seven ordered objections, not as a defense of the thesis. For each objection: say who raises it (agency staffer, industry policy person, or ML researcher), phrase it the way that person would actually put it, mark roughly how likely and how damaging it is, give me a two-minute reply, and name the place where that reply is weakest. For each, also flag what I should concede outright and what I should hold. Order them by a rough product of likelihood and damage, most dangerous first, and tell me if the likely order of arrival differs. End with the question I'm hoping nobody asks — the one my own framing most invites — and the first two sentences of a response if it comes anyway.

If this request rests on a confusion, say so plainly instead of complying politely. In particular, if you think "compute governance" simply relocates the verification problem and the distinction between capability thresholds and compute thresholds collapses under inspection, or that the letter as written does not actually commit a signatory to any implementation of the moratorium, tell me I've built the talk on a mistake and re-pose the task.

Hard limit: 1,100 words, excluding the quoted passage. Keep it scannable from a sheet of paper at a podium.
