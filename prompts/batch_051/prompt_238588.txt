For the past two years I have been one of the editors of my high school's senior profiles, a spring publication in which every graduating senior receives a short biographical profile based on an interview. The editorial board is made up of students with no formal training in journalism or ethics, we produce roughly eighty profiles in a two-week window each year, and the faculty advisor reads and signs off on every profile before it is printed. Our repeated problem is that the profiles fail in one of two ways: they turn into hagiography, piling up honors and superlatives while giving readers no sense of the person, or they try to be candid and slide into gossip and takedowns. In a history class I read that Plutarch wrote biographies less to record events than to train his readers' moral judgment, and that for him a small, revealing detail could show character more clearly than public honors or scandalous episodes. I would like to convert that idea into a checklist my editorial team can actually run on the material from each interview. Please give me an ordered sequence of questions an editor should ask about any proposed detail, a standard for deciding when each question has been answered well enough to move forward, a clear point at which the checklist rules material out, and a clear point at which the checklist stops and hands the decision to the student being profiled or to the faculty advisor because the matter is ethical rather than editorial. Then show the checklist at work on two draft profiles: (1) a student body president with an excellent public record who, several classmates say privately, has spent years manipulating peers; and (2) a reserved student with almost no public accomplishments whose friends describe a quiet and persistent act of generosity. Please make sure at least one case shows the procedure producing a verdict the editorial team would not initially like, and end by saying what the procedure cannot decide by itself.
