I'm in a modern philosophy course right now, and we're on Hume's Enquiry — section twelve, "Of the Academical or Sceptical Philosophy," part one. Two friends in my study group, Elena and Marcus, got into a whole thing about this passage, where Hume's describing the Cartesian method of doubt and then attacking it:

"It recommends an universal doubt, not only of all our former opinions and principles, but also of our very faculties; of whose veracity, say they, we must assure ourselves, by a chain of reasoning, deduced from some original principle, which cannot possibly be fallacious or deceitful. But neither is there any such original principle, which has a prerogative above others, that are self-evident and convincing: Or if there were, could we advance a step beyond it, but by the use of those very faculties, of which we are supposed to be already diffident. The Cartesian doubt, therefore, were it ever possible to be attained by any human creature (as it plainly is not) would be entirely incurable; and no reasoning could ever bring us to a state of assurance and conviction upon any subject."

They asked me to referee and I genuinely couldn't. Here's the back-and-forth:

1. Elena: "Hume's objection is circularity. Descartes wants to doubt everything — including his own faculties — and then prove the faculties reliable from some original principle. Hume says that's impossible: even if you had the principle, any step beyond it would excercise the very faculties you're trying to certify. That's basically the problem of the criterion: any test of your cognitive faculties has to use those faculties or some other faculty that's equally in question."

2. Marcus: "Sure, but read the last sentence. Hume says the doubt is not even attainable by a human creature. So the point isn't just that Descartes is circular. It's that we were never actually in the skeptical predicament. Our basic trust in our senses and our causal reasoning comes from nature and instinct, not from a chain of reasoning. There's no outside standpoint from which the whole thing needs certifying."

3. Elena: "That feels like a cop-out. 'Nature forces us to trust our faculties' doesn't show the faculties are trustworthy. Someone in the grip of a delusion can't help trusting it either. The skeptic's question was about justification, and 'I can't help believing' is a psychlogical fact, not an answer to it."

4. Marcus: "But then what would an answer even look like for you? A proof that used the very faculties in question? That's the standard that needs to be dropped. Hume's point is that the demand for a faculty-wide validation is both unmeetable and unnecessary — our ordinary practices don't validate everything at once, and they don't pretend to."

5. Elena: "Except Hume goes on to build an entire science of human nature — custom, causation, the passions — using those same faculties he says can't be independently certified. If there's no standpoint from which to judge our faculties, why isn't his own project just as circular as the one he's mocking Descartes for?"

6. Marcus: "Because he's not pretending to be outside the faculties. He's using them to describe themselves, which is coherent even if it can't be certified from outside. Descartes said he'd doubt until he found an absolute foundation; Hume says there isn't one, and then keeps working anyway."

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify each briefly, then say who's more right overall, and say what, if anything, both of them are missing about what Hume himself is doing in that passage.
