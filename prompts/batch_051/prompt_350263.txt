I'm in a political theory class on the ninteenth-century British empire, and we've been reading Mill's Considerations on Representative Government, specifically chapter XVIII, "Of the Government of Dependencies by a Free State." Mill there tries to distinguish two kinds of dependencies: ones settled by people from the ruling country, who can get representative institutions, and ones that are more like conquered territories where the population is judged not ready for representation. He argues that in the second case the free state has to govern through a kind of benevolent despotism, and the only thing that makes it legitimate is that the rule is actually directed at improving the governed and preparing them for self-government. That's Mill's line, not a direct quote.

Then a friend and I reconstructed the argument like this, and now I'm stuck because the conclusion seems way too strong.

(1) A free state may legitimately govern a dependency without its consent only if the dependency is not yet capable of representative institutions, and the ruling state is using its power to prepare the dependency for such institutions.

(2) Whether a people is capable of representative institutions is a factual question about their social conditions, habits, and degree of civilization.

(3) A free state that judges a dependency incapable is the one that gets to decide whether the condition in (1) actually holds.

(4) If a free state may legitimately rule a dependency without its consent whenever it judges the dependency incapable, then any state that is more powerful than another people may legitimately rule that people without their consent whenever it judges them less fit for self-government, provided it claims to be improving them.

(5) So, on Mill's reasoning, the British conquest and rule of India was not just historically explainable but morally legitimate at the time, and the same would go for any colonial conquest of a people the conquering power deems backward.

(6) That conclusion is unacceptable, because it would license virtually any imperial annexation on the basis of the conqueror's own self-serving judgments.

So I have to give up something, but I don't know what. I'm inclined to keep (1) because the distinction between settlement colonies and dependencies seems real, but I also really don't want to accept (5). Is the flaw in the move from (3) to (4), or is it already in (1) because the capability test is doing more work than it looks like? Or is the argument actually valid and the only way out is to reject Mill's premise about benevolent despotism? Where does it go wrong, and what does the least bad fix cost?
