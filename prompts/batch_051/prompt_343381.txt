I've come around to the view that being rational under severe uncertainty doesnt require a single probability function: you should carry around a set of probabilities (a credal set) and update each member by ordinary conditioning as evidence comes in, so the set evolves pointwise. For action, I'm inclined to something like maximality — you pick an act only if no other option has higher expected value under every single probability in your set. I know the obvious complaints: dilation, where conditioning on a partition somehow makes your interval wider even though you learned something; the set going stale or empty; indecision when two acts are incomparable; and the worry that this is just a fancy way to hide an arbitrary choice about how wide the set is in the first place. What I want is a serious map of what would actually have to be true for this to be wrong. Include: formal results that would sink it (not just 'sometimes you can't decide'), intuitions that a defender like me should admit count against it, and cases where a precise Bayesian genuinely does better. Then sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether the view is at risk of being insulated — for example, by answering every counterexample with 'the interval is just your evidence.' If you had to bet on the single most likely way the view collapses into ordinary precise Bayesianism or into something trivial, what would it be, and what would the collapse look like?
