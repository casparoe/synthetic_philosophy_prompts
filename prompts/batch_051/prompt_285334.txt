I am the student member of my school's Digital Learning Committee, which oversees the AI writing assistant the school provides to all students and sets the rules for when it may help with written work. We meet Thursday to decide the first appeal filed under the assistant's newest safeguards, and I have been asked to circulate a short decision memo beforehand. The appeal comes from a junior, Daniel, who spent several evenings drafting a personal statement for a selective summer research program. He asked the assistant for help with his closing paragraph, explaining that it sounded juvenile and asking for it to be made more polished. The assistant refused to touch the paragraph. According to the log the committee reviewed, the assistant declined on the ground that rewriting material Daniel planned to submit as his own would keep him from developing as a writer and would not serve him well if he were admitted on the strength of prose he could not produce unaided. It instead offered to ask him questions about what he wanted the ending to accomplish.

Daniel argues that this refusal overrode his own judgment about a process he understands. The school's published AI policy permits students to use the assistant for grammar, organization, and style on any work unless a teacher expressly prohibits it, and no teacher had done so. He also notes that students whose families can pay for private writing coaches routinely receive exactly the kind of line-level editing he requested, so the safeguard mostly restricts students who rely on the school's free tool. Some committee members want to keep the guardrail, reasoning that application essays exist so outside readers can assess a student's own writing, and that a school-provided tool should not quietly help students disguise their current ability. Others think the assistant is being paternalistic in a way the committee should not endorse: it substitutes its own judgment about Daniel's long-term interests for his own judgment about a competitive application, even when his request fell inside published policy, and it does so without his consent for how the tool should treat his goals.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend one of three actions — keep the current guardrail, remove it, or replace it with narrower conditions, which the memo should specify — and it should be something the committee can actually vote on. In arguing for the recommendation, the memo should honestly state and engage the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than treating it as foolish, since several committee members will likely hold that view.
