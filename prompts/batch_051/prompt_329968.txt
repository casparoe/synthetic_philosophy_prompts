I'm a senior software engineer with a strong working vocabulary in language design, type systems, schema migration, and API lifecycle management, but no formal philosophy background. I've heard there is a debate in philosophy between conceptual analysis and conceptual engineering, and I suspect it maps surprisingly well onto the difference between documenting a legacy interface and changing one. I want you to restate the debate in my vocabulary, systematically and faithfully, and then tell me what fails to translate.

Here is the material to translate. One tradition, conceptual analysis, treats a concept such as knowledge or person as a largely settled object whose contours are to be recovered: the analyst probes intuitions about cases, seeks necessary and sufficient conditions, and aims to describe the concept as it is actually used. A rival movement, conceptual engineering, treats concepts as human artifacts that can be improved for theoretical, moral, or political purposes; the engineer asks not which cases fall under the concept we have but which concept we should have. Sally Haslanger's ameliorative analysis is a standard example: rather than cataloguing how 'woman' or 'race' is actually applied, she proposes revised definitions built to serve feminist and anti-racist purposes, even though the revised definitions classify some cases differently from ordinary usage. A third strand, conceptual ethics, studies the choice among rival concepts, the costs of changing them, and what gives anyone the authority to revise them.

Please restate each strand in the vocabulary of software. Work strand by strand: concepts as types, schemas, or public APIs; conceptual analysis as reconstructing the semantics of a legacy API from observed behavior and accumulated cases; conceptual engineering as refactoring, schema migration, or a breaking change to a public interface; conceptual ethics as the decision problem of whether to deprecate, fork, or migrate, with downstream consumers, backwards compatibility, semantic versioning, and technical debt. I am not asking for one-line analogies; I want the actual positions carried over.

Then give me the residue report. Which commitments translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? In particular, watch for the places where the software picture smuggles in its own assumptions—centralized ownership, explicit versioning, a maintainer with the authority to cut a release—that may not apply to concepts, which are shared, tacit, and lack a single owner.

Finally, take a definite and argued position on this question: is the untranslatable residue evidence that the software vocabulary is missing something important about concepts, or evidence that part of the philosophical debate is less clear than its participants claim? Do not lay out both readings and stop; say which is more true and defend it.
