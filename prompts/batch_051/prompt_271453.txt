Hey — I'm a philosophy PhD student working on population ethics and suffering-focused ethics, and I'm drafting a short "commitments" statement for a workshop. Can you act as a professional philosopher who does consistency audits, think through it carefully, and not go easy on me? I hold, provisionally, all of the following:

(a) An asymmetry between suffering and happiness: no amount of positive well-being can justify knowingly inflicting a sufficiently intense episode of suffering (a threshold view, not strict lexicality for pinpricks).

(b) Because every new life runs a real risk of severe suffering and nobody can consent to that risk, bringing a new person into existence is at least presumptively wrong.

(c) The same reasoning extends to nonhuman animals, so wild animal suffering — predation, parasitism, starvation — is one of the most important moral problems there is, and gives us pro tanto reasons to intervene in nature (wildlife contraception, vaccination, maybe more).

(d) But I also hold a strong precautionary principle about ecological intervention: ecosystem effects are to unpredictable, and interventions have a way of increasing suffering overall, so most concrete intervention projects are probably not justified.

(e) And I want to keep a Millian side-constraint: I'd oppose paternalistically overriding a competent adult's choices even when doing so would reduce their own suffering.

Which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what would the least costly revision be? Start with a quick brainstorm of the pairs before ranking. Then rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each tension say what would have to give. Finally, be self-critical about the audit itself: what argument or consideration would change your mind about the diagnosis or the ranking, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
