I am a PhD student working on how modal and probabilistic notions relate, and I have been turning over the following argument because each step seems plausible in isolation while the conclusion seems to force a choice I thought I did not have to make. I would be grateful for a step-by-step verdict: is the argument valid, and if not, exactly which step fails, and what is the most charitable repair?

(1) If an agent takes a proposition P to be possible, then the agent should not assign P credence zero, since credence zero is the agent's way of saying that P is ruled out.

(2) A rational agent's credences obey the usual probability calculus: in particular, the probability of a union of mutually exclusive outcomes is the sum of their individual probabilities.

(3) Take a fair, sharply defined pointer spun over a continuous circle, with the probability of landing in any arc proportional to its length. Choose a point x on the circumference in advance, and let P be the proposition that the pointer lands exactly on x. P is possible: every point is on the same footing, and nothing in the setup rules any exact landing point out.

(4) In the standard continuum model, P receives probability zero: the singleton set {x} has zero length, and no point can receive positive probability, since if each point had some positive probability ε, then a finite set of more than 1/ε points would already have probability greater than one, violating the calculus. So all exact landing points must be assigned probability zero in the uniform continuum model.

(5) By (1), an agent who takes P to be possible must give P positive probability; by (2) and (4), the agent must give P probability zero. Contradiction.

(6) Therefore one of the following must go: the link between possibility and positive probability in (1); the probability calculus in (2); or the assumption in (3) that the pointer can land on an exact point at all.

I half-suspect that there is an equivocation in the word "ruled out" between an outcome that is excluded by the setup and an outcome that is merely assigned measure zero, but I cannot make the diagnosis precise. Which step fails, and if the argument is valid as far as it goes, what exactly has it shown? In answering, please distinguish, if they are distinct: the measure-theoretic notion of a null event, the modal notion of an impossible outcome, and the epistemic state of assigning credence zero. Please write the answer in British English.
