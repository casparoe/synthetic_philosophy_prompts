Something's been bugging me about how the laws of war treat soldiers. If your side invades another country, you as an ordinary soldier can legally kill the soldiers defending it, so long as you fight by the book: don't target civilians, don't kill prisoners, don't use banned weapons. After the war, the leaders may be tried for aggression, but ordinary soldiers aren't punished just for having fought on the aggressor's side, and prisoners from both sides get the same protections. Yet the same system does punish ordinary soldiers for in-war crimes — kill a prisoner and you can be hanged. Its not obvious to me that these two halves fit together. What view of moral responsibility does this practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that an ordinary soldier can't be expected to judge whether the war itself is just, even though he's expected to judge whether killing this particular prisoner is wrong? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice actually needs it or could be justified some other way, and spell out what would have to change in the laws of war if we instead held that a soldier fighting for an unjust cause is acting wrongly whenever he kills an opposing soldier.
