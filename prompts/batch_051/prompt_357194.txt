I spend half my time in a CS program where people say things like "learning Haskell rewires your brain" and "a programming language is a language, so it shapes how you think — its literally Sapir-Whorf for code." The classroom version runs something like this: natural languages differ in what they force you to encode (tense, evidentiality, number), and that habitual encoding nudges attention and memory even if the strong "language determines thought" version is dead. My friends use this to argue that everyone should be made to learn a functional language — Haskell, Scheme, whatever — because the language's grammar (no assignment, no loops, recursion everywhere, types as constraints) supposedly forces a way of carving up problems that stays with you even when you go back to Python.

I go back and forth on whether this is deep or just a metaphor with a famous name attached. On one hand a programming language does have something like a grammar, and it does seem to make some thoughts easy and others almost inexpressible. On the other hand programming languages are designed artifacts, learned deliberately and late, used for a narrow task, with inspectable semantics; natural languages are picked up in childhood, used for everything, and their categories are mostly invisible. I want someone to actually audit the analogy instead of shrugging at it.

Do this as a philosopher of language who also knows some cognitive science and enough programming. Start by building the correspondence table: what in the natural-language/Whorf picture is supposed to map onto what in the programming-language picture (grammar, obligatory categories, lexical distinctions, private thought, acquisition). Then go through the disanalogies and tell me which ones matter for the specific conclusion my friends are drawing and which are just irrelevant differences — and say whether a better analogy would do the work instead. Then commit: take a definite position, with reasons, on whether the "learn a functional language to change how you think" argument survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the Sapir-Whorf analogy was doing all the work. Don't just lay out the options and leave me to decide.
