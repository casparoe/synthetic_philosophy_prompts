Midterm question (20 points): "Some philosophers hold that a compelling theory can override a firm pre-theoretical intuition; others hold that theories are ultimately answerable to intuition. When, if ever, should a compelling theory override a firm intuition, and when should the reverse happen? Discuss at least two concrete examples."

Student answer:

"The relationship between theory and intuition is usually handled through reflective equilibrium: you go back and forth, adjusting principles to fit particular judgments and adjusting particular judgments to fit principles. But on a Rawlsian picture, our considered judgments are fixed points that any adequate theory has to preserve. If a theory conflicts with one of them, that's a reason to reject the theory, not the judgment.

In ethics, this means intuition trumps theory. Take the standard trolley case. Act-utilitarianism implies you should push the large man off the bridge to stop the trolley and save the five. Nearly everyone has the firm intuition that this is impermissible. Since it's a considered judgment about a concrete case, it constrains the theory: we revise or qualify consequentialism (say, with a doing/allowing distinction or the doctrine of double effect) rather than abandon the judgment.

Science is different. Special relativity conflicts with the pre-theoretic intuition that simultaneity is absolute, but the theory should win there, because it has enormous predictive success and the intuition is about a contingent, empirically discoverable matter rather than about what we ought to do. So the general lesson looks domain-relative: in physics, theory beats intuition; in normative ethics, intuition beats theory.

But there's a futher wrinkle. Evolutionary debunking arguments show that whenever a moral intuition can be given an adaptive explanation, it loses its authority, because a belief selected for survival need not track the moral facts. So any intuition with an evolutionary story behind it can be set aside in favor of a compelling theory. That means theory gets the last word even in ethics, as long as the theory is scientifically respectable."
