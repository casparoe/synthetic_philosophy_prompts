Over the weekend I was at a barbeque where two friends—one a math PhD student, the other a software engineer who reads a lot of philosophy—got into a long argument about the foundations of mathematics. I only caught part of it but it got surprisingly intense. The exchange went roughly like this:

(1) She said Brouwer's intuitionism is the only one of the three classical schools that takes seriously the fact that mathematical objects are our mental constructions, so it makes no sense to assert P or not-P before anyone has constructed a proof either way. (2) He replied that Hilbert-style formalism doesn't need mental constructions: mathematics is a game with symbols, and the excluded middle is just a rule of the game, no more problematic than the rule that a chess bishop moves diagonally. (3) She said that dodges the question of what makes the rules correct, and that logicism was supposed to answer that by reducing math to logic, but Frege and Russell had to import non-logical axioms like infinity and reducibility. (4) He said even if old logicism failed, later revised versions fixed that, and anyway Hilbert's program was aimed at proving consistency by finitary means, so the symbol game isn't arbitrary. (5) She said Gödel's second incompleteness theorem killed Hilbert's program: a formal system can't prove its own consistency, and stronger systems just push the problem back. (6) He said that only shows we can't have absolute certainty, not that Brouwer wins—basing all of mathematics on private mental constructions is just psychologism.
