I'm an econ PhD student, and my department is currently in the middle of a governance fight I keep getting dragged into. The proposal on the table: any change to the graduate program—teaching load, field requirements, funding allocation, who gets seminar slots—can be adopted only if it's certified as a "safe Pareto improvement." As the proposers define it, that means (a) no enrolled student or current faculty member is expected to be worse off under the change, (b) the comparison has to include the predictable ways people will respond to the policy itself, not just a static before-and-after snapshot, and (c) if within two years someone can show to a standing commitee that they did end up worse off, the people who gained have to compensate them on a schedule fixed when the policy passed, with no ability to renegotiate or wriggle out. The stated goal is to block majority-rule changes that quietly dump costs on a minority. I don't think the proposers have thought the mechanism through, and I want to be the person who has.

Treat it as a mechanism, not a moral question. What would this actually incentivize? What kinds of policies get proposed and what kinds get certified; how do students and faculty game the certification itself—say, by shaping the status quo, hiding benefits, or exaggerating how responsive they are to what gets adopted; and what does the two-year ex post compensation clause do to behavior, including behavior before anyone even votes? Where does the whole scheme fail first? Also, can the obvious failure modes be patched without just collapsing it back into plain majority rule or a plain Pareto rule? At the end, tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims and single out the point you're least sure about.
