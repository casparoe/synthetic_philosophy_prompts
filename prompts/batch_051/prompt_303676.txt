I help run a small online marketplace where buyers and sellers are strangers, and we lean heavily on a karma/rating system. I have always found the basic argument for it convincing: if someone rips people off or flakes, their rating tanks and future customers avoid them, so even a selfish person has a reason to be decent. But lately Ive noticed that good reputation has gotten weirdly cheap. You can buy aged accounts with years of clean history, or run bots that post nice-looking interactions and farm karma for less than it would cost to actually be reliable for a few months. If reputation can be bought or faked, the whole threat that your bad behavior follows you seems to dissolve, because you can just burn one account and start fresh or keep a few pre-farmed ones in reserve. That has me wondering alot about whether the original argument for karma systems still goes through, needs repair, or is just dead once identities are cheap.

You are an expert on trust and reputation systems, and maybe a bit of a philosopher about them. Please think carefully and start by brainstorming what the original argument actually needs to be true in order to work. Then tell me which part the buying and farming of reputation breaks, and whether the argument can be rebuilt in another form rather than just patched with some platform tweak. I want to understand what happens to the argument itself, not a list of moderation features.
