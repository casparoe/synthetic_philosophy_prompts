Van Inwagen's special composition question—under what conditions do some things compose a further thing—has been debated for decades, and the debate seems to me stuck in the same shape it had near the start. Nihilists say composition never occurs; universalists say it always occurs; restrictionists say it sometimes occurs, whether because the parts are caught up in a life or for no informative reason at all. What puzzles me is that all three camps seem to accept the same underlying facts about how particles are arranged, and each camp seems to know exactly what the costs of the other two views are—violence to ordinary belief, a bloated ontology, or a restriction that looks arbitrary. Each side treats those costs differently, but no one has moved. My working hypothesis is that this is no longer a single metaphysical disagreement but three different questions being run together: what the fundamental ontology actually contains, what the semantics of our plural and sortal terms should be, and how much weight ordinary beliefs deserve as evidence in metaphysics. I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement the special composition question has become—substantive, verbal, methodological, or some mix—and of whether the question as posed is well-formed. Given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction that separates the questions, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that the stalemate should be expected to persist?
