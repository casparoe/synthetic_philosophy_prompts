For a novel I am drafting, I need to make the internal procedures of a national-security advisory body feel real enough that the plot can turn on them without seeming convenient. The body operates under a rule I have invented and am now half-afraid is too clever to survive contact with actual people. The rule is this: whenever a paper invokes a historical analogy to justify a course of action — the obvious cases being Munich, Vietnam, or the Thucydides trap — the author must append a "disanalogy brief." The brief has two mandatory sections. First, the author must state, in concrete terms, the single most important respect in which the present situation differs from the invoked precedent, without softening it. Second, the author must construct the strongest available analogy to a different historical episode that cuts against the recommended action, and explain why that competing analogy should not carry the day. If the author cannot produce a credible competing analogy, the proposal is automatically shelved for a month and returned with a note that it has not yet earned its analogy. I want to use this device to show a system trying to discipline its own historical storytelling.

My question is not whether the rule is sensible in the abstract, but how it would actually behave if it were enforced in earnest. Walk me through the incentives it would create for the analysts and for the senior officials who commission their memos: how they would game it, what kind of gaming would be hard to detect, how the meaning of the two brief sections would evolve as people became fluent in them, and what second-order effects it would have on which decisions get proposed, deferred, or disguised. Then assess whether the rule as written selects for better judgment or merely for better rhetoric, and whether any amendment could close that gap without collapsing into the very analogical free-for-all it was meant to control. I would rather have an unflattering portrait of my invented institution than an elegant one.
