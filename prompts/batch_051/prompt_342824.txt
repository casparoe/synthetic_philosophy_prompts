I'm on the board of a small philosophy journal (mostly metaethics, ordinary-language stuff, and decision theory), and we keep having the same fight: one referee says a paper should be rejected because its conclusion is absurd on its face, another says that's the whole point, sometimes the best theory really does overturn a firm intuition. So the board is floating the following rule for submissions. Any time a paper's argument requires rejecting an intuition that a referee flags as widespread and firm, the author has to run a pre-registered survey of at least 50 people who haven't studied the relevant literature, using the exact vignette in the paper. If fewer than 80% of respondents actually have the intuition, the paper can go forward and the author may report that the intuition turns out not to be shared after all. If 80% or more have it, the paper can still go forward, but only if it includes an error theory (a story about why the intuition would be so common even though it's false), and that error theory has to make at least one new, testable prediction about some other intuition. A one-line "people are terrible at this" doesn't count. And the error theory itself is covered by the same rule: if answering the "why is the intuition so common" question leans on some other counterintuitive claim, the author has to survey that too, and if that just keeps going, the paper is desk-rejected.

The board has asked me to write a memo about how this would actually play out, not whether it's a good idea in the abstract. What does it do to what gets submitted, to how authors choose and word their vignettes, to which intuitions get surveyed, and to who is able to get published? Where does the system get gamed first? And can the worst failure modes be fixed without gutting the rule? If you think the rule would end up quietly legislating an answer to the theory-vs-intuition debate, say which one. Keep the memo under 800 words.
