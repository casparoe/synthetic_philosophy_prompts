I'm teaching a second-year historiography seminar with about 18 students, and we have a two-hour block (really 90 minutes of usable time once we get settled) to spend on how we know anything about the distant past. We've already discussed Herodotus's habit of reporting what various people say even when he doesn't believe it, and Thucydides being dismissive about how carelessly people accept stories about the past, and my students can parrot the distinction between an event and a report of an event. But they don't feel it. The thing I most want them to come away with is that agreement between sources means almost nothing until you know whether the sources are independent, and that what survived is itself the product of choices and accidents, so confidence in a reconstruction has to come from the shape of the transmission chain rather than because three sources happen to say so.

My idea, which you should improve or replace if it won't work, is a simulated event: a suspicious fire in a temple treasury, or a bribery scandal at a festival. Each small group gets a packet of five or six sources with different hidden lineages — a chronicle written shortly after the event but surviving only in a ninth-century copy, a later chronicle that silently copies it, an independent but wildly biased letter, an oral tradition written down two centuries later, and a tenth-century forged charter that purports to be contemporary and looks very plausible. The groups have to reconstruct what happened, rank thier confidence in each element of the story, and write a one-paragraph account. Then they compare and discover they've built substantially different events from overlapping material, which is where I hope the lesson starts to bite.

But I need the whole thing designed for actual use: the packets themselves with the hidden lineage spelled out, the setup and roles, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments (two texts agreeing because one copied the other, an early copy of a late source being trusted as if it were earlier, a forged document filling exactly the gap the other sources leave) onto the theoretical distinctions. Please also be honest about what the exercise can't reproduce, since real source criticism works over hundreds of manuscripts in languages we know imperfectly, so the simulation will have to cheat somewhere. What will predictably go wrong the first time — do groups manufacture consensus, do they trust whatever looks oldest, does the forging motive get ignored? And I'm nervous the activity will turn into a whodunnit where everyone just wants to solve the puzzle rather than notice how provisonal the solution is. How do I set it up so the takeaway is about the hole in the evidence, not the cleverness of the reconstruction?
