I'm a software engineer who builds planning and decision-making components, and I've been trying to model an agent's beliefs without the standard possible-worlds semantics I picked up from colleagues, because that setup makes the agent logically omniscient: it ends up believing every tautology and every consequence of anything it already believes, even when no real computation could have drawn those consequences. As far as I can tell, the workarounds on offer divide into these five families. (1) Impossible-worlds semantics adds worlds where sentences and their Boolean combinations do not obey classical logic, so a logically valid sentence need not hold at every world the agent considers possible. (2) Awareness semantics keeps the worlds but adds a per-state awareness set of formulas, and counts the agent as knowing only those formulas that both hold throughout its accessible worlds and belong to that set. (3) The explicit/implicit belief split keeps a logically closed implicit operator but adds an unclosed explicit one, and treats the explicit notion as the realistic one. (4) Syntactic or deduction-based models represent beliefs as formulas plus inference rules rather than as sets of worlds, so believing is a matter of what has actually been stored or derived. (5) Algorithmic and complexity-bounded approaches relativize belief to what a specific program, with specific time and space bounds, can compute from its local state. Each of these seems to me to buy non-omniscience either by bolting an arbitrary filter onto an otherwise classical semantics or by replacing the clean semantics with a bounded inference engine whose settings are mostly engineering choices.

I'd like you to give me one more approach that is not on this list and is structurally different from all five of them, not a relabeling, special case, or combination. As a concrete test case to calibrate the proposal: suppose an agent knows a modest set of axioms and knows φ, and suppose ψ follows from φ by a derivation so long that no physically realizable system could perform it within any practical time budget. Tell me exactly what your approach says about that agent with respect to ψ, and why that classification is not just the standard possible-worlds answer in disguise. I'm asking for a committed response: pick one sixth approach, state it precisely enough that I could implement a toy version, explain how it blocks both closure under entailment and knowledge of all validities, and say plainly what desirable property of the possible-worlds semantics it sacrifices. If your honest conclusion is that no real sixth approach exists and one of the five has to be repaired or accepted, defend that conclusion as a definite position rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose.
