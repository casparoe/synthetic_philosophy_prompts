I'm a journalist who covers AI and the cognitive sciences, and I've been chewing on a parallel that I can't tell is real or just pattern-matching. In reinforcement learning there's the temporal-difference reward prediction error: roughly, the mismatch between the value you predicted for your situation and the value you actually encounter once the next moment arrives — reward now plus the discounted value of what comes next, minus what you expected. It's the signal that says, roughly, that went better than expected, or that went worse than expected, and it's what drives an agent to update its predictions; dopamine neurons look like they broadcast something like it in the brain. Now the leap. I keep reading about resentment and envy in the philosophy of the moral emotions — including Rawls's thought that envy can be excusable rather than merely vicious when someone stuck at the bottom of a badly unequal situation can hardly be expected not to feel it, and that resentment is a properly moral response to injustice rather than just bitterness. And I can't shake the feeling that resentment and envy are, structurally, error signals too. Resentment seems to fire when a social outcome falls short of the treatment you took yourself to be owed. Envy seems to track a comparative gap — your position against somebody else's — the way certain algorithms compute an advantage against a reference point rather than a raw outcome. Both are aversive, both seem to carry information about the social world, and both seem to motivate some kind of updating: of your expectations, your self-assessment, or your sense of what's fair.

But I'm not sure the analogy does any real work beyond the word "signal." I'd like you to take it apart. Please put on your hat as a philosopher who knows both the moral-psychology work on resentment and envy and the basics of reinforcement learning. Spend some time brainstorming the mapping before you commit to an answer: what exactly is supposed to correspond to the value function, the reward, the temporal dimension, and the update rule, when we move from TD learning to a person's emotional life? Then tell me wether the resemblance is deep, loose, or confused. Specifically: what would have to be true of both sides for a result from reinforcement learning to genuinely transfer to moral psychology, and what would that buy us? And can you point to something the parallel predicts that is actualy false — for instance, I'd have thought that if resentment were a prediction error, its intensity should track the size of the gap between expected and actual treatment, but real resentment seems sensitive to intentions and blame in a way a bare error signal isn't. Is that a fatal disanalogy, or does it just mean the "expectation" on the moral side is much richer? I want a verdict I can use in a column: is this a real structural identity, a fruitful metaphor, or just the word "error" doing too much work?
