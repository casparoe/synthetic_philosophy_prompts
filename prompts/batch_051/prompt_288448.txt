I want a working definition of "equal opportunity" that can sort the following cases rather than just gesture at fairness. Assume throughout a society with formally equal legal and political rights, and set aside whatever happens after the starting positions are fixed.

Case A: one child is given a forty-million-dollar trust fund at birth, while another child of equal ability and motivation is given no inherited wealth. Case B: two children of equal ability and equal family resources enter a school system, but one is assigned to a school with a quarter of the per-pupil funding of the other because of where she lives. Case C: two children of identical family wealth and social standing are born with sharply different native aptitudes for the society's most rewarded occupations. Case D: two children of identical wealth and native ability are raised in similar schools and neighborhoods, but their parents invest very different amounts of time, conversation, and instruction in their early development. Case E: one child inherits no money but inherits her parents' professional network, work habits, and inside knowledge, while another child inherits neither. Case F: the state taxes all inheritance at one hundred percent and spends the proceeds to make schools roughly equal, while family environments and social networks remain as unequal as before.

I am confident that A and B are failures of equal opportunity, and I lean toward saying C is not a failure, but D, E, and F leave me uncertain, because they push the concept toward family, social capital, and institutions rather than money. Propose the definition, apply it to all six cases, and indicate where it forces you toward a surprising or revisionary verdict. If the definition gives no determinate answer in some case, say so and say whether the problem lies in the definition or in the case. Please write in American English.
