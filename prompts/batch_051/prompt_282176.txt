You are a professional philosopher of mind and formal epistemology who specializes in how agents with limited perceptual apparatus can converge on the true structure of their world. I'm writing an essay that treats Edwin A. Abbott's Flatland (1884) as a thought experiment about two intertwined problems. As I shall use the terms, ontology identification is the problem an agent faces when its observations already arrive carved up by a native conceptual scheme — in the story, a flat plane of lines, shapes, and brightness — and it must nonetheless infer the underlying categories and dimensions of the world that produced those observations. Inaccessible information is the companion idea that some real, causally relevant structure may be unreachable from within a given ontology, not simply unknown but inexpressible without first changing the ontology itself. I want you to read the central arc of the book — A Square's encounters with the Sphere, from the first appearance of a circle that grows and shrinks for no visible reason, through his vision of Spaceland, to his failed attempt to preach the reality of a third dimension — through this pair of ideas.

Please begin by brainstorming several possible mappings between elements of the story and these two problems, then commit to the most defensible mapping and reason carefully through it. At a minimum, address the following. What exactly is A Square's evidence for the Sphere, and why might the hypothesis of a single persisting three-dimensional object be underdetermined, or even unformulable, from inside his flat ontology? Is the Sphere's appearance better described as information the Square cannot access or as a new ontology he must identify? What is the epistemological status of the analogical leap from a cube, and of the Sphere's claim that there is a direction orthogonal to the whole plane, one that appears nowhere in the Square's geometry — are these inferences, changes of conceptual scheme, or something else? Then turn the lens around. What does Flatland reveal about the limits of treating ontology identification as a tidy inference problem? Consider, for instance, that the Square cannot convince his countrymen, that Flatland's authorities have a social stake in suppressing talk of a third dimension, and that the book is simultaneously a satire of Victorian hierarchy. I want a reading faithful in both directions: do not reduce the story to a mere illustration, and do not stretch the technical notions until they lose their edge. Say plainly where the lens illuminates the story, where the story resists the reading, and what the story contributes to the philosophical problem that argument alone cannot.
