Here's a legal counterfactual I've been chewing on. Suppose the defenition of a refugee got broadened: instead of the 1951 Convention's rule that persecution has to be on account of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion, imagine a new rule that defines a refugee as anyone outside their country who faces a serious threat to life, liberty, or physical integrity that their own state won't protect them from. Everything else about the regime—non-refoulement, individual status determination, the usual evidentiary standard—stays unchanged. Predict, with reasoning, what happens over the following twenty years. I'm not asking for a story; I want the chain of mechanisms. Which groups start getting recognized who wouldn't have been before, and does the number of recognitions rise as much as the legal change would naively suggest? How do states adapt in practice—more restrictive evidentiary standards, detention, pushbacks, safe third country arrangements, or just quietly watering down the new rule? Does the broader definition make the international protection system more fragile or more legitimate? Which of your predictions are robust, and which hinge on assumptions about state capacity, migration networks, or politics? Finish with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing a one-sentence summary of your overall prediction.
