Consider a proposal, explicitly framed as an attempt to put Aristotle's account of money into institutional practice. Aristotle held that money is a conventional instrument whose function is to make different goods commensurable in exchange and to serve the bounded needs of a household; he distinguished this natural, limited use of money from the unnatural, unlimited acquisition of money for its own sake, and he regarded the earning of money from money as a deformation of money's proper function. A small municipality, animated by this distinction, adopts the following rules. First, it issues a local currency whose notes expire sixty days after issue, after which the municipal exchange office no longer accepts them. Second, the municipality sets a household holding cap, estimated as what a household needs for two weeks of ordinary purchases, and any balance above the cap at the twice-monthly declaration is forfeited. Third, the municipality voids all contracts that charge interest or give a lender anything beyond the return of the principal, including compensation in goods, in foreign currency, or in labor. Fourth, a resident may not buy a good with local currency in order to resell it at a higher local-currency price; the exchange office keeps a registry of transaction prices and penalizes residents whose resales show a systematic mark-up.

Treat the proposal as a mechanism rather than a moral position. Work through how it would actually behave: what each rule incentivizes among residents, merchants, and the officials who run the exchange office; how participants would game the rules or route around them while staying formally compliant, including arrangements that function as interest without being called interest; what second-order effects emerge in the use of goods, labor time, gifts, favors, and alternative currencies; where the scheme is most likely to break down; and whether the failure modes could be repaired by adjusting the rules without abandoning the underlying aim. Conclude by stating whether the mechanism preserves Aristotle's distinction between money as an instrument of bounded use and money as an end, or whether it merely pushes the accumulation motive into some other instrument.
