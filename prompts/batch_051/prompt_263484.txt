I’m drafting a novel set during a political crisis, and I’ve been reading around in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century debates about rights because one of my characters keeps invoking “natural rights” against the government while another character dismisses him as sentimental. A friend of mine, who studied some political theory, summarized Bentham’s critique of natural rights for me like this: Bentham thought the whole idea of rights that exist before government is incoherent, which is why he called natural rights rhetorical nonsense. On Bentham’s view, my friend said, rights are not discovered but created — they are legal devices, the product of government decision — so a right can exist only where there is some authority prepared to enforce it. My friend then drew a conclusion: if rights are just whatever a legal system grants, then on Bentham’s account a government cannot really violate anyone’s rights; it can only change which rights they have. That means appealing to “rights” against what the law currently permits, or against a new law that takes a protection away, has no critical purchase. A careful defender of Bentham would, my friend thinks, have to admit that on this view the language of rights can never be used to condemn the law itself. I also came across a related line of Bentham’s, which I’ll paraphrase: he complains that the wish for a right is being mistaken for the right itself — wanting something is not supplying it, and hunger is not bread. I’ve read enough to sense that something is off in my friend’s summary — Bentham clearly wasn’t indifferent to bad laws — but I can’t pin down exactly where the misrepresentation is or whether the “hunger is not bread” point actually cuts the way my friend assumes.

Please lead with the bottom line in your first sentence: is my friend’s summary fair, mostly fair, or unfair? Then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question. Walk through my friend’s summary piece by piece — what is accurate, what is a plausible but misleading compression, and whether the final conclusion about rights-language losing its critical force actually follows from the accurate parts. In particular, address what Bentham’s “want is not supply, hunger is not bread” analogy is really doing: is it the dismissal of all pre-legal rights that my friend takes it to be, or is it aimed at something narrower in the French revolutionary declarations? Finally, apply the corrected account to a concrete case my friend cited: if a legislature passed a law reinstating chattel slavery, is my friend right that a Benthamite would have to say the enslaved cannot appeal to violated rights because the law has simply redefined what their rights are?
