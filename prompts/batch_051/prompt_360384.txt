Draft the joint statement that a committed mereological universalist and a committed mereological nihilist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the special composition question — the question of when, if ever, some things compose a further thing. Treat universalism as the view that any non-overlapping things whatsoever compose something (so there is both a table-shaped fusion of the particles in front of you and a fusion consisting of your left ear and the moon), and nihilism as the view that composition never occurs: only mereological simples exist, and all talk of composite objects is a useful but strictly false way of talking about simples in arrangements.

Produce the document in three parts.

Part 1: a numbered agreed-claims section. It should include, at minimum:

- what both sides accept about classical mereology as a formal system — the axioms governing parthood, overlap, and fusion — and about how the special composition question is best stated within that system;
- what both sides accept about the shared empirical record, in particular that the underlying particles and their arrangements are the same on both views, with a clear statement of what this does and does not imply about how much of their dispute is substantive rather than verbal;
- what the argument from vagueness establishes against restricted, commonsense answers to the special composition question, and why both sides agree it rules out the middle positions but not which of their two extremes is correct;
- at least one further substantive claim both camps would sign that is not simply a restatement of their shared formalism.

Part 2: a short section identifying which part of the disagreement is, in your judgment, merely verbal — for example, how far to trust ordinary object-talk, or what "exists" is being used to mean — and which part is substantive. State the residual disagreement in wording each side would accept for its own view and reject for the other's.

Part 3: a disagreements table with at least five rows, one per crux. For each row give:

(a) the crux stated as a neutral question both sides accept as well formed;
(b) the universalist's position, in wording a universalist would sign;
(c) the nihilist's position, in wording a nihilist would sign;
(d) a concrete case, observation, or thought experiment that both sides agree bears on the crux;
(e) what each side concedes, in advance, would count as evidence against its own view.

Choose the cruxes yourself. Good candidates include: the literal truth of ordinary furniture statements; the possibility of gunk (a world in which everything has proper parts); whether composite objects earn their keep in causal explanations or are causally idle; whether the firm intuition that there are composite objects should be treated as data or explained away; and whether unrestricted composition's proliferation of arbitrary fusions is a vice to be avoided or an acceptable price for a simple theory. You may substitute others if they cut deeper. Where a crux turns out, on analysis, to be only a verbal difference, say so explicitly and restate what both sides could jointly sign once it is rephrased. Mark clearly which entries in column (d) are genuinely runnable tests and which are diagnostic thought experiments, so that neither party ends up signing a document whose conditions for changing their mind are feigned.
