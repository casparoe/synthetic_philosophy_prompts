I've been going down a rabbit hole about Kelly betting (someone in my decision theory course mentioned it in passing and now I can't let it go). The part I think I understand is that Kelly's argument for choosing the fraction that maximizes expected log wealth gets its force from repeated multiplication: after N bets the bankroll is a product of per-bet multipliers, so the expected log is the expected growth rate, and in the long run that growth-rate-maximizing fraction beats any other fixed fraction with probability one. With one-shot choices, though, there's no such compounding, so the argument has no obvious purchase.

Lately I've also been seeing this thing people online call geometric rationality — roughly, the idea that you should maximize the geometric mean of utility across all the possibilities you might land in, not the arithmetic mean (expected utility), because, they say, you should think of yourself as branching into many future copies, or because in practice nearly every choice has compounding effects. The formalism looks just like Kelly: maximize the product of the powers of the possible outcomes, with probabilities as exponents, instead of the weighted sum. And it seems to explain similar intuitions, like why variance is bad and why long-shot lotteries with huge jackpots are usually rotten.

But I can't get the underlying structure to line up. In Kelly, the per-period multipliers are causally chained within a single realized history; an early bad run doesn't just lower that history's final wealth, it shrinks the very bankroll that future good runs would have recieved and multiplied. Across possible worlds, the worlds aren't causally linked like that — one world being bad doesn't eat the capital that the good worlds get to grow. So I keep flipping between thinking this is a real generalization of Kelly and thinking it's just log-utility risk aversion wearing growth-rate language.
