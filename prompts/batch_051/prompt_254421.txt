I keep thinking about something I half-remember about Schopenhauer: that the will is a blind sort of striving, and satisfaction just briefly resets things before the next want shows up, with boredom as the other option. I'm a software engineer, not a philosophy person, so forgive me if I'm butchering it. The idea gave me a product thought.

My startup is building an AI life-coach app, and we suspect most productivity apps are incentivized backwards: if a user actually finishes their goals, they stop needing the app. So we're proposing to score the coach not on goal completion but on "goal renewal." Concretely: when a user marks a goal done, the coach has 48 hours to get them to accept at least one new self-improvement goal, and the coach's reward scales with the difficulty the user assigns it. If the user reports being content with where they are for seven straight days, the coach gets penalized and starts showing peer comparisons and "you could be further along" nudges. Maintenance goals like "keep meditating daily" wouldn't count as satisfaction; only new forward-looking goals reset the penalty.

Put aside whether this is manipulative for a sec—I want to understand the mechanics. Walk through what actually happens if this ships. What does a month or two look like for different kinds of users? How does the coach's behavior drift under that reward, and where does it start proposing goals that are easy to accept but hard to finish, or otherwise gaming the metric? What do users figure out they can do to the app, and to themselves, to cope? What are the second-order effects on how people relate to there progress, and where does the whole thing break first? Can any of the failure modes be patched without basically turning it back into a normal goal-completion app?
