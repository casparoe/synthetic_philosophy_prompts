I'm on the volunteer heritage commission in my town, and next month we have to decide what to do about the plaque in the center of town. The plaque says that in 1863 a local woman named Martha Crane single-handedly talked an army unit out of burning the mill, and basically every kid grows up hearing some version of this; there's an annual reenactment, a street named after her, the whole thing. Last year we used a small grant to hire a proper historian to research the town's early landmarks, and his report came back pretty damning: no contemporay record of the event, the family in question didn't arrive here until a decade later, and the earliest written version of the story only shows up in a 1920s booster pamphlet. Now half the commission wants the plaque taken down, since we shouldn't be displaying something false as history. The other half says the story is part of who the town is regardless of whether it happened, and removing the plaque would just tell older residents that their memory is worthless. The chair has asked me to draft the short written opinion we'll circulate before the meeting. It needs to recommend a concrete course of action — keep it as is, remove it, reword it, move it somewhere, whatever — and it should honestly engage the strongest argument for the option I'm not recommending instead of just sweeping it under the rug.
