I've been rereading the Wilt Chamberlain section of Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* and I keep feeling like I've caught him in a sleight of hand. As I understand it, the argument goes: pick whatever distribution you consider just — Nozick says he'll grant you that starting point — then imagine a million people freely handing over a quarter each to watch Wilt play basketball. Wilt ends up with a huge pile of money and everyone else has slightly less, so the resulting distribution no longer matches whatever pattern you originally endorsed. Nozick says this shows that free exchange will inevitably disrupt patterned distributions, and that's meant to be a problem for anyone who defends a patterned principle of justice.

Here's what bugs me. If I'm an egalitarian, or more generally someone who thinks distributive shares should track some pattern, my obvious reply is: fine, then the new distribution is just unjust. The argument only gets its bite if I've already conceded that voluntary, uncoerced transfers preserve justice — but isnt that concession basically the entitlement theory he's trying to establish? So isnt the whole example question-begging? Im not sure what the *voluntary transfer* premise is actually doing in the argument, and whether Nozick is trying to prove the final distribution is just, or something weaker.

You're a political philosopher who teaches Nozick and distributive justice. Help me see what I'm missing. Start by laying out the structure of the argument as you understand it, then address the circularity worry directly — and don't just give the standard textbook gloss; really push on whether my objection sticks or whether I've mislocated what the argument is trying to show.
