In a conversation about whether adult children have special obligations to care for their aging parents, a friend of mine ended the discussion by saying that they do, because blood is thicker than water — as if the bare fact of a biological relation made the question go away. I think the saying is doing real argumentative work there, but I can't tell what work exactly, and I don't want to attack a strawman. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan might be making when deployed that way, and evaluate each?

I suspect the ambiguity lies partly in what "blood" is meant to pick out — genetic relatedness, socially recognized kinship, a shared history of dependence — and partly in what "thicker" is comparing it with: chosen relationships like friendship, mere acquaintance, or no relationship at all. Please treat those as parameters rather than limits: give me a menu of readings, including ones I haven't listed. For each reading, say whether it is a descriptive claim about how people actually feel, a normative claim about what they owe, or both, and say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. Then say which readings would actually get my friend the conclusion he wanted — namely, that adult children owe care to their parents specifically in virtue of being their children, and not merely in virtue of affection, gratitude, or reciprocal goodwill.

I'm especially interested in how the readings interact with a view I keep wanting to defend: that what grown children owe their parents — and what parents owe grown children — is whatever their present relationship calls for, roughly as with friends, rather than repayment of a debt accumulated in childhood. On that view, a warm ongoing relationship generates obligations, and a merely historical one need not. Which readings of "blood is thicker than water" contradict that view, which are compatible with it, and which just restate one side of the dispute in slogan form? One objection I can anticipate is that friendship is chosen while family is not, so the friendship model can't get filial duties off the ground; does that objection correspond to one of the readings, and if so, is it the one doing the work?

Finally, when you assess the broader debate about filial duty, please separate what is well established from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it, and don't present a contested position as the consensus.
