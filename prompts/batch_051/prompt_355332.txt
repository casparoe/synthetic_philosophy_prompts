My opening line says 'Mosquitoes carry malaria', and she wants it cut. Her argument is that a sentence like that is basically a stereotype: it generalises over a whole class, it's made true by only some members, and readers will take away a false universal. Her line is that if we wouldn't run 'Pit bulls are dangerous' or 'Men don't ask for directions' in a news story, we shouldn't run 'Mosquitoes carry malaria' either. I'm fairly sure the analgoy is doing too much work, but I can't say exactly where it breaks. Can you audit the analogy 'generic statements are stereotypes' for me? Give me the correspondence table first: what in the stereotype corresponds to the kind, the property, the exceptions, the inferential habit, the normative baggage. Then run it through 'ducks lay eggs' (obviously true, obviously riddled with exceptions), 'mosquitoes carry malaria' (true enough for public health, but the exceptions matter), 'pit bulls are dangerous' (the stereotype case), and maybe 'ticks carry Lyme disease' if that's useful. Flag which disanalogies actually support the editor's conclusion that we should kill the line, which ones are just rhetorical loading, and whether there's a better analogy for what generics actually do. Then give me a bottom line: does 'Mosquitoes carry malaria' survive the audit, and if not, what should replace it?
