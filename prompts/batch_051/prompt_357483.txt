I work in economics — my working toolkit is public goods, externalities, real options, non-use value, social discounting, and decision-making under genuine uncertainty — and I keep being puzzled by the way philosophers of science defend public funding for cosmology. The research I mean is not applied astronomy such as tracking asteroids or monitoring space weather, but pure cosmology: mapping the early universe, testing inflation, studying dark energy, or probing the first galaxies. Such work has no foreseeable technological application, yet it attracts serious public resources, so I want you to take the standard arguments that it ought nonetheless to be funded and restate them, one by one, in my vocabulary.

The arguments I have in mind are these. First, a spillover argument: even if cosmology itself has no application, the instruments and methods it forces people to invent eventually produce technological returns. Second, an intrinsic-value argument: there is something good about knowing the history and structure of the universe that is not reducible to downstream use. Third, an epistemic argument: a society that refuses to investigate such questions suffers a distinctive kind of error or loss — it has, in some sense, failed to be curious about its own situation and about what it can legitimately hope to understand. For each, give me the most faithful translation you can into the concepts I actually use — spillovers as positive externalities with uncertain size and timing; the intrinsic-value claim as a component of non-use or existence value in a social welfare function; the epistemic argument as something about the value of information under ambiguity or the cost of remaining uncertain. Mark where the translation is clean, where it requires distortion, and where there is no counterpart in my vocabulary at all.

Then deliver what I mostly care about: the residue report. Of the material that does not survive translation, how much should I interpret as a shortcoming of the economic vocabulary — a real consideration it cannot represent — and how much should I interpret as evidence that the underlying argument was less precise than it looked? Do not assume either direction in advance; I want the residue sorted and defended, not merely catalogued.
