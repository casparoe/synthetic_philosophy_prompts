You are an expert on Orwell's political thought and on the diagnosis of stalled intellectual debates. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the distinct ways a debate like this one can be stuck before settling on your diagnosis.

A working group I belong to has been stuck for months on one passage from George Orwell's "Politics and the English Language":

Now, it is clear that the decline of a language must ultimately have political and economic causes: it is not due simply to the bad influence of this or that individual writer. But an effect can become a cause, reinforcing the original cause and producing the same effect in an intensified form, and so on indefinitely. A man may take to drink because he feels himself to be a failure, and then fail all the more completely because he drinks. It is rather the same thing that is happening to the English language. It becomes ugly and inaccurate because our thoughts are foolish, but the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts. The point is that the process is reversible. Modern English, especially written English, is full of bad habits which spread by imitation and which can be avoided if one is willing to take the necessary trouble. If one gets rid of these habits one can think more clearly, and to think clearly is a necessary first step toward political regeneration: so that the fight against bad English is not frivolous and is not the exclusive concern of professional writers.

Two camps have formed. One reads the passage as fundamentally structural: language decays because political and economic life decays, and the feedback from language to thought is real but secondary — a symptom that can amplify the disease without being the disease itself. The other reads the same paragraph as licensing the opposite emphasis: bad language habits are an independent, changeable cause, and since Orwell says that thinking clearly is "a necessary first step toward political regeneration," cleaning up language is a legitimate first intervention rather than a merely cosmetic one. Both camps accept the same text and the same ordinary facts about euphemism, vagueness, and cliché; what they cannot agree on is how to weigh the two causal directions and what kind of claim Orwell is making.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is really about what a satisfying explanation of political and linguistic decline should look like — a structural cause or a usable lever — rather than about Orwell or about psycholinguistics. But I am not confident. Is this a verbal dispute, a difference about standards of explanation, a hidden empirical question about whether language reform actually changes political thought, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — or should I expect none?
