I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been asked to maintain our team's checklist for deciding which machine learning systems go through a consciousness-impact review before deployment. By conscious here we mean having subjective experience of any kind, not merely passing behavioral tests. Our current checklist, loosely based on Integrated Information Theory, treats a system as conscious when: (1) its states carry information about their own causes and effects, rather than merely about inputs; (2) that information is integrated, so the system as a whole specifies more than its parts do separately; (3) the system is irreducible in practice, meaning no lossless decomposition into independently working components can reproduce its behavior; and (4) its current state constrains its own past and future states over a rich repertoire. The case: a 70-billion-parameter decoder-only transformer used as a chatbot. Within a single inference call it is strictly feedforward, from token context to a probability distribution over the next token; it has no recurrent state inside a call, although the full chat history is fed back as part of the input on the next call. Its attention heads appear to split into many nearly independent circuits, and a much smaller distilled model reproduces most of its outputs on behavioral tests, though the residual failures are not negligible. Does this system qualify as conscious under our four criteria? Work criterion by criterion, flag every place where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and then say whether the case shows that any criterion should be revised or dropped.
