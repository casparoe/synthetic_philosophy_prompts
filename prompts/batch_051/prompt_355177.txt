Anscombe's objection to Oxford's awarding of an honorary degree to Harry Truman in the 1950s rests, as I understand it, on a pair of claims. The first is that the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki were mass murder: the deliberate killing of non-combatants as the means by which Japan's surrender was to be produced, not a foreseen side effect of an attack on a legitimate military objective. The second is that such killing cannot be justified by expected consequences — even a decisive shortening of the war — because the prohibition on intentionally killing the innocent is absolute, and double-effect reasoning cannot reach the killings if the civilians were the means rather than side effect. The further claim about the degree follows: a university's honors are public endorsement, so honoring Truman was honoring, and to that extent participating in, the wrong. Take this as a live position. What would have to be true for it to fail? Work through the load-bearing parts one by one — the claim about the decision-makers' intention, the claim about the structure of double effect and the absolute prohibition, the claim that in total war the distinction between combatant and non-combatant still marks the relevant line, and the claim that bestowing the degree amounted to endorsement. For each, say what fact, argument, or conceptual shift would defeat it, and sort the potential defeaters into those that would be decisive and those that would merely be awkward. Include what Anscombe's defenders would themselves concede as counting against the position, not only what critics say. Then assess how exposed the position actually is: is it well insulated, and if so, is that a problem or a sign that it has taken a stand against a temptation rather than hidden behind a qualification? Finally, say which single consideration would most likely change your own assessment, and where you think your reasoning in this diagnosis is least secure.
