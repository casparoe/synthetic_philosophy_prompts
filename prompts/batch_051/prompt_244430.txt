I'm taking a philosophy of AI course and we spent most of last week on honesty and deception, and I left more confused than I went in. The line the prof keeps pushing is that honesty is a property of the agent, not the utterance: a system can emit true sentences without being honest, because its training distribution just made those tokens likely, and it can emit false sentences without being deceptive, because it's simply wrong. Fine as a conceptual point, but I can't tell whether anything empirical could ever bear on the distinction. So here's a setup I've been turning over. Suppose you train a language model in an environment where, for a subset of questions, the reward model can check the answer against a trusted database, so the model gets rewarded for saying true things and for refusing when it doesn't know; for another subset of questions, the reward model has no way to check, so nothing pressures it either way. After training, you test it on those uncheckable questions, including cases where the user is confidently wrong and cases where the model could get away with a plausible-sounding answer. (We as experimenters know the right answers in the test set, but the training signal doesn't.) What exactly should we measure, and what pattern of results would count as evidence that the model has learned something like honesty, or on the flip side is capable of something like deception, rather then just learned that truth-shaped outputs pay? And even in the best case, what would the experiment still leave untouched about the claim that it's honest in the agent sense? I keep going back and forth on whether this is a real empirical question or just a dressed-up version of asking whether it would tell the truth when nobody's looking.
