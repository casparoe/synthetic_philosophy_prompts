I am the chair of my school's three-member Student Initiatives Committee, and I have been reading about the discursive dilemma. I would like an expert's help with a variation that came up in our meetings.

Our committee issues a recommendation, R, on whether the principal should approve a proposed student event. Under our old charter, R is warranted exactly when two sub-questions are both answered yes: P, that the proposed event satisfies the budget cap, and Q, that it has a confirmed faculty supervisor. Each of the three members votes yes/no on P, on Q, and on R itself, and you may assume that each member's individual ballot respects the rule that R holds exactly when P and Q both hold. Recently the ballots were:

| Member | P | Q | R |
|---|---|---|---|
| Member 1 | yes | no | no |
| Member 2 | no | yes | no |
| Member 3 | yes | yes | yes |

If I aggregate the premises first — a majority vote on P, a majority vote on Q, and then apply the conjunctive rule — I get P yes (2–1), Q yes (2–1), hence R yes. If I instead take a direct majority vote on R, I get R no (2–1). This is, I take it, the standard three-member discursive dilemma.

Now, purely as a thought experiment, suppose the old charter had been replaced by a disjunctive rule: R is warranted exactly when at least one of P or Q holds. The members, again each individually respecting this new rule, cast the following votes:

| Member | P | Q | R |
|---|---|---|---|
| Member 1 | yes | no | yes |
| Member 2 | no | yes | yes |
| Member 3 | no | no | no |

Aggregating the premises first, I get P no (2–1) and Q no (2–1), hence R no under the disjunctive rule. Directly aggregating the conclusion, I get R yes (2–1).

Please walk me through how the standard analysis of the discursive dilemma handles this variation. Does the dilemma disappear when the rule is disjunctive, does it remain unchanged, or does it persist in a mirrored form? What feature of the original case was doing the work — the logical shape of the rule, the aggregation procedure, or the particular spread of individual votes? Finally, if our committee had to adopt one aggregation procedure for both the conjunctive and the disjunctive versions of the charter, which would you advise us to adopt, and what would we be sacrificing either way?

Act as a professional philosopher specializing in social choice theory, collective decision-making, and formal epistemology. Begin by brainstorming the candidate ways of resolving the tension, then work through the two cases step by step, and only then commit to a verdict. Flag any assumptions you are making about what makes a group decision fair, consistent, or rational.
