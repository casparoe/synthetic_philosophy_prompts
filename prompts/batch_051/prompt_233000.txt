I'm on the ideas desk, and we're putting together a package on moral maxims that everybody can recite but almost nobody has stress-tested. I drew the golden rule, and I want to open the section with a short poem instead of another explainer. Could you write a villanelle where the two refrains are loose paraphrases of the two classic formulations of the rule — roughly, do for others what you'd want them to do for you, and don't do to others what you'd hate them doing to you. The form has to carry the argument: the refrains keep returning as if they're the same rule in different outfits, but each time they come back a test case has pulled them apart — the person with unusual tastes, the person whose preferences aren't yours, the bystander who'd rather be left alone. By the time the refrains finally land next to each other in the closing couplet, its genuinely undecidable whether the negative form fixes the positive one or just makes it too weak to require anything at all. Leave the patched-or-demoted question open, and make the poem accurate enough that an ethics professor wouldn't wince.
