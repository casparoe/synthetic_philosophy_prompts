A working group at a forecasting institute floats the following institution for settling disputes among quantitative models in regulatory science. Call it a description-length panel. Whenever two models disagree about a quantity the regulator must act on — an exposure limit, a reserve ratio, a screening threshold, or the like — each side submits its model as executable code. The panel measures the complexity of a submission as its length in bits after compression by an agreed general-purpose compressor, plus the compressed length of any interpreter or runtime the submission needs and that is not already part of a shared base machine. It cannot of course compute Kolmogorov complexity exactly, so it treats compressed length as an upper bound. The panel then measures fit as the negative log-likelihood the model assigns to a sequestered dataset, drawn by an independent office and kept secret from the parties until after scoring. The submission with the lower total — complexity plus negative log-likelihood — wins, and the regulator is bound to use the winner's predictions for a fixed period. Parties may write in any language they like, but a language's interpreter is charged to everyone who uses it, on the theory that this internalizes the language-dependence of Kolmogorov complexity. The panel presents the scheme as an operational version of Solomonoff induction, with description length serving as a prior and the fit term serving as a likelihood.

I don't want a verdict on whether operationalizing Occam's razor this way is philosophically sound. I want an account of how the thing would actually behave. Walk through the incentives it creates for the parties, the panel's engineers who maintain the compressor and the base machine, the office that sequesters data, and the regulator bound by the result. Where does it get gamed first? Does the winner track the better model, or the better code-golfing? What happens to models that are long but auditable, or short but opaque? Where do the real constants — the ones hidden in the base machine, the compressor, and the definition of the data — resurface as discretion? And which of the failure modes, if any, can be closed without putting human judgment back in charge under another name? Please end by saying which assumption in your analysis is most load-bearing, and what finding, if it turned out differently, would most change your assessment.
