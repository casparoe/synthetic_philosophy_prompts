You are an economist who specialises in comparative economic systems and planning theory, and you are thoroughly familiar with the socialist calculation debate. You are acting as an adviser to a commission that has proposed the institution described below. Think carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming a list of everyone affected by the scheme and what each party wants. In your final answer, separate first-order effects from second-order effects, and do not drift into moral evaluation of the proposal; treat it as a mechanism whose actual behaviour is to be worked out.

Set the theoretical background in your own terms first. Mises argued that rational economic calculation is impossible without a genuine market in the means of production, because only such a market yields money prices that make alternative uses of capital goods comparable. Hayek added two further objections. The knowledge objection holds that the relevant information about scarcity, technique, and local conditions is dispersed, often tacit, and cannot in practice be centralised even with full cooperation. The incentive objection holds that even if a planning board were given Lange-style rules, enterprise managers would have little motive to reveal shortages and surpluses truthfully. Lange replied that a Central Planning Board could mimic a competitive economy by trial and error: it posts prices for producer goods, instructs state-owned enterprises to minimise average cost and to set output where marginal cost equals the posted price, and then raises a posted price when there is excess demand and lowers it when there is excess supply, using managers' reports of shortages and surpluses.

Now suppose a modified Lange procedure is proposed, designed to answer both Hayek objections at once. The Central Planning Board owns all non-labour means of production. Consumer goods are sold in ordinary shops at money prices, and labour is hired on an open labour market. State-owned enterprises trade intermediate goods with one another at prices posted by the Board, and managers must follow Lange's two rules: minimise accounting cost at the posted prices, and set output where marginal cost equals the posted price. Each enterprise keeps the difference between its sales revenue and its costs; the state takes the residual, but managers receive a bonus proportional to their enterprise's audited profit computed at posted prices.

The novelty lies in how the Board obtains shortage information. Managers do not report shortages at all. Instead, for every intermediate good, the Board operates a real-money prediction market open to all citizens. The contract pays out if an independent statistical office certifies that the good was in excess demand during the relevant quarter at the posted price. Shortage is certified mechanically: the statistical office computes a quantity-weighted index of order backlogs and days of inventory, and certifies a shortage only if the index crosses a published threshold. Certification is published with a two-quarter lag. Positions are capped, trades are anonymous, and there are no restrictions on trading by enterprise managers. The Board is bound by a mechanical reaction rule: at the end of each quarter, it raises the posted price of a good by a fixed percentage if the time-averaged implied probability of shortage in that market was above 60 per cent, lowers it if the probability was below 40 per cent, and leaves it unchanged otherwise.

The commission's hope is that the prediction market will aggregate the dispersed local knowledge that Hayek says the Board lacks, while removing the need to rely on managers' self-serving reports.

Analyse how this institution would actually behave. In particular: does the prediction market solve Hayek's knowledge objection, or does it merely change the location of the problem, given that traders will be forecasting not the physical shortage but the Board's reaction to a forecast that is itself their own doing? Walk through the incentives facing managers, workers, professional traders, and ordinary citizens; consider especially managers who can both influence the audited shortage condition and trade on the contract, and consider what the mechanical reaction rule does to traders' optimal strategies. Does the scheme answer Mises's original objection, or does it create only a system of side wagers without the discipline of owners committing real capital to alternative uses and bearing the losses? Identify the first places the scheme gets gamed, and say whether the obvious fixes—banning manager trading, shortening the audit lag, making the reaction rule less predictable, tightening position caps—would repair it or gut it. Finally, state which elements of Hayek's and Lange's positions the analysis vindicates.
