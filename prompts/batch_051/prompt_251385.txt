Consider a letter-string analogy microdomain of the kind used in the Copycat project associated with Douglas Hofstadter. A system is shown the pair `abc -> abd` and asked to extend the relation to new strings. A deflationary description says it has extracted a fixed rule: replace the rightmost letter by its successor. The rule works without strain for `ijk -> ijl`, but it seems to slide around as the strings change. Moved to `xyz`, the operation might reasonably migrate to the leftmost letter and become a predecessor operation, producing `wyz`; moved to a string such as `aabbb`, it might reasonably treat the repeated block `bbb` as the object of change and replace the block with its successor group, producing `aaccc`. Please explain the conceptual issue these cases bring out. In particular: (a) what is conceptual slippage in this setting, and why is it different from a system that simply stores several rigid rules and selects among them by surface features of the string; (b) what the small, tightly constrained microdomain contributes — that is, why placing slippage in such an artificial setting is supposed to make it visible rather than leaving us with a toy curiosity; and (c) what would distinguish a computational model that genuinely exhibits conceptual slippage from one that merely reproduces the same outputs without the underlying fluidity.
