I co-teach an undergraduate philosophy-of-mathematics seminar of about fifteen students, one eighty-minute session, and I want an activity that makes them feel, rather than merely be told, why the open-set definition of continuity is not an arbitrary convention but a definition that earned its place. A friend and I just spent a long argument about this: he kept saying the topological definition is only the one that happened to generalize, and I kept insisting there is a reason it outcompeted the alternatives. Design the activity around the move from metric to topological continuity, with concrete rules and materials, so that students first propose their own definitions of continuity, then test them against a bench of agreed cases and a challenge round of functions on spaces where distance is or is not available, so that they feel the pressure toward the preimage-of-open-sets formulation themselves. Include a debrief mapping each failed definition to the case or theorem that defeated it, noting what will predictably go wrong when I run it — for example, students quietly smuggling in distances through sequences or coordinates — and an honest statement of what the exercise cannot reproduce, namely the accumulated mathematical judgment that this definition buys the right theorems, which the instructor still has to supply. The students should leave with a live sense that definitions are chosen for a job: cover the clear cases, exclude the bad ones, and generalize under pressure.
