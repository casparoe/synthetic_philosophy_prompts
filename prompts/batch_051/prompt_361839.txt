Write a short dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about how to classify a delusion. The case is Capgras delusion: a patient insists that his wife has been replaced by a near-identical impostor, even though he generally treats her as he always has and cannot be moved by his family's protests.

One character holds that the patient's statement expresses a genuine belief, and that the two-factor theory explains its formation: a neuropsychological anomaly produces a compelling but misleading experience, such as the absence of the usual felt familiarity when he looks at his wife, and a second deficit in belief evaluation allows an absurd interpretation of that experience to become fixed. The other character denies that the patient genuinely believes what he says. He argues that the state fails too many of the functional tests for belief, since it does not consistently guide action, inference, or emotion the way the patient's standing beliefs about his wife do, and he proposes that it is better understood as a pathological imagining, a compelled verbal report, or a fragment of a delusional world the patient only half-endorses.

Both characters should be equally sharp, press the hardest version of the other's position rather than a simplified one, and answer one another's actual points instead of talking past each other. Keep the dialogue under 900 words, and write it in British English.
