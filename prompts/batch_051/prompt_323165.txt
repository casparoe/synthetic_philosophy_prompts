I have been thinking about gratitude on my own, without any academic background in philosophy, and I keep tripping over what the word actually picks out. I do not just mean the warm feeling; I mean the state we say someone ought to be in when a benefit has come their way, and the judgement we make when we call someone ungrateful. I would like you to propose a working definition of gratitude, in that everyday sense, that gets the following cases right.

1. A friend drives you to hospital at two in the morning and stays with you for hours. Afterwards you feel warm towards them and go out of your way for them. I take this to be a clear case of gratitude.
2. A stranger finds your lost wallet and posts it back with every card and note inside, asking nothing. You say you are "so grateful". I also take this to be clear.
3. A storm changes course overnight and spares your house. You catch yourself saying you are "grateful", but there is no one to be grateful to. I do not know whether that is gratitude or just relief wearing gratitude's clothes.
4. A well-meaning relative quietly pays off a debt you never asked them to pay. You did not want the help, and now it sits between you as something they expect to be thanked for. Are you ungrateful if you mostly resent it?
5. Your grandfather saved for years to help pay for your education but died before you were old enough to thank him. It matters to you to say you are grateful to him, even though he cannot receive it. Is that gratitude, or something adjacent — respect, or a debt to a memory?
6. On a train, someone's careless stumble knocks you clear of a falling sign. They never meant to help, and never even realise what happened. You feel something intense towards them. Is that gratitude, or merely a sense of owing someone an accident?
7. You are glad that a rival for a job withdrew because of a family crisis. I would insist that this is not gratitude, however much it benefits you, because nothing like a benefit was offered to you.
8. You think about having been born in a wealthy, stable country and feel something that sounds almost like gratitude. But no one chose it for you, and the arrangements behind it are tangled up with a good deal of injustice. Can one be grateful for an inherited advantage of that kind?

I am fairly confident about 1, 2 and 7. The others I keep limping between two answers on. Please propose a definition, run it through all eight cases, and tell me where it surprises you — where it forces a verdict I was not expecting, or contradicts one I said I held. If it gets one of the clear cases wrong, say so and revise the definition rather than papering over the problem.

Then, because I am less interested in the label than in the obligations that trail behind it, tell me what your definition implies about what, if anything, a grateful person owes to benefactors who meant to help, to luck or accident, and to the past. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the proposed definition as a whole, and single out the specific point you are least sure of.
