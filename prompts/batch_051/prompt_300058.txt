I'm in a class on ancient atomism and I've got an argument I can't get out of my head. It seems to prove atoms can't touch, which would wreck the whole Leucippus and Democritus setup, so something in it must be off, but every step looks right to me. Here it is, numbered:

(1) On the atomist picture there are only two things, atoms and void. So when two atoms meet, either some bit of one atom is exactly where some bit of the other is, or there's void between them.
(2) If there's void between them, they haven't met, so that isn't touching.
(3) Thus touching requires at least some part of one atom to be exactly where some part of the other atom is.
(4) But if part of atom A is exactly where part of atom B is, the same region is filled by both atoms at once.
(5) Atoms are impenetrable—that's the whole point of calling them solid and full, unlike the void, which is empty and gives way. Two atoms can't occupy the same region.
(6) So atoms can't touch.
(7) But the atomists need atoms to collide, rebound, and latch onto each other by their shapes to build worlds. That's contact.
(8) So the atomists' own commitments make their physics impossible.

I'd most like too keep (5), because without impenetrability an atom is just empty space with a name. So where does this argument go wrong, and which premise do I give up? What does giving it up cost the rest of the atomist story about collisions and world formation? If the right move is to say atoms don't strictly touch but something else happens at a collision, spell that out in atomist-friendly terms.
