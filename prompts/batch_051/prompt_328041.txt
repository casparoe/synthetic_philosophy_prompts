After a long argument with a friend about rigid designators and the necessary a posteriori, I wrote down the claims I found myself endorsing in the course of the discussion. I suspect there may be strains among them, but I cannot tell which strains are real, which are merely apparent, or what I would have to sacrifice to resolve them, so I am listing the claims flatly rather than arranging them into a position.

1. Proper names are rigid designators: each picks out the same object in every possible world in which that object exists.
2. Natural kind terms are rigid as well: once the reference of 'water' has been fixed in the actual world, the term picks out that same kind in every possible world.
3. True identity statements that involve only rigid designators are, if true, necessary: 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' and 'water is H2O' could not have been false.
4. Those necessary identities are nonetheless a posteriori: we had to discover empirically that Hesperus is Phosphorus and that water is H2O, and no amount of conceptual reflection alone would have settled either.
5. The difference between 'Hesperus is Hesperus' and 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is a genuine difference in what someone knows: a competent speaker who understands both names can rationally, and without any confusion, be certain of the former while doubting the latter.
6. Names contribute only their referents to the propositions expressed, so 'Hesperus is Hesperus' and 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' express the very same singular proposition, namely the proposition that Venus is self-identical.
7. Whether a sentence is trivial, informative, a priori, or a posteriori is determined by the proposition the sentence expresses, not by the sentence itself or by the words used to express it.
8. Had the environment in which our word 'water' was introduced contained the clear, drinkable, lake-filling stuff XYZ rather than H2O, 'water' would have rigidly referred to XYZ instead, and 'water is H2O' would not have been true.

(3) Apparent tensions that dissolve (about 250 words). Identify any commitments that look as though they conflict but can be accepted together once the relevant distinctions are drawn, and explain how the appearance of conflict arises and why it is only apparent.

(5) The single revision that helps most (about 150 words). Tell me which one change to the list would resolve the most trouble at the lowest cost, what tension it leaves behind, and whether the revised set is one a reasonable person could hold.
