Been thinking about Nietzsche’s *On the Use and Abuse of History for Life*, especially the bit in the preface where he says:

“We need it for life and action, not as a convenient way to avoid life and action, or to excuse a selfish life and a cowardly or base action. We would serve history only so far as it serves life; but to value its study beyond a certain point mutilates and degrades life.”

He then claims history serves life in three ways: monumental history holds up past greatness as a model for action, antiquarian history preserves and cherishes the everyday past, and critical history drags the past before a court and condemns it.

Here’s a border case that’s been bothering me. A local historical society has spent years digitizing everything: old tax records, diaries, maps, even menus. When a researcher publishes evidence that the town’s founder helped lead a violent land seizure, the society links the paper from its homepage and archives it carefully, but also keeps the founder’s statue, the annual Founder’s Day parade, and the name of the central square. Their line is: our job is preservation and remembrance, not judgement.

Is that history in the service of life, in Nietzsche’s sense? Does it fall under one of the three modes, or is the three-way scheme too coarse to handle it? And does this case show that the “serves life” criterion is doing the real work while the three modes are just a rough taxonomy?
