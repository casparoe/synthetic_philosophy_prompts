I am an undergraduate in a course on judgment under uncertainty, and I have noticed an asymmetry in how I treat evidence about my own projects versus evidence about other people's projects.

When I estimate how long my own dissertation chapter will take, I enter into the specifics: the structure of my argument, the sources I already know, my recent writing pace, and the particular difficulties of my topic. I treat the base rate of completion times for students in my program as background information rather than as the central judgment. But when a fellow student estimates how long his chapter will take, and he offers similarly detailed specifics, I mostly disregard his specifics and start from the base rate. I think, in effect, that he is too inside his own case, while I feel justified being inside mine.

Is there any defensible principle that would justify this asymmetry, or is it simply a self-serving inconsistency I live with? One candidate justification is that I genuinely have more access to my own attention span, my own capacity for self-deception, and the quality of my own outline than I have to his. But I am not sure that access does the work, because my stronger access is also accompanied by stronger motives to flatter myself. I want to know what the strongest version of the asymmetry would look like, and whether it survives when I imagine my friend and me having identical observable records, equally detailed outlines, and the same amount of evidence. Under those conditions, should I keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up? If my framing of this as an inside/outside asymmetry is itself misleading, I want to be told that directly rather than getting an answer that politely works within it.
