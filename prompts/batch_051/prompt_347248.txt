I spent most of my life in geophysics and only started reading formal epistemology after I retired, so forgive me if I mangle some terminology. I keep coming back to the Condorcet jury theorem. As I understand it: if n voters each have an independent probability p > 0.5 of getting a yes/no factual question right, and the group votes by simple majority, then as n grows the probability that the majority is correct goes to 1. In the lab we used to say that independant instruments can share a common calibration error, and I suspect the same sort of thing breaks the theorem's independence assumption. So here is the variant I would like someone to work through. Suppose each voter has probability p equals, say, 0.55 of being right, except that with probability c equals 0.2 a 'shared calibration event' occurs: some common source (a miscalibrated standard, a widely recieved but misleading summary) sends the same signal to every voter, and in that event every voter votes according to that common signal, which has probability s equals 0.6 of being correct. Nobody can tell when such an event has happened. What does the probability that the majority is correct converge to as n gets large? Does it still converge to 1, or to something lower, and how does the asymptote depend on c and s? Is a very large jury still better than a single voter with the same marginal accuracy? And if the common signal is the problem, what actually fixes it: adding more voters from the same pool, or splitting the voters into groups that each rely on a different source and then taking a majority of the groups' decisions? Run the calculation and also say in plain words which part of Condorcet's setup is doing the real work.
