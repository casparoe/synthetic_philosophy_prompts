Been chewing on a thing I read a while back about Anscombe's protest over Oxford giving Truman that honorary degree, and a friend has now argued me into a corner. Her line, as I understand it, is that the bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki were aimed at whole cities, so the civilian deaths were not just a bad thing he foresaw while going after a military target; the killing of civilians (or at least their terror) was the very thing meant to force Japan to surrender. That, she says, makes it murder rather than just a grim act of war.

I keep trying to push back and end up in this argument, which I can't fault step by step. (1) If you choose a plan knowing it will kill civilians, you are responsible for those deaths. (2) Truman chose the bombings knowing they would kill civilians, and so did a general we can call G, who bombs a munitions factory knowing the workers' housing is right next to it and the civilian deaths are as certain as the explosion. (3) The only difference between the two is meant to be that Truman's civilians were the means (their deaths or terror were what was supposed to force surrender), whereas G's civilians are a side effect he merely forsees and would be glad to avoid. (4) But this means/side-effect line can't do moral work, because both men chose a plan knowing the civilian deaths were part of what would happen; if G really couldn't accept those deaths he could refuse the raid, and if Truman couldn't accept his he could refuse the bomb. Saying one set of deaths is 'means' and the other 'side effect' just records how the deaths happen to be wired into each plan, and moral responsibility shouldn't hinge on that wiring. (5) So either G is as much a murderer as Truman, or Truman is no more a murderer than G. (6) The first horn makes nearly every deliberate factory raid that knowingly kills civilians into mass murder, which seems to drain the word; the second horn lets Truman off, and I don't want to let him off. So the distinction the whole debate turns on seems to either collapse or not matter.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep the idea that Truman's act was murder while G's is not, or at least not murder. If you think (4) is the weak spot, explain in plain terms what the intended/foreseen distinction actually does, and whether Anscombe can hold it without either convicting G or watering down what was wrong with Hiroshima. And please tell me where your own answer is shakiest: what argument or variation on the case would most likely change your mind.
