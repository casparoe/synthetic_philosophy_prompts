I'm a volunteer EMT, and for the past few months I've been reading philosophy in my spare time — mostly stuff about whether you're actually obligated to save the greater number when you can't save everyone. I've ended up with a view that I know is going to go down badly at work. In a situation where I can save one stranger or five strangers, I don't think the fact that there are five on one side is, by itself, the thing that settles it. Each of them is a whole person, and if I automatically head for the five, the one never even gets a look. I'm not saying I'd always save the one; I'm saying fairness might require giving the one a real chance — maybe a weighted coin — rather than letting the numbers do all the deciding.

Next week I'm supposed to present this at are monthly training night, to about twenty EMTs and paramedics, a couple of whom are instructors. This crew treats triage as basically sacred: do the greatest good, save the most salvageable, don't freeze up second-guessing yourself. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a philosophy lecture. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually hear from this audience, in the words they'd use (I expect at least one "so you'd let five people die because of a coin flip?"), ordered by how likely and how damaging they are. For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a note on where that reply is weakest. Then tell me the question I should be praying nobody asks — and be straight with me about whether the view even survives the evening.
