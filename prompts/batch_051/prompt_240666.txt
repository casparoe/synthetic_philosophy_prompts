i've had a little framework rattling around in my head for years and i want it stress-tested before i keep using it. i call it bid-ask modesty. the setup: any claim you believe gets two quotes, like a trade. the bid is the worst odds you'd still accept to bet that it's true; the ask is the odds you'd demand to bet that it's false. if the two quotes are identical you've got a point estimate; if they're far apart you're running a spread, and i say the width of that spread just is intellectual modesty. a friend of mine will defend his startup thesis at even money and also take the other side at even money; i'd defend mine at even money too, but you'd have to offer me maybe 3-to-1 to bet against it. our resting guesses are pretty much the same, but i'm running a wider spread, and i think it's fair to call me more modest. and no, i'm not just talking about cases where i'm nervous—the same thing happens when i'm the expert in the room. the actual claim i want vetted is the normative one: modesty is a virtue only when, and exactly insofar as, the spread tracks how much hidden information the person on the other side could plausibly have relative to you. a wide spread against rank amateurs or agreeable friends who'd never correct you isn't humility, it's a failure to price the evidence you already have; a tight spread against someone who's read six papers you haven't is arrogance. so humility on my account is a market-making policy for beliefs, not low self-regard and not a taste for hedging. i can already here the objection that this is just imprecise probabilities with finance-bro language, and maybe it is, though i think the normative bit about matching the spread to your counterparty is doing separate work. give me an honest assessment—the strongest version, the nearest existing idea, what it actually gets right, where it breaks, and whether it's actually distinct—as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction, no conclusion.
