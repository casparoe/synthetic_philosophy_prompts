I edit the Ideas section of a science and ideas magazine, and roughly once a week I have to decide how to frame an expert controversy for a general audience: is the disagreement between two researchers or two schools a substantive empirical or philosophical disagreement, or is it merely a disagreement about words? Recent examples from our pages have included whether viruses are alive, whether Pluto is a planet, whether addiction is a disease, and whether a large language model can be said to understand language. I have two worries. On the one hand, our writers and I sometimes conclude too quickly that a dispute is just semantics, and I suspect that some controversies we dismiss as definitional actually encode substantive empirical bets or normative commitments. On the other hand, I do not want to commission a long feature about a clash of terminological preferences. The stakes are editorial trust: if we tell readers a controversy is verbal when it is not, we have misreported the state of a field, and if we present a definitional squabble as a real fight, we have wasted their attention.

I know some of the philosophical machinery but not how to run it reliably. I know the rough idea that a dispute is merely verbal when the parties agree on all the nonlinguistic facts and differ only in how they use a word, the technique of banning the disputed term and asking each side to restate its position without it, and the further complication that some disputes concern not which concept is correct but which concept we ought to use. But I do not want a summary of that literature; I want a decision procedure an editor without graduate training could run in under an hour, with an output that would be defensible to our news editor before we assign a story.

Please write that procedure for me. It should be an ordered set of tests or a checklist in words. For each step, say what question I am asking, what kind of answer counts as sufficient to move on, where the procedure concludes that the dispute is merely verbal, and where it concludes that the dispute is substantive. Mark explicitly the points at which the procedure cannot proceed mechanically and must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is a value choice or a judgment call. Then run the procedure on three demonstrations: whether viruses are alive, whether Pluto is a planet, and whether a large language model can be said to understand language. On that third case my own impulse is to declare the whole thing verbal and move on; I want the procedure to show me whether that impulse is defensible or lazy. It should also be honest about the ways it will misfire, including speakers who cannot articulate the rule behind a useful distinction they draw, terms with partially overlapping concepts, and wording that itself carries downstream consequences for law, funding, or education.
