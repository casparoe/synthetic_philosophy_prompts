In a jurisprudence course, the instructor introduced the problem of desuetude—the idea that a statute may lapse as law through long, consistent non-enforcement—and set out three conditions for a rule to count as law currently in force, as opposed to a merely unrepealed text: (1) it was enacted through the proper procedures by a body with legislative authority; (2) it has not since been repealed or judicially invalidated; and (3) it is actually treated by officials as a live standard for decisions, not as a dead letter. Now consider a city ordinance enacted in 1902 that makes it a misdemeanor to operate a horse-drawn carriage in the central district. Passed just as motor vehicles were displacing carriages, it has never been the basis of a prosecution; officers do not cite it; successive city attorneys have described it as obsolete; and a modern ordinance regulates carriage tours under a different, more demanding scheme. The old ordinance was never repealed. Last month, a prosecutor sought to charge a carriage-tour operator under it in a case the modern scheme does not reach, arguing that the statute is still valid law because it satisfies the first two conditions and has merely been underused. The defense objects that reviving a statute the public has long treated as dead would defeat the fair notice and reliance interests the rule of law is supposed to protect. Does the old ordinance count as law in the relevant sense? Go through each condition and say whether it is satisfied or only partly satisfied, then evaluate whether the three conditions themselves draw the boundary in the right place—that is, whether the dispute is really about the statute, about the criteria, or about an independent rule-of-law principle the criteria omit.
