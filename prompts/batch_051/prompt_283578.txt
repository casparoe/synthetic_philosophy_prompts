I'm the compliance lead for a company that makes household chemicals and rechargeable battery packs, and I'm the one who signs hazard labels — flammable, corrosive, irritant, poisonous if inhaled, that sort of thing. I do maybe three of these a week, and the downside is a recall or a fine if I get it wrong, but if I'm too cautious the warehouse stops shipping and an operations VP is at my desk. The current rule is: run the standardized test, classify off what actually happens. If it didn't do the bad thing in the test, no label. I've come to think that rule gets things wrong in two directions, and I want you to help me replace it with a real procedure, not a vibe.

One: sometimes the test changes the thing it's testing. We have a concentrate whose corrosive ingredient gets driven off during the drying step of the corrosivity test, so the test says "not corrosive", but the stuff on the shelf still is. A philosopher friend called this a "fink": the disposition vanishes when you poke it.

Two: sometimes the thing has the property but the test stops it showing up. We sell a liquid that will burn just fine once its vapor-suppressing additive has evaporated, but the standard test puts a flame to it while the additive is still active, so it passes as not flammable. The property is really there; it's wearing a mask.

The case that actually keeps me up is a battery pack with a pressure release that shuts down thermal runaway if you short it. A fresh unit passes the short-circuit test. But the release can corrode shut after a year in humid storage. Is the pack "short-circuit-hazardous" or not? The bad disposition is still inside the cell, but the thing that would stop it showing up is a part that can fail.

So: write me a checklist, or a flow in words, that a non-philosopher on my team can run in 20 minutes per product to decide wether a hazard counts as present for labeling, given that tests can fink or mask the thing. Don't make us read the metaphysics; define any jargon at the point of use and keep the rest in plain language. It should tell us what evidence lets us label a hazard present despite a clean test, and what lets us call it absent despite a scary worst-case story. It should force us to record the conditions the label is true under, so the label reads "not flammable while the suppressant is intact" rather than just "not flammable". Then run the procedure on the three cases above, make sure at least one of them comes out in a way I won't like, and say plainly what the procedure can't settle and where I still have to make a judgement call.
