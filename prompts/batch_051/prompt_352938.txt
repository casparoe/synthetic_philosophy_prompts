I have spent the past eighteen months as a machine learning engineer on the growth team of a consumer application, and I have recently been moved onto a project that has left me unsettled. Our team's mandate is engagement: we tune the feed-ranking model, the wording and timing of notifications, autoplay and endless-scroll behaviors, and the scheduling of recommendations, and we are scored on session length and daily active use. The same company also ships a set of features aimed at helping people manage their time on the product—a dashboard showing minutes per day, optional per-app limits, and a reminder that interrupts unusually long sessions. I was asked to build that reminder into the same product whose ranking model is, elsewhere, evaluated on how rarely users interrupt their own sessions. That apparent contradiction is what I want to understand.

What view of human welfare, agency, or autonomy does this arrangement presuppose? It seems to me the engagement side presupposes something like revealed preference: what people keep doing is the best evidence of what they truly want, so keeping them in the app serves them. The wellbeing side seems to presuppose something else: that a person's considered judgment outside the session has authority over their within-session choices, or that the customer in the dashboard and the user in the feed are in an important sense two different agents. Are these genuinely incompatible, or can the two halves be reconciled by one consistent theory—for instance, that users have stable higher-order preferences and the product systematically hijacks lower-order impulses, so the same respect for the person requires both optimizing the feed and interrupting it? Be precise: distinguish what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify where the arrangement is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories could license both halves. Then tell me what would concretely have to change in the product if the company replaced the engagement side's implicit theory with a non-preference-based account of welfare—say, one on which attention itself is a scarce good that can be wasted even when people freely give it.
