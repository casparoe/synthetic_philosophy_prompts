I'm a cog sci PhD student, and I've somehow ended up refereeing a two-month standoff in our reading group between a die-hard Fodorian and someone from the massive-modularity/evo-psych camp, and they still havent actually agreed on what would settle anything. The Fodorian only grants genuine modularity to peripheral input systems — vision, early language parsing, maybe some motor control — where you get the full list of Fodor's marks: domain-specificity, informational encapsulation, mandatory and fast operation, shallow outputs, and fixed neural implementation. Central cognition (belief fixation, weighing evidence, analogical reasoning) is, on this view, domain-general, isotropic, and Quinean, and calling it 'modular' is either a metaphor or a category mistake. The massive modularist says that's biologically bizarre: natural selection would have built a mess of fast, specialized systems — cheater detection, face recognition, theory of mind, folk physics, mate choice — each with its own proprietary representations, and what we call 'domain-general reasoning' is either a confederation of those systems or a description of our ignorance. They each think the other is stretching the word 'module' to save there picture.

I'd like to have them produce the adversarial-collaboration document they should have written at the start. Format it as a numbered 'agreed claims' section followed by a disagreements table, then a cruxes section. The agreed-claims should cover what makes something modular in the first place, which of Fodor's marks a system has to satisfy to earn the label, and what each camp takes to be evidence for and against treating central cognition as modular — phrased so both can sign without wincing. The disagreements table should state each position in wording its own side would accept, not a strawman the other wrote. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions where an answer would actually move at least one side. I suspect one crux is the content effects in the Wason selection task: the massive modularist sees them as evidence of a domain-specific cheater-detection system, while the Fodorian says they only show that central reasoning can recruit domain-specific knowledge while remaining general-purpose — but I don't know what prediction each would stake on a genuinely novel content domain, or what experimental outcome both would treat as counting against them. Other things worth testing as cruxes: whether 'domain-general' is a substantive computational claim or a name for our ignorance, and whether the fact that outputs of one central 'module' are usable by others breaks encapsulation in a way that matters. For each crux, give the concrete case or experiment both sides agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. If some of the fight turns out to be verbal, say which part and why.
