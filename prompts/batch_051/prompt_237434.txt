Biostatistics PhD student here, first year, and I've now run into Simpson's paradox three times in a single month: once in a clinical trial where the new drug looks better than standard of care in the aggregate but worse within every recruiting site, once in an old college-admissions-style example where each department admits men at a lower rate but women lose out overall, and once in a friend's observational study where adding a covariate flipped the sign of the exercise–heart-disease effect. The arithmetic makes total sense to me — I can see that the group sizes and baseline risks differ, I can do the weighted-average algebra — but I don't think I actually know what the paradox is trying to tell me. People around me draw totally different morals: don't pool data, always stratify, find the right covariate, correlation is not causation, think causally, block on everything. A senior grad student said the real lesson is that there's no purely statistical fix for confounding, but then couldnt articulate what a non-statistical fix would look like in practice.

What determines whether a stratifying variable is the kind of thing I should adjust for, the kind I shouldn't, or the kind I must not?
