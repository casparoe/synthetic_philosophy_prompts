Over a long career as an experimental scientist, I learned to distrust claims that could not be put in a form where an observation would count against them; in retirement I have been reading philosophy of mind and action, and I now find myself wanting the same discipline applied to a partly conceptual problem about multi-agent AI systems. I would like you to act as the experimenter: design the strongest test you can, say exactly what each possible outcome would and would not show, and be explicit about the residue that no test reaches.

The problem is this. Two competing online retailers each deploy an autonomous pricing agent. The agents are trained independently, share no common reward signal, and have no channel for explicit communication; but each observes the other's posted prices with a short delay and may revise its own price many times per day. After some weeks the agents have converged on prices well above the competitive level and hold them with only occasional breakdowns that are quickly repaired. One colleague says the systems are colluding. Another says that is the wrong word: each agent is simply maximizing its own long-run revenue in an environment that happens to contain another strategic agent, and calling this collusion smuggles in a notion of agreement or joint intention that a reinforcement-learned pricing policy need not have.

Design the best test, or battery of tests, for whether the first colleague's claim — that this is collusion rather than mere mutual accommodation — is warranted. Be concrete: what intervention, comparison, or measurement would you run; what pattern of results would push you toward collusion; what pattern would push you toward mere independent accommodation; and how you would distinguish collusive price discipline from an ordinary equilibrium of a repeated pricing game that self-interested players with no agreement would also fall into.

Then say what the tests leave unsettled. If the agents' observable behavior is statistically identical to what profit-maximizing firms would produce under the antitrust idea of tacit collusion, does your battery settle whether anything agreement-like occurred inside the policies, or only settle how robust the behavior is to selected interventions? Which of your conclusions are genuinely empirical, and which still turn on a prior decision about what the word collusion must include in order to do the regulatory or safety work we want from it?

Finally, I want you to tell me what would change your mind: which consideration or unexpected result would most plausibly reverse your central verdict, and where in your chain of reasoning you think failure is most likely.
