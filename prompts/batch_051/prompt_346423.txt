I write about science and ideas for a metropolitan paper, and in that capacity I serve as the community representative on the ethics and community advisory committee of our regional hospital system. The committee has been handed a dispute about our spiritual-care service. The chaplaincy was built around credentialed clergy from several major traditions, but our patient population now identifies largely as non-religious, and our surveys and bereavement follow-up calls routinely surface unmet needs around meaning, isolation, and existential distress among patients who explicitly do not want a religious visit. A working group has proposed creating two secular spiritual-care positions — one a former hospice chaplain who no longer identifies as religious, one trained in contemplative practice — to see patients who decline religious care but request what they describe as spiritual or existential support. The chaplains object that this is a category error: without a tradition, a text, a community, and some metaphysics, spiritual care is just counseling by another name; there is no shared curriculum, no credentialing body, and no way to distinguish competent practice from unstructured affirmation, and the money would be better spent on social work and palliative care. The proposal's supporters reply that the needs are real and documented, that offering only a religious visitor or no visitor is precisely the kind of inequity the system claims to want to fix, and that a doctrine-free practice can meet patients where they are without smuggling in a creed.

The committee has asked me to draft a short policy memo it can act on. It should recommend whether to create these secular spiritual-care positions at all, and if so, under what working definition of spiritual care and with what guardrails for training, supervision, and scope — above all, how to keep the role distinct from psychotherapy, social work, and wellness coaching, and how to catch a provider who simply re-imports religious content under neutral language. If the recommendation is against the positions, the memo should say what the system ought to do instead for the patients now reporting these needs. Take the strongest version of the opponents' argument seriously and show, rather than assert, where the boundary between non-religious spiritual care and the neighboring practices falls — or argue that no defensible boundary exists. If you think the premise itself is the problem — that the religious/secular framing is the wrong way to carve patient need, or that the notion of secular spiritual care is incoherent in a way that the committee's options are missing — I would rather you say that plainly than write a memo that politely papers over it. The bottom line should be unambiguous.
