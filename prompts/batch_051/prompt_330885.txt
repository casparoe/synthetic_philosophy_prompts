I'm putting together a feature on what counts as evidence, and I want a worked example that doesn't just repeat the usual ravens-paradox spiel. The classic setup: hypothesize that all ravens are black; Hempel noticed that a white shoe seems to confirm it too, because it's a non-black non-raven and the hypothesis is equivalent to all non-black things being non-ravens. Standard Bayesian treatments say the white shoe confirms but only absurdly slightly. Fine.

Here's the variation I want to run. Suppose a sensor network has cataloged every object in a closed valley: exactly one million objects, exactly 1,000 of them ravens, and exactly 500,000 of them non-black. You know these totals but you don't know the overlap; the software now draws an object and tells you it's a white shoe. Consider two versions:

(i) the object is drawn uniformly from the full catalog of one million;
(ii) the system deliberately restricts to the 500,000 non-black objects and draws uniformly from those.

Question: does the white-shoe observation confirm "all ravens are black" in either or both versions? Which version gives stronger evidence, and why? More importantly, does the equivalence between "all ravens are black" and "all non-black things are non-ravens" pull the same weight in both sampling regimes, or does the sampling procedure do most of the work once you make it explicit? I'm trying to figure out wether the standard Bayesian line survives this tidy version or whether it was quietly assuming a sampling scheme all along.
