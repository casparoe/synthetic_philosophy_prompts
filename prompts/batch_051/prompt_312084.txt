I've been kicking around something I call the forecast veto. The claim is that if you are currently deliberating about whether to do something, you can't coherently assign a determinate credence to what you'll end up doing, not as a matter of psychology but as a matter of what a forecast is supposed to be. Here's the argument. A genuine probability should be evidence-responsive and aimed at facts that are independent of the act of assigning it. But a probability about your own present choice isn't like that: the moment you settle on, say, 0.7 that you'll leave your job, that number becomes a fact about your own leaning. If you endorse it, you've decided rather than predicted; if you resist it, you're treating your own best estimate as a distortion, which is a strange position for a forecaster. So deliberation, which requires being genuinely unsettled, and prediction, which requires being settled about what is likely, can't occupy the same moment. I know people defend the idea that you can have credences about your own actions, and I know there's a literature on the difference between asking what will I do and what should I do, but I want to know whether my version is actually a new argument or just a restatement of the old one. What does it get right, and where does it break, for example, can't I be uncertain about my own willpower, or run a prediction market on my future self, or make a forecast about a choice I'll only face after my mood changes? And please be candid: tell me which of your objections is most likely to be wrong and what argument would change your mind.
