I am compiling a taxonomy of objections to stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI), the proposal to cool the planet by injecting reflective sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere. A colleague has argued that every objection advanced in the literature can be assigned to one of six families:

(1) the moral hazard objection — the availability of SAI would weaken the incentive to cut emissions, so that even researching it risks locking in a high-carbon trajectory;

(2) the termination shock objection — if deployment is interrupted by political collapse, technical failure, or deliberate choice, the unmasked greenhouse forcing returns as a temperature rise compressed into a short interval, which is more damaging than the gradual warming it replaced;

(3) the governance objection — SAI is cheap enough and global in its effects that a single state or coalition could deploy it unilaterally and without legitimate authorization, while the temperature target it would pursue would inevitably express some parties' preferences over others';

(4) the distributional objection — sulfate injection would not cool uniformly; it would alter regional precipitation patterns, and some model scenarios suggest it could weaken monsoon systems, so it stands to benefit some regions while harming others in ways that track neither responsibility for warming nor vulnerability to it;

(5) the ocean acidification objection — because SAI leaves atmospheric CO2 in place, it masks the thermal symptom of the carbon problem while ocean acidification and other non-thermal effects of elevated CO2 proceed unchecked;

(6) the epistemic objection — a planetary intervention of this kind cannot be tested at sub-deployment scale, so the decision to deploy would rest on global climate models whose skill at forecasting this exact intervention is unproven and unprovable without running the full experiment.

The colleague then claims there is a seventh objection of a different kind: one that would retain its force even if SAI were shown to be safe, cheap, reversible, equitably governed, and distributed fairly, and even if all six objections above were answered. They call it the residual objection and decline to spell it out.

Your task is to state the strongest candidate for this seventh objection. Give it a name, present it as an argument with premises and conclusion, and make sure it is not a restatement, special case, or combination of any of the six families. Then honestly assess whether it is a good objection: once the empirical and institutional risks are set aside, does it identify a genuinely distinct kind of wrong, or does it collapse into one of the families it claims to surpass, most plausibly moral hazard? I am less interested in how it would fare in practice than in whether it is truly a seventh kind of objection rather than a rhetorical recasting of a familiar one.
