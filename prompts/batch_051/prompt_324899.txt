philosophy phd here, working on a chapter about risk. I keep getting pulled toward an aggregative view: if a policy imposes a one-in-a-million risk of death on ten million people in order to prevent, say, twenty expected deaths from some other cause, that seems almost obviously worth doing, and the fact that we can't ask each person for consent doesn't seem like it should matter much. Refusing to aggregate tiny risks because each person didn't consent looks like it leads to total paralysis — no roads, no vaccines, no fire trucks driving fast. Plus, ex ante, each affected person can be better off in expectation even though a few statistical people will end up dead.

But I also cant shake the intuition that imposing a risk on someone without their consent, in a case where I could have asked, is wrong even when nothing bad actually happens. A contractor who skips a safety check because it's cheaper, and that slightly raises the risk to the people next door, or a delivery driver who takes a shortcut through a playground. It feels like a wrong done to that specific person, not merely a failure to maximize expected value — and I don't think the intuition is just about the victim being identifiable.

I don't want a verdict for one side. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to go, what it would take for the aggregation to go, and whether there's a distinction (ex ante vs ex post, doing vs allowing, compensability, a threshold of de minimis risk, something else) on which both could survive. If you had to broker the truce, what would you grant each side and why?
