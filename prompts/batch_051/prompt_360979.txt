I am taking an upper-level epistemology seminar this term that has been organized around two ideas. The first is the KK principle: if a subject knows a proposition p, then she is in a position to know that she knows p. The second is luminosity, its condition-level analogue: a condition C is luminous just in case, whenever C obtains, the subject is in a position to know, by attending or reflecting, that C obtains. We have been studying anti-luminosity arguments, which aim to show that no nontrivial mental condition is luminous. The central example goes roughly as follows. Consider a series of cases a0 through a100 a few seconds apart, beginning with the subject's hand in ice water and ending with it in comfortably warm water, where each adjacent pair is chosen so that no introspective or behavioral signal could let the subject discriminate one case from the next. Let C be the condition of feeling cold. If C is luminous and the subject is attending throughout, then whenever she is cold in the series she is in a position to know she is cold, and so she does know it. But knowledge requires a margin for error: if she knows she is cold at ai, then she is not in a relevantly close case in which she is not cold, and by construction ai+1 is relevantly close. So her knowing at a0 that she is cold forces C to obtain at a1, which forces C at a2, and so on until a100, where she is plainly warm. Contradiction. So C is not luminous. I accept this much, at least for the purposes of the seminar.

For the final paper I tried to run the same machinery on an ordinary external condition, and the result now looks to me like an argument that proves far too much. Let E be the proposition that the room is dark. Consider a second series b0 through b100 in which a subject watches a room while a dimmer is raised so slowly that each adjacent case is perceptually indiscriminable from the next; b0 is pitch dark and b100 is brightly lit. The argument is this. (1) At b0, the subject looks, believes that E, and, since this is a maximally clear case of a true perceptual belief formed under normal conditions, she knows that E. (2) Margin for error: if the subject knows E at bi, then E obtains at bi+1. For bi+1 is a relevantly close case, and because bi and bi+1 are indiscriminable she would still believe E at bi+1; if E failed there, her belief could too easily have been false, so she would not know E at bi. (3) Inertia of knowledge: if the subject knows E at bi, and bi and bi+1 are indiscriminable from her own point of view, then she knows E at bi+1 as well. Knowledge cannot come and go across two cases that are exactly alike from the subject's perspective. (4) From (1) and (3), she knows E at b1; from (3) again, she knows E at b2, and so on through every bi. Meanwhile, (2) takes the knowledge at each bi to secure E at bi+1. Iterating delivers E at b100. (5) But b100 is brightly lit, so E is false there. Contradiction. (6) Therefore premise (1) is false: she does not know, even in an utterly clear case, that the room is dark. Since any ordinary perceptual belief could be placed at the start of such a gradual series, the same reasoning would show that no ordinary perceptual judgment ever amounts to knowledge.

None of us in the seminar wants to accept (6). I would most like to keep the margin-for-error principle and the thought that there is ordinary perceptual knowledge; I am prepared to give up luminosity, and perhaps the KK principle along with it, but I do not see how to block this external-condition generalization without drawing an apparently ad hoc line between the mental and the nonmental. Where exactly does the argument go wrong, and if I reject the offending premise, what does that cost me in the original debate about luminosity and KK?
