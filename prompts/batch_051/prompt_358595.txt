I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar that's been doing panpsychism for the past few weeks, and the syllabus never connects it to something I keep thinking about. Take the classic split-brain experiments from the 1960s and 70s, done on patients whose corpus callosum was severed to treat severe epilepsy. A word or picture is flashed in the left visual field so it is processed only by the right hemisphere; the patient reports seeing nothing, but their left hand can point to the matching object. Normally that gets discussed as a puzzle about whether there are two subjects in one skull. But we've been doing the combination problem, and I think the same experiments are basically a real-world case for it in reverse. The combination problem asks whether lots of little subjects can add up to one big unified subject; the split-brain cases look like evidence about whether one big subject can come apart into smaller ones.

Our course has covered these positions, roughly: constitutive micropsychism says the macro-level subject is grounded in many micro-subjects, and it gets hit with the subject-summing problem. Panprotopsychism swaps in proto-phenomenal bits that arent themselves subjects, hoping to soften that problem. Cosmopsychism flips it and makes one cosmic subject fundamental, so it inherits a decombination problem about how the one divides into many. And strong emergentism says the macro subject just emerges, which dodges the summing but looks like brute emergence to everyone else. My question is: which of these positions does the split-brain data actually support or embarrass, once you are careful about what the experiments show? Does the apparent fact that severing the corpus callosum leaves two partial centers of agency make the subject-summing problem worse, easier, or irrelevant? And how much of the analysis depends on the contested two-streams interpretation of the split-brain results, as opposed to deflationary readings where there is still one subject with weird divided access? I want a careful mapping, including where each position simply has nothing to say about the case, and what the episode in turn tells us about whether the combination problem is a real problem or just an artifact of how the positions are set up.
