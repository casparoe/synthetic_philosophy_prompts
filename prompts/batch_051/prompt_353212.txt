I am one of the students drafting the rules for a new society at my school, temporarily called the Reasoning Dojo. The starting assumption is that rationality is less like a body of facts one learns and more like a martial art: it consists of moves that must be drilled until they become automatic, and it is better tested through performance than through written essays. We are proposing the following structure.

Members progress through five belts. To advance one belt, a student must pass three katas. A kata is a short, scripted exercise with a fixed rubric, drawn from a small public bank. Examples: (1) given a claim from a news article, state the strongest objection that a reasonable defender of the claim would concede; (2) given a belief and three pieces of new evidence, give a probability update and justify its size; (3) given a plan for a school event, write a pre-mortem naming at least four plausible failure modes; and (4) given a statistical claim, identify the neglected base rate. Senior belts grade the katas against a checklist.

Members also attend weekly sparring. In a spar, two students are randomly assigned opposite sides of a school-policy question and defend their side for four minutes before switching. Spectators watch the list of scoring moves on a screen and tap whenever they see one; a student's sparring score is the number of moves per minute, not a judgement of whether the argument as a whole succeeded. The scoring moves include responding to the strongest version of an opponent's argument, conceding a point while explaining why the concession does not destroy one's position, and stating what evidence would change one's mind. The rubric does not distinguish between a move performed on a true premise and a move performed on a false premise.

Belts carry privileges: third belts and above may chair debates; fourth belts and above may vote on changes to the kata bank and rubrics. Senior belts earn points for grading, and grading duties are assigned to whichever fourth belts volunteer most.

Walk through how this system would actually behave if it ran for two years. What would it incentivise members to practise, and what would it silently select for? Where would it be gamed first, by students seeking belts and by senior belts who grade? Would the kata skills transfer to reasoning about real questions outside the dojo, or would they mostly transfer to performing katas and spars? Distinguish failure modes that could be fixed by changing specific rules from failure modes that seem baked into the analogy between rationality and martial-arts training. Please write your answer in British English.
