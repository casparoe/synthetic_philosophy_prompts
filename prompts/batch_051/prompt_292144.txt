I'm working on a theory of joint action and I keep noticing that the field's stock examples are doing two jobs at once without anyone saying which is which. The classic case is two people carrying a sofa up a flight of stairs, but I want a theory that also handles a flash mob that self-assembles without any shared plan, a committee that votes yes for mutually incompatible reasons, an employee executing a policy they privately oppose, and a trading algorithm that acts for a firm. If a theory gets the sofa case right by requiring a shared intention with common knowledge, it seems to punt on the others; if it goes permissive enough to catch the flash mob, it starts counting a traffic jam as collective action. Before I fix my view I want the specification of what a theory here has to get right, not a survey of who said what.

Give me the desiderata: the cases it must handle, the distinctions it can't collapse (acting together vs acting in parallel, having a shared goal vs having a shared reason, being part of a group vs merely coordinating with it), and the facts about groups and agency it can't just override. For each desideratum, give the motivating case or consideration in a sentence or two. Then mark which are non-negotiable and which are more like camp preferences — some people will insist joint action requires mutual awareness, others won't, and I want to know which side treats that as data rather than doctrine. Flag any pairs you think can't be jointly satisfied, since I'd rather learn that now then later. Finally, in a few sentences, say which single desideratum does the most to separate the Bratman-style shared intention picture, the Gilbert-style joint commitment picture, and the Searle-style we-intention picture from each other — but keep the weight on the list, not a scorecard. Answer in American English.
