I teach a course on epistemology and the philosophy of mind in which I spend several weeks on Sellars's critique of the myth of the given. The target of that critique, as I present it, is the idea that empirical knowledge must ultimately rest on a stratum of brute, non-inferential awareness — something that simply impinges on the mind and, in virtue of being given, confers justification on the judgements built upon it. Sellars's objection is that causal ancestry is not epistemic support: a state can justify a belief only if it already stands in normative, inferential relations to other beliefs, so the supposedly raw stratum turns out to be conceptually organised after all.

Recently I have been reading about the foundations of machine learning, and I have been struck by what may be a structural parallel. In supervised learning the labelled training set often seems to be assigned the role Sellars denies to sensory givens: it is treated as a neutral, pre-theoretical starting point whose authority the model's later generalisation inherits, and its standing is frequently expressed in terms of raw data or letting the data speak. Yet the construction of such a dataset plainly involves prior judgement — the specification of the task, the choice and definition of labels, decisions about class boundaries, and the cleaning and curation of examples. That looks to me like the very circularity Sellars diagnosed, transposed from the sensory given to the data given.

I cannot decide whether this is a deep structural identity, a loose but suggestive analogy, or a mere pun on the word given. I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis. If the mapping is to be taken seriously, should the training examples be aligned with sense-data, with perceptual judgements, or with something else entirely? What would have to be true of machine-learning practice — of the status of evidence, of the role of the task specification, and of the relation between training and validation — for Sellars's argument to transfer with its full force? And does the parallel commit anyone to a prediction that turns out false, for instance about whether larger, cleaner, or more directly recorded datasets could supply the neutral foundation the practice seems to want?
