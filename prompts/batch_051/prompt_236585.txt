I have been developing a principle I call simulative attenuation, and I want an honest assessment of it rather than a sympathetic reconstruction. The principle bears on the debate over expected-value fanaticism about the far future. Standard longtermist reasoning holds that a tiny probability of influencing humanity's long-run trajectory can dwarf the expected value of near-term interventions, because the far-future stakes are astronomically large. I accept that arithmetic, but I think the simulation argument changes the epistemic standing of those stakes in a way the longtermist picture has not absorbed.

I distinguish two kinds of uncertainty about far-future payoffs. Actuarial uncertainty can be represented by a stable probability distribution over outcomes, as with a lottery of known structure. Simulative uncertainty arises when a non-negligible share of the relevant outcomes is fixed by the design choices and termination policies of the simulators who, on Bostrom's simulation argument, may be running our world as an ancestor simulation. The central claim of simulative attenuation is this: to the extent you assign credence to the simulation hypothesis, far-future expected-value estimates shift from actuarial to simulative uncertainty, and under simulative uncertainty the ordinary expected-value framework is the wrong instrument. One should not treat far-future payoffs as draws from a fixed distribution whose probabilities are the best-guess numbers in one's model; one should instead apply a robustness constraint that disproportionately dampens interventions whose entire case rests on a minuscule probability of a vast far-future payoff, while leaving near-term, multiply realizable benefits essentially unaffected. This is not time discounting, and it is not the claim that simulated lives lack moral status.

Example. A longtermist might say that a one-in-ten-thousand chance of preventing a catastrophe that would otherwise extinguish 10^16 future lives carries an expected value of 10^12 lives, and therefore dominates present health interventions. Simulative attenuation replies that the one-in-ten-thousand figure is not actuarial: conditional on our being in a simulation, whether the catastrophe is permitted, how many future lives the simulation contains, and whether our intervention propagates through the simulated timeline are all functions of parameters outside anything we can causally influence, so treating those possibilities as a fixed probability papers over the relevant uncertainty.

I suspect this may collapse into Knightian uncertainty, ambiguity aversion, or ordinary model uncertainty; my conjecture is that the simulation argument adds something specific, namely a principled reason to regard this class of far-future uncertainties as non-actuarial. Please reconstruct the principle in its strongest form, say whether it is already defended under another name, and give me the three strongest objections, distinguishing the fatal ones from those that merely require repair.
