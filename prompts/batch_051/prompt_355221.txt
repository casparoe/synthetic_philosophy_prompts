Bertrand Russell never saw an LLM, but his whole theory of descriptions was aimed at phrases whose surface grammar makes them look like they refer when, on his view, they don't — the old kind of case about the present king of France being bald. Now we have transformers that handle those constructions smoothly: ask one whether the present king of France is bald and it'll say something sensible about there being no such king, without ever assigning anything like the theory's existence and uniqueness conditions and without any explicit logical paraphrase. What do you think Russell would have made of this? I'm not asking whether he'd be impressed; I want you to reconstruct what he'd have to say given his actual commitments. Which would he endorse: (a) the model is just pattern-matching and is doing something shallower than what the theory describes, (b) the model is a surprising empirical confirmation that the semantic content of definite descriptions is exactly what the theory says, just implemented implicitely, or (c) the theory was never a claim about how language is processed, so this tells us nothing about it. Or is there a fourth option he'd be forced into? Pin down which specific thing in his view would have to move if he took these systems seriously. And tell me where your reconstruction is shakiest: what fact about either Russell's actual commitments or how these models represent descriptions would most change your answer.
