I've noticed an asymmetry in how people judge frugality, and I want to know whether it can be justified. Most people admire someone who lives frugally — who mends their own clothes, keeps a working phone long past its upgrade date, or cooks at home instead of eating out — and this admiration does not seem to depend on how much the thrift actually changes; a frugal friend is admired even when the savings are trivial and the waste avoided is negligible. Yet a person who tries to make other people frugal — who urges family members to waste less, campaigns for reduced consumption, or publicly argues for simpler living — tends to be seen as preachy, meddlesome, and intrusive, even when that effort would plausibly save far more money and waste than one person's private frugality ever could. If frugality is genuinely good, why should promoting it be judged worse than quietly practicing it? Give me the strongest principle you can find that explains both judgments, test it against nearby cases, and say honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. In particular, I want to know whether the asymmetry points toward a deontological reading of frugality as a private virtue or duty, or toward a consequentialist reading on which we should care at least as much about producing the outcome as about performing the act.
