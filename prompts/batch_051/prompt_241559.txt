Write a Petrarchan sonnet spoken by a physicist who is trying to decide whether mathematical elegance is evidence for a scientific theory's truth. The octave should make the strongest pro-elegance case: not that beauty is pleasant, but that formal constraints such as symmetry, unification, and economy of principles have repeatedly led to genuine empirical success, and that nature's deepest laws keep turning out to have that same spare, unifying structure. At the turn, the speaker should notice why this apparent track record cannot bear the evidential weight being placed on it: the record is sampled from winners; elegant theories that failed have been dropped from the story; elegance is cheap to produce within a formalism; and a theory's beauty may track our notation rather than the world. The sestet should salvage what can survive that objection, and the closing couplet should state, in terms a philosopher of science would accept, the difference between elegance as a search heuristic or a prior and elegance as posterior confirmation. The poem should leave it genuinely open whether elegance ever carries evidential weight on its own, achieving the kind of fair-mindedness that a specialist would recognize rather than find caricatured. Then, in a short prose note after the poem, separate what is broadly settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, flag the places where the poem leans on a contested reading for the sake of the form, and avoid presenting any contested view as the consensus.
