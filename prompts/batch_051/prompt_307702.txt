I teach political philosophy and lately I keep coming back to the problem of liberal neutrality in AI alignment—roughly, that whoever builds the Al (or the state that licenses it) shouldn't be the one deciding which thick conception of the good is correct, but the AI has to answer value-laden questions somehow. Someone at a workshop floated a mechanism and I want it stress-tested, not in the abstract but as a piece of machinery.

Here's the proposal. Every frontier assistant ships with a 'neutrality filter.' When the model flags a request as contested in a value-laden way (abortion, religious practice, how to vote, what makes a life go well), it can't answer directly. Instead it convenes a micro-assembly: 50 people drawn from a standing paid pool of 40,000, stratified by district. They meet online with a facilitator model for up to 72 hours and must produce a one-paragraph policy for that class of request. The first 36 hours are behind a veil: panelists may not reveal religion, party, profession, income, or ethnic background, and the facilitator kills any proposal that can only be justified by a comprehensive doctrine. If at the end two-thirds accepts a policy, that policy is cached and applied to everyone else whose requests are marked as similar; otherwise the assistant falls back to a neutral explainer that lays out the main views and recommends nothing. Panelists are paid, but the bonus is contingent: the policy goes to a second 25-person assembly that has read the first transcript and votes on whether the policy genuinely counts as public reason. If it fails, the first panelists lose their bonus, and enough failures gets them dropped from the pool. Labs pay per assembly and can appeal a cached policy to a third assembly, but they can't touch the default explainer.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a position on whether neutrality is right. How would it actually behave? Walk through the incentives for users, panelists, labs, and activists. What gets gamed, by whom, and how? What does the pay-for-surviving-review do to the kind of reasoning people produce? What happens to requests the cache doesn't cover, or requests engineered not to trip the flagger? Where does the whole thing break, and which failure modes could be fixed without abandoning the mechanism?
