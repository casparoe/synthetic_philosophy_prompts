I am caught between a theory of exploitation that attracts me on reflection and an intuition I cannot get myself to surrender.

The theory is the mutual-benefit account. On this view, a transaction is not exploitative when the parties consent without fraud or direct coercion and each ends up materially better off than her next-best alternative would make her. I find this account compelling because it explains why ordinary bargains between roughly equal parties do not seem exploitative, because it respects the choices of poor workers rather than judging those choices against a rich-country baseline, and because it declines to condemn exactly the exchanges that improve the worst-off. Applied to a sweatshop, the theory says: if an adult worker freely takes the job and earns more than she could earn in the village, on the family farm, or in the next factory, then the wage is low only by our lights, and the exchange is not exploitative in any morally condemnable sense.

The intuition is that some such jobs are exploitative anyway. It is clearest to me in cases where an employer with overwhelming bargaining power presents take-it-or-leave-it terms to a worker with no acceptable fallback; where the surplus from the exchange is divided overwhelmingly in the employer's favor; and where the worker's consent, though real in the narrow sense, is manufactured by a background of poverty she did not choose and cannot escape. The fact that the worker is better off than she would have been does not extinguish the thought that something is being extracted from her — a fair share of the surplus, control over her labor, or standing as an equal in the terms of the deal.

I do not want to be told which side simply wins. I want the strongest version of each. In particular, give the strongest case that the intuition should be debunked: perhaps it is rich-world revulsion at visible poverty, a confusion between the unfairness of the background and the fairness of the transaction, or a status-quo bias about what sorts of work are acceptable. Then give the strongest case that the theory should be amended: perhaps it ignores unequal bargaining power, vulnerability, domination, or the way consent can be engineered by unacceptable alternatives. Finally, deliver a negotiated verdict. Say what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a reading of 'exploitation' — for example, as a distributive defect, a failure of standing, or a wrong in the terms of exchange — on which a transaction can be both mutually beneficial and genuinely exploitative, so that both the theory's welfare insight and the intuition survive.
