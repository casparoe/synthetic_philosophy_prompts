Me and a few flatmates have been going round on a thought experiment for the old problem that a post online is simultaneously subject to a dozen legal systems. Suppose the world tried to solve it properly, with a treaty. Two versions keep getting proposed. Under the "home rule" version, every online platform chooses a single home country when it launches, and the speech law of that country alone applies to everything on the platform, for every user anywhere; other countries agree not to prosecute, sue over, or block content on the basis of their own speech laws. Under the "strictest-rule" version, any piece of content reachable from a country has to be legal under that country's law, so a post is effectively governed by the most restrictive jurisdiction in which the platform is accessible; platforms may geoblock, but anything they don't block must satisfy every state.

Pick whichever version looks more interesting to you (or a natural variant of it) and analyse it as an actual mechanism rather than a legal principle. I want to know how it would behave in practice: what countries, platforms, governments and litigants would do in response, what kinds of speech would thrive or vanish, how it would be gamed, and where the fixes for those games create second-order problems. Don't just tell me it would be good or bad in the abstract. At the end I want a committed verdict: does the scheme survive its own incentives and collateral damage, and if it doesn't, is the failure a bug you can patch or the same problem the internet already has wearing a different jacket?
