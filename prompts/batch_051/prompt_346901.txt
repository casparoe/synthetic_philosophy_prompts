At lunch last week two devs on my team, Dan and Priya, got into it over whether "democratic backsliding" is a real, usable concept or just a partisan label, and I've been chewing on it since. They were both making decent points and I honestly couldnt tell wether they were disagreeing about the facts or using the word differently. Here's the exchange as I remember it.

1. Dan opened with: backsliding is a specific pattern, not old-school authoritarianism. Leaders get elected, then start disabling the institutions that could remove them—courts, election commissions, independent media—while keeping elections formally intact. Hungary after 2010 and Poland after 2015 are the clean cases: nobody cancelled voting, they tilted the field and then kept winning on the tilted field. If there's a repeated pattern we refuse to name, he said, we're stuck describing symptoms forever.

2. Priya said the term mostly gets used by whoever is out of power against whoever is in. In the US, one side points at gerrymandering and voter ID, the other at platform censorship and lawfare, and both call the other side a threat to democracy. If every side can point at some ingredient—a court fight, an emergency order, a sketchy redistricting—and call the other side autocrats, then "backsliding" starts to mean "my side lost." She also said the usual baselines, like an independent judiciary or a neutral civil service, are ideals plenty of democracies have never actually met, so it's not clear what we're measuring decline from.

3. Dan called that the both-sides-ism that treats a failing test in one module and a total build break as the same thing. Backsliding isn't any abuse of power. It's a cluster of referee-capturing moves made by the same actors in a short window, before or between elections: packing a constitutional court by changing nomination rules, forcing judges to retire early, turning state media into a government mouthpiece, rewriting electoral law after winning under the old rules. You can read that off the statute book without guessing at motives. Normal partisan hardball is ugly, but it doesn't try to remove the referee.

4. Priya answered that "referee-capturing" is exactly the disputed label. Who decides which institutions are the referees? V-Dem and Freedom House do, mostly, and they're committees of researchers scoring things like "civil society" and "liberal democracy" from news reports—which are the contested concepts, now wearing a number. She also said there's a threshold problem: France pushed a pension reform through without a parliamentary vote, and a UK government suspended parliament to run down a deadline. Were those backsliding? If yes, the term covers normal hardball and means nothing. If no, what principle excludes them but includes gerrymandering? She's never heard one.

5. Dan said she's demanding a universal cutoff that no workable concept has. Backsliding is a trajectory property, like a performance regression: you don't need a formal spec of "fast" to know latency went from 60ms to 900ms after a refactor. Hungary looks the same—one party writes a new constitution, then uses it to entrench itself, all in a short arc. You can see direction and pace. Demanding a crisp binary is like refusing to say someone has a fever unless every thermometer on Earth prints the same number.

6. Priya said fevers are a bad example for his side, because a fever literally has a diagnostic criterion and "declining democracy" doesn't. And trajectory needs a baseline: Hungary 2010, or some ideal it never matched? She also said the "cluster of power-concentrating moves" describes a lot of ordinary majority government—Roosevelt's court-packing plan would be backsliding by that standard, and nobody calls FDR an autocrat. The real work is being done by words like "entrench," which just relabels majoritarian moves you don't like.

7. Dan said the FDR case helps him: Roosevelt tried, mostly failed, and the institutions held, so the episode didn't become backsliding. The question is whether the referees actually get replaced, not whether someone takes a swing at them. In Hungary the court got packed and stayed packed, and later elections were run under rules the winner wrote after winning. In functioning democracies there are tripwires—someone with standing sues, an upper house balks, the press exposes it—and when all the tripwires get cut in the same year, that's the phenomenon.

8. Priya said that makes the concept retroactive: if institutions hold it wasn't backsliding, if they don't it was, so you can't use it to warn anyone in real time, because whether it's happening depends on how it turns out. That's fine for a warning—warnings are supposed to be forward-looking—but then it's advocacy, not diagnosis. And warnings mostly get issued for countries you want to pressure (Turkey gets the label) and not for allies or yourself.

9. Dan finished by saying sure, it's partly forward-looking, but "this code path is fragile" is also a judgment about the future and engineers still find it useful. She keeps demanding either a crisp binary or nothing, when the sensible position is a structured, mechanism-based, fallible judgment. And every label can be abused; "unemployment" and "inflation" are contested too, and we don't throw those out.
