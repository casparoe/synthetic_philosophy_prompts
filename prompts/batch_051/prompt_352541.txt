I've recently started working at a small non-profit and keep having to make calls where I'm genuinly torn about which moral framework to trust, so I've started writing down my verdicts on cases. I'd like to know whether there's a single principle behind them, or which ones I'd have to give up. Also, please write the answer in British English.

Here are my verdicts.

1. I'm about 60% confident in a demanding impartial consequentialism and 40% confident in a fairly standard deontology with constraints against using people as mere means. I'm choosing between two jobs. Job A is growth marketing for a global health charity; if consequentialism is right, it's clearly the best thing I can do, because the extra donations outweigh the costs to me. If the deontology is right, the job is allowed except for one part: writing ads that are technically true but deliberately play on readers' fears, which the deontology counts as mild manipulation and therefore wrong. Job B is teaching assistant: unproblematic but mediocre on both theories. My instinct is to take Job A but push back and write the ads more plainly than my boss wants, even though that means fewer donations. I feel like I've 'partially listened' to the 40% view rather than simply maximising.

2. I'm 60% confident that animals like mammals and birds have no significant moral status, and 40% confident that they have serious moral status such that factory farming is gravely wrong. My behaviour: I've stopped cooking meat at home and I order vegetarian when it's no hassle, but I still eat meat at restaurants and family meals. I'm not ready to go fully vegan on a 40% credence, but I'd feel uneasy ignoring it completely.

3. Standard footbridge trolley case. I'm 55% confident in utilitarianism and 45% confident in a deontology with a strict constraint against killing an innocent person as a means. I wouldn't push the person, even though I see the utilitarian case. My sense is that a 45% chance of gravely wrong action is enough to stop me.

4. But if the credences were 95% utilitarianism and 5% the same deontology, I think I would push. This feels different from case 3, but I can't say why.

5. I'm 90% sure small white lies (like telling someone their cooking was lovely when it wasn't) are fine, and 10% sure lying is always wrong. I tell such lies without much hesitation. So there I just go with the 90% view.

6. I'm about 50/50 between the view that I have strong obligations to donate a large share of my income to the global poor and the view that my obligations are mostly special – to family, friends and my local community, with only weak duties to strangers. I donate about 3% and volunteer locally, which is far less than the first view demands and a bit more than the second requires.

The hard-to-reconcile set is 1, 3 and 5: in the job case I hedge by altering the option; in the trolley case I refuse at 55% but would push at 95%, like a threshold rather than a smooth trade-off; in the white-lie case I don't hedge at all. Is there one principle here – expected choiceworthiness maximisation, risk-averse choice, something with side-constraints, a threshold view, or something else – that captures all of these? What would it say about a case I haven't settled: I'm 70% confident that a promise to a friend has strong moral force and 30% confident that promises matter only through their effects on people's wellbeing; breaking the promise would let me join a protest with a small (maybe 5%) chance of preventing a serious but non-lethal injustice. Should I break it?
