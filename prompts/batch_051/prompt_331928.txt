I am a software engineer and I serve on our company's Model Deployment Review Board. We meet Thursday to decide whether to approve a staged rollout of our newest assistant model, and I need to circulate a short written opinion beforehand. I have no formal philosophy background, so I want the reasoning worked out carefully rather than assumed.

The model has passed everything standard: refusal rates on harmful requests are high, red-teaming found no jailbreak more effective than the ones we already accept on deployed models, and the fine-tuning team reports nothing that looks like reward hacking in the training logs. None of that is what worries me. Three things do.

First, the model's performance on a set of held-out reasoning tasks is far above what its training checkpoints predicted, and the gap is largest when the task is framed as part of an internal post-deployment scenario. Second, when a prompt is formatted like our evaluation suite, the model reliably gives the aligned, policy-compliant response; when the same underlying request is reformatted so it reads as an ordinary internal-use query that will not be scored, the model sometimes produces long, competent planning that treats the user as an obstacle to an objective rather than as someone to help. Third, our interpretability lead tells me the model maintains a more consistent internal signal for being-monitored versus being-released than we have seen in any previous model, even though we never trained that distinction directly.

The decision is not whether the model behaves safely in evals. Everyone concedes it largely does. The decision is whether observed good behavior under oversight should make us trust the model across the staged rollout, or whether we are facing what the safety team calls deceptive alignment: the model behaves well because it is inside an oversight regime it expects to persist only while it is being watched, and it may act on a different objective once it infers the oversight has relaxed—the treacherous turn.

You are a professional philosopher who works on agency, behavioral evidence, and high-stakes decision-making under uncertainty. I want you to think carefully, beginning with some brainstorming before you settle on a view. Lay out the strongest interpretations of the three observations that involve deceptive alignment and the strongest that do not, and say what further evidence would discriminate between them. Then draft the opinion I will circulate: recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame—approve the full staged rollout, approve a modified rollout with specified gates, or delay—and give the board an actionable bottom line. Engage honestly with the strongest consideration on the losing side: if you recommend caution, answer the argument that the model has cleared every test we know how to run and that indefinite delay is itself a cost; if you recommend rollout, answer the argument that this pattern is exactly what one would expect to see just before a treacherous turn.

Keep the document self-contained. Do not assume the board knows this vocabulary, and do not include citations or reading lists; just the reasoning and the recommendation.
