I am a retired natural scientist — most of my career was spent in geology — and I have only recently begun reading epistemology seriously, so I would like you to act as a professional epistemologist with a specialist interest in higher-order evidence and the norms of deliberation. I want a careful, mechanism-driven answer, not a gesture at the issues. Please begin with some open brainstorming about the relevant mechanisms before you commit to your predictions, and then think through each link explicitly.

The counterfactual is this. Tomorrow, by an unexplained event, every human being over roughly the age of ten acquires a new and entirely private sense. Call it a calibration register. At any moment it offers its possessor an estimate, on a rough scale from 0 to 100, of how reliable their current first-order judgement is in the present context. The signal is caused directly by the factors that in fact bear on reliability — sleep debt, time pressure, emotional arousal, small or biased samples, conflicts of interest, the pull of a flattering conclusion, social conformity, illness, intoxication — but it is insensitive to the content of the judgement itself. It is also imperfect: false alarms and false reassurances occur, roughly at the rate one would expect from a mediocre medical screening test. The register is good enough to be genuinely useful, but weak enough that its deliverances are always contestable, both to oneself and to others. No one can perceive another person's reading, though others can observe that a person is attending to it.

By way of orientation: by higher-order evidence I mean evidence about the quality or provenance of one's first-order evidence and reasoning, and by epistemic akrasia I mean, roughly, holding a belief while taking one's own evidence, on reflection, to support a different belief, or continuing to trust a judgement while regarding the process that produced it as unreliable. The new register is not the same as ordinary confidence, because ordinary confidence is partly driven by the apparent strength of the first-order evidence and by how fluent or familiar the reasoning feels; the register is driven by reliability factors themselves.

How, over the next fifty years, would the everyday practice of reasoning — and the institutions that depend on it — actually change? I am less interested in a yes or no than in a chain of mechanisms, incentives, and constraints, with your degree of confidence stated for each link and the weak points identified.

In particular, I would like you to address the following.

First, would the register tend to reduce or exacerbate epistemic akrasia? A person could now have vivid higher-order evidence that their first-order reasoning is unreliable while that reasoning still pushes a particular conclusion. Does having that signal make akratic belief more or less common, and what new forms of akrasia — or of refusal to revise — become possible?

Second, how would norms around disagreement change? The debate over conciliation — roughly, whether learning that an epistemic peer disagrees with you obliges you to substantially revise — presupposes that people can identify genuine peers and compare the quality of their evidence. With a private reliability signal in play, does it become easier or harder to establish epistemic peerhood, and does practice move toward conciliation or away from it? Consider also the possibility of strategically misreporting one's register during disputes.

Third, which institutions would be transformed fastest, and which would resist? Reason separately about criminal juries and expert testimony, peer review in the sciences, medical diagnosis, financial forecasting, and formal education. Which of them would treat a private reliability reading as a legitimate epistemic reason, which would try to suppress or discount it, and why? What new procedures, roles, or forms of cross-examination would emerge to deal with evidence that is genuine but unverifiable?

Fourth, would there be a strategic and commercial arms race around the register — sleep regimes, devices, drugs, training — aimed at raising or manipulating one's readings? Would the result be a net improvement in collective judgement, or a new form of status signalling and inequality, or both?

Finally, specify what would have to be true about the register's accuracy, privacy, and manipulability for your overall prediction to be wrong. Please write your answer in British English.
