I am taking a course this term on language and political argument, and we recently spent two weeks on Orwell's essay about the connection between careless prose and careless political thought. As I understand the claim, inflated, vague, or evasive writing does not merely cover up muddled thinking; it also makes further muddled thinking easier, and the discipline of saying plainly what one means has political value. I have started applying a version of this as a personal epistemic rule. The rule is this: when I read a work of political or social theory, I give it one careful reading and then try to restate its central thesis and its best argument in ordinary everyday words, without technical vocabulary. If I cannot do this, I treat the obscurity as a mark against the view itself rather than against my own reading, and I either reduce my confidence in the view or stop engaging with it. Last week the rule led me to dismiss an assigned article about welfare state retrenchment after I spent two hours trying to determine what the author thought actually causes welfare states to shrink; the article kept returning to phrases such as the performative logic of neoliberal governance and the discursive rearticulation of risk. A classmate told me I was using the rule to avoid learning unfamiliar vocabulary and that some ideas simply cannot be expressed in plain paraphrase.

I am not asking whether Orwell's original essay was right, but whether my imported use of it as a reading filter is defensible. Is this rule a reasonable epistemic shortcut given that I cannot read everything with unlimited time, or is it a stylistic bias dressed up as a principle? Under what conditions does unparaphrasability signal confusion, and under what conditions does it signal my own lack of training? Is there a revised version of the rule that keeps the worry about language being used to avoid thought while not penalizing legitimate technical precision?
