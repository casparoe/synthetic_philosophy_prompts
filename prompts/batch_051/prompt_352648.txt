I cover the science-and-ideas beat for a general-interest magazine, and for the past couple years I've been reporting on the consciousness debates. I keep finding myself weirdly persuaded by the illusionists — the people who say phenomenal consciousness, the what-it's-like ingredient of experience, is an introspective illusion. The view has real virtues. It doesn't so much solve the hard problem as dissolve it: there's no extra stuff, just brains doing their physical business and representing themselves to themselves in ways that systematically mislead us. It's parsimonious, it sits comfortably inside physicalism, and it gives a debunking answer to the meta-problem — explaining why creatures like us would be convinced there's a hard problem in the first place, as a kind of user-interface trick rather than a window onto a private glow. Alot of the illusionist picture feels right to me.

But then I stub a toe or sit with a migraine, and I find the view impossible to believe. The pain is the one thing that isn't inferred — its right there, given. I get that illusionists use the word seems in a deflationary way, and that they claim the only thing really present is a false introspective judgement, not a feeling. But the obvious reply keeps hitting me: an illusion is a case where things seem one way and are another, and seeming itself is an experience. So an illusion of consciousness sounds like consciousness doing the dreaming. The theory seems to demand that I describe myself as having no one home while I'm the one redecorating the house. I know the illusionists have answers here — that I'm smuggling in the phenomenal reading of seems that they've already rejected, that introspection is itself a fallible representation — but the answers feel like they're arguing me out of the only thing I can't be wrong about.

So here's what I want. Present both sides at their strongest and give me a negotiated verdict, not just a winner. Is the intuition I keep having one that ought to be debunked and dropped — and if so, what would a debunking need to show to actually earn that, rather than just pointing out that introspection is fallible? Or should I amend or abandon the illusionist picture, and if so, what happens to the virtues that drew me in? Is there a middle reading on which the two are compatible — say, by distinguishing the phenomenal claim itself from the judgement I'm making about it? I want you to spell out what each move costs, the way I'd have to weigh it before writing the piece for a skeptical editor. And write it as continuous prose, like an email or a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
