In three weeks I am giving a work-in-progress talk at a comparative politics and political theory workshop. My thesis is that the left–right distinction is real: beneath the historical churn there is a single stable axis — roughly, an attitude toward hierarchy and its justification — and the familiar two-dimensional picture (economic vs. cultural) is an artifact of how issues happen to cluster in particular decades, not evidence of two underlying dimensions. The audience is not friendly. It includes two comparativists who think left and right are nothing over and above the coalitional structure of party systems in particular countries; a political psychologist who has published on the economic/cultural two-dimensional model; and a historian of political thought who holds that left and right are indexical labels whose referents have shifted so often since 1789 that no shared content survives. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, not the friendly version, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, phrased the way those three kinds of audience member would actually put them, and indicating which of them would raise which. For each objection give a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Also tell me what I should concede up front, which reply has to land for the paper to survive, and the question I am hoping nobody asks.
