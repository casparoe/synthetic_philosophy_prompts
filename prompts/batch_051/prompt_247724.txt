I keep going back and forth on a version of the induction problem, and I'd like someone to just force-rank the standard replies so I can see which ones are doing real work. Here's the setup.

The no-free-lunch theorem for supervised learning says, roughly, that if you average over all possible functions from inputs to outputs, any two learning algorithms have exactly the same expected error on examples they haven't been trained on. A philosopher friend of mine reads this as a formal version of Hume's worry: unless you've already built in an assumption that the world is one way rather than another, no policy for extrapolating from past cases is better than any other, so induction can't be justified from the data alone.

Rank the following nine responses from best to worst at actually answering that challenge — not at being technically clever, but at either justifying induction, dissolving the worry, or honestly explaining why we should carry on anyway. Ties are allowed, but say why. For each, give one sentence on what the move buys and what it quietly gives up. And if you think two of them are basically the same move wearing different clothes, merge them or say so.

1. Solomonoff induction: put a universal prior over computable hypotheses that favors shorter programs and predict with it; there are theorems that it converges fast on any computable data-generating process.
2. Bayesian subjectivism: there's no such thing as prior-free inference; all induction is induction relative to a prior. The theorem just shows you can't get a guarantee for free, which nobody should have expected.
3. PAC/VC theory: assume the target function lives in a hypothesis class of limited complexity, and you get finite-sample guarantees; the no-free-lunch result is just the degenerate case where the class is the set of all functions.
4. Minimum description length / structural risk minimization: regularize toward simple hypotheses; if the world has a short description, simple rules generalize, and if it doesn't, no method has a shot anyway.
5. The uniform-average-over-a-pathological-universe reply: the average over all functions is dominated by maximally random, unstructured functions that no real learner ever faces. What matters is performance on structured problems, where some algorithms do dominate.
6. Pragmatic vindication: induction can't be shown to succeed, but if any method of prediction succeeds, then plain induction from observed frequencies succeeds too, so induction is never worse than the best possible method and we loose nothing by using it.
7. Meta-induction: keep a pool of candidate prediction methods, track their recent success, and imitate whichever is doing best; there's a theorem that this does nearly as well as the best method in the pool without your knowing in advance which one it is.
8. Popperian falsificationism: science never needed induction; it makes bold conjectures and tries to refute them, so a theorem about inductive extrapolation doesn't touch the actual method of science.
9. The evolutionary story: our inductive biases were selected in a world with stable regularities, which is why they generalize; the theorem only shows that no bias works in every possible world, and we don't live in every possible world.

Finally, label each one as either a theorem that earns induction a guarantee under stated conditions, a story about why the challenge never gets off the ground, or a concession that induction isn't justified but we should act as if it is anyway.
