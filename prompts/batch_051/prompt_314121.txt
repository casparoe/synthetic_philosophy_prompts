I'm a political theory PhD student and I keep going back and forth on Arendt's banality of evil, so I'm hoping for a careful take. The thesis as usually read depends heavily on her portrait of Eichmann as a thoughtless, cliché-spouting nobody, someone who failed to think rather than someone who willed evil. But the later publication of his writings and interview transcripts from Argentina seem to show him privately committed to the extermination and proud of his role, which sounds like exactly the ideological fanatic Arendt said he wasn't. What I want to know is whether the banality of evil survives that development. I'm not really asking 'was Arendt wrong about the historical Eichmann'—I'm asking whether the concept collapses if the biographical claim fails, or whether it can be rebuilt as a claim about the form of modern organized evil, or about how people partition their moral attention even while holding terrible beliefs. Which premise does the development actually bear on, and what's the smallest repair that saves the useful core, if there is one?
