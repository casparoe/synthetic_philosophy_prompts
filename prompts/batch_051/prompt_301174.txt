A friend of mine has defended a version of the golden rule for years, and conversation with her has left me with a set of fairly settled judgments about what she thinks the rule requires, even though she has never stated the underlying principle in a single formulation. She holds that the rule is no license for us to project our own tastes onto other people: she said a host who served his guests an intensely spicy meal—on the ground that he would not have expected a guest to complain about a host's menu—had misapplied the rule by reading it through his own palate. She also holds that the rule can require looking to the other person's considered view rather than to the agent's anxieties: she approved of a doctor who honored a patient's settled wish to hear a difficult diagnosis even though the doctor herself would have preferred not to be told. At the same time, she denies that whatever another person currently wants sets the standard of correct treatment: she dismissed the reasoning of a man who gave his drug-dependent brother money for drugs because he imagined he would want someone to give him what he was begging for in that desperation, saying that wantedness is not the same as good will and that the rule does not hand moral authority to the addressee's immediate cravings. And she thinks the rule does not dissolve role responsibilities: she objected to a judge who declined to impose a sentence because he imagined his own dread in the accused's place, arguing that imagining oneself in the other's position must include the whole position—what a fair practice asks of and permits for each side—rather than one person's fear.

Now I have a fifth case on which I cannot predict her. A university lecturer has discretion to allow a make-up final exam for a student who missed the scheduled sitting because she was caring for a sibling through a medical crisis; the course rules do not provide for make-ups, but the lecturer may make exceptions. The lecturer imagines herself in the student's situation and believes she would want the exception made: the student completed the coursework, was not irresponsible, and would be harmed by the rigidity of the rule. But the lecturer also sees that if she grants a private exception to this student without committing herself to any general policy, she will be doing for this one student what she would hesitate to do for the next similarly situated student, and she is unsure whether she could will everyone in her position to make such exceptions as a matter of rule.

What would my friend say the golden rule, as she understands it, directs the lecturer to do? State the readings of the golden rule that fit her four earlier judgments, identify which of them genuinely compete on this new case, and say what each competing reading predicts. Then offer your best guess about which reading is really hers, and specify a further case that would most sharply discriminate between the remaining contenders.
