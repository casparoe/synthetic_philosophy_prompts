I'm on the discipline committee of a state engineering licensing board, and we have a case that's split us down the middle. Dana, a junior civil engineer, spent two years doing flood modeling for a big dam project. The key inputs—flood-frequency parameters, channel roughness values—were handed to her by the senior engineer, and changing them wasn't really in her remit. Her models where technically accurate given those inputs, but she came to suspect the parameters understated downstream flood risk. She flagged it once in a footnote to an internal draft; the senior engineer deleted the footnote before the environmental impact statement went out, and she never raised it again. The EIS leaned on her models as independent expert analysis, the dam got built, and a few years later a flood overtopped it and people downstream died. The senior engineer is already being sanctioned. Everyone on the committee agrees Dana didn't cause the flood—the project would have gone ahead and some modeler would have been found if she'd walked away—but the question is whether she's complicit enough in the misleading EIS to deserve discipline, and if so how much. Our options range from no action, to a private letter of concern, to public censure, to suspension. Half of us think a junior engineer who did accurate work on bad inputs and got overruled isn't culpable; the other half says her signed-off models gave the EIS its credibility, and that's exactly the kind of participation that makes you accountable even when you're not a but-for cause.

Please draft the short decision memo I'll circulate before Friday. It should recommend a concrete outcome with reasons the committee could actually adopt, and it should engage the strongest version of the argument for the opposite outcome rather than dismissing it. Since this is a memo, start with the memo itself—no preface, no commentary—and lead with your bottom line in the first sentence.
