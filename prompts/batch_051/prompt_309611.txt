Act as a research coordinator synthesizing a year-long argument inside an AI lab. The lab must decide what evidence should count as sufficient to warrant the claim that a high-stakes AI assistant is aligned before deployment. The two positions described below are held by equally competent researchers; your task is to draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration would produce. Treat each side as strong and sincere; neither is a strawman.

The certifier argues that average-case evidence is the wrong unit for safety. The dangerous behaviors at issue are sparse, input-dependent, and catastrophic only within a small region of state space, so a low measured error rate can easily hide a failure the test suite never sampled. In this view, a release decision needs a non-vacuous worst-case guarantee: a proof or exhaustive check that a specified prohibited behavior cannot occur within a specified class of environments. The certifier grants that the first certificates will be narrow and over-approximate, but treats them as a foundation to widen rather than a reason to abandon the project.

The evaluator argues that formal worst-case guarantees for contemporary deep learning systems are currently unavailable or vacuous: either they cover toy models and properties unrelated to the failures that matter, or they rest on over-approximations so loose that they permit the very failure the lab cares about. In this view, waiting for a genuine certificate is not a safety policy but an indefinite stall. The strongest available substitute for a bound is measured performance under adversarial and synthetically shifted evaluation distributions, with the test suite continuously rebuilt until it stops finding failures; this provides a stochastic, empirical approximation to worst-case reasoning.

Write the document the two researchers should have produced, with these components.

(1) Agreed claims: four to six numbered claims both sides can sign. Make them substantive rather than truisms, but genuine common ground.

(2) Exact disagreements: a section in neutral wording that states each side's position so accurately the side would accept the wording. Address at least three disputes: whether a non-vacuous formal guarantee is reachable for a useful property of a current system; whether adversarial evaluation can serve as a de facto worst-case bound; and what the default decision rule should be when one kind of evidence is missing.

(3) Cruxes: two or three sub-questions such that resolving them would materially move at least one researcher.

(4) Tests: for each crux, a concrete case or experiment both researchers would agree bears on it. Make the setup implementable in a toy environment, for example a policy trained with a hidden trigger and then compared on a formal over-approximation versus a fixed-budget adversarial red team. For each test, record in advance each researcher's prediction and what would count as a concession.

Use American English spelling and punctuation. Do not add citations, footnotes, or reading recommendations. Keep the entire response, including the note below, to no more than 900 words. After the document proper, add one short paragraph stating how confident you are that these formulations would actually be signed by both researchers, and identify the single point in the document you are least sure about.
