Decision theory textbooks sometimes defend the transitivity axiom of expected utility theory with a money pump. An agent whose preferences cycle—A preferred to B, B to C, C to A—is presented as a standing money pump: starting with C, they will pay a small fee to swap it for B, pay again to swap B for A, pay once more to swap A for C, and so arrive back where they started with less money; repeat until the agent is broke. The conclusion drawn is that rational agents cannot have cyclic preferences, because a rational agent cannot be exploited into a sure loss.

Treat the money pump as an analogy rather than as a decorative example. The implicit picture is that the agent's preference structure is a mechanism with a defect, and the trader running the pump is an engineer or croupier who converts the defect into guaranteed profit; rationality is thereby identified with being a mechanism no such trader can exploit. Audit how far this analogy holds. Give the correspondence table: what do the trader, the fees, the cycle, and the sure loss map onto in the target case of a person's standing preferences over ordinary options? Identify where the mapping breaks—what the mechanism picture quietly imports that a human chooser need not have, and what work is being done by the assumption that the agent will accept each swap in turn. Then say whether the intended conclusion—that transitivity, and along with it the coherence argument for expected utility maximization, is a genuine requirement of rationality—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some better analogy would carry the load more honestly.
