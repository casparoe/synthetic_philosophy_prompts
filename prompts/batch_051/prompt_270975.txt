I've been running a decision-theory reading group for a few years, and every time we do Newcomb's problem we end in the same stalemate. The one-boxers keep saying that taking only the opaque box is what you should do if you think the predictor is almost always right, because conditional on your choice, the million is probably there. The two-boxers keep saying that the money is already in the box or not by the time you act, and taking both boxes leaves you a thousand dollars richer whatever the predictor did. Both sides can state the others reasoning, nobody disputes the payoffs or the predictor's accuracy, and nobody has moved in fifty-plus years. Nozick's original essay, Newcomb's Problem and Two Principles of Choice, frames the problem as a collision between two principles—dominance and expected utility—and he treats at least one as having to give, while noticing that people's intuitions about which one to keep don't split neatly along a single line. I've started to think the real disagreement isn't about the boxes at all; it's about whether rationality should look at the causal effect of your act or at what your act would be evidence about. But that hypothesis predicts there should be lots of movement between Newcomb-style variants, and in my group there isn't. So: what kind of disagreement is this, exactly? Is it verbal, substantive, or two different questions being asked under one name—one about what an ideal agent would do, one about what to do when your choice carries information about a state it cannot cause? And if it's substantive, what would actually count as progress: a new formal result, an agreed taxonomy of cases, or something else? I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not another argument for one side.
