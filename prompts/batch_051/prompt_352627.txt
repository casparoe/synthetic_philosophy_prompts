I came across a passage in the Franck Report—the June 11, 1945 report by the Committee on Political and Social Problems of the Manhattan Project—that has been on my mind since I read it for a history class. The passage is:

"It is undoubtedly true that the discovery of flying, for example, has so far brought much more misery than enjoyment or profit to humanity. However, in the past, scientists could disclaim direct responsibility for the use to which mankind had put their disinterested discoveries. We cannot take the same attitude now because the success which we have achieved in the development of nuclear power is fraught with infinitely greater dangers than were all the inventions of the past."

Reading this made me think about how people use historical analogies when they argue about transformative AI. I have been keeping track of my own verdicts on particular analogical moves people make, and I would like to know what principle, if any, captures them. Here are my verdicts.

First, the Franck Report's own argument. It says scientists could disclaim responsibility for past inventions, but cannot disclaim responsibility for nuclear power because nuclear power is dangerous in a way that differs in kind, not just degree. I find that move persuasive. What does the work in the passage is not the word "infinitely" but the supporting point made elsewhere in the report: that science cannot provide adequate defense against nuclear weapons, so protection can only come from political organization. I accept that as a good argument.

Second, a blog post I read applies the same structure to AI. It says: just as the scientists who built the atomic bomb could not disclaim responsibility for its use, AI researchers cannot disclaim responsibility for how their models are used. I reject this. It borrows Franck's conclusion without borrowing the feature that justified it. AI is not a single device whose destructive effects have no technical countermeasure, so the analogy just asserts itself.

Third, my history teacher says the Industrial Revolution shows that disruptive technologies cause temporary pain but eventually raise living standards, so people who worry about AI are making a Luddite mistake. I do not buy this. The Industrial Revolution analogy seems to hide the fact that the "temporary" pain lasted for generations and involved immense suffering. If anything, the analogy makes me more worried, not less.

Fourth, a friend argues that nuclear weapons produced a stable balance of terror, so advanced AI will produce a stable balance of power among states. I find this almost worthless as an analogy. The stability of mutually assured destruction came from very particular features—second-strike capability, the impossibility of defense, the concentration of weapons in a few states. None of those features transfers to AI.

Fifth, a journalist writes that the printing press broke the medieval Church's monopoly on knowledge, so AI will break modern elites' monopoly on expertise. I actually think this analogy works, but not for the journalist's reason. The printing press did not liberate because information wants to be free; it worked because it drastically lowered the cost of copying text. AI lowers the cost of producing competent text. The liberating effects are contingent on who controls the new means of production, and the printing press analogy, read carefully, predicts that elites often reassert control.

Here is the tension that bothers me. In my first verdict I accepted the Franck Report's claim that nuclear power differs in kind from everything before it. That would seem to make nuclear weapons a bad analogy for AI. But when I compare all the candidate analogies—printing press, Industrial Revolution, aviation, nuclear weapons—I also think the nuclear analogy is the best guide we have to how transformative AI should be governed. If nuclear power is unique in kind, why would it provide the best analogy? Those two verdicts seem to be in conflict.

Is there a single principle about historical analogy—perhaps something about whether the relevant mechanism transfers rather than whether the outcomes are similar—that delivers all my verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts should I give up? And what would the principle you propose say about a case I have not decided yet: someone argues that the electrification of factories is a good analogy for AI because both are general-purpose technologies that diffuse across many sectors?
