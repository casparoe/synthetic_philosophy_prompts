I'm organizing a small adversarial-collaboration exercise between two members of my philosophy-of-neuroscience reading group, and I want you to draft the joint statement they'd end up with after a good-faith attempt. Person A thinks the free energy principle is a substantive theory of brains and bodies: perception, action, and attention really are forms of variational inference, and minimizing variational free energy is something living systems do, not just a way we can describe them. Person B thinks the principle is formally elegant but close to empirically empty: with freedom to choose the generative model, the priors, and where to draw the Markov blanket, you can cast nearly any system as free-energy-minimizing, so it doesn't stake out a risky claim about how brains actually work. They're both technically literate, they've agreed on a shared vocabulary and a stock of examples, and they want to find genuinely common ground rather than produce a press release.

Write the document itself: first a numbered "agreed claims" section they would both sign (covering the math of variational inference, what it takes for an agent to be said to have a generative model, what standard predictive-coding architectures do and don't establish on their own, and what "falsification" would have to mean for a modeling framework like this). Then a disagreements table. For each row: the exact point of disagreement, phrased in wording each side would accept for its own view; a verdict on whether the disagreement is substantive or at least partly verbal; and the crux question such that settling it would move at least one of them. For the main cruxes, add concrete cases both accept bear on the question — at minimum a thermostat, a simulated agent whose behavior is generated by a known rule and then rewritten in free-energy terms, and an in vitro neural network that adapts without an obvious action-perception loop — and record, in advance, what each side predicts or expects to find in that case. Flag any positions the two camps cannot currently separate with a case they can construct, rather than pretending the table resolves everything. End the entire thing with a one-sentence summary of the residual disagreement, on its own line.
