I keep going back and forth about microhistory and the history-from-below impulse behind it. The classic move is: instead of the history of elites and states, you zoom in on one strange, ordinary person with an unusually thick file — a miller with his own cosmology, a village of heretics — and you claim this dense, exceptional life is a better window onto ordinary mental life than any survey of kings and parliaments could be. The defense was never that the guy is statistically representative; it's that the cracks and exaggerations in his testimony reveal the common culture that produced them, the way a break can show you the structure of a bone. Fine. But now we're drowning in digitized archives, and you can string together records for hundreds of thousands of ordinary people — parish registers, census entries, court dockets — and actually count how normal people lived, married, moved, and died. That seems to undercut the original excuse for using the exceptional case, namely that ordinary people left no usable traces. Does the microhistorical argument survive this? Or does it split into two claims — one about evidence, which digitization kills, and one about interpretation, which it doesnt touch? I'm after which premise breaks and whether there's a repaired version that still justifies doing microhistory in an age of big data. Not looking for sources.
