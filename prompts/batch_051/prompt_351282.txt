A friend in my philosophy of mind seminar walked me into this and now I cant find the exit, because the conclusion strikes me as absurd but every step looked fine when he said it.

(1) Chase and Sanborn are two experienced coffee tasters who both say they used to like one brand of coffee but now they dislike it. Chase says his experience of the coffee's taste hasn't changed at all; what changed is his standards, so the same taste he once judged fine now strikes him as bad relative to his more demanding standards. Sanborn says the opposite: his standards are exactly the same, but the coffee now tastes different to him. The taste itself changed for Sanborn; for Chase, only his evaluation of the same taste changed.

(2) For an outside observer, there is no possible observation or experiment that could tell whether a given taster is a Chase case or a Sanborn case. All you ever get is the same kind of report: I used to like it, now I dont.

(3) If there really are two introspectively different situations here, then that difference should in principle make a difference somewhere. A real difference with no possible downstream difference at all is not a real difference.

(4) So the introspective data cant actually support the distinction between changed taste and changed standards. The distinction is just a gloss the tasters put on the same behavioral change.

(5) But a workable notion of qualia requires exactly this: that introspection can support claims like the taste itself stayed the same but my standards changed, and distinguish them from the taste itself changed. If it cant, qualia are posits that do no work.

(6) So qualia are an incoherent posit.

(7) The same reasoning applies to basically every introspectively described feature of experience. Whenever two accounts of my experience differ only in how I describe them from the inside, with no possible downstream difference, they arent really different accounts.

(8) So my first-person access to my own experience is not a special authority. I cant know whether my standards changed or my experience changed, because that distinction does no work.

(9) But that's bonkers: I do know, aparently with certainty, whether it's my standards or my experience that changed. I have some kind of access to how things seem to me.

This seems to prove too much. I'd like to keep (9), but I want to know which premise I have to give up and what that would cost. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does?
