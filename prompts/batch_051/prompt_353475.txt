I'm writing an essay on the epistemology of recorded evidence, and I want to work through a film rather than only through arguments. The organizing idea is the epistemic backstop: photographs and video have long been treated as the evidence that settles disputes when testimony, memory, and paperwork conflict, precisely because a recording seems not to depend on anyone's say-so. Deepfakes are philosophically threatening because they remove that backstop. Read Ridley Scott's Blade Runner through this lens. The film is usually discussed through personhood, memory, and empathy — the test that separates replicants from humans, implanted memories, the origami unicorn — but I am interested in a different through-line: the film repeatedly treats photographs as a last court of appeal about who someone is and what really happened, even as it shows that a photograph only has that power because of the story attached to it, and that the story can be supplied by someone else. Think of Rachael's childhood snapshot and the old photographs Leon guards. Where does the epistemic-backstop reading genuinely illuminate the film, and where does the film resist or complicate it? Be specific about scenes. Address three things: (a) once an old backstop fails, what does the film suggest people reach for next — a better test, a new technology, an institution, a private anchor — and does it believe any replacement can hold? (b) Is the film optimistic, pessimistic, or ambivalent about how people respond socially to losing that backstop, and which moments carry that judgment? (c) What does Blade Runner add to the philosophical debate over deepfakes that the standard arguments and examples don't capture? Be honest about the places where the reading strains. Hard limit: no more than 1,000 words.
