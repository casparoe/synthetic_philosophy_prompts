I keep coming back to an argument that seems to prove too much, and I can't find the exit. It's a cleaned-up version of my friend's argument:

(1) When I decide whether to spend the next dollar or hour on doing good, what matters is the expected value of my counterfactual contribution.
(2) For a well-run direct intervention—say, distributing bednets or deworming pills—an extra dollar buys a discrete, near-certain unit: a net that would not otherwise hang, a dose that would not otherwise be given.
(3) For a systemic intervention—a campaign to change a policy, a social movement, a lobbying push—my individual contribution almost never flips whether the thing succeeds. The chance that my one effort is pivotal is tiny, and my cost is real.
(4) So the marginal expected value of my contribution to systemic work is usually much smaller than the marginal expected value of my contribution to direct work.
(5) Therefore, at the margin, an impartial do-gooder should always spend the next dollar and hour on direct work, not systemic work.
(6) But "at the margin" is true for everyone, every time, so the reasoning seems to entail that no individual should ever work on systemic change.
(7) A world where everyone follows that is foreseeably much worse than a world where some people invest in structural fixes, because the structural causes of the same problems never get addressed. The same line also seems to make voting and recycling pointless, which even my friend admits is more than we bargained for.
(8) A principle that makes everyone worse off when everyone follows it is a bad principle.
(9) So the marginal-impact principle in (1)–(5) must be rejected, and with it the usual claim that direct work reliably beats systemic work.

I badly want to keep (1) and (2), and I have a vague feeling that something shifts between (5) and (7), but I can't name it and I could be wrong about that. Which step should I reject, and what do I pay for rejecting it?
