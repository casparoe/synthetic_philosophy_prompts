Like most novelists, I have acquired a working language for choice and constraint that belongs to my trade rather than to philosophy. When I take a decision apart I think in terms of character-defining choices, scene objectives, stakes, throughlines, and the sense that a story may strain almost anything except a protagonist's core commitments: break one of those without earning it, and the audience feels the tale has cheated. I am far more fluent in this vocabulary than in the formal idiom of moral philosophy, and I want a real translation into it, not an analogy.

That is how I would like to approach the paradox of deontology. As I understand it, the consequentialist argues that if a prohibition on killing an innocent person exists to protect the innocent, then the prohibition's own rationale seems to require the single killing when that act prevents several killings; refusing to violate the constraint looks like privileging one's own clean hands over the very lives the constraint is meant to protect. The deontologist replies that the prohibition is not a value to be promoted across persons but an agent-relative restriction on one's own action, not a goal one may advance by violating it. The sceptic answers that this relocation merely relocates the difficulty, because the constraint can now leave more innocent people dead than a single violation would have.

Translate this debate, as faithfully as you can, into the vocabulary of narrative craft I described. Distinguish, as a novelist would, between a hard rule the story never allows the protagonist to break and a plot that merely totals consequences across a cast, and say where each side of the paradox would have to live inside that scheme. Then give me the report I most want: what did not survive the translation. Which claims carry over cleanly, which had to be distorted, and which had no expression at all? And tell me in which direction the residue points: whether my vocabulary is missing something the philosophers are trying to say, or whether the philosophical debate is partly a story argument in disguise. Please write the answer in British English.
