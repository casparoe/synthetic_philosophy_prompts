I am a PhD student in social philosophy, and I will be moderating a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who defends an account of social kinds on which categories such as race, gender, and money are mind-dependent constructions yet nonetheless objective and causally potent: on his view, they are made rather than discovered, but they are not thereby arbitrary, and their constructed character is not a reason to treat them as unreal or merely subjective. The audience will be a mix of philosophers of social science, anthropologists, and political theorists, so the questions should assume familiarity with the broad terrain but not with the fine details of the speaker's apparatus.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond restating the view. I need at least one question each on (a) the money–race/gender disanalogy: whether a model built primarily for institutional facts like money can extend to kinds that involve embodied marks, perception, and material consequences; (b) Ian Hacking's looping effect: how the classified respond to classification, and how the speaker prevents that feedback from making the extension of the kind radically unstable or unknowable; (c) the normative question of what makes some constructed kinds better than others once correspondence to a mind-independent structure is off the table; and (d) the worry that stressing the reality and causal power of categories such as race ends up ratifying, rather than debunking, the classifications that critics of constructionism most want to challenge. For each of these, include a follow-up question keyed to the answer the speaker is most likely to give: for instance, if he says that social kinds are real because institutions give them causal powers, press on what remains distinctively social about a kind whose effects have become embodied and perceptible; if he says better constructions are those that better serve our purposes, press on whose purposes count and how they are identified without taking up a standpoint outside the construction. The final question should try to force him to say whether social kind names a unified category at all, or rather a family of quite different mechanisms that have little in common beyond the label.
