The exam question was: "Explain why total utilitarianism is standardly taken to imply the Repugnant Conclusion, being precise about the role of the assumption that the added lives are barely worth living. Then consider two proposed escapes—average utilitarianism and critical-level utilitarianism. For each, say whether it avoids the Repugnant Conclusion, and whether it does so only by incurring some other objectionable implication. Conclude by stating which of the three views you find most defensible."

The student's answer, reproduced in full, was: "Total utilitarianism ranks populations by summing individual welfare. Since lives that are barely worth living still have positive welfare, we can take any population Z of ten billion people with excellent lives and imagine a much larger population A whose members all have lives barely worth living. If A is large enough, its total welfare exceeds Z's, so total utilitarianism must rank A as better. The argument turns on 'barely worth living' meaning positive rather than negative; if those lives were below zero, the comparison would fail.

Average utilitarianism avoids this because it ranks populations by mean welfare, and adding a very large number of people below the current mean drives the average down. Average utilitarianism is, in effect, a person-affecting view: it judges outcomes by how well the people who already exist are doing, which is why it regards the mere addition of new happy people as making things worse. Its main drawback is that it implies a world containing one extremely happy person is better than a world containing billions of people only slightly less happy, which many find hard to accept.

Critical-level utilitarianism avoids the Repugnant Conclusion by subtracting a fixed threshold c from each person's welfare before summing. Because the threshold is set above zero, lives that are barely worth living fall below it and add no positive value, so no number of them can outweigh a population of excellent lives. This keeps what is attractive in total utilitarianism—that adding a happy person can make an outcome better—since lives above the threshold still count in the ordinary way. It therefore avoids the Repugnant Conclusion without any comparably objectionable implication, and I take it to be the most defensible of the three."
