I am a licensed family therapist, and a large share of my caseload is couples who are raising their children without religion inside an extended family that is devoutly religious: one or both sets of grandparents attend services weekly, holidays are organized around worship, and the children are regularly invited into prayers, blessings, and religious milestones. The parents agree on two things only — they will not train their children to lie, and they do not want the children to lose their grandparents. Beyond that they disagree, often sharply, about whether a child should bow her head during grace, accept a religious gift, attend a cousin's christening, or answer a grandparent who asks, Do you believe in God?

I keep returning to a philosophical principle that seems right but that I cannot yet hand to a parent in usable form. Roughly, truthfulness attaches to what a person asserts rather than to every impression others form; participating in a communal practice is not by itself an assertion of belief; and a family can preserve both honesty and belonging only if it carefully distinguishes privacy (what the child may keep to herself) from secrecy (what the family treats as shameful and must actively conceal). I want this turned into a reusable decision procedure my clients can run on their own.

You are a professional philosopher specializing in applied ethics and the ethics of assertion, who also writes practical material for clinicians. Think carefully before writing, and begin by brainstorming the hardest recurring situations such a procedure will meet. Then write the following sections in this order, with roughly the indicated lengths:

1. The Principle Restated for Non-Philosophers (150–200 words): translate the principle into plain terms a parent can use, keeping the distinctions that do real work and dropping what a parent does not need.

2. The Decision Procedure (400–500 words): a series of questions in fixed order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure returns a clear answer, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. It must be runnable by two tired parents in under twenty minutes, with no therapist present, before a family visit or holiday, and it must mark the steps that require both parents to agree rather than one parent deciding.

3. Judgment Calls (150–250 words): identify the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment, and state the error it is most likely to make at each such point.

4. Three Worked Applications (300–400 words): run the procedure on these three cases: (a) a grandparent asks a seven-year-old whether she is looking forward to her First Communion when no First Communion is planned; (b) the family is invited to a niece's baptism and the parents disagree about whether the child may light a candle; (c) the child refuses to say grace at Thanksgiving and the parents cannot agree on what to do at the table. At least one worked application must produce an answer the parents will not like; state clearly which one it is and why the procedure still gives it.

5. What the Procedure Cannot Do (100–150 words): identify the family problems this procedure does not address and the outcomes it cannot guarantee.
