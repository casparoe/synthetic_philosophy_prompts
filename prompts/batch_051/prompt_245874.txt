I follow a policy about my own beliefs that I call a personal Ulysses contract. Once a year, during a week when I am not under any unusual emotional, social, or professional pressure, I compile a short list of questions and declare each of them closed. A question makes the list only if I have already examined it from more than one side over a long period and, typically, changed my mind about it at least once before settling. This year's list includes a religious claim I spent a decade examining before rejecting, a political ideology I once held and later abandoned, and a judgment about a former friend's betrayal that I made in circumstances I still trust. After a question is on the list, I no longer treat new arguments or evidence about it as material to weigh; I read and listen only in order to state and defend the settled view, never to test it.

The rationale is my own track record. I almost never reopen closed questions because some calm improvement in the evidence has arrived. I reopen them at two in the morning, or in a conversation with someone I want to impress, or when holding the settled view has become socially expensive. Those reopenings are motivated, and I can predict their conclusions in advance. I therefore bind my future self roughly the way a constitution binds a legislature, or the way Odysseus binds himself to the mast: the calmer version of me sets rules the tempted version would like to break.

A friend of mine says this is not self-binding but a machine for being rigidly wrong forever. Her objections, as I understand them, run as follows. First, the policy assumes that the annual, calm version of me is the rational one, but calm is not the same as reasonable, and a settled mind is not the same as an improved one. Second, if my later self is genuinely so unreliable, the proper response is to repair that unreliability, not to pass a rule that lets me ignore future evidence. Third, a policy that announces in advance that no possible future evidence could change my mind is indistinguishable from fanaticism, whatever good intentions produced it.

Give me a committed verdict rather than a survey of possible answers. Is this policy defensible as it stands, defensible only in some amended form, or indefensible? If some amended form survives, state its precise rule and what it would have to change about my annual list. And answer the charge about my entitlement: given the psychology I have described, is a rule of this general shape genuinely available to me, or am I giving my own rigidity a constitutional-sounding name?
