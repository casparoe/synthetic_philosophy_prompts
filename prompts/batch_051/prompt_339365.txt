In welfare-to-work policy the standard enforcement device is the sanction: a claimant who misses an appointment, fails to carry out an agreed number of job-search actions, or refuses a mandated activity loses part or all of her benefit for a fixed period. I have been writing a policy memo for a course on comparative social policy, and I keep circling a tension in the usual defense of sanctions. Proponents say conditionality is needed to keep the work-focused bargain and its public support alive; critics say sanctions withdraw subsistence from people whose noncompliance is often a symptom of disorganization, chaotic caring arrangements, or despair, and that in practice sanction decisions become a bureaucratic judgment about who deserves help. I want to know whether a less familiar alternative would behave any better, and I would like you to pressure-test it as a mechanism rather than as a slogan.

The proposal is to replace sanctions, and the hardship top-ups that sometimes follow them, with a compliance escrow. Each month a claimant is deemed fully compliant with her individualized claimant commitment — the same set of activities, coaching meetings, and job-search obligations that currently carry sanction risk — the state credits an account in her name with an amount equal to 15 per cent of that month's benefit. The account cannot be touched while she is claiming. It vests, and the full balance is paid out in cash, once she enters paid work at or above the hours threshold stated in her commitment and stays in it for three continuous months. Noncompliance is penalized only by the loss of that month's credit; there is no benefit reduction. A balance that has not vested within 24 consecutive months of claiming is cancelled. If she leaves benefits, receives a payout, and later returns, the account restarts at zero. Caseworkers lose the power to cut benefits and instead only certify months as compliant or noncompliant against the existing commitment.

Walk me through how this would actually play out. What changes in the week-to-week calculus of a claimant compared with sanctions, and does it do anything for the person who is chaotically noncompliant but not malevolently so? Who is made better off and who worse off among the long-term unemployed, lone parents, short-term job-hoppers, and the relatively job-ready? Where would claimants and caseworkers first game it — inflated compliance certifications, strategic three-month jobs timed to trigger the payout followed by a return to claim, front-loaded compliance followed by collapse near the 24-month cancellation cliff — and can any of that be fixed without reintroducing the sanction? Finally, what does the scheme do to the public justification of conditionality and to the line between the deserving and undeserving poor: does the escrow make desert legible, or does it just move the boundary to the 24-month cutoff? Please say how confident you are in each major behavioral claim, and end by singling out the point you are least sure of.
