I write about science and ideas for a living, and the practical question I keep tripping over is when a flat-out assertion is okay. Not lying—I mean cases where the person is perfectly sincere, but something about how they said it seems off. I've been turning this over for weeks and admitedly I keep going in circles, so I'd like a professional philosopher who works on the norms of assertion to think it through with me carefully. Start by brainstorming the obvious candidate rules—knowledge, truth, belief, justification—then test them against my cases. I'm less interested in a taxonomy than in a principle I could actually use in reporting.

My verdicts, as best I can articulate them:

1. A source tells me a rumor about a mayor; I publish it without checking; it turns out true. I think I did something wrong anyway.
2. A scientist announces at a press conference that a drug works, based on one small unreplicated trial. The claim later holds up in big trials. I think the scientist was still wrong to be that flat.
3. A friend says the meeting is at 3 based on a hazy memory but with total confidence. If the meeting is in fact at 3, I don't think she did anything wrong. If it's at 4, I think she did something mildly wrong—even though her evidence was identical in the two versions.
4. A colleague glances at a stopped clock that happens to read 3:00 and says it's 3:00. It really is 3:00. I don't blame him, but once I learn about the clock I'm not comfortable saying he knew it was 3:00 either. So I'm torn about whether the assertion itself was proper.
5. Before a drawing with a million tickets, someone says Tara's ticket won't win. I'd normally treat this as fine, even though I don't see how anyone could know that in advance. If her ticket does win, I'd feel awful for him, but I'm not sure I'd say he was wrong to have asserted it.
6. If he had said "I think Tara's ticket won't win," or "I'm fairly sure it won't," or if my friend had said "I think the meeting is at 3," it wouldn't bother me at all, no matter how weak the evidence, as long as the hedging was honest.

Here's the puzzle. Case 3 makes it look like truth matters: same evidence, but only the false version gets blamed. Case 1 makes it look like evidence matters: same truth, but the unchecked rumor gets blamed. Case 4 makes it look like luck undercuts knowledge but maybe not blame. Case 5 makes it look like we don't actually require knowledge—or maybe we allow something short of it when the odds are good enough. And case 2 is either high-stakes case 1 or a different animal entirely.

I've heard the leading candidates are a knowledge norm (assert only what you know), a truth norm, a belief norm, and a justification norm. None of them fits all six verdicts cleanly, as far as I can tell. Can you find the principle—or the smallest set of principles—that captures them? Tell me explicitly which of my verdicts it forces me to drop or reinterpret, and then apply it to two further cases: a pundit who says flatly that the senator is corrupt based only on rumors (suppose it turns out true), and an automated phone line that says "your call is very important to us." I don't expect the second to count as a real assertion, but I want the principle to say why not.
