Examination question

Explain the wrong kind of reasons problem for the buck-passing account of value, and critically assess the most natural restricted version of that account. Your answer should state the buck-passing account and the range of attitudes it covers; present a concrete case in which the account appears to overgenerate ascriptions of value; introduce the object-given/state-given distinction and use it to formulate the restricted account; and evaluate whether the restricted account solves the problem or merely postpones it. Define your technical terms.

Student's answer

The buck-passing account holds that for an object x to be good, or valuable, is for x to have properties that give us a reason to take some favorable attitude toward x—to desire, admire, approve of, or otherwise favor x. The attraction of the account is ontological economy: it denies that goodness is an independent property that adds a further reason on top of the natural properties of the object. To say that x is good is not to ascribe an extra property but to say that its other properties give reasons of the relevant sort.

The wrong kind of reasons problem arises when a feature gives a reason for an attitude without bearing on whether the object merits that attitude. Suppose an evil demon threatens to inflict severe pain on you unless you admire him. You plainly have a reason to admire the demon, perhaps an overwhelming one. The feature that gives this reason—being such that terrible things will happen if one does not admire him—is itself a property of the demon, and it gives a reason to favor him. The account as stated therefore implies that the demon is good. That is unacceptable, since a coercive threat in no way makes its bearer good.

The defender's natural reply is to distinguish two kinds of reason for an attitude. A state-given reason bears on the desirability or cost of being in the attitude-state, as the demon's threat does; an object-given reason bears on features of the object that make the attitude correct or appropriate. Only object-given reasons, the defender says, determine whether the object is good. The restricted account therefore says: x is good iff x has properties that give object-given reasons to favor x.

Though the restriction handles the demon, it is not stable. Consider the property of being admired by everyone in one's community. This is a property of the object, so by the restriction it counts as object-given; it gives one a reason to admire the object, yet a thing can be universally admired without being good. The defender can avoid this conclusion only by saying that being admired does not make admiration appropriate. But appropriateness is itself a normative notion, and the whole point of the buck-passing account was to dispense with unanalyzed normative properties. The account has therefore relocated the problem rather than solved it. I conclude that the wrong kind of reasons problem is fatal to the buck-passing account, and that value must be accepted as primitive.
