I spent my career in the natural sciences and have only recently begun to read legal and political philosophy systematically. The habit of mind from my old discipline makes me dissatisfied with a bare list, so I want to understand what, if anything, unifies the idea of a protected or suspect ground of discrimination: a basis for treating people differently that is singled out for special scrutiny, in contrast to the countless ordinary distinctions we draw in hiring, grading, pricing, and social life without a second thought.

I can produce examples, but I cannot state the principle behind them. What makes a ground of discrimination suspect? I am not asking for a survey of doctrine or the history of particular legal systems. I want a working definition — a criterion or small set of criteria — that does the classificatory work, and I want it tested against the following cases, which I have chosen precisely because they embarrass simple answers:

1. Race: clearly suspect, if anything is.
2. Religion: protected in most systems, and yet in an important sense chosen and bound up with belief.
3. Sex: protected in most modern systems.
4. Height: immutable and morally arbitrary, a real source of disadvantage in some domains, but generally not treated as suspect.
5. Left-handedness: historically stigmatized, immutable, and morally irrelevant, but no longer a live axis of subordination.
6. Age: protected in some employment contexts, yet plainly relevant to many decisions and rarely treated as a fully suspect ground.
7. Body weight: increasingly stigmatized, partly behavioral, partly constitutional, and contested in its health relevance.
8. A genetic predisposition to a serious disease: immutable and unchosen, yet genuinely relevant to insurance and some employment decisions.
9. A criminal record: heavily consequential, often functioning as a proxy for race, but grounded in past conduct.
10. Political affiliation or opinion: voluntary, tied to expression, protected in some contexts but not treated as a classically suspect category.

For each case I want more than a bare verdict. Run the proposed definition through the case: on the definition, why does it fall where it does? If the definition gives no answer, gives the wrong answer, or forces you into a conclusion you find implausible, say so explicitly, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. At the end, tell me which of your verdicts you regard as least secure, and whether the concept of a suspect ground is best understood as one thing or as a loose family of grounds with different underlying rationales.

My own hunch, which you should stress-test rather than vindicate, is that three considerations do most of the work — immutability, moral irrelevance to the decision at issue, and a history of group subordination — but that no one of them is sufficient, and that no clean definition keeps all three.
