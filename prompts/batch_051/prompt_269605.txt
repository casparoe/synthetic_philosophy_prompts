In my physics class we were discussing why mathematics, a product of human thought, works so well for describing the physical world. When I asked whether that fact needs an explanation, my teacher closed the discussion by saying that I was simply gesturing at the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences. I have since read part of Wigner's 1960 essay of that title, and I was struck by a passage near the end where he remarks that the appropriateness of the language of mathematics for formulating physical laws is a miracle, a gift that we neither understand nor deserve. The phrase now seems to me less like an explanation and more like a name for a problem, but I want to be careful before saying that. What are the different precise claims someone could be making with this slogan, from the trivial to the radical? I would like a table with the following columns: the precise thesis; whether it is trivial, substantive, or radical; whether it is defensible; what it would actually support the person in my lesson in concluding; and whether philosophers of mathematics treat it as settled or contested. Please keep separate what is genuinely settled from what is contested, and do not present one side as the consensus. British English, please.
