Two people in my philosophy club, Maya and Leo, got into it at lunch about a Kierkegaard reading we did on the press and this thing he calls the public. Maya said it's basically a prophecy about social media, only it turned out worse than he guessed. Her argument was that Kierkegaard thinks the press turns concrete individuals into a public, which is an abstraction, like a phantom made of people who all watch and opine but nobody actually puts their life on the line or acts. And the public levels everyone by making everybody a spectator with a take. Maya said that is exactly what a feed does: a billion people performing opinions into a void. Leo cut in and said no, the part of Kierkegaard she always skips is that the press was anonymous. A few editors writing editorials for a passive readership was the whole mechanism. On social media people are not passive and not anonymous in that way: they post, they have handles, they build reputations, they get called out in front of everyone. So alot of what he was describing isnt actually running anymore—the premise that you can opine with zero personal exposure is gone. Maya said Leo is confusing being named with being answerable. A handle is not a person, it's a profile, and the setup is still anonymous in the way that matters, because you can delete the account, post from a burner, or sink into a comment section where nobody knows anyone. The crowd stays the crowd even if everyone shows a screen name. Leo said but that changes a real thing: when a newspaper editorial talked at you, you could not talk back, and nobody could see how you reacted. Now your reactions are public and they pile up into a reputation, so you do stake something each time you post. That is not the same abstraction, it's a different thing with its own problems. Maya said reputation on a platform is exactly the kind of abstraction Kierkegaard meant, it's just the public with a nicer interface. They asked me to break the tie and I could not. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about which specific point, and whether by the end they are still arguing about Kierkegaard or have changed the subject?
