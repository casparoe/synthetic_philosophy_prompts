During a recent seminar on language evolution, a fellow student laid out an argument that I cannot shake. Each premise seems, on its own, almost undeniable; the conclusion, however, strikes me as obviously wrong, and I would be grateful to know precisely where the reasoning overreaches. I work on ambiguity in natural languages, so I ought to be able to locate the flaw myself, but my attempts keep collapsing into hand-waving.

(1) A communicative code is well-adapted to the extent that it enables a hearer to recover a speaker’s intended message reliably. (2) Homonymy—where a single linguistic form (a segmental string, a pitch contour, a tonal specification) carries two or more unrelated meanings, with context left to select among them—forces the hearer to choose among interpretations, and any selection procedure can miscarry. (3) Therefore, other things equal, a code that admits such preventable ambiguity is less efficient than one that avoids it. (4) The cost of avoidance appears small: a speech community could simply fail to preserve forms that come to collide, since the orphaned senses can be covered by existing near-synonyms, borrowing, and periphrasis. (5) Thus, under ordinary communicative pressures, natural languages should either emerge without homonyms or progressively eliminate them once they arise. (6) Yet natural languages stably retain homonymy at many levels, with collisions among ordinary, high-frequency lexical items as well as rare ones, and speakers treat such forms as wholly unremarkable; communication does not collapse. (7) Therefore either the argument fails at some step, or natural languages are simply not well-adapted communicative systems.

I want to reject (7) and, if possible, to retain (1) together with the assumption that languages are shaped by communicative pressures. Which premise or transition should I give up instead, and what does that concession cost? Is homonymy best understood as a tolerated cost, as a sign that the argument has omitted a competing set of pressures on the system, or as something else? Please address each numbered step individually; I am less interested in a general catalogue of how ambiguity is resolved than in identifying the point at which the inference outruns the premises. I would also ask that the response be written in British English.
