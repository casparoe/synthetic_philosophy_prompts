There is a standard philosophical challenge to petitionary prayer: an omniscient God cannot be told anything he does not already know, and an immutable God cannot be moved to alter what he was always going to do, so asking for some external outcome seems either pointless or redundant. The standard reply is that prayer can still be causally efficacious because God eternally willed to bring about the outcome through the prayer as an instrument: the prayer is part of the one unchanging plan, not a perturbation of it. A third view holds that the only genuine effect of prayer is on the person praying — consolation, resolve, attention to others — and that any apparent effect on external events is simply downstream of those inner changes.

I want to know how much observation could actually do to adjudicate among these three positions. Suppose we ran a large, rigorously controlled study of intercessory prayer for health outcomes that the recipients cannot know about, so that their own beliefs cannot be doing the work, and suppose the results were strongly positive, robust across sites, and tracked the number of people praying rather than anyone's awareness of being prayed for. What precisely would that show about the claim that an omniscient and immutable God makes external outcomes causally depend on prayer? What would a convincing null result show, and why is a null result likely to be easier for the theological positions to absorb than a positive result would be? Then set out the residue: in particular, whether any observation could distinguish the view that prayer moves God from the view that God eternally uses prayer as a means, or whether those two are empirically equivalent and the remaining disagreement is purely conceptual. Design the strongest test or sequence of tests you can, and state exactly what each outcome would and would not settle.
