I've been trying to formalize what goes wrong when we refuse to treat naked statistical evidence as sufficient for a finding of liability, and I've arrived at a distinction I want pressure-tested.

The familiar cases. Blue Bus: a pedestrian is struck by a bus that leaves the scene; the Blue Bus Company operates 80% of the buses on that route, so the probability that a Blue bus caused the injury is 0.8. Gatecrasher: 1,000 people attend an event for which only 499 tickets were sold; an attendee is selected at random, and the only evidence against him is that 501 attendees gate-crashed, so the probability he gate-crashed is 0.501. In both cases the evidence makes the defendant's liability more likely than not — and in Blue Bus, quite likely — yet most people and most courts would not find liability on that evidence alone. The same reluctance, magnified, appears if we ask whether the statistic would suffice to convict beyond a reasonable doubt.

My proposal is this. A finding of liability requires evidence to be trace-indexed: the probative force of the evidence must depend on a causal trace left by the defendant's own conduct. More precisely, evidence E is trace-indexed just in case, had the defendant not done the alleged act, E would have been different in a way that tracks that difference. Naked statistical evidence is trace-free: the fleet proportion would have been exactly the same whether or not this Blue bus struck the pedestrian, and the gate-crasher rate would have been exactly the same whether or not this attendee paid. My claim is that trace-indexing, not probability, is the ingredient missing from naked statistical evidence, and that standards of proof implicitly require it.

I want an honest assessment. First, is this a clumsier version of a position that already exists? It feels adjacent to sensitivity and safety conditions in epistemology, and to the legal idea of individualized evidence, but I cannot tell whether the counterfactual formulation adds anything. Second, where does the principle break? I can already see trouble: database DNA hits, which feel like legitimate evidence, may not be trace-indexed in the required sense. Give me the strongest case in which trace-free evidence should nonetheless support a finding of liability, and the weakest case in which trace-indexed evidence should not. Be candid about whether the distinction is doing real explanatory work or merely restating the intuition it was meant to explain.
