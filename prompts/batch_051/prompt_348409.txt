What if everyone did that? It keeps coming up in arguments, and I am a software engineer, so I started treating it as a kind of generalization test: take an action, simulate a world where everyone in relevantly similar circumstances does it, and see whether the underlying practice still works. After an argument with a colleague who brags about never paying for the train while I always do, I wrote down my actual verdicts on a set of cases to see whether any one principle underlies them. My first candidate was: an action is wrong when the practice it takes advantage of would get significantly worse if almost everyone in relevantly similar circumstances did it, and I benefit from that practice staying intact. That felt like the natural way to catch free-riding, but it fails against several of my own cases.

The cases:

1. Jumping the turnstile on a nearly empty train, with no inspector in sight: fine.
2. Shoplifting a two-dollar phone charger from a large retail chain: not fine.
3. Not voting in a midterm election: fine.
4. Underreporting a few hundred dollars of freelance income on my taxes: not fine.
5. Lying to a murderer at the door about where my friend is hiding: fine.
6. Dropping a wrapper in a pristine national park when a bin is a few steps away: not fine.
7. Having a third child while knowing it makes my household's emissions far above average: fine, though I feel the weight of it.
8. Running an ad blocker on a website whose entire business model is ad revenue: fine.
9. The case I cannot settle: rotating free-trial accounts of a paid service so that I use it indefinitely without ever paying. It is parasitic the way cases 1 and 8 are, but something about it feels closer to case 2, and I go back and forth.

The obvious fix, and the one I gather goes back to Kant, is to run the test at a more general level of description of the action rather than on the concrete act: in case 5, lying gets tested and fails, while lying to a pursuer to save an innocent friend passes. But then I can choose the level of description to get whichever verdict I already wanted, which feels like overfitting a model by adding features until it memorizes the training set. I would like the principle that captures as many of these verdicts as possible at the right level of generality, even if the honest answer is that no single principle gets all nine and some of my verdicts have to go. Please state the principle, or the two principles if the cases split cleanly, as precisely as you can; run it through the nine cases; say plainly where it disagrees with my verdicts and why; and tell me what it predicts for case 9 and for one further case of your own choosing that targets the weakest point in your account.
