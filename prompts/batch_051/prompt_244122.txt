I'm a backend engineer, and I've spent this week refactoring an entity system, so I've been living inside the distinction between reference equality and structural equality. Two objects can be structurally identical — every field the same — and still be two objects; `is` vs `==` in Python (or reference equality vs `.equals()` in Java) is exactly the difference between "this very thing" and "something with all the same properties." It hit me that this maps suspiciously well onto the old metaphysical split between primitive thisness (haecceity) and the identity of indiscernibles: one picture says being this very individual is something over and above having such-and-such properties, the other says identity just is the totality of properties.

Then I started thinking about identical particles in quantum mechanics. Swapping two electrons changes nothing physical about the state — the wavefunction has to be symmetric or antisymmetric under exchange, and Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac statistics fall out of that. The usual gloss is that quantum particles of the same type don't have primitive identities the way classical particles do. So it looks to me like quantum mechanics is a world with structural equality but no reference identity: the universe treats identical particles like value objects, and `is` simply isn't defined for them. I cant tell if this is a deep structural parallel or just the word "identity" doing too much work. If it is deep, does anything transfer? I keep half-convincing myself that the counting of bosons, fermions, and distinguishable classical particles is just the difference between counting multisets, sets, and sequences — but maybe that's too clean and quietly assumes the classical picture it's meant to illuminate. And where does the analogy actually break down?
