A colleague of mine has, over many years, produced an unusually stable set of verdicts about cases in which a serious harm is foreseen in the course of securing an important good. She insists she is not applying a simple consequentialist principle, and she does not treat all unintended deaths as equally problematic. I would like you to reverse-engineer the principle she is actually using from her record, and then apply it to a new case.

In each case below, assume that the agents know the stated facts, that everyone involved is innocent, that the act described is the only available way to secure the good in question, and that she regards the good effect as proportionate.

1. The Sidetrack. A runaway trolley will kill five track workers unless a signalman throws a switch, diverting the trolley onto a siding where it will kill one worker instead. Her verdict: permissible.

2. The Footbridge. A runaway trolley will kill five unless a passerby pushes a heavy man off a bridge onto the track; his body is what stops the trolley, and the five are saved. Her verdict: impermissible.

3. The Harvest. A surgeon can kill one healthy patient and distribute his organs to five patients who will otherwise die. Her verdict: impermissible.

4. The Depot. A pilot in a just war can destroy a munitions depot, foreseeing that the blast will also kill one civilian in an adjacent house. The civilian's death plays no role in destroying the depot. Her verdict: permissible.

5. The Infusion Pump. A physician can program an infusion pump to deliver a large dose of morphine to a dying patient to relieve otherwise intractable pain, foreseeing that the dose will also depress the patient's breathing and hasten death. Her verdict: permissible.

Now I want to know how she would rule on the following case, and why.

The Hinged Footbridge. A runaway trolley will kill five. A heavy man is standing on a maintenance hatch in an overhead walkway directly above the track. A bystander in a signal box can throw a switch that opens the hatch; the man falls onto the track, and his body is what stops the trolley; he dies and the five are saved. The bystander never comes into physical contact with him.

Instead of merely guessing her verdict, I want you to do the following. State the rule that most naturally systematizes her five recorded verdicts. State at least one rival rule that fits the same five verdicts equally well. Say what each rule predicts for The Hinged Footbridge. Then give your best prediction about what she would say, with the strongest reason for preferring one rule over the other, and state the main weakness of your preferred reading. Finally, describe a further case, not among the five, that would most cleanly decide between the competing rules, and say what each rule predicts in that case.
