I'm a journalist who covers cognitive science and evolutionary ideas, not a trained philosopher, so forgive me if I mangle some of this. For a piece I've been working on about whether meaning can be explained without any spooky ingrediants, I've gone deep on naturalizing mental content, and I keep finding myself attracted to teleosemantics and then immediately rebelling against it. You're a professional philosopher of mind who works on teleosemantics; please think carefully, and before you give me a verdict, brainstorm the strongest version of each side and spell out the costs of each possible move.

The theory side, as I understand it: teleosemantics, especially the selected-effects or etiological version, says a brain state's content is fixed by what the mechanism was selected to do, its proper function. What appeals to me is that this actually earns the right to talk about error. A frog's fly-detector means fly, not fly-or-pellet-or-shadow, because the mechanism was selected to detect flies; snapping at a pellet is a malfunction rather than a different content. Without the selection history you just have covariance, and the disjunction problem swallows you: the state "means" whatever triggers it. So the history isn't a decorative add-on, it's the part doing the normative work. That's a genuinely tidy naturalistic story.

But then there's the swamp duplicate. Lightning rearranges swamp muck into a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of me at this exact moment, including the brain state I'm in as I think "the coffee is getting cold." No ancestors, no evolutionary history, no learning history. My gut insists: he's thinking the very same thought. If his internal states are type-identical to mine right now, the semantic facts have to be identical too. His "coffee" means coffee and his "cold" means cold; it can't be that my states are about the world and his are just chemistry, purely because my lineage got selected and his didn't. Meaning can't be a matter of genealogy. I find the theory's implication unacceptable, but I also dont trust the intuition, because "from the inside everything would seem the same" is exactly the kind of thing that could be a parochial bias.

So which yields? Should I treat the Swampman intuition as an artifact of imagining duplicate first-person life and debunk it? Should I amend the theory to let current structure, or ongoing use, or something ahistorical, ground content, and if I do, have I just reinvented causal-information semantics and lost the solution to the disjunction problem? Or is there a reading on which both are right, because the intuition tracks honest interpretability or behavioral indistinguishability rather than original content? I want to know what would justify each of those moves, and which one you'd actually make if you had to.
