I am persuaded by the standard quantitative-genetic account of heritability: the heritability of a trait is simply the proportion of phenotypic variance in a specified population that is statistically accounted for by genetic variance — in the narrow sense, h² = V_A/V_P — and a high value implies nothing about whether a trait is fixed, developmentally canalized, or biological in any ordinary sense. Yet I also have a firm intuition that some traits are not the right kind of thing to be called heritable at all. Consider a society with a rigid sartorial code: everyone with a certain common genetic variant is required by law to wear green, and everyone without it is required to wear blue, and the rule is followed without exception. Since the variant is inherited and the dress code tracks it perfectly, the variance in wearing green versus blue is almost entirely statistically accounted for by genetic variance, so on the account I accept, wearing green comes out highly heritable — indeed, nearly perfectly so. But calling a legally enforced dress code a heritable trait seems absurd. Does the statistical account force me to accept that it is heritable, or does this case reveal that the account is missing something? Present the strongest version of each side, and then offer a negotiated verdict: should the intuition be debunked, should the definition be amended (and if so, how, without losing what makes heritability useful), or is there a reading on which the two are compatible?
