In a lecture on the ethics of belief, my lecturer summed up William James's argument in the essay *The Will to Believe* like this. James, he said, was reacting against W. K. Clifford's principle that it is always wrong to believe anything on insufficient evidence. According to my lecturer, James thought Clifford's rule was too strict because it treats every belief the same way, whereas in real life we must often decide what to believe before the evidence is in. James's so-called will to believe therefore supposedly says that whenever we want a proposition to be true, and it makes us happier to believe it, we are entitled to believe it on no evidence at all — provided only that the proposition cannot be settled either way by science. The lecturer added that the obvious objection is that this would license believing in astrology, lucky charms, or any comforting superstition, and that James's later appeal to the idea of a living hypothesis just narrows the field in a question-begging way. He closed by saying Clifford's principle remains untouched because James never actually refuted it; he only changed the subject from evidence to desire. I have a feeling this cannot be quite right, because James himself described his position as a form of empiricism and said he was defending only a very narrow exception, but my lecturer is a professional. Go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful reader of James would accept, which are subtle misreadings, and whether the lecturer's final objection survives a more careful reading of the essay.
