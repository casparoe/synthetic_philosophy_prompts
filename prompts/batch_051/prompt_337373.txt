Hey — I'm a PhD student in decision science, writing a chapter on satisficing and bounded rationality, and I keep running into this scissors metaphor that I want to pressure-test before I let myself use it (or before I let my supervisor use it against me). The metaphor, as it usually gets deployed, is this: rational behavior is like a pair of scissors, where one blade is the agent's computational limits — finite memory, time, attention — and the other blade is the structure of the task environment, like how costly search is or how options are laid out. Cutting is competent behavior under constraints, and the punchline is that you can't judge whether a heuristic is "irrational" by staring at the inside of the head alone. My supervisor uses it to argue that satisficing rules — stop at the first option that clears your aspiration level — aren't a cheap second-best to optimizing; they're just well-matched to environments where search is costly and options arrive one at a time, so a rule that looks lazy in the lab might be exactly right in the wild.

I'm sympathetic, but I suspect the metaphor is smuggling in alot more than the conclusion its licensing. Could you walk me through how far it actually holds? In particular: what maps onto what in the scissors picture; where the disanalogies are (for instance, real scissors are two matched blades made by an outside designer for a particular cutting job, and the blades don't reshape each other — both of which seem off for an agent embedded in an environment it partly builds); which disanalogies actually threaten the conclusion that you can't evaluate a heuristic apart from its environment and which are just aesthetic; and whether the conclusion survives once you set the bad parts aside. If the scissors metaphor is mostly doing quiet work for a designer-shaped picture, what's a better analogy that keeps the good bit without the baggage?
