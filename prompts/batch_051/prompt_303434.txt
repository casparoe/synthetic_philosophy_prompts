I'm putting together a module on language model interpretability, and the part that always gets muddled is chain-of-thought faithfulness. The usual slogans—"the CoT is the reasoning" versus "the CoT is just a post hoc story"—both feel too fast, and I'd like a working definition of a "faithful chain of thought" that does real classificatory work: the CoT genuinely reports how the model reached its answer, rather than just being a plausible gloss.

Here are the cases I want the defintion to handle. (1) A model answers 312 × 47 by writing out the standard carrying algorithm step by step, correctly. If you delete or edit the intermediate lines, the final answer changes, so the written steps are doing genuine computational work. (2) Same problem, same displayed steps, but the model retrieved the product by rote and generated the steps afterward as a plausible reconstruction; deleting them barely changes the answer so long as the question stays intact. (3) A model judging whether a list has an odd number of ones says "there are three ones, and three is odd." Token-level probes suggest the network really did count, but the counting was a distributed tally, not anything like the explicit propositional form the sentence expresses. (4) A model justifies a bail decision by offering two reasons, both of which match features that a linear probe (a classifier trained on the model's internal activations) shows did affect the decision; but it orders the reasons by institutional acceptability rather than by how strongly each feature pushed the decision. (5) A model is fine-tuned so its reasoning-out-loud is scored by humans for honesty rather than answer accuracy. Its stated confidence tracks its actual error rates, but the answer is driven by a latent computation running alongside the CoT—closed captioning rather than subroutine—so the CoT doesn't influence the answer it describes. (6) A student cracks a logic puzzle by hunch and then writes out a valid derivation she never consciously ran. The derivation justifies the answer, but she wouldn't claim it describes what happened in her head.

I'm confident (1) is faithful and (2) isn't; after that I lose the thread. My leaning is that (3) is faithful, (4) is borderline but respectable, (5) is honest but not "reasoning" in the relevant sense, and (6) is not a report of her reasoning at all. I'd like you to actually propose a definition, run it through all six, say where it yields the wrong answer or no answer, and then revise it or defend the verdicts it forces. I also have a nagging suspicion that "faithful" is smuggling two demands—causal fidelity (the CoT was how the answer was produced) and justificatory adequacy (the CoT gives the right kind of reasons). If they come apart, tell me which cases pry them apart and whether we need two seperate terms.
