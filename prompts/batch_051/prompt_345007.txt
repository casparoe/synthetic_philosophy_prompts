I've been trying to think through a resemblance between possibility and probability that I keep coming back to. Both seem to be ways of distributing something across a space of alternatives. A probability model starts by laying out a set of possible outcomes and then assigning a weight to each one, subject to the usual rules that weights are nonnegative and add up to one. A possible-worlds treatment of necessity and possibility also starts by laying out a space of worlds, and then sorts statements according to which worlds make them true: a statement is necessary if it holds in all of them, possible if it holds in at least one. On this way of looking at things, possibility looks like the bare skeleton and probability like the same skeleton with a measure attached. That would suggest that 'possible' means something like 'somewhere in the space' and 'probable' means something like 'occupying a large portion of that space.'

I cannot tell whether this is a genuine identity of structure or just a misleading analogy. What I would like is a careful mapping between the two sides, an account of what would have to be true of both for results to transfer from one to the other, and a clear verdict on where the analogy breaks. In particular, the parallel seems to predict that a proposition is possible exactly when it receives nonzero probability; but I am aware that one can define cases, as with a point on a continuum, where a single outcome is possible and yet receives probability zero. Does a counterexample of that sort refute the whole parallel, or merely show that the mapping needs to be adjusted? And is probability best understood as possibility plus a measure, or are the two notions built for different jobs from the start?
