In my generics seminar this week we were discussing why "ducks lay eggs" is judged true even though most ducks (males, ducklings, etc.) don't, and why "mosquitoes carry malaria" is true even though only a tiny fraction of mosquitos carry the parasite. When I asked what he meant, he said something like "counterexamples don't automatically kill the generalization," but then he couldn't say why, or whether the phrase says anything stronger than that. I want you to act as a professional philosopher of language, think carefully, and spend a moment brainstorming before you answer. Tease apart the distinct claims that could be packed into "the exception proves the rule": the trivial ones, the substantive and defensible ones, and the outright false ones. Then give me a committed verdict, not just a menu of readings: which reading do you think actually does the work, and which one could legitimately license dismissing a counterexample to a generic like "mosquitoes carry malaria"?
