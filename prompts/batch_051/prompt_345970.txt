I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been asked to run a 90-minute session at my company's offsite for 16 engineers on decision-making under uncertainty. I recently encountered an idea I believe is called the unilateralist's curse: when many people each have the power to unilaterally take an irreversible action on behalf of a group, and each decides on the basis of their own private estimate of whether the action is a good idea, the group can end up taking the action even when it is bad overall, because the outcome is effectively controlled by whoever happens to be most optimistic while everyone else's caution carries no veto. I would like the participants to feel this tension directly rather than have me explain it.

I have a rough design in mind, but I am open to a better implementation. The group faces a single binary decision, such as whether to ship a change that will either save the company a significant sum or cause a serious incident. The true state is fixed in advance and kept secret. Each participant privately receives a noisy test signal that is right with some known probability, perhaps 0.7, and each then secretly and simultaneously decides whether to act. If at least one person acts, the group receives the action's result; if nobody acts, the group receives the safe status quo. The stakes should be small but real, for example points that convert into a small shared prize, so that participants care about both gains and losses. During the decision phase, communication and coordination should be forbidden or made impossible, since preventing coordination is itself one of the conditions we want to make vivid. There should be no lecture up front, and the group should not hear the name of the phenomenon until the debrief.

Please provide the full runnable design: the exact materials to print, how the true states and signals are drawn, the payoff matrix, a timeline for the 90 minutes, plain-language instructions for participants, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the underlying idea (the unilateral trigger, private estimates, irreversibility, and the observation that nobody behaved irrationally even though the group outcome was biased). Also include a list of predictable failure modes and how to handle them, and an honest note on which feature of the real phenomenon the game cannot reproduce and that I will need to supply myself in discussion.
