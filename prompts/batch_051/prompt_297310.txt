I chair my university's general education committee, and we're voting Friday on a proposal to require every first-year to take a short module called "Building Hope." It's positive-psychology fare: students are taught to dispute pessimistic attributions, frame setbacks as temporary and specific rather than permanent and global, and visualize successful outcomes. The proponents have solid retention numbers and a plausible empirical case. A couple of us on the committee thinks this is a strange thing for a university to require: it trains students to adopt beliefs about themselves and their futures that aren't particularly answerable to evidence, which looks like cultivating an epistemic vice even if it makes them happier and more persistent. The other side says hope is a moral virtue, not a truth-tracking instrument, and that helping students avoid despair is no less legitimate than teaching them to write clearly. Draft the short memo I'll circulate before the vote. I need a concrete recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with criteria the committee can actually apply, and an honest statement of the strongest argument on the side you don't take. Also, please seperate two things we keep running together in meetings: hoping that p, which seems compatible with a clear-eyed view of how likely p is, and believing or expecting that p will probably come about. Which of those does the module actually train, and does the distinction matter for our decision? End the memo with a final line of the form \answer{adopt}, \answer{reject}, or \answer{adopt with conditions}.
