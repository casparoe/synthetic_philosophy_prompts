ok so I've fallen down a rabbit hole about whether trusting what people tell you is its own basic source of knowledge, or just induction applied to speakers, and I keep coming back to this passage in Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (in the section "Of Miracles"). He writes:

"It will be sufficient to observe, that our assurance in any argument of this kind is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony, and of the usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses. It being a general maxim, that no objects have any discoverable connexion together, and that all the inferences, which we can draw from one to another, are founded merely on our experience of their constant and regular conjunction; it is evident, that we ought not to make an exception to this maxim in favour of human testimony, whose connexion with any event seems, in itself, as little necessary as any other."

So as I read him, a kid has to pile up a bunch of cases where what people said turned out true, and only then does testimony start counting as evidence. But then there's Reid (Thomas Reid, the common sense guy) who basically said no, we're built to trust other people's speech before we've checked any track record, so testimony is evidence from the start rather than evidence we earn. And now I can't tell wether that's a real empirical disagreement or just two ways of describing the same thing.

If it is testable, whats the strongest test? I'm imagining something with little kids or even infants, before they could have compiled much of a track record about speakers being accurate, to see whether they already treat someone's statement as evidence about the world. Design the best version of that you can: the actual setup, the pattern of results that would push toward Hume, the pattern that would push toward Reid. Then be really explicit about what the experiment would not settle even if it came out clean — like, could Hume always say the relevant induction just happened faster or earlier than we designed for, and would that make the whole debate unfalsifiable? I'm honestly more interested in that leftover part than in the experiment itself.
