I have been trying to write about paternalism and find that I cannot state what the term actually requires, even though I apply it confidently in easy cases. I would like you to give me a careful working definition of paternalism — not a survey of positions, but a criterion with enough structure that it can be applied case by case.

Here are the cases I have been using to test candidate definitions: (a) a competent adult is legally prohibited from buying heroin for her own use; (b) a physician withholds a terminal diagnosis from a competent patient because the physician believes the truth would cause pointless suffering; (c) a restaurant is legally required to print calorie counts on its menus; (d) a government runs an advertising campaign urging people to exercise; (e) employees are automatically enrolled in a retirement savings plan but may opt out with a one-page form; (f) cigarettes carry a steep excise tax explicitly designed to reduce adult smoking; (g) a friend hides the car keys of a heavily intoxicated person who insists she is fine to drive; (h) a competent adult is prohibited from selling one of her kidneys on the open market. I am confident that some of these are paternalistic and some are not, but the middle ones, especially (e), (f), and (h), leave me unsure, and my intuitions shift depending on whether I emphasize coercion, deception, motives, or outcomes.

Please propose a definition, apply it to each case, and say where it yields the wrong answer or no answer; if you must revise it along the way, do so, and if it commits you to a surprising verdict about one of the borderline cases, defend that verdict. In particular, I would like the definition to settle whether nudges that preserve freedom of choice — like the default enrollment in (e) — fall under the term, and whether the relevant feature is the paternalist's aim or the structure of the intervention. If the term turns out to do two distinguishable jobs, you may say so, but only after showing that a single definition cannot do the work.
