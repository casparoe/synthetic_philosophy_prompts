Explain the problem of petitionary prayer for a God who is omniscient and perfectly immutable. Then critically discuss two standard responses. (20 points)

And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

Petitionary prayer is a problem because if God is omniscient, He already knows what we need before we ask, so prayer can't inform Him of anything. And if He is immutable, His will can never be moved or changed by what we do, so our requests can't affect what He was going to do anyway. Prayer therefore looks either pointless or, at best, something God commands just for its own sake, like keeping up a relationship.

The first response is open theism, which basically solves it by giving up immutability and, usually, full foreknowledge of free future choices: if God genuinely learns new things and can change His plans, then prayer can make a real difference by giving God information and influencing Him. That handles the problem pretty directly.

The second response is the classic prayer-changes-us-not-God move, which is Aquinas's view in the Summa. On this view God never actually responds to petitionary prayer; the only effect is on the person praying—it trains our desires, produces humility, and so on. Since an immutable God can't change at all, He also can't act in time, so petitionary prayer is really just a kind of meditation addressed to God. That dissolves the problem, because prayer was never really about getting anything from God in the first place.

So, given immutability, petitionary prayer can only be justified as self-transformation, and that justification is enough.
