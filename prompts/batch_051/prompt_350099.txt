I direct communications for a health-research charity that funds clinical studies and turns their results into press releases, donor updates, and policy briefings, where a wrong call can mislead patients and erode donor trust. I am the sign-off for borderline public claims, several times a week, usually under a deadline measured in hours. Our current rule is a single red line: we never knowingly assert a falsehood, and a staffer who does is treated as having committed a serious, potentially firing-level offense. But almost none of the cases that reach me are outright false assertions. They are literally true sentences doing misleading work: a favourable subgroup effect reported as though it were the main finding, a "no evidence that" sentence placed so the reader takes it to mean the opposite of what the study supported, a qualifier placed after the claim instead of before it, a photograph of a child arranged next to a sentence that is true in general but false as applied to her. Our current practice treats those as presentational problems—"fix the wording"—rather than as violations of the same order. I have come to suspect that the lying/misleading distinction is doing a large share of our moral work while functioning mostly as a loophole.

I am familiar with the standard philosophical move: a lie is the assertion of something the speaker believes false, while misleading works through what the audience is led to infer from a literally true statement. I do not need that literature summarized. I need the defensible core of it turned into a reusable decision procedure.

Write the procedure as an actual checklist that a junior communications officer, untrained in philosophy, can run in under twenty minutes. It should: (1) give the questions in the order they should be asked; (2) say what counts as a sufficient answer to each; (3) say where the procedure stops with a "no" and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts; and (4) flag the steps where the simplification drops a real distinction, and what the procedure will therefore get wrong. I would ask you to taboo the words "lie" and "mislead" in the checklist itself, as far as you can: make it run on the underlying features of a communication. If a statement can escape the procedure by being technically true while leaving the audience with a false belief, the procedure has failed. In particular, say whether literal truth should appear in the procedure at all, or only as an indicator of features that do the moral work—the speaker's deceptive intent, the foreseeability of the audience's false belief, the breach of the audience's trust, the harm, and the speaker's ability to retreat to the literal meaning later. Where those features conflict, say how to weigh them, and where they cannot be weighed by the checklist, say that plainly.

Then run the procedure on the following three cases and show the step-by-step output, including at least one case where the procedure returns a verdict my communications team will not like and what I should tell them:

(a) We issue a press release saying, "Participation in the program was associated with a 31% reduction in hospital readmissions." The cited study found that effect only in a pre-specified subgroup of younger patients; the primary analysis showed no statistically significant effect in the full population, and the release does not say so.

(b) A reporter asks whether the board has discussed pausing funding for one of our long-running research groups. Our spokesperson answers, "The board has not taken any decision to pause funding." This is literally true, but the board has discussed the question twice and deferred it, and a reasonable reporter will take the answer to mean that pausing has not been on the table.

(c) A donor update pairs a true general sentence—"Early intervention can mean the difference between dependence and independence"—with a photograph and first name, Maria, from a separate file, arranged so that donors will believe Maria received and benefited from our services. Maria was not enrolled in the program.

Close by saying what the procedure cannot do: the cases it will systematically misjudge, the situations in which we should override it, and how we should record an override without silently changing the rule.
