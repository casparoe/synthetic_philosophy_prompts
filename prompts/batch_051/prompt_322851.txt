In a graduate seminar on distributive justice, the instructor laid out the leveling-down objection as a reason to reject the view that equality is intrinsically valuable. The objection runs roughly as follows: if equality itself were a source of value, then making the better-off worse off without making anyone better off could still be an improvement in at least one respect, and many find that implication implausible. A classmate then dismissed the egalitarian position with the line, 'Equality isn't what matters; what matters is how badly off people are.' I suspect that line is doing more work than its surface grammar shows, so before the next meeting I want to work out exactly what it could mean.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses that one-liner might be used to assert, ordered from the near-tautological to the deeply substantive. For each candidate reading, say (1) whether it is independently plausible or actually false, (2) whether it really distinguishes the resulting view from egalitarianism, and (3) whether it supports, undercuts, or merely changes the subject with respect to the leveling-down objection. In particular, address whether the slogan itself commits anyone to a specific prioritarian weighting function—for example, aggregating a strictly concave function of individual welfare—or whether it is also consistent with sufficientarianism, bare welfarism, or views that care only about absolute thresholds. End by identifying which reading the classmate would need for the line to do the anti-egalitarian work he was using it to do, and note any reading under which the position would still face a residual leveling-down problem.
