People in my philosophy of language course kept going back and forth about empty names, and it got me chewing on the standard possible-worlds fix for truth in fiction. The move is something like: Sherlock Holmes doesn't refer to anyone actual, but Sherlock Holmes lived in Baker Street is still true in the stories, because it holds in the worlds where the Conan Doyle narratives are accurate reports of real events rather than fiction; Sherlock Holmes was a ballet dancer doesn't hold in those worlds, so it's false in the stories. That whole set-up seems to assume a fixed, single-author corpus and a stable line between what the text implies and what it merely leaves open.

But that assumption looks basicly obsolete for the fiction people actually fight about. Star Wars has had a canon hierarchy for ages, and a while back the old Expanded Universe was demoted to non-canonical Legends while a corporate story group decides what counts going forward. The special editions changed what happens on screen in the cantina scene, so the same work makes different sentences true-in-the-story depending on which edit you're watching. The SCP wiki is worse: thousands of authors post articles that deliberately contradict one another, and the community norm is that there's no single canon, just overlapping canons you can adopt. And then there's headcanon, which fans hold while knowing it binds nobody else.

So I'm trying to figure out: does the possible-worlds account of truth in fiction survive once the corpus is no longer a stable single-author text but an institutionally managed, continuously revised pile of media with no settled answer to what the story is? Which premise actually gives out, and is there a repaired version that still says something useful—maybe by relativizing truth in fiction to a canon policy, an edition, or an interpretive community—or does the apparatus just dissolve into fan sociology?
