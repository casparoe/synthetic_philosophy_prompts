You are a moral philosopher who works on punishment, ritual, and collective responsibility. Take your time, and begin by brainstorming several candidate principles before you settle on and defend the strongest one.

Consider the following asymmetry. In ordinary criminal justice, we treat it as nearly axiomatic that guilt and punishment may not be transferred: if one person commits the crime, jailing someone else instead is not merely ineffective but a further injustice, and a court that knowingly sentenced an innocent person to restore the community's sense of order would be adding a wrong rather than repairing one. Yet a strikingly similar transaction lies at the heart of many sacrificial traditions: a community ritually places its sins, impurities, or guilt onto an animal or scapegoat, drives it out or kills it, and then treats itself as cleansed rather than as having committed an additional wrong. Nor is this confined to antiquity—organizations sometimes sacrifice a scapegoat for collective failures, and participants often experience the act as a genuine resolution rather than a fresh injustice.

Is there a principle that can justify this difference—something that makes ritual guilt-transfer intelligible or even permissible while still condemning the punishment of an innocent person in a court of law? Or is the difference merely that the scapegoat is not, or is no longer seen as, a full member of the moral community, making the purification a form of collective self-deception? Formulate the strongest version of whatever principle you find, test it against nearby cases—a willing substitute, a condemned prisoner, an animal, a symbolic effigy, a scapegoat who is fully human—and give an honest verdict on whether the practice should be kept, modified, or abandoned.
