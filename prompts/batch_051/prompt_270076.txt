Okay I don't have any background in philosophy, I just went down a rabbit hole reading about Maimonides after a conversation about animal sacrifice that turned into an argument on a forum. What I understand from what smart people were saying: Maimonides held that the animal sacrifices in the Torah were not the ideal form of worship, they were a concession. The surrounding nations worshiped by slaughtering animals and offering them to gods or demons, and if the Torah had just banned that style of worship cold turkey, the Israelites would have felt like worship without sacrifice wasn't real worship. So God redirected it, restricted it to one place, and gradually moved them toward prayer and thinking about God directly. The sacrifices were like training wheels.

So here is where I get interested. Right now there are groups that want to rebuild the temple in Jerusalem and restart actual animal offerings, and theres a whole debate about whether lab-grown meat counts as meat for halachic purposes since it comes from real animal cells but no animal is killed, or bled, or really ever conscious.

I want to know what Maimonides would say if you could ask him just this one thing. Not what he'd think of electricity or modern politics—just: does his own theory of sacrifice commit him to wanting the temple rebuilt and the offerings restored when it becomes possible, or would he think trying to restart them is a step backwards because the whole point of the concession was that animal sacrifice was supposed to be temporary? And what does lab-grown meat do to his view? Would he say thats actually the first version that fits: all the form of the commandment with none of the killing. Or would he think the killing—or at least the giving up of something alive—was the whole pedagogical point, so a bloodless sacrifice fails at the very thing the law was trying to teach?

I guess the deeper question is whether a person could accept Maimonides' whole account of why sacrifices existed and still come down on either side of the modern debate, or whether being consistent forces you into one answer. I'd like you to actually take the questions on the chin instead of saying its impossible to know what a 12th-century person would think.
