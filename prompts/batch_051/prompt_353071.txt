As someone who has for years defended a broadly Nozickian, truth-tracking analysis of knowledge, I would like to commission from you a careful map of the position's vulnerabilities. The view I hold has two central pieces. The first is a tracking condition on knowledge: a true belief amounts to knowledge only if, in the nearby worlds where the proposition is false, the subject would not hold the belief (sensitivity), and in the nearby worlds where the proposition remains true but things differ slightly, the subject would still hold it (adherence). The second is a rejection of epistemic closure under known entailment: one may know that p, know that p entails q, and even competently infer q from p, without thereby knowing that q, because the tracking conditions are not guaranteed to survive the inference.

The standard attractions of this package should be familiar. It promises to explain why I can know that the animal in the pen is a zebra while failing to know that it is not a mule painted to look like a zebra, and why I can know that I have hands while failing to know that I am not a brain in a vat being fed the experience of having hands. More generally, it lets us resist skeptical arguments that run through closure while preserving a great deal of ordinary empirical knowledge.

What I now want is an inventory of what would count against this position, ordered by how seriously I should take each item. I am especially interested in the following questions, though I do not want you to confine yourself to them. Are there familiar, uncontrived cases of knowledge that the tracking conditions get wrong, and would these failures be fatal or repairable by a small amendment? Does the denial of closure force me into positions I cannot plausibly maintain in the epistemology of deduction, testimony, or memory? Is sensitivity too demanding for some domains of knowledge and too permissive for others? Which of these objections would a committed tracking theorist acknowledge as genuine costs, and which would they dismiss as question-begging or as costs any fallibilist theory must pay? And finally, has the view, after its standard replies are added, become insulated from the possibility of refutation — and if so, is that insulation a virtue of a philosophical analysis or a reason to suspect it has stopped making contact with the phenomena?

I would like the considerations ranked from those that should most reasonably move me to revise the view down to those that are at most awkward. Be explicit about which defeaters can be absorbed by a refinement of the tracking conditions and which strike at the denial of closure itself. I should add one personal note about what I would find most persuasive: a clean demonstration that the tracking conditions fail for a wholly ordinary piece of knowledge, or a rigorous argument that rejecting closure commits me to an incoherent combination of claims elsewhere in my epistemic life, would move me far more than any appeal to the intuitive oddity of knowing something while failing to know its obvious consequence.
