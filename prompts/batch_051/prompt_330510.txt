Exam question: "Chesterton's fence is routinely invoked in debates about reform. Explain what the fence example is meant to illustrate, and then evaluate whether the principle it illustrates commits Chesterton to a Burkean conservative view of institutions. Be explicit about what Chesterton thinks should happen once a reformer has gone away and worked out why the fence is there."

Student answer: "Chesterton asks us to imagine a reformer who comes across a fence blocking a road and wants to tear it down. Chesterton says the reformer should not be allowed to do so until he can explain what the fence is for. The point is that the reformer's inability to see a reason for something is not evidence that no reason exists; he may simply be ignorant of the function the fence serves. Chesterton is therefore making an epistemic point about the limits of individual reason. This is basically the same as Burke's defense of prejudice, where prejudice is a stock of inherited wisdom that the individual cannot fully articulate. Burke thinks long-established institutions contain the accumulated wisdom of past generations, and Chesterton's fence is just a vivid restatement of that idea. Both hold that the mere fact that an institution has survived is a decisive reason to keep it, even when we cannot see its rationale. Chesterton's reformer, if he comes back and cannot explain the fence's purpose, would have no case for removing it, which shows that for Chesterton long-standing institutions should be preserved whether or not anyone today can justify them."
