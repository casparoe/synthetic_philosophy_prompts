Here is an exam question from an upper-level ethics course:

"In her essay 'Moral Saints,' Susan Wolf argues that although the moral saint exemplifies moral goodness, the saint's life is not one we should want for ourselves or our loved ones. Reconstruct her argument, distinguishing the two kinds of saint she discusses, and then critically evaluate the claim that a maximally morally good life must crowd out non-moral goods. Use at least one concrete example."

Here is a student's answer:

"In her essay 'Moral Saints,' Susan Wolf distinguishes the Loving Saint, whose concern for others flows naturally from her character and who genuinely enjoys helping, from the Rational Saint, who shares the same moral commitments but must act on them out of duty while suppressing her contrary inclinations. Wolf argues that neither saint is an attractive personal ideal. The Rational Saint is forever self-denying, since she must constantly override desires she cannot fully eliminate. The Loving Saint is happier, but because every interest she has is subordinated to the question of whether it contributes to moral improvement, she lacks room for wit, aesthetic taste, athletic excellence, and friendships pursued simply for their own enjoyment. Wolf concludes that a moral saint would be a boring, humorless person, and therefore that moral sainthood is not morally good. That inference, however, fails: even if a saint would be boring, it does not follow that she is not a good person. Furthermore, Wolf simply assumes that a saint cannot value non-moral goods for their own sake. This is an empirical claim she never defends. Plenty of people are unusually honest and generous while also loving music and being devoted parents, so maximal moral goodness is perfectly compatible with a personally rich life."
