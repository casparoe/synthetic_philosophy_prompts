I'm a reporter who covers philosophy and cognitive science, and I'm working on a piece about how Ibn Sīnā's flying man thought experiment keeps getting revived in modern debates about self-awareness. I built the following argument for the piece, and now I suspect it proves too much, because the conclusion is something almost nobody I interview wants to sign up for. Here it is.

In the so-called flying man scenario, imagine a person created all at once, fully formed and healthy, but suspended in empty air with his eyes covered and his limbs arranged so they don't touch each other or anything else. He has no sight, smell, hearing, taste, touch, no bodily pain or pressure, and no remembered sensations from before. Avicenna's point, as I understand it, is that this person would still affirm that he exists, with certainty.

1. The suspended person, with no sensory input and no memory of any sensory input, would still be directly aware of his own existence.
2. So awareness of one's own existence does not depend on the senses, on bodily contact, or on having had a history of experiences.
3. If awareness of my own existence isn't something I get from my body or senses, then it belongs to something non-bodily: a soul or intellect that apprehends itself directly.
4. And if the soul can apprehend itself directly, independently of the body, then it can have that self-awareness even in the absence of any external world.
5. But then my most basic and certain piece of knowledge, that I exist, is wholly self-contained and puts me in direct contact only with myself.
6. If that is the most basic bit of knowledge, then awareness of things outside me—this laptop, your face, my own hands—can only be a further step: an inference or a construction from sensory material, never as direct as self-knowledge.
7. That seems too much. In ordinary experience, seeing a table is just seeing a table; it doesn't feel like running an inference on private sense data.
8. So either ordinary perception is a kind of theory all the way down, or the flying-man argument ran off the rails somewhere after the first step.

Where does this train jump the track? I'd most like to keep (1) and (2), and I'd be least sorry to lose (3) or (6), but maybe giving up (3) leaves Avicenna with no argument about the soul at all. Which premise do I reject, and what exactly do I pay for rejecting it? If the flaw is an equivocation between two senses of a term, name the term and the two senses.
