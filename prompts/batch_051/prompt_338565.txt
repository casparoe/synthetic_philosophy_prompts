Just got back from a two-hour argument with a friend about wether Japan is actually as rule-bound as the examples people always trot out, so this material is very fresh in my head and I now have to grade a stack of midterm short answers on the tight–loose cultures unit. The question I set was:

"Explain what the tightness–looseness dimension is supposed to capture in cross-cultural psychology. Is a tight society best understood as one that simply has a lot of rules? Why or why not? Illustrate with an example."

One student wrote this (copied from there submission):

"A society's tightness or looseness is about how strongly it expects people to follow norms and how much it tolerates deviance. Tight societies have strict norms, sanction violations harshly, and leave little room for individual judgment; loose societies are permissive and tolerate a wide range of behavior. For example, Japan is usually described as tight and the United States as loose. Tightness basically tracks collectivism: tight societies are tight because collectivist values make everyone enforce the group's standards, and loose societies are loose because individualists do not care much about shared norms. So yes, in practice a tight society is one that has a lot of rules—Japan regulates bowing, gift-giving, even how you apologize—and the number of rules is what makes a society tight. A tight society therefore does demand more rule-following than a loose one."

Grade this out of 10 and give me the specific point-by-point comments you'd write, including anything a hasty grader would miss. I mostly want to know whether the "tightness = lots of rules" line and the collectivism stuff hold up, and how much they should cost the student.
