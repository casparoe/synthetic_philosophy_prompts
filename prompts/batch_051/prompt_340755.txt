I hold that Harsanyi's aggregation theorem does the substantive work in the foundations of utilitarianism, and I want an honest map of what would count against that position. Stated precisely: suppose each individual's preferences over lotteries obey the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so each individual can be represented by a cardinal utility function unique up to positive affine transformation; suppose further that a social preference over lotteries obeys those same axioms and respects a strong Pareto condition — if every individual is indifferent between two prospects, society is indifferent between them; and if at least one individual strictly prefers one prospect and no individual strictly prefers the other, society strictly prefers the former. Harsanyi's theorem then forces the social utility function to be a positively weighted sum of the individual utility functions. I take the theorem to show that, once individual rationality, social rationality, and Pareto are granted, utilitarianism is not an additional ethical commitment but a consistency requirement; the weights express the ethical judgement of equal concern once a suitable normalisation of each person's utility is supplied.

Tell me what would count against this position, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. I am not asking for a bibliography or a historical survey. For each candidate defeater, say whether a committed defender of the position should concede that it genuinely counts against them, and distinguish what it damages from what it leaves intact. In particular, address at least the following charges; if there are further defeaters you regard as weightier, include them.

First, the representation-dependence charge. Because von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities are unique only up to positive affine transformation, the coefficients in the weighted-sum representation are artefacts of the scale chosen for each person; without some prior fixing of each person's zero and unit — an interpersonal comparison or a stipulated normalisation — the theorem cannot establish that each person is to count equally, or indeed any determinate weighting at all. On this charge, the utilitarian conclusion is an artefact of the representation rather than a result of the axioms.

Second, the charge that the Pareto condition takes subjective preferences as its input, and that this is the wrong input where preferences are misinformed, adaptive, or antisocial.

Third, the charge that the social-level von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, especially the independence axiom, quietly rule out ex ante fairness and other non-utilitarian concerns. A social preference that is indifferent between a benefit going to one person and its going to another, but strictly prefers a fair lottery between those outcomes, violates independence; so the theorem does not show that such concerns are inconsistent with rationality — only that they fail an axiom chosen in advance to exclude them.

Fourth, the charge that the normalisation rescue is unstable. Normalising each person's utility to their best and worst feasible outcomes restores uniqueness, but makes the weights depend on the choice set; introducing an additional alternative changes the normalisation, and with it the apparent relative weighting of two persons — and the social ranking of two existing prospects under the fixed weights can reverse. If so, the weights cannot play the role of fixed ethical standards.

Having worked through these, step back and assess whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability by retreating from a foundation for utilitarianism to a mere representation theorem, and whether such a retreat would be a problem. Be explicit about which parts of my view survive even if all four charges succeed, and where a defender should draw the line between responsible qualification and ad hoc insulation.
