I run a small monthly argument club at a brewery—nobody in it has any formal philosophy, we just like arguing—and two of the regulars, Mira and Dev, have been stuck on the same fight for months. They've agreed to try to write a joint statement of where they actually stand, but every draft one of them writes smuggles their own side into the "agreed" part, so they asked me to get someone neutral. I'd like you to draft that document.

Their argument, as best I can summarize it: suppose you've looked into a question as carefully as you can—like whether a local ballot measure is worth supporting—and you find out that someone just as smart and just as well-informed as you, who has looked at the same evidence, disagrees. Should that lower your confidence? Mira says yes, and substantially: if it doesn't move you, you're treating your own head as less fallible than everyone else's. Dev says no: your confidence should track the evidence, and someone else's judgment isn't your evidence, or at best is a small piece of it—you can't reason from inside their skull, and if you update every time you meet a smart dissenter your beliefs just get yanked around by whoever is most confident. Neither of them is a straw man of the other, and that's part of the problem.

They've also been through a bunch of side-fights: whether Mira's rule means two people who both follow it simply meet in the middle (she says yes; he says that lets the most sincere-sounding confident person drag everyone toward them), whether Dev's rule lets someone ignore any number of dissenters and protect their own bias (he says there's a difference between weighing evidence and treating disagreement itself as decisive evidence), and whether you can even judge that someone is your "peer" without already using the very reasoning under dispute.

Please write the joint statement they could both sign—the kind of text where each one reads it and says "that's my view, better put than I could put it, not tipped against me." Use these sections and lengths:

1. Agreed claims (roughly 10, numbered): what both accept about the question they're disputing, what each side is explicitly not claiming, and any points they agree are not actually between them.
2. The two positions, stated exactly (one paragraph of 80–120 words each): word each so its holder would sign it as a fair statement of their own view, with no hidden tilt.
3. Where the fight might be verbal (if anywhere): the bit they should stop arguing about, and what it's distinct from.
4. Cruxes (3–5): the sub-questions such that if one side gave ground on one, the other side's position would actually move.
5. Predictions: for each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence both would accept bears on it, and what each predicts seperately. A table works fine here if that's clearer.

I don't need background or a literature lesson—just the document. If there are standard names for these positions, mention them once so we can look them up, but don't let the names do any work.
