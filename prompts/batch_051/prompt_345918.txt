I find myself pulled in two directions about what it is to infer a person's preferences from what they do, and I want a principled way to settle it.

On one side I am persuaded by the basic modeling stance of inverse reinforcement learning: a person's preferences are whatever reward function makes their observed behavior rational—or near-rational once we allow for noise, ignorance, and the constraints the situation imposes. The stance has genuine systematic virtues. It anchors preference inference in something observable rather than in unverifiable inner states. It unifies explanation and prediction, since the reward function that rationalizes what someone did is also what forecasts what they will do next. It applies to any agent whose internals we cannot inspect, from nonhuman animals to artificial systems. It even has an answer to its own underdetermination problem—many reward functions rationalize the same behavior—namely, impose a preference for simpler or more entropic reward functions and treat the result as the inferred preference. And it absorbs the obvious counterexamples: the person who chooses among a restricted menu of options, or under threat, or while misinformed, is still maximizing some reward given constraints and beliefs; the apparent gap between behavior and preference is a misspecified environment or a wrong belief, not a fact about preference beyond behavior. On this view, if behavior and preference seem to come apart, the model is wrong, not the identification of the two.

On the other side, I have an intuition I cannot shake: there are cases where behavior is precisely not authoritative about what a person really wants. The person in withdrawal who uses, the worker who signs under serious duress, the person whose judgment forges ahead and acts against her settled better judgment, the depressed patient whose choices are narrowed in ways that a healthier version of herself would disown. In these cases the point of saying 'that is not what they really prefer' is to drive a wedge between what the behavior reveals and what the person wants. The wedge matters for how we treat them: we do not infer from the coerced signature that the person genuinely consented, or from the addict's use that the addiction expresses a preference we should help them satisfy. Here preference looks like a real psychological state distinct from choice, with behavior as fallible evidence for it rather than what constitutes it. If that is right, a reward function fitted to behavior can be systematically wrong about what a person actually wants, however parsimoniously it rationalizes the behavior afterward.

I want the strongest version of both positions and a negotiated verdict. Which is the right move: debunk the intuition as folk psychology that a mature science of preference inference should shed; amend the theory so that revealed preference and genuine preference come apart—perhaps by adding explicit constraints, mental states, or introspective report—at the cost of the theory's austere virtues; or insist the two are compatible because IRL only ever aimed at recovering a behavioral construct, not the genuine article? If the last, is that a reconciliation or a retreat? Tell me what would justify each move, and which of my convictions ends up having to go.
