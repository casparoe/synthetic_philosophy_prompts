I've got an epistemic policy that I follow pretty consistently, and I'm not sure whether it's discipline or just protecting my own beliefs. The rule is: once I've crossed over into actually knowing something—not just being pretty sure—I stop treating new evidence against it as a serious contender. I still read the counterevidence, but I approach it as a puzzle about where the mistake is, not as a reason to lower my confidence. For example, I know my moms birthday from years of celebrating it, and if I found a hospital record with a different date, I'd assume the record is wrong rather than that I was mistaken. I also know the proof that there are infinitely many primes, and if a serious-looking mathematician claimed to have proven the set finite, I wouldn't update even a little; I'd look for the error.

A friend of mine calls this the dogmatism paradox: if I genuinely know p, then any seeming evidence against p must be misleading, because it's evidence for a falsehood, so it's rational to ignore it. But that makes knowledge a license to never change your mind, and people have known things and later discovered they were wrong. So is my policy defensible? Is there a principled version of it that keeps what's good without the crazy part, and how would I tell from the inside when I'm rationally discounting bad evidence versus sealing myself off from correction? Does it matter how I came to know the thing—memory, proof, testimony, long experience—or what's at stake if I'm wrong?
