Our ICU's policy permits letting a patient die but forbids killing, and the line collapses every time an actual case arrives — withdrawing a ventilator, withholding antibiotics, administering morphine. I'm the clinical ethicist, and I need a reusable decision procedure that a resident or charge nurse with no philosophy training can run in thirty minutes, at 3 a.m., and that I can defend at committee review the next morning. Ground the procedure in the philosophical distinction between killing and letting die, and the wider distinction between doing and allowing harm, but translate every technical concept — intention, causation, foresight — into a concrete question or observation. Please specify the questions in order and why each one earns its place; what counts as an adequate answer to each; where the procedure should stop and rule that the action is permitted as letting die or ruled out as killing; where it should decline to decide and send the case to the full committee; and which steps are value judgments dressed up as procedure. Then run the procedure on three cases: withdrawing a ventilator at a competent patient's request; withholding antibiotics from a patient with advanced dementia and pneumonia; and giving morphine in escalating doses for intractable pain when the team knows the dose may also hasten death. End by naming one step the procedure cannot settle, and one case in which it will return an answer the ICU staff are likely to resist.
