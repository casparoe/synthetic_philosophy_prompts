Act as an aesthetics professor who works on the paradox of tragedy — the old puzzle about why people seek out and enjoy artworks that make them feel sad, scared, or horrified, even though they normally avoid exactly those feelings. I'm putting together material for an upper-level seminar and I've assembled what I take to be the standard resolutions. My worry is that the seminar will just cycle through them, so I want to add something fresher.

Here's my running list:

- Catharsis: tragic art gives the emotions a harmless discharge, leaving us relieved or cleansed, which is why the overall experience counts as pleasant.
- Conversion (artistry over content): the skill with which the painful material is handled — formal shape, style, the sense of a well-made whole — turns the distress into aesthetic pleasure; the same events told flatly or lived in fact would not please.
- Distance/security: the fictional frame blunts the emotion; what we get is sadness-like but stripped of its worst sting, and we can savor it from a safe ledge.
- Meta-response: its not the sadness we value but our response to our response — e.g., satisfaction or approval at being the kind of person who is moved by undeserved suffering. A positive second-order attitude to a negative first-order one.
- Hedonic contrast / sweet sorrow: the pain makes the return to ordinary life sweeter; or low-dose negative feeling inside a safe frame is itself a kind of luxury, the way chili heat is part of the pleasure.
- Insight: the felt cost is the price of admission for hard-won understanding of loss, luck, and vulnerability; the gain, not the pain, carries the pleasure.
- Emotional practice: fiction works as a low-cost gym for the emotion system; we use sad art to rehearse grief, compassion, and fear, and that calibrated practice is the payoff.
- Social bonding: communal weeping, shared shivers, and collective rituals around tragic works produce bonding (maybe via the body's own reward chemistry), and that social reward is what we are really chasing.

I suspect these eight come close to exhausting the familiar options. I want a ninth that is none of them: not a rewording, not a special case, and not a mash-up of two or three. It can be a little heterodox, but it should be something a sharp student could defend for ten minutes in discussion. Think carefully — first brainstorm three or four candidate moves, then pick and develop the strongest. After you present it, tell me straight whether it is any good: does it actually dissolve the original puzzle, or does it just move it back one step? Also say which item on my list it is closest to, and why it still is not the same move.

And please write the whole answer in American English.
