I work for a regional land-management agency, and I have been puzzling over a feature of contemporary conservation policy that strikes me as philosophically unstable. In many jurisdictions, a developer may drain a wetland, clear a patch of coastal scrub, or build over a stretch of floodplain provided the damage is offset elsewhere: the developer must create, restore, or protect a habitat at another site deemed comparable, often by purchasing credits from a mitigation bank that prices the claim in acres or habitat units. The working premise is fungibility. The lost site's value is inventoried as a list of functions—water filtration, flood storage, carbon sequestration, habitat for listed species—and if the replacement scores high enough on the same indicators, the loss is treated as settled, however distant the substitute and whatever particular features of the old place are gone permanently.

Yet the same regimes also honor values that resist this arithmetic. They exempt certain old-growth forests and rare ecosystems from offsetting altogether, as though some places were irreplaceable in a way no functional score can capture. Public hearings record residents insisting that the new wetland is not the place where they fished and walked; agencies sometimes describe species and ecosystems as valuable independently of their usefulness; and critics argue that offsetting quietly converts a self-organizing ecosystem into a bundle of services that could in principle be supplied anywhere. The practice appears to be running two rival pictures of natural value at once.

What theory of nature's value does the core offsetting practice presuppose? Reconstruct that theory as precisely as you can: does the practice strictly presuppose an instrumental view, on which a site's value is exhausted by the services it provides and can therefore be relocated, or is it equally compatible with a relational view—value in the bond between a particular community and a particular place—and an intrinsic view—value in the organisms and ecosystem themselves? I am especially interested in where the practice is genuinely underdetermined and where it is not. Finally, say what the practice would have to change if it took the relational or intrinsic view seriously: what would compensation amount to, and which losses could never be offset, no matter how favorable the functional ratio?
