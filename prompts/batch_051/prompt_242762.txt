After a career in evolutionary biology, I have only recently begun reading political philosophy, and I keep returning to Chesterton's fence. I would like an honest assessment of a principle I have been developing, which I call the selectionist reading of the fence. The usual lesson of the fence is that one should not abolish an old institution until one understands why it was put there. I want to defend a stronger claim: the bare persistence of an institution across many generations is itself positive evidence that it serves some function, even when no one can state the function, because persistent social arrangements are exposed to a loose selection process that tends to eliminate arrangements with severely damaging effects. On this reading, the fence principle is not deference to tradition for its own sake but an inference from survival to function. My worry is that I may have carried the adaptationist fallacy from biology into politics. Is this a defensible reconstruction of Chesterton's fence, or does the analogy to natural selection do more work than it can bear? Where exactly does the inference break down? For instance, institutions may persist because they benefit a powerful minority at everyone else's expense, or simply because they are costly to dismantle, and I am unsure whether the selectionist reading can distinguish those cases from genuine function. I would welcome a candid assessment, including whether this is already a recognized position.
