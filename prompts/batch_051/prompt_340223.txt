Lately I've been struck by a structural parallel between the way policymakers reason from history and the way a machine-learning model generalizes from training data. On this view, Munich and Vietnam function as labeled examples: from one or a few highly salient cases we induce a rule (appeasement invites aggression; involvement in other countries' civil wars produces quagmires), and each new crisis is classified by its similarity to one of these canonical cases. If the parallel is more than superficial, the familiar failure modes of learning from a small, biased sample—overfitting to outliers, ignoring base rates, extrapolating outside the support of the data, treating correlation as if it were the causal law a policy needs—should map onto the familiar failure modes of historical analogizing. I'd like to know whether this is a genuine identity of structure or a loose metaphor. If it is genuine, what would history have to be like for its lessons to transfer the way inductive generalizations do, and what is one prediction the parallel makes about when a Munich-style analogy will mislead that turns out false?
