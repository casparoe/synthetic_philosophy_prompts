A friend and I have just spent a long evening arguing about the Skokie free-speech controversy, and I want to subject our dispute to a more disciplined analysis than either of us managed in the moment. I was defending a Millian line: the only ground that could justify coercing the marchers was the prevention of harm to others, and offense, revulsion, and even severe psychological distress fell short of that. My friend argued that a neo-Nazi march through a village of Holocaust survivors was itself a form of harm, not merely offense. Take the Skokie case of 1977–78 in its essentials: a small neo-Nazi group led by Frank Collin announced a uniformed, swastika-bearing demonstration in Skokie, Illinois, a village with a large population of Holocaust survivors; the village responded with permit and insurance requirements together with bans on military-style political uniforms and on certain hate literature; the ACLU defended the group; the courts ultimately invalidated the ordinances; and the demonstration never occurred in Skokie, with the group rallying in Chicago instead. Analyze that episode through Mill's harm principle.

I want four things. First, state carefully what the harm principle, applied in earnest, says the different actors (the village, the courts, the ACLU, the marchers, the residents) were facing and should have done. Second, identify where the principle fits the case and where it strains: how it treats intense but indirect psychological distress; whether that distress can count as harm without collapsing the principle into a general warrant for censorship; how the usual proviso about speech that incites immediate unlawful action does or does not reach a peaceful, orderly march; and whether the principle is even capable of registering the residents' claim that the march threatened their standing and dignity rather than their safety. Third, run the analysis in the other direction and say what the case shows about the principle: does Skokie vindicate the harm principle as the right limit on coercion, does it expose a gap that forces a Millian to adopt an auxiliary notion of offense or group harm, or does it show that the principle was not doing the real work once the dispute entered American First Amendment law? Fourth, mark explicitly the points at which the harm principle is silent and must be supplemented, rather than making it speak to questions it never addressed.
