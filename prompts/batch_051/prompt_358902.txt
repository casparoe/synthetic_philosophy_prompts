So I hit this bug at work where my code calls a polynomial root solver and then behaves differently depending on what order the roots come back in. I was about to complain to the solver's author when it hit me that the solver wasn't doing anything wrong—there isn't a canoncial order to put the roots in, and now I can't stop thinking about when a choice of labels is harmless and when it isn't.

I'm trying to collect cases of the same disease: you have to pick something (a root, an orientation, a coordinate system) that the mathematics itself doesn't pick out, and the thing you build only looks well-defined after the choice. So far I have:

1. The two square roots of -1. You call one i and the other -i, but complex conjugation swaps them and fixes the reals, so nothing expressible from the reals can tell the two apart.
2. Roots of an irreducible polynomial. The Galois group moves the roots around among themselves, which is why algebra books tell you to take an arbitrary root rather than pretend a particular one is special.
3. Orientation. You have to choose a handedness before the cross product in R^3 is even defined, and the opposite choice gives a perfectly good mirror-image operation.
4. Matrices. A linear map is a real object, but its matrix only shows up after you choose a basis, and changing basis conjugates the matrix.

Give me one more that isn't just another finite set of symmetric options getting permuted. I want a case where the choice is more like a continuum, or where two choices give things that aren't cleanly the same object with the labels swapped. Then say what that case adds to the question of whether well-definedness and having a canonical choice are actually different things, and whether it breaks a pattern the first four share.
