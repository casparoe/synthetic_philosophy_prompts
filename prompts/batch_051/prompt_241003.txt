I edit a small literary magazine devoted to Black fiction. We are currently split over a story set in rural Georgia: an elderly couple’s marriage is slowly undone by a private grief. The writing is extraordinary, but the story makes no overt social or political claim. Half the board argues that, at this moment, to publish such a story is a retreat from the magazine’s purpose; the other half argues that demanding every story serve a cause is a form of philistinism. I have been the swing vote. In the middle of the disagreement I returned to W. E. B. Du Bois’s 1926 essay “Criteria of Negro Art,” and this passage seemed to decide against the story:

“Let me tell you that neither of these groups is right. The thing we are talking about tonight is part of the great fight we are carrying on and it represents a forward and an upward look -- a pushing onward.”

But I remained uneasy. Du Bois is discussing art in relation to a struggle, but he does not say every individual work must announce a political program. Would publishing this story be an instance of standing outside the fight he names, or is the board reducing his argument to something cruder? What would Du Bois’s criterion require of an editor deciding whether a quiet, apolitical-seeming story belongs in a magazine explicitly committed to racial justice?
