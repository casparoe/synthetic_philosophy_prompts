I've spent the past few weeks replacing the leader-election logic in a replicated storage service whose nodes are deliberately configured to be indistinguishable. I ran straight into the standard impossibility result in distributed computing: deterministic processes starting from a symmetric state cannot elect a unique leader, because any deterministic rule must treat indistinguishable nodes identically, and the usual remedies are either an injected source of randomness or a pre-existing asymmetry such as a unique ID. The moment I understood that, it read to me as Buridan's ass rewritten as a formal theorem: an agent facing options symmetric with respect to every available reason has no deterministic, reason-respecting rule by which to choose, so the only escapes are an arbitrary tie-breaker such as randomness or an ungrounded act of will, or an exogenous asymmetry such as one bale's being marginally closer or the agent's having a standing habit. Is this a genuine structural identity, or an evocative but loose analogy? If genuine, what transfers: does the impossibility result give theorists of the will a reason to say that some symmetry-breaking element beyond reasons is necessary, and what would the analogy predict that turns out false? If superficial, where exactly does the mapping between elections and choices come apart?
