I have spent the past few months designing a decision-support system whose job is to forecast the downstream effects of policy changes inside a large organization, and I have run into what feels like a philosophical wall rather than a technical one. The system is itself one of the actors in the organization; its forecasts circulate, get read, and alter the environment they describe. Whenever I try to make its model complete, it seems to require a model of itself, which requires a model of itself, and so on. Out of this frustration I built a general argument, and I now find it too strong to accept but too clean to dismiss.

Here is the argument.

(1) An agent chooses rationally only if it chooses on the basis of predicted consequences of its available actions.

(2) A prediction of consequences is only as good as the model that generates it.

(3) An embedded agent — an agent that is itself a physical subsystem of the very world in which it chooses — sits inside the causal chain connecting its actions to their outcomes; its own future computation, resource use, and errors are themselves factors in what happens.

(4) Therefore, to predict the consequences of its actions, an embedded agent needs a model that includes its own future processing and behavior, not merely the outside world.

(5) But a complete model that includes the agent's future processing would have to include the model the agent is using, which would have to include itself, and so on without end; no finite system can contain a complete representation of its own representational activity.

(6) Therefore, no embedded agent can possess a model adequate for predicting the consequences of its actions.

(7) If an embedded agent cannot predict the consequences of its actions, then it cannot choose rationally.

(8) Therefore, no embedded agent — and thus no human being and no actual computer — can choose rationally.

This conclusion strikes me as untenable; plainly people occasionally choose rationally. Yet I cannot find the step I am willing to surrender. Where does the argument first go wrong, and why did that move look so secure? I want the exact cost of rejecting it: what would a theory of rational choice for embedded agents have to give up at that point, and can the remaining steps survive under their most plausible reading? If, contrary to my suspicion, the argument is valid, then tell me what rational choice would have to mean in order for the conclusion to be livable.
