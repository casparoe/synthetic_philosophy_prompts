I have been mulling over an image that a friend of mine reaches for whenever we talk about whether the world has a deepest level. He says reality is like a tower. Each floor is held up by the floor below it, and each floor can do its holding only because something below holds it up in turn. If you imagined a tower with infinitely many floors going down and no bottom floor, he argues, nothing would actually be supported; the whole thing would be hanging in midair. So, he concludes, there must be a bottom floor: a most basic layer of reality that does not depend on anything further and on which everything else rests, with physics at the bottom and chemistry, biology, and the rest stacked above it. The picture feels clean, but I have started to worry that it is doing too much work. In a real tower, support is a matter of weight, gravity, and contact between solid parts, and the bottom floor is held up by the ground rather than by another floor. The way chemistry depends on physics, or the way a thought depends on a brain, does not look like bricks pressing on bricks, and an endless descent of explanations may not be as unstable as an endless stack of floors. I would like you to walk through the tower analogy carefully and tell me how far it really holds. What corresponds to what between a tower and the layered picture of reality? Where does the mapping begin to fail? Which features of towers does the analogy quietly import into the picture of reality without argument, and are those the very features doing the work of the conclusion? In the end, does my friend's argument that there must be a bottom level of reality survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or would we need some other image to make his conclusion stick? If there is a better analogy, I would be glad to hear it. Please write this up as continuous prose, the way you would in a letter or an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
