I'm a PhD student in philosophy of religion, and I'm writing about divine hiddenness. A colleague has floated the following model, and I want to see whether it actually behaves the way its defenders want it to.

The model: God fixes a single public evidential base — the ordinary deliverances of natural theology, the historical record, and the canonical texts of the relevant tradition — but makes its persuasive force diverge by person. Specifically, the evidence becomes individually luminous in proportion to the person's sustained practical openness: an inquirer who treats the question of God as a purely theoretical puzzle receives only the standing public evidence, while one who acts as though moral and spiritual demands might really be binding receives, over time, a progressively clearer sense of the evidence's force. Access does not track intelligence, institutional position, or skill with the original languages; it tracks something like a volitional stance. The model is offered as a way of explaining why there are capable, earnest people who fail to believe, while avoiding both total hiddenness and overwhelming public proof.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a theological assertion. What does it incentivize among individual inquirers, among religious communities that claim to mediate access to God, and among skeptics? How would it be gamed — through feigned openness, performative seeking, manufactured doubt in oneself or others, or status hierarchies of visible "seeking"? What happens to the sincere person who seeks and still receives nothing? Where does the mechanism most plausibly fail, and can any of those failure modes be fixed without collapsing the policy into either total hiddenness or total publicness?

Please explicitly separate what follows just from the structure of the model from what rests on genuinely contested assumptions about evidence, divine psychology, or what actual traditions claim, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Keep the response to no more than 500 words.
