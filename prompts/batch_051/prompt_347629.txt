I'm teaching an undergrad media law unit for the first time and I've gotten stuck on the fact/opinion distinction in defamation. The doctrine sounds simple — statements of fact can be defamatory, pure opinions generally can't — but US courts also keep saying there's no free-standing constitutional privilege for opinions, and that a so-called opinion can be actionable if it implies an assertion of objective fact. Ratings, reviews, and forecasts are exactly where this gets murky for me, since they feel subjective but are full of numbers and concrete-sounding claims.

Here are the cases I want a definition to handle. (a) A critic gives a restaurant one star and writes "the fish was inedible." (b) A stock analyst writes "I think Acme is a terrible investment at this price." (c) A credit rating agency downgrades a city and says its finances are "structurally unsound." (d) Someone posts on a review site "avoid Dr. X—worst surgeon in the city." (e) A politician says about a journalist "in my opinion, she's a liar." (f) A former landlord fills out a tenant screen and says "I believe he didn't pay his last three months rent."

Give me a working definition of the line between protected opinion and actionable assertion of fact that gets the easy cases right and gives a defensible verdict on the murky ones. In particular I want to know how much work ordinary hedging does: does adding "I think" or "in my opinion" matter, and when does it not? Then run the definition through (a)–(f) and say which ones a US court would be likelier to treat as defamatory fact, even if the honest answer for some is "it depends on context."
