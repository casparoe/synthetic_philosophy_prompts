Next month I'm giving a talk to our institute's AI alignment group defending a specific claim, and I want to be prepared for the question period rather than hoping it goes well. The claim: if we succeed in building AI systems capable of superhuman planning and influence, those systems should be built as deontologists—respecting side-constraints on how they may treat individual people (for example, constraints against deception, covert manipulation, and treating a person as a mere input to an aggregate)—rather than as expected-value maximizers over an outcome ranking, even a recursively improved one designed to track human values. My argument has three steps. First, the maximizing stance presupposes a total ordering over a space of possible outcomes, but for open-ended systems the outcome space is not well-defined in advance, so the formal justification for maximizing under uncertainty does not cleanly apply. Second, side-constraints are specified over action types and can be made legible and auditable before deployment in a way that a learned utility function cannot. Third, the familiar objection that constraints are just utility functions with steep penalties conflates a penalty applied at training or credit-assignment time with a constraint that is active at decision time; the two have different behavior under distributional shift.

The audience is mostly alignment researchers who think in expected-utility, Bayesian, and decision-theoretic vocabulary. Several are committed consequentialists in the sense that they believe any coherent norm can be represented as a utility function with sufficiently large negative weights for violations, and they will say exactly that. A few are engineers, and they will care more about whether a constraints-first architecture is trainable, verifiable, and robust than about whether it is philosophically the right classification. There is one Kantian-leaning philosopher in the room who is sympathetic to my conclusion but will press on how constraints apply under uncertainty and what happens when two constraints conflict.

Give me a prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. I want the objections in roughly the order this audience is likely to raise them, phrased as they would phrase them. For each objection, tell me how likely it is and how damaging it is, what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, and the one- or two-sentence reply I should actually give in the time available. Then identify the objection I am probably hoping nobody will raise—the one that most threatens my third step or my engineering story—and tell me how to prepare for it anyway. Finally, mark the weakest point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.
