I'm a linguistics PhD student and I keep getting pulled into the same argument at department happy hours and family dinners: is there any sense in which a sentence can be grammatically wrong full stop, independent of how native speakers actually talk? The descriptivist side says no—grammar just is the system speakers carry around in there heads, so something like "I ain't got none" is only bad in a social or prestige sense, not linguistically. The prescriptivist side says yes—there are real standards (agreement, case, not ending sentences with prepositions, whatever) and violating them is an error even if a hundred million people do it every day.

I'm not asking who's right. I'm asking why this argument never moves. My working hypothesis is that a lot of it is verbal: the two sides mean different things by grammar (an internal cognitive system vs a normative code), and if you disambiguate that, some of the heat goes away but not all of it. I think there's a leftover non-verbal dispute about whether prestige dialects deserve their prestige or merely have it, and about whether error is the right word for using a low-prestige form at all. Is that the right diagnosis of the structure? Or is there a hidden empirical question in there—like what actually counts as linguistic competence, or how stable native speaker judgments really are—or a disagreement about standards of evidence that keeps it stuck? And given whatever the diagnosis is, what would count as progress: a distinction, a rephrased question, a real empirical result, or is the impasse basically permanent because the two sides start from different intuitions about norms with no shared court of appeal?
