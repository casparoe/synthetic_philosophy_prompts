I've been collecting my verdicts on a family of small everyday cases—cases where someone takes the benefit of something others maintain without contributing to it, which I'd describe as free riding and, less charitably, as social parasitism. My underlying question is what, if anything, is wrong with taking without contributing, and where the line is between benign free riding and wrongful parasitism. I can't find one principle that draws the lines where my gut does, and I'd like you to help me find it, or else tell me which of my verdicts I should surrender.

1. A coworker drinks from a small office coffee fund every day but never puts money in, even after the costs are pointed out to him. I think this is simply wrong.

2. A neighborhood association takes up a collection each summer for a fireworks show launched from the common green. In years when I haven't given, I still watch from my porch, where the show is impossible to miss. I can't convince myself that I'm doing anything wrong, though I feel a bit sheepish when the collector knocks. And yet this looks, structurally, just like the coffee fund: benefit without contribution. I can't say what the difference is.

3. A family in a small community orchard takes more than its share of the apples every fall but never shows up for the spring pruning or the summer watering. I think that's wrong, and worse than the coffee case, because the good is finite and they're taking more of it.

4. A company builds its product on a freely licensed open-source library, uses it commercially, makes a great deal of money, and never contributes code or money back to the maintainer. The license explicitly permits this. I can't call it wrong, but it troubles me in a way the fireworks case does not.

5. A student in a graded group project does none of the work but receives the group's grade. I think that's wrong.

6. A healthy family declines all recommended vaccines for its children and sends them to a school where high vaccination rates keep the diseases out. I think that's wrong, but I suspect it's wrong for a different kind of reason: they seem to impose a risk on others, rather than merely taking without contributing. I'd like to know whether this case belongs with the rest.

The principle I keep reaching for is something like this: you wrong the contributors when you knowingly take a benefit that exists only because enough people contribute, you decline to do your fair share even though you could contribute at moderate cost, and you would be worse off—or at least put out—if enough other people behaved as you do. But that principle condemns my firework-watching, which I can't accept; it also starts to condemn the open-source company in a way I only half-accept; and it gives me no way of saying why the vaccine case might be different in kind. So I don't trust it.

Is there one principle—or a small package of principles—that gets these verdicts, or nearly all of them? Please state it precisely and say which case motivates each element of it. Then apply it to a case I can't settle: a neighbor who never shovels the shared walk in winter but uses the cleared path others maintain; and a household in a town with a donor-funded volunteer fire brigade that never donates but knows the brigade would come if its house were burning. Finally, say which of my six verdicts, if any, the principle has to overturn, and whether there's a simpler principle that does better than a complicated one.
