I have been working out a view I call the Trophic Replacement Principle. Its central claim is that the moral assessment of a food choice should not track the harms a person can point to directly—the cow slaughtered, the hen's confined life, the field mouse killed at harvest—but the net change in the standing population of sentient life, weighted by expected welfare, across the whole food web that the choice tends to enlarge or suppress. The unit of comparison should be something like welfare-weighted existence-years: for a fixed amount of land and calories, compare the actual sentient populations to the counterfactual populations under the nearest alternative use of that land, and ask which world contains more expected net suffering. On this principle, the familiar ranking of plants as best, chicken in the middle, and beef as worst becomes unstable and may even invert. A grass-fed steer is plausibly one large sentient life per very many calories, while the equivalent in chicken involves many more shorter, intensively farmed lives, and row crops can kill large numbers of small field animals; at the same time, displacing a wild ecosystem can eliminate many short-lived wild animals whose lives may contain little welfare and much suffering. I am aware this sounds like it could rationalize nearly anything, so I want an unsparing assessment. Is this a coherent principle, or just a vivid way of double-counting and comparing incomparable things? Is there already a recognized version of this idea in animal ethics or in discussions of wild animal suffering, and if so how does mine differ? What are the three strongest objections to it, and is any of them fatal rather than merely uncomfortable? If the whole framing is confused—for example, if the currency of standing welfare is the wrong way to think about moral accounting, or if the counterfactual alternative cannot be specified well enough to do the work—tell me that plainly instead of politely trying to salvage the principle. Please answer in American English.
