A colleague and I have been arguing about whether a speaker who asserts something false on the strength of excellent evidence has thereby done something improper, and the exchange exposed how unstable my wider judgments about assertion are. I am trying to work out, from my own reactions to concrete cases rather than from theory labels, what rule governs whether an assertion is proper. By an assertion I mean a statement put forward as true on the speaker's own say-so—not a hedge, not a report of what someone else said, and not an utterance whose wrongness is fully explained by dishonesty; please set lying aside. I would like you to take my verdicts as data, extract the principle that best captures them, tell me whether some of them must be revised, and apply the resulting account to a case I have not settled.

Here are the cases. First: a colleague tells a visitor that the afternoon meeting will be in room 204 because that is what the normally reliable room schedule says; the schedule is wrong. I do not regard the assertion as improper—false, but exactly what a conscientious speaker should do. Second: someone with no evidence at all, on the strength of a hunch, tells me a particular horse will win a race; the horse wins. I find the assertion improper despite its truth—he had no business saying it. Third: a driver in an area dense with highly realistic barn façades points at one of the few genuine barns and asserts that it is a barn. Here too I want to call the assertion improper, even though the belief is true and, from the driver's seat, perfectly reasonable; but I cannot say exactly why without invoking the very environmental luck the driver could not have known about. Fourth: whenever someone asserts a sentence of the form p, but I do not believe that p—or p, but I do not know that p—the utterance strikes me as defective even when both conjuncts happen to be true. That suggests to me that assertion is tied to belief and knowledge, not merely to truth.

So my reactions seem to imply that falsity alone does not make an assertion improper, truth alone does not make it proper, and justified true belief is still not always enough, while disavowing belief or knowledge in the same breath is never all right. That points toward an epistemic norm but not cleanly toward any of the familiar options—a knowledge norm, a truth norm, or a belief norm. Please set out which principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures and explains these four verdicts; say which of them, if any, I should overturn; and tell me what the principle predicts about a further case. The further case: a person asserts p in a high-stakes matter, p is true, and she has exactly the evidence that would ordinarily suffice for her to know p in a lower-stakes context. Is her assertion proper, improper but excusable, or neither? I also suspect I have been running together two distinct questions—whether an assertion violates its governing rule, and whether the speaker deserves criticism for violating it—and I want the principle you extract to force these apart rather than leave me sliding between them.
