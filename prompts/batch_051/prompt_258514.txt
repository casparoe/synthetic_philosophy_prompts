I spent my career in physical chemistry and only started reading philosophy after I retired, and its been Harry Frankfurt's little book on bullshit that I keep coming back to. Rather than start from a definition, I've been writing down my gut verdicts on concrete cases, and Im hoping you can tell me what principle, if any, lies behind them.

1. A finance minister goes on television and says the deficit is under control, when he has just seen the internal numbers showing it isn't. Verdict: lying.

2. The same minister, half an hour later, is asked why bond yields have risen. He launches into a smooth explanation involving global liquidity and term premia; he has no idea whether any of it is true, and he hasn't checked. He just wants to sound on top of it. Verdict: bullshit, not lying.

3. A postdoc in my old lab once reported an enzyme concentration from a spectrophotometer that had drifted out of calibration overnight. He genuinely believed the number. Verdict: honest error, neither lying nor bullshit.

4. A supplement salesman at a health expo tells customers the pill keeps their T-cells up, waving a paper he hasn't read. He doesn't know whether the paper supports the claim and he doesn't care, as long as the bottles move. Verdict: bullshit.

5. A friend at dinner holds forth about how the Medici bank's collapse caused the Renaissance, inventing causal links on the spot. He isn't trying to deceive anyone, and if pressed he'd cheerfully admit he was making it up. Verdict: harmless conversational bullshit, not lying.

6. A defense attorney believes her client is guilty, but argues to the jury that the prosecution hasn't met its burden. She never asserts anything false and uses only weaknesses that are actually in the record, though she's clearly trying to produce a certain impression. Verdict: not bullshit, even though she's managing impressions, because the truth constrains every move she makes.

7. A large language model, asked to summarize a court case, confidently says the court held X when the opinion says Y. This is the one I go back and forth on. Part of me wants to call it error, since the thing has no aims at all. But Frankfurt's bullshitter is marked by lacking concern for the truth, and the model certainly has no concern for the truth. So maybe it's a purer instance of bullshit than any of the humans above, even though it also has no concern for anything else either.

So: is there one principle about a statement's relation to the truth that gets 1 through 6 right and then settles 7? If not, which of my verdicts would you overturn? And please tell me what your principle says about a case I didn't list: a doctor tells a patient a drug's side effects are mild, which is true for the median patient but not for a small minority, and she knows the word will keep the patient from asking follow-up questions.
