I am drafting a short essay for a seminar on the philosophy of computation, and I would welcome detailed critical comments on the argument sketched below. My thesis is that mutation is hard to think about because it forces one to keep two distinct notions of identity in play at once: the identity of a value, which is governed by equality and substitution, and the identity of a location or object, which persists through changes in what it contains. In a purely functional setting these two largely collapse; a variable is a name for a value, and replacing equals by equals is safe because there is no further fact about which instance of the value one is talking about. Assignment makes the split vivid. In a statement such as x = x + 1, the occurrence of x on the right contributes the value currently stored in a cell, while the occurrence on the left names the cell itself, which survives the operation unchanged in identity even though its contents are replaced. The same visible sign thus alternates between naming the container and naming the contained, and the reasoner must track the difference between an object remaining the same and merely having the same value. I go on to suggest that imperative programs are thereby committed to something like bare particulars: cells and object references whose identity cannot be read off their observable content but is constituted by their role in a space of states, so that two numerically distinct cells can hold equal values, and two syntactically different names can refer to a single cell whose current value gives no hint of their convergence. Aliasing illustrates the latter point: the value alone never reveals that one is dealing with the same cell.

I would appreciate comments on the argument's accuracy and interest, as well as on its weakest steps. Specifically: Am I conflating variables with memory cells or object references in a way that undermines the diagnosis? Is the standard functional response—that state can be modeled explicitly through things like monads, linear types, or separation logic, and that this simply makes the hidden parameters explicit—an objection to the philosophical claim, or an indirect confirmation of it? Does the bare-particular analogy earn its keep, or is it an ornamental comparison? And if you had to defend the strongest version of the thesis, where would you place the emphasis and what would you concede?
