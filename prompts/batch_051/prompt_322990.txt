Suppose that, for the next twenty years, a rich Western country is cut off from the material basis of large-scale veganism: imported legumes, soy-based meat and dairy replacements, and fortified plant foods become scarce and expensive, and B12 supplements are rationed, while locally produced dairy, eggs, and some fish remain cheap and reliable. Everything else about the country stays the same. Predict, with a visible chain of reasoning, how public debate about the ethics of eating animals changes over those decades, how advocacy and actual eating habits shift, and where food policy and agricultural subsidies end up. Give mechanisms rather than just outcomes: which arguments now become prominent because the claim that animal products are permissible when alternatives are unavailable is no longer hypothetical, which prior moral commitments soften or harden, and where the new fault lines form among people who previously agreed. At each stage, flag which predictions you are most and least confident in, and name the background assumptions about moral psychology and economics on which that confidence depends—for example, whether people's normative judgments track what is materially feasible for them, or whether animal-welfare commitments survive independently of scarcity.
