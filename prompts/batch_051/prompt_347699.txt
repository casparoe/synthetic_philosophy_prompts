Im working on a novel where a character gets arrested under a city ordinance making it a crime to "loiter in a manner that annoys passersby," and a lawyer friend tells me the charge probably dies on void-for-vagueness grounds. The idea: a criminal law has to give fair notice of what it prohibits and can't invite arbitrary enforcement. But every word is a little vauge at the edges, and cops and juries have to interpret something. What picture of language and meaning does this doctrine actually presuppose—that words have a stable core the state must light up in advance, or something looser? Do the two rationales (notice to the citizen, constraint on the official) presuppose the same theory, or do they come apart, since fair notice seems to care how the text hits an ordinary reader while anti-arbitrariness cares about an officer's discretion? And how is the practice supposed to be coherent when courts strike down vagrancy-type laws but happily live with terms like "reasonable person"? If we instead thought meaning only gets made in the encounter between a text and a situation, so that fair notice is partly a fiction, what would the doctrine have to become? I'm trying to get at what the law implicitly assumes about meaning and interpretation—where it's principled, where it's underdetermined, and where it's just a line in the sand.
