For years I've been the science-and-ideas reporter at my paper, and lately the definition of death keeps crossing my beat. The legal test in most U.S. states, roughly, is that death is either the irreversible stopping of the heart and breathing, or the irreversible stopping of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem. Two doors to the same verdict. A philosopher I interviewed kept returning to that second door, and to the word all.

Consider a case I've been following. A 19-year-old is in the ICU after a near-drowning. He fails the neurological exam: no responsiveness, no gag or cough, no pupilary responses, and no effort to breathe when the ventilator is removed long enough for carbon dioxide to climb. After the required waiting period and a second exam, the attending says he's dead by neurological criteria. But the nurses keep pointing out that his brain is not uniformly dead. His pituitary is still secreting vasopressin, so his kidneys hold on to water and he never gets the massive urination you would expect if that system had died. A research EEG shows occasional small bursts of activity. And when someone rubs the sole of his foot, his leg jerks, though that gets dismissed as spinal cord.

So, working the criteria honestly: does he qualify as dead? Under the circulatory criterion, evidently not, since his heart is beating away on the ventilator. Under the whole-brain criterion, the exam says yes but the literal words might say no, because not all functions of the entire brain have stopped. Go through the pieces and tell me whether the remaining functions matter or are just noise. Is the law using the whole brain as a stand-in for something else, like the permanent loss of consciousness and personhood, while pretending it has drawn a biological line? Or is the exam right that he is dead, and the lingering chemistry and reflexes irrelevant? And should the definition be changed so the words say what hospitals actually do?
