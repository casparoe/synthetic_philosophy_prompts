I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about who has a say in what happens to a neighborhood, and I've noticed that my gut gives different answers to situations that, on paper, look a lot alike. I'd like to see whether there's a single principle behind my reactions, or whether some of my calls need to be given up.

Consider these cases and what I find myself thinking about each one.

1. An owner wants to demolish a rent-controlled apartment building and put up condos, evicting ten families who have lived there for between twelve and twenty years. My call: he shouldn't be allowed to simply do that. The tenants should be able to block the demolition or force a settlement that genuinely covers the cost of being uprooted.

2. A developer proposes a four-story building in a wealthy neighborhood of single-family homes, with a fifth of the units set aside as below-market-rate housing. The local homeowners' association organizes against it, saying it will destroy the quiet character and strain the parking. My call: their campaign feels wrong. The language is about character and parking, but it reads as keeping poorer people out.

3. A developer wants to put up luxury condos at the edge of a historically Black neighborhood. A neighborhood association opposes the project, arguing that it will raise property taxes and push out long-time residents. My call: I'm with the neighbors, even though this is structurally another case of existing residents trying to stop new housing.

4. A woman wants to convert her detached garage into a small apartment to rent out. Her neighbors object about parking and about renters. My call: she should win. The neighbors' irritation doesn't seem like enough to stop her.

5. A man inherits his late aunt's house in a neighborhood that has been getting more expensive. He has never lived there. A developer offers him well above market value, and he accepts; the house will be torn down and replaced with townhouses. Neighbors say he is helping erase the neighborhood. My call: I can't bring myself to say he did anything wrong. It's his house, and no one was living in it.

6. A couple has been renting an apartment for two years. Their landlord sells the building, and the new owner wants the unit for a relative, so the couple is evicted with the legal minimum notice. My call: it's sad and I suspect the rules are too harsh, but I don't feel the same outrage as in the first case. I can't explain why two years feels so different from fifteen.

7. The city council votes to allow duplexes and triplexes in every neighborhood that had been zoned for single-family homes only. Many long-time homeowners say the city is breaking an implicit promise they relied on when they bought. My call: the city is doing the right thing. I don't think buying a house earned them a permanent veto over the block.

8. Commuters who drive into a neighborhood for work demand that a proposed apartment building include a large parking garage, because otherwise the new residents will take the free street parking the commuters currently use. My call: the commuters' preference barely counts here. They don't live in the neighborhood, and a housing shortage shouldn't be made worse to protect their free parking.

I've tried to fit these under a rough principle: the people with the deepest stake in a place, measured mainly by how long they've lived there and how much their lives depend on it, should have the strongest say in what happens to it. That seems to handle cases 1 and 3, and it explains why the commuters in case 8 count for little. But it runs into trouble with case 2: the suburban homeowners have lived in their neighborhood for decades and plainly depend on it, yet I still think their blockade is wrong. It also struggles with case 5, where the heir has no history in the neighborhood but I still defend his right to sell.

What I'd like is for you to take these verdicts and either find a principle that captures all of them, or tell me which verdicts I should revise and why. If the principle needs exceptions or clauses, state them precisely and say which case motivates each one. Then tell me what the principle says about a few cases I keep turning over: (a) a renter of three years versus a renter of twenty years — where, if anywhere, does the difference begin to matter? (b) a long-time resident who opposes a new homeless shelter in the neighborhood, on the grounds that it will bring "undesirable people"; (c) someone who grew up in a neighborhood but moved away a decade ago and now wants a voice in a development fight; and (d) a city that demolishes a low-income apartment complex, relocating its residents, in order to build a larger mixed-income building on the same site. I'd also like an honest statement of which of my verdicts, if any, your principle has to reject.
