I work as a reinforcement-learning engineer. I am fluent in the formal machinery of RL — states, observations, actions, policies, reward signals, value functions, reward prediction errors, model-based versus model-free learning, options, and intrinsic motivation — but I have only an amateur's knowledge of the philosophy of emotion. Reading about emotion at a lay level, I keep noticing that the main philosophical positions sound like claims about different components of an RL agent: one sounds like a claim about the value function, another like a claim about the reward signal, a third like a claim about the world model. I want to know whether that resemblance can be made rigorous, and where it comes apart.

Take the three dominant families of theories of emotion — judgment theories, on which an emotion is, or essentially involves, an evaluative judgment such as that one has been wronged or that a situation is dangerous; feeling theories, on which an emotion is the felt experience of a patterned change in the body; and perceptual theories, on which an emotion is a perception of an evaluative property in roughly the way visual experience is a perception of shape or color — and restate each family as precisely as possible in the vocabulary of RL. I am not after loose analogies of the form "anger = negative reward." I want, for each family, a specification of what commitments a proponent would be taking on if the translation were accepted: which construct the emotion gets identified with, what computational role it plays, and what conditions on the environment or the learning process the theory implicitly imposes. At each step, flag any presupposition that comes along with the RL construct but would not be accepted by the theorist.

Then provide the residue report. For each family, say which commitments translate cleanly, which can be represented only by stretching an RL construct (and name the distortion), and which commitments have no counterpart in RL at all. Pay particular attention to three things: (i) the phenomenal character of an emotion — what it is like to feel it; (ii) the normativity of emotions — the fact that an emotion can be fitting or unfitting, not merely useful or costly; and (iii) emotions such as shame, envy, or gratitude whose objects and triggers are constitutively social. Conclude with your own assessment: where the translation breaks down, is the failure a limitation of the RL vocabulary, or does it expose an ambiguity inside the philosophical theory? And does the exercise end up favoring one of the three families — and if so, is that favoritism earned, or is it an artifact of translating everything into RL terms in the first place?
