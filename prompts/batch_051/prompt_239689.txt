I host a small political economy podcast, and next month's guest is an economist who defends a modernized version of Oskar Lange's market socialism: the idea that a central planning board can set prices by trial and error, watching shortages and surpluses, and tell enterprise managers to follow parametric rules (produce where price equals marginal cost, expand when price exceeds marginal cost, and so on), and that this gets you market-like efficiency without capitalism's pathologies. He knows the Hayekian objections cold, and his standard move is to say that that critique only applies to the 1930s paper version of the model, not to computationally sophisticated planning. I need eight to ten questions that push past the restatement of Lange. I want at least one question each on: (a) Hayek's point that the relevant knowledge is dispersed, often tacit, and gets generated only through actual exchange, so it isn't data simply handed to the board; (b) innovation and discovery — the trial-and-error story seems to presuppose a fixed list of commodities and techniques, so how does the board discover new goods or production methods, and is that just an entrepreneurial function the model silently imports from capitalism; (c) incentives and the soft budget constraint — why would managers faithfully follow the board's rules rather than game the plan when their survival isn't tied to profit and loss; and (d) the Amazon-style logistics challenge — does Amazon vindicate Lange, since a giant center sets quantities across a supply chain, or vindicate Hayek, since Amazon still forms prices from consumer behavior and competes with rival platforms. For each question, include the follow-up you'd ask after his most likely reply, and design at least two of the questions so they all force him to reuse the same move — I can just add that feature to the model — untill that answer starts costing him. You can reference the Soviet experience in a couple places, but don't let the episode become a USSR history lesson; keep the analytical issues in front. And skip the generic podcast questions like what drew you to economics. The audience knows the basic Mises–Hayek vs. Lange debate, so it's fine to go into the weeds.
