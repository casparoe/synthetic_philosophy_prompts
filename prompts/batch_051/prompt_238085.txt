Act as a philosopher who works on the ethics of participation — complicity, enabling, ratification, collective wrongdoing — and also does compliance training for hospitals, so you know what a usable screen looks like. Think through each step before you write it.

I'm a contract manager at a regional hospital system. I sign off on ten to fifteen supplier contracts a week: medical devices, linens and gowns, software, facilities maintenance. Our general counsel just told me she wants a moral-complicity screen added to my review, not just the legal checklist. Twice in the last three years we got burned: a data broker we used was exposed for harvesting patient data from a hospital without consent, and a uniform supplier was caught using forced labor at a subcontractor. In both cases someone argued we didn't cause it — our contract didn't make them do it, and they'd have done it anyway. The GC's reply was that complicity isn't causation: a person or an institution can take part in wrongdoing without making it happen, by enabling it, covering for it, giving it legitimacy or a market, or profiting from it.

I think there's something right in that, but I have no idea how to use it on a Tuesday. I need you to turn that one-line principle into a procedure I can actually run.

Here are my constraints. I have about fifteen minutes per contract. I can't investigate beyond what's in the file, the vendor's public disclosures, and whatever our procurement team already flagged. The suppliers will deny wrongdoing when asked. There is no option to just use my judgment — the risk office wants a defined decision path with four outputs: APPROVE, APPROVE WITH CONDITIONS, FLAG FOR REVIEW, or REFUSE. There's an ethics committee for the genuinely hard value calls, but I can't sent everything to them.

So do this. First, give me a short plain-language statement of the principle you're operationalizing, so I can check it's what the GC means and not something looser. Then write the decision procedure: questions in the order I should ask them, what kind of answer counts as a sufficient yes or no for each, where the path stops, and which output it stops with. Mark clearly the steps where judgment can't be eliminated — where I'd be making a values call that just looks like a checkbox. Be honest about what the procedure cannot settle; I don't want a machine that tells me buying from a bad actor makes me a co-owner of everything bad they do, but I also don't want a screen that let's each of us off the hook because no single purchase tips the scale.

Then run it on these cases, actually showing the reasoning. At least one of these should come out in a direction I'm not going to like, and I want that flagged.

1. Surgical gowns. Our long-time vendor was named in a labor-rights report last year: one of their overseas subcontractors used forced labor. The vendor says a remediation program is under way, but it hasn't been independently verified. We're well under five percent of this vendor's global revenue; if we drop them, the subcontractor problem almost certainly continues. But renewing with them feels like putting our name behind their promises.

2. Readmission-risk software. A small analytics vendor has a model that predicts which discharged patients will be readmitted. It's good, and cheap. But the model was trained partly on patient posts scraped from online health forums without consent. The scraping is done; our buying the model won't cause one more post to be scraped. What it will do is make us the first major health system to adopt it, and the vendor will use our name in every sales pitch after that. I want this one to be clean, because the budget works. I suspect it isn't.

3. Elevator maintenance. Our building's elevator contractor is fine — good service, no complaints. Its parent company also operates a private-prison business I find morally indefensible. The contractor itself does nothing wrong, and our money can't be traced into the parent's prison operations. Is signing with them complicity, a boycott question, or neither? Where in the procedure does that distinction show up?
