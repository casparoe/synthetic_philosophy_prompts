A friend of mine works in an IVF clinic and spends much of her day vitrifying human embryos for later use, yet she regards cryonics as something between a superstition and a con. She is not unusual. Sperm, eggs, embryos, blood plasma, and some transplant tissues are banked at deep-freeze temperatures as a matter of routine medicine, and the ability to thaw and use them later is treated as an unambiguous good. But an adult who arranges to have their brain or whole body vitrified immediately after legal death, in the hope that a future medical technology will repair the damage and restore them, is widely seen as irrational, ghoulish, or the victim of a scam. I have been trying to state the principle that separates these two practices and keep failing.

The obvious candidate is that freezing reproductive material demonstrably works, whereas no one has ever been revived from cryonic preservation and the project rests on speculative future technology. But that seems to locate the difference in the current state of the evidence rather than in the practice itself: before the first live birth from a frozen human embryo, that procedure was also an unproven bet, and the rational question about cryonics appears to be a question about the estimated probability that future technology can recover the preserved system, not about whether the practice works today. A second candidate is that an embryo has never been conscious while a cryonics patient is a person who has died, so preserving a possible future life has a different moral shape than refusing to accept the death of an actual person. But that principle is not applied consistently: retrieving and freezing a dead man's sperm for his partner to use later is increasingly accepted, and organ donation also intervenes in a body immediately after death. A third explanation is simple reputation: reproductive cryopreservation became respectable incrementally inside established medical institutions, while cryonics entered as a fringe movement and never shed that association.

I want to know whether anything actually justifies the asymmetry. What is the strongest version of the principle that distinguishes cryonics from the routine freezing of human reproductive material and tissue, and does it survive the cases I have described? If the principle is evidence and probability, at what estimated likelihood of revival would the contempt become unreasonable? If it is about persons and death, why does it not condemn posthumous sperm retrieval? And if no principle survives, should we conclude that the asymmetry is status-quo bias, and adjust our judgment of one or both practices accordingly?
