I keep coming back to a thought experiment built on the unilateralist's curse—the idea that when a bunch of people each have the power to unilaterally take some irreversible action that affects everyone, and they honestly disagree about whether it's a good idea, the group predictably ends up doing whatever the single most optimistic member wants, even if the typical member thinks it's a terrible idea. Normally the fix is a consensus rule or supermajority, because otherwise one optimist can trigger the thing on their own.

Here's the counterfactual I want you to reason through. Suppose that in 2028, after two brutal heatwaves, the world's governments set up a binding international agency for solar geoengineering with 150 member states. As a compromise to avoid gridlock, the founding treaty says that any single member state may, acting alone, authorize a real deployment: stratospheric sulfate injection designed to cool the planet by roughly one degree Celsius. Assume the deployment is technically proven, costs about a billion dollars (nothing for a mid-size country), is reversible only on a timescale of a few years after you stop, and has lopsided regional effects—some countries lose monsoon rainfall, some get easier winters, some barely notice anything. Each member state relies on its own national experts, acting in good faith, to estimate the costs and benefits to itself and the world. Nothing else about international law or politics changes.

Predict, in argument form rather than story form, what actually happens over the next thirty years. Which countries press the button first, and why? Does the median country's view matter at all, or does the whole distribution get overiden by its most optimistic tail? How does the mere existence of the rule change countries' incentives before anyone acts? Does the rule survive the first deployment, or does it get replaced by consensus requirements—and what are the side-effects either way? For each step of the prediction, tell me how confident you are, which assumption is doing the most work, and what would have to be different for the outcome to go the other way.
