I'm a clinical psychologist in a community mental health team, and I keep having to make the same sorting decision a few times a week. Some of the doubts clients bring are ordinary uncertainties — is this lump serious, will my partner leave, did I choose the wrong school for the kids — and with those it makes sense to help the person weigh the evidence. Others are a different animal: a client gets stuck wondering whether everyone around them is a machine, whether their entire remembered past was inserted this morning, whether the floor might not hold. Standard training tells me to treat those as intrusive thoughts and withhold reassurance, but the middle range defeats that rule constatly. A client who checks the door thirty times is doing something partly rational (doors do get left open) and partly compulsive; a client who is ninety percent convinced their headache is a tumour needs reassurance and needs something else. It feels like I'm lacking a criterion, not just confidence. The stakes are medium-to-high in a quiet way: misclassifying a doubt can keep someone in a thought loop for weeks, or, at the physical end, make me miss something that needs a medical referral.

I've been working through Wittgenstein's On Certainty and the hinge epistemology that grew out of it, and it seems to describe the distinction I'm groping for. Some commitments aren't justified by evidence but aren't ordinary beliefs either; they're the fixed scaffolding that makes ordinary enquiry possible, so doubting them isn't like making a bold conjecture — it's more like pulling up the frame the picture sits in. There's also the idea in there that these certainties show up in what people do without hesitation rather than in what they say they believe, which seems clinically useful. But the literature is aimed at scepticism, not at a fifty-minute session with someone who is frightened.

I want this turned into a reusable procedure I can apply in about ten minutes after a session, before I write my notes: a sort of decision tree or checklist that sorts a client's expressed doubt into (a) ordinary uncertainty to engage with on its merits, (b) anxiety-maintaining safety behaviour to respond to with exposure or response prevention, and (c) hinge-type doubt where argument and reassurance are the wrong tools. It has to be usable by another clinician on the team who hasn't read any of this, it has to be honest about the points where I'm making a judgement call and just dressing it up as a procedure, and it should handle the hard cases — a hinge-type doubt aimed at something genuinely uncertain, a doubt that shifts category over time, a client whose background makes the 'hinge' much less stable than mine. Then apply it to three sketched cases, one of which gives me an answer I won't like (say, it tells me not to reassure someone I badly want to reassure). And say plainly what the procedure cannot do — for instance, what it can't tell me about whether the hinge is true, or about my duties around risk, or about what my supervisor will make of the note.
