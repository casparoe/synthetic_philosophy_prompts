Two of my coworkers have been going back and forth about a side project one of them wants legal to approve, and I've become the de facto audience for the whole argument. The project is a proprietary desktop app that summarizes build logs. It doesn't link against any GPL code, but at runtime it shells out to a GPL-licensed command-line analyzer, feeds the analyzer a reformatted version of the user's log, parses the analyzer's text output, and folds the results into it's own report. The two of them disagree about whether the app counts as a derivative work of the analyzer. It went something like this:

Dana, the one who wrote it, said the app is clearly fine: it doesn't copy or link any GPL code, it just talks to a program through stdin and stdout the way a human user would. Using a GPL program doesn't make your own program a derivative, or else every shell script that calls grep, and every novel typed in a GPL text editor, would be GPL too.

Ravi said that analogy is weak. The app isn't a person typing at a keyboard; it's purpose-built to drive this specific analyzer, it transforms the log into exactly the shape the analyzer wants, and it parses the analyzer's output into the app's own data structures. If Dana had linked the analyzer as a library instead of shelling out, it would obviously be a derivative work. So the only thing standing between the app and "derivative" is the choice of a process boundary, and drawing the legal line at address spaces is absurd.

Dana replied that the process boundary isn't arbitrary, it's the difference between combining two programs into one and using a program. If "purpose-built to drive a specific GPL tool" were the test, every Makefile, wrapper script, and CI plugin ever written would be infected. The line has to be about whether you copied or adapted the protected expression, not about how much your program causally depends on the tool.

Ravi came back that derivative works have never been limited to literal copying — translations and adaptations count too. A frontend that speaks the analyzer's input/output language and maps its output into the app's internal objects is more like a translation of a novel than like reading one: no sentence is copied, but it's still derivative. And the GPL's idea of mere aggregation only covers works that are genuinely separate, not two programs that are functionally one product and ship together.

Dana said then where do you stop? A bash script that greps the analyzer's output for one field? A GUI that displays the same fields a human could read? Ravi's line seems to come down to "how much the author cared about that particular tool," which Dana thinks is unworkable.

I genuinely don't know which of them is right, and I'm supposed to summarize the issue for the rest of the team next week. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say who is more right overall. If there's a distinction both of them are missing or blurring, I'd want to know that too.
