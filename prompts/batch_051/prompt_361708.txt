I am a professor of philosophy who works on causation, agency, and historical explanation, and I am spending this year as the scholarly consultant for a documentary production company preparing a ten-part series on the origins of the Cold War. My actual job is to review every draft script before the broadcast historians see it, with particular attention to the causal and blame-assigning sentences that structure the narration. The work is recurrent rather than one-off: the same company is planning further series on the roads to the two world wars and on decolonization, and I want a reusable procedure I can hand to the other reviewers rather than a set of once-off reactions. The producers are not trying to be one-sided, but they face a standing pressure: a serial narrative needs assignable actors and turning points, so draft after draft drifts toward sentences of the form "X started the Cold War." At the same time, the legal department worries about attributions of responsibility, the scholarly advisors press in different directions, we have two or three days per script, and the final sign-off rests with the executive producers, who receive our written ruling for every flagged sentence. The procedure therefore has to be runnable by people who are not historians of the period, and it has to be defensible enough to travel with the ruling.

The material I want you to draw on is the long debate about exactly this question. The orthodox account assigns the primary initiative to Soviet expansion into Eastern Europe; the revisionist account assigns it to American economic and strategic expansion, with its emphasis on open markets, conditional reconstruction aid, and the use of the atomic bomb as a diplomatic lever; the post-revisionist account treats the outcome as arising from an interacting system of mutual misperception and what is usually called the security dilemma. Cutting across these schools is the philosophical worry that "who started it?" is not one question but at least three, routinely fused: who made the first discrete decision that crossed a threshold; whose standing policy made the conflict overdetermined or structurally likely; and who, if anyone, deserves moral blame for the harms that followed. I suspect these questions require different tests and come apart in practice, but I want you to work out whether and how they do, and to build the result into the procedure itself.

Please write the procedure as a sequence of questions or tests to run, in order, on any contested causal sentence in a script, with explicit stopping rules at the points where a sentence is cleared, must be qualified in a specified way, or is refused because no defensible attribution of "having started it" exists at the sentence's level. For each step, say what kind of evidence would count as sufficient, and flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as factual ones or points where the decision must be handed to a person because it is one of values rather than facts. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot do — in particular, where it cannot settle how much moral blame attaches to a great power for helping create a system whose consequences it did not intend. Then run the procedure on three of our current working sentences: (1) that the Soviet Union started the Cold War by imposing communist regimes across Eastern Europe between 1945 and 1948; (2) that the United States started it by making reconstruction aid conditional on opening European markets and by using the atomic bomb as a diplomatic weapon; and (3) that no state started it, because the security dilemma and mutual misperception made the conflict impersonal and overdetermined. Make sure at least one of these cases comes out in a way the executive producers will dislike, and say exactly how the sentence would need to be revised to survive the procedure.
