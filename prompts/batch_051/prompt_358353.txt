I run a pedagogy workshop for new STEM graduate student instructors, and the second of its two sessions is a ninety-minute meeting with about twenty participants. The session is built around the claim that many of students' most persistent errors are not gaps or careless slips but behave more like theories: they hang together, generalize to new cases, make predictions, and survive contact with counterexamples. I want the participants to feel this from the inside rather than have it asserted at them, so I am looking for a structured activity rather than a lecture or a discussion of examples.

Design an activity in which the participants themselves end up constructing and defending a misconception-like theory in a domain they have not studied. I have tried doing this with real physics or biology examples, but participants fall back on what they already know and end up role-playing confusion instead of experiencing it. So build the exercise around a small artificial system — a made-up classification of stylized creatures, a board game with hidden rules, an abstract sorting task, or something similar — with enough structure that a wrong but coherent rule can be genuinely predictive for a while. The activity should move through three stages: groups infer a rule from a limited set of examples, commit to it by making predictions about new cases, and then meet an anomaly of the sort that invites either a patch or an ad hoc exception. The design should make defending the rule feel natural rather than foolish, and it should record enough of each group's stated reasoning that the debrief can hold their own moves up to the light.

Please provide complete rules and materials, including the example set, the transfer cases, and the anomaly; a timed run of show for the ninety minutes; and a facilitator script for the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments — the initial rule formation, the first transfer prediction, the encounter with the anomaly, the patch — onto the features of student misconceptions the session is meant to make vivid: systematicity, coherence, predictive power, and resistance to counterevidence. It should also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure (for example, groups treating the task as a puzzle with one right answer rather than defending their own rule, or participants refusing to patch because they suspect that is the pedagogical trap). Finally, be explicit about which features of an actual novice's misconception this artificial system cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator in the debrief.
