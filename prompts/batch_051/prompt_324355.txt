For a philosophy of math seminar I'm puting together, I want to rank the standard positions on the identity problem for mathematical structuralism by how deflationary they are about the sentence '2 is an element of 4.' Here are the five positions as I understand them; correct me if I've mangled any:

- Set-theoretic reductionism: numbers just are particular sets, so the sentence has a determinate truth value relative to a chosen reduction. Under the von Neumann ordinals, 4 = {0,1,2,3}, so 2 ∈ 4 comes out true.
- Benacerraf-style eliminative structuralism: numbers aren't objects; arithmetic is really about any omega-sequence, so asking whether 2 ∈ 4 is a category mistake, not a substantive question about numbers.
- Shapiro-style ante rem structuralism: there's one abstract natural-number structure, and its places have no internal composition; elementhood belongs only to systems that instantiate the structure, not to the places 2 and 4 themselves.
- Hellman-style modal structuralism: talk of 'numbers' is short for what holds in any possible progression, so an elementhood question only gets sense relative to a chosen progression, and not as a free-standing question about the number 2.
- Balaguer-style full-blooded platonism: every possible set-theoretic reduction of arithmetic exists, so 2 ∈ 4 is true in some reductions and false in others, and there is no fact of the matter about which reduction gives the real numbers.

Rank these five from most deflationary about '2 ∈ 4' (by which I mean: the one that most treats the question as lacking a real, system-independent answer) to least deflationary. Give me one sentence per position for why it lands where it does; ties are fine if you say what the tie is.
