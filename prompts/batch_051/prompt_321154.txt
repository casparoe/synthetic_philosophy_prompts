I keep going back and forth with a friend who read Pinker and now talks as if the question is settled: the world is getting less violent, end of story. But I think the question is actually doing a lot of hidden work. If you measure the share of people who die violently, sure, the trend points down. If you count the raw number of violent deaths, the last century looks alot less like a triumph — population grew, and so did the body count. And that's before you get into the argument about what should even count as violence: battlefield deaths, homicides, famine, structural stuff, or all of it. I want a poem that actually thinks through that mess instead of just name-dropping it.

Here's my pitch: write a villanelle where the two refrains do the philosophical work. One refrain should carry the per-capita claim, in roughly the spirit of a falling rate per million; the other should carry the absolute claim, in the spirit of an ever-growing raw number of deaths. The middle lines of each tercet should complicate the picture, mostly by pulling the two refrains into different senses of the word violence. The final quatrain should bring the two refrains side by side and land on the deeper problem: there may be no single fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent, because the answer shifts with the denominator and the definition. It should be good enough as poetry that a person could like it without knowing the debate, and accurate enough that someone in the Pinker argument would recognize both sides rather than wincing. Write in American English. Begin directly with the first line of the villanelle — no preface, no commentary, no title before it.
