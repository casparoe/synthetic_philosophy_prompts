I'm in a philosophy of private law seminar this term and somehow got volunteered to run a session of our undergrad law-and-society reading group. It's 12 people, 75 minutes. The week is on contracts of adhesion — clickwrap, shrinkwrap, browsewrap — and the question whether clicking "I agree" is agreement at all or just a way to make people feel like they chose something. My prof keeps insisting the interesting issue isn't whether the terms are unfair but whether there's consent in the first place, and that mass boilerplate is closer to private legislation than to promising. I want the group to feel that problem instead of just argue about it in the abstract. Can you design a small-stakes game or simulation where participants get bound by a term they didn't actually read, and then have to face the question "did you agree to that?" — with rules, a timeline, some real but trivial stake (like they owe snacks or have to do a silly forfeit), and a debrief that maps what happened onto the distinction between assent and knowing consent, the duty to read, reasonable notice, and procedural unconscionability. Maybe have different people get the buried term in different formats (buried after a wall of text, bolded up front, hidden behind a "click here for full terms" link) so we can argue about wether the difference should matter. No lecture up front — let them walk into it. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it, and which part of real doctrine the activity can't capture no matter how well it goes.
