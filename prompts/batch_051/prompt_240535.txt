The essay is about the moral saints debate — specifically whether maximal moral goodness is actually a desirable ideal. Here's my rough idea. I want to argue that the literature, including Susan Wolf's classic paper on moral saints, runs together two different things: (1) being morally flawless in the sense of never falling short of what morality requires, and (2) being a saint in the psychological sense of letting moral considerations systematically dominate all of one's reasons, so that non-moral goods like wit, making art, goofing off, or an idle afternoon are the first thing sacrificed. My claim is that these can come apart. A person could be morally perfect without having the cramped, single-minded psychology Wolf describes, because part of moral goodness toward others is being a real friend rather than a hollow moral calculator — and being a real friend sometimes requires doing 'non-moral' things for their own sake. Conversely, someone could have the saintly dominated psychology and still go morally wrong, say through scope insensitivity or a misplaced scrupulosity.

If that's right, the standard debate is somewhat off. Wolf's charge that the moral saint is boring, humorless, and unhealthy only threatens one particular maximizing conception of moral goodness, not the ideal of being as morally good as one can be. But I also don't want the conclusion to be the boring 'virtue ethics already fixed this' line. So the sharper thesis is: maximal moral goodness is systematically under-described in this literature. Once you try to fill it out, it either collapses into the dominating-psychology reading (unattractive but optional) or goes indeterminate, because you can't specify moral perfection independently of a broader story about what makes a life and a character good overall.

Does this line work?
