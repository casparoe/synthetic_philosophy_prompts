I spent my working life in the biological sciences, mostly at the molecular end, and have only recently given serious time to the philosophical side of evolutionary theory. The controversy that keeps drawing me back is the old one about the level or levels at which natural selection acts: genes, individual organisms, or groups. I have been trying to reconstruct the strongest form of the case for the gene's-eye view, since I would like to know exactly how much the view really establishes, and I would be glad to have a philosopher examine the joints of the argument. As I understand it, the case runs like this:

1. A unit of selection is whatever entity changes in frequency in a population as a causal consequence of natural selection.
2. In any single generation, what natural selection does is cause some whole organisms, and in some social species some whole groups, to survive and reproduce at different rates than others.
3. But sexual reproduction and recombination break up an individual's particular combination of genes in every generation, and a group's composition dissolves even more readily; only gene copies are transmitted more or less intact from one generation to the next.
4. Consequently, whatever goes on within a generation, the only frequency changes that can accumulate under selection across many generations are changes in gene frequencies.
5. Therefore, in the long-run sense that matters, genes are the only true units of selection; talk of organisms or groups being selected is at most a convenient description of the machinery through which gene copies are retained or discarded.

My own instinct is that this argument is a little too smooth, perhaps because it slides between something being selected in a given generation and something being the lasting thing whose frequency is tracked across generations, but I cannot locate the exact point of failure. I would very much appreciate a step-by-step assessment. Is the argument valid? If it is not, identify the first numbered step or transition that fails and explain exactly how it fails. If a premise turns out to be true only on one reading of a key term, give the strongest such reading and then say whether the conclusion still follows once the whole argument is read consistently.
