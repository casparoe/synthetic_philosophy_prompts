Act as a philosopher who works on collective action and the ethics of contribution. I am trying to figure out my own view about benefiting from shared goods without contributing, and I have collected my verdicts on a set of concrete cases. Please help me find the principle behind them, or tell me which of my verdicts have to go. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming a few candidate principles before settling on the one that fits best.

My cases:

1. I never donate to the public radio station I listen to almost every morning. I think that is fine, or at most mildly ungenerous.

2. I take a large handful of extra napkins from a fast-food dispenser to keep in the car. Fine.

3. I get on a mostly empty bus and ride without paying. Not fine, even though my one lost fare makes no noticeable difference to the service.

4. I block ads on an independent website that depends on ad revenue, though I would not have clicked the ads anyway. Mildly bad, but basically fine.

5. I contribute nothing to a group project, knowing that my classmates will cover the missing work. Not fine—perhaps the clearest case on the list.

6. I take one fish from a lake whose stock is collapsing under legal but unsustainable fishing. Not fine, even though I cannot honestly say that my one fish caused the collapse.

7. I decline to help pay for a flood wall my neighbors are building, while planning to benefit from the protection once it is done. Not fine.

8. I use the tools in a community garden all season without ever signing up for the shared weeding and maintenance. I keep going back and forth: sometimes it feels like the group project, sometimes like the napkins.

Is there one principle—or a small set of candidates—that captures these verdicts, especially the tension between the bus case and the ad-blocker case, and between the napkins and the fish? State it precisely, say what it predicts about a case I have not settled (not joining a union while accepting the pay raise the union negotiates for everyone), and be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, if any.
