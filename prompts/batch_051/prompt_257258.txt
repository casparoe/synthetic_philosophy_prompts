I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine — readers who will happily think about epistemology for twenty minutes but have never heard of Mīmāṃsā and dont know a word of Sanskrit. Next month I have about 1,800 words to explain a genuinely foreign-looking idea: the classical Indian Mīmāṃsā school held that the Veda has no author (apauruṣeyatva) — not "we lost the name of the author," but no author at all, ever — and that verbal testimony is intrinsically valid (svataḥ prāmāṇya): you are entitled to accept testimony by default unless something specific defeats it, rather than having to earn trust from the speaker's credentials first.

Don't write the piece — plan how I should teach it. I keep going back and forth about whether to open with the authorless claim or with the default-trust claim, because opening on "the Veda has no author" feels like it makes the whole school sound nuts before I've earned the right to say it. So: what has to come first, and in what order do the pieces have to go, so the position ends up feeling like a defensible epistemology rather than a museum curiosity? Give me the one carrying example that should do most of the work, and say what it must have and what it must lack — I suspect the tempting "anonymous letter" example teaches exactly the wrong thing, so if you agree, tell me why and swap it for something better. Then the misconception map: which wrong ideas will my readers most likely form — my guesses are "authorless just means the author's name got lost" and "intrinsic validity means the text is self-certifying and can never be challenged" — and exactly where in the sequence should I head each one off? I also need to know what I should openly refuse to explain at this length, and how to say so without making the whole thing sound like apologetics. End by giving me one question I can put in the final paragraph to check whether a reader actually got the two distinctions — unknown author vs. no author, and default trust vs. indefeasible certainty.
