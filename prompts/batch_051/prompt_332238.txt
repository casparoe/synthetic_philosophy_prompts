Me and a friend spent most of last night arguing about whether you literally see through photographs. He's a photographer and he won't budge: he says a photograph of his late grandmother isn't just a picture of her in the way a sketch or a painting is, because the photo was made by the actual light that bounced off her hitting film (or a sensor) mechanically, with no artist's hand in between. So looking at the photo is, he says, more like looking through a telescope or a window than looking at a drawing — you really are seeing her, just indirectly, even if what you're seeing is a past bit of her. I kept pushing back that this is loose talk: when you look at the photo you see a flat pattern of marks, a depiction, and the original person isn't before your eyes, so calling it 'seeing her' is just a metaphor we've picked up because photos are causally reliable. We couldn't get anywhere, mostly because neither of us could think of anything that would count as evidence either way — the dispute seemed to be about the word 'see' more than about the facts.

I then remembered someone telling me this is a real philosophical debate — I think its called the transparency thesis — so now I want to know whether anything empirical can get a grip on it. Here's the claim to work with: 'photographs are transparent: when we look at a photograph of something, we literally see that thing, not merely a representation of it.' Please design, as concretely as you can, some test of that claim — an experiment, a measurement, a physiological observation, whatever you think is best. My friend floated eye-tracking: do people's eyes scan a photo the same way they scan the live scene? I don't know if that's the right sort of thing. Tell me what result would count for transparency and what would count against it, and then say exactly what a positive result would and would not settle. The part that nags me is that a positive result could always be brushed off as 'the photo just triggers the same visual machinery', while a negative result could be brushed off as 'indirect seeing doesn't require identical machinery anyway.' So be explicit about how much of this dispute is genuinely empirical and how much is a leftover conceptual choice no experiment can reach. And if I've set the whole thing up badly — for instance, if ordinary-language 'seeing' just isn't something you can test, or the question can't be repaired — tell me that plainly instead of politely inventing a study. Oh, and could you write in British English, please?
