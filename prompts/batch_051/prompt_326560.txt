I've started a policy internship where I help put together cost-effectiveness analyses for a health system, and one thing in the method keeps bothering me. The basic procedure is: a new treatment gets scored by how many "quality-adjusted life years" it produces — a year in perfect health counts as 1, a year with severe disability might count as 0.4, death counts as 0 — and then the commitee divides the cost of the treatment by the QALYs gained and compares that to a funding threshold. So the system treats your year at 0.4 and my year at 0.4 as literally the same quantity, and treats moving one person from 0.2 to 0.6 as worth exactly the same as moving anyone else from 0.2 to 0.6. But in an economics class I was told utility is supposed to be ordinal, and that comparing utility across persons is dubious if not meaningless. So here's the question: what theory of utility, welfare, and interpersonal comparison does a QALY-based allocation system really presuppose? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, and to separate the parts the practice strictly needs from the parts it merely happens to sit comfortably with. Is the presupposition internally coherent, or does the practice quietly smuggle in a controversial comparability claim while pretending to be just technical bookkeeping? Then tell me what would have to change, concretely, if we rebuilt the system on a view where interpersonal comparisons are not meaningful — if it would collapse, say exactly where and why.

Act as a philosopher of economics who has spent real time thinking about measurement and aggregation in health policy. Think carefully, start by brainstorming the candidate commitments you spot, and then settle on your best reconstruction and defend it. Give me a definite, argued verdict rather than a survey of possibilities.
