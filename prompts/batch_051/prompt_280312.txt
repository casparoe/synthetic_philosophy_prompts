I've been freelancing since the start of the year (first kid, we needed the flexibility) and I read a few essays on Kelly betting and geometric rationality: when your bankroll compounds over repeated bets, you should maximize expected log growth, not expected value, because a big drawdown multiplies what's left, and going to zero is game over. Lately I've been applying the same logic to promises and commitments, and I've been calling the idea 'geometric capacity.'

The thought is that my time, energy, and reputation behave more like a compounding bankroll than a daily budget that resets. If I take on a project I'm not actually equipped for and it blows up, the damage isn't additive: a missed deadline or a bad deliverable multiplies down my reputation and, honestly, my own belief that I can trust myself, and I can't claw that back by simply doing more work later, the way I could win back a lost dollar with another bet. So the rule I've been trying is to commit only a fraction of my capacity to any one promise, sized roughly the way Kelly sizes a bet, so I'm maximizing the expected log of my remaining capacity instead of the expected payoff of the commitment itself. Concrete example: a client wants a big rush job, 60% chance it goes great and lands me more work, 40% chance I miss the deadline and damage the relationship. Mine normal expected-value brain says obviously take it, but Kelly-ish reasoning, with a modest edge and a real chance of a reputation hit, says keep my total exposure small.

Am I onto an actual concept or is this just an analogy wearing math clothes? Has someone worked this out under another name? I know there's a thing about 'time Kelly' or applying Kelly to time allocation, but I mean specifically promises, reputation, and self-trust rather than hours. And where does it break if I try to actually run my working life this way — be candid about whether the math is doing any real work here or just giving me permission to be scared.
