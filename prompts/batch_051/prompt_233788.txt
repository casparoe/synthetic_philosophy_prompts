I am the student representative on my school district's mental health advisory committee. The committee must submit a recommendation to the superintendent on whether the district should adopt universal mental-health screening for grades 9–12: each fall, students would complete a short self-report questionnaire on mood and anxiety, and students scoring above a set threshold would be offered a follow-up conversation with a counselor. One member opposes the program on the grounds that telling a student she may have a depressive or anxiety disorder can change how she understands herself, how her teachers and peers treat her, and which symptoms she reports in the future — so the screening could itself help produce the conditions it is meant to detect. Another member replies that this is at most a caution about wording and referral procedures, not an objection to screening. Write the committee's decision memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action to the superintendent, set a date for reviewing the policy, and take the first member's concern seriously rather than reducing it to a communication issue.
