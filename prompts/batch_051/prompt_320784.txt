You are a philosopher of physics with a side interest in ancient philosophy. Think carefully, and before you answer, take a few passes at the correspondences, including the ones you will end up rejecting.

I am a novelist, and while revising a manuscript I keep thinking about an analogy I once came across in the ancient atomists, Lucretius especially: atoms are like the letters of the alphabet. A small stock of letters, differently arranged, yields everything from an obituary to a love scene; a small stock of atoms, differently arranged, yields everything from bronze to flesh. The comparison is made to do real work, not just decorate. It is used to support the claims that nothing comes from nothing, that generation and destruction are only the recombination of enduring pieces, and that a finite alphabet of elements can produce unlimited variety with no designer doing the arranging.

I want you to audit the analogy before I borrow it for my own thinking about composition. Start by brainstorming the correspondences, and give me an explicit table: what is the letter to the atom, the word to the compound body, the poet's arranging hand to what, the rules of spelling to what? Then go through the disanalogies, paying special attention to what the atomists themselves insist atoms are — solid, impenetrable, moving through void — features letters plainly lack. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusions the analogy was meant to carry from the ones that are beside the point. Finally, tell me whether those conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the letter analogy was quietly doing the work. If there is a better analogy, name it.
