I'm a novelist, and I keep bumping into something I've started calling the participation limit. The idea: for any choice I'm still in the middle of making, I can't assign a probability to my own action without that probability becoming part of the choice. If I say "70% I take the job," saying it nudges me one way or the other; if I try to correct for the nudge, the correction nudges too, and it regresses. It's not that I lack information about myself the way an outside observer might. It's that the observer's stance and the chooser's stance can't be occupied at the same time, by the same person, about the same decision. The only stable probabilities I can give my own choices are ones that are really decisions with a hedge — a 99% is just a decision wearing an epistemic costume. But I can predict other people, even people very like me, without this problem, because my prediction isn't one of the moves in their deliberation. So my theory is that first-person probabilities about your own undecided choices aren't merely hard to get; they're a category mistake, or at best performative rather than descriptive. I can already hear the objection: a smoker can think "I'll probably fail to quit" and still try, so the two stances seem to coexist. Does that sink the whole thing, or is that just a prediction about a future self treated as another person? Tell me if this is a real position under another name, what it gets right, and where the reasoning is most likely to fail — what argument or case would change my mind.
