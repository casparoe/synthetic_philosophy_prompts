I am a doctoral candidate in moral philosophy, and the chapter I am drafting defends a position about interpersonal respect that I want to stress-test before I commit to it. The position holds that the basic form of respect we owe to other persons is an attitude—a way of regarding them and being answerable to them—rather than a set of actions. On this view, to respect someone is, first, to hold them in view as a person whose claims make demands on me, and to be disposed to the corresponding responses: resentment when they slight me, guilt when I slight them, and a standing readiness to justify myself to them rather than merely to manage their behavior. Outward behavior matters, but derivatively: an act counts as respectful or disrespectful in virtue of the attitude it expresses, not in virtue of the act-type itself. The rival I am arguing against reverses this explanatory order: it holds that what we owe one another is ultimately a matter of what we do and refrain from doing, and that inner attitudes matter only insofar as they cause, predict, or constitute the required conduct.

I want an honest map of this view's exposure rather than a defense of it. Specifically: what would have to be true for the attitude-first position to be false? Which observations, cases, or arguments would count decisively against it, and which would merely force its defenders to add qualifications they could absorb without abandoning the view? I would also like to know which objections someone sympathetic to the position ought to concede carry weight, as distinct from the stock objections its critics press. And I want to know what the view predicts that its action-first rival does not, and which of those predictions it could not surrender without collapsing into that rival. Finally, I want a judgment about whether the view, in its most defensible formulation, is well insulated against counterexample, and whether that insulation is a feature or a flaw. For what it is worth, the evidence I would personally find most difficult to explain away would be a case in which someone treats another person correctly in every outward respect while the underlying attitudes remain deficient, and yet no residual moral complaint against the person seems to remain; I ask that this particular case not be allowed to constrain the assessment unduly.
