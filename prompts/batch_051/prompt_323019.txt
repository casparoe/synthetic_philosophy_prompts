For an explainer I am writing about why even ideal randomized experiments can leave some causal questions unsettled, I want a compact but rigorous example of a disagreement up and down Judea Pearl's ladder of causation: two modelers look at exactly the same data, agree on every rung-one (association) claim and every rung-two (interventional, do-type) claim, but give conflicting answers to a rung-three counterfactual question about a single unit.

Make the case small—one treatment variable and one outcome variable is enough—and make it medical, social, or biological rather than contrived. Constraints: the causal graph is agreed and has no hidden confounding; neither modeler makes a statistical error; and the disagreement must arise entirely because rung-one and rung-two facts underdetermine the rung-three answer. No perfect predictors, no exotic physics, no omniscient observers. The point of the example is to show a reader why agreement on data and trials is not yet agreement on counterfactuals, so a case that merely stipulates the conflicting answers rather than deriving them from two structural models that fit the same observed evidence would miss the point.

After setting out the case, do the following. State the shared causal graph and, for each modeler, give the structural equations or an equivalent specification of the unobserved types. Verify that both modelers assign the same joint distribution to the observed variables and the same quantity to every available intervention. Put the counterfactual question in plain language—for example, for an untreated patient with a bad outcome, would the outcome have been different if treated? Then show the two answers and explain, in terms of the two structural models, how they come apart. Finish by isolating the modeling choice that carries the disagreement and describing what an identifiability result would have to assume to rule this kind of divergence out.
