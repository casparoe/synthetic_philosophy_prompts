I've been working through Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator, and I'd like a consistency check on the reading I've settled into before I present it. Here are the commitments I've come to:

(1) Moral judgment is grounded in sympathy, and sympathy is imaginative perspective-taking into the situation of the person judged; it is not mere emotional contagion.
(2) The impartial spectator is formed by internalizing the responses of actual bystanders—we learn to see ourselves as others see us.
(3) The impartial spectator can, and sometimes should, condemn the unanimous moral judgments of the society we actually live in.
(4) Sympathy is information-sensitive rather than automatic; the impartial spectator judges from a standpoint of full relevant information and disinterest.
(5) The impartial spectator is nothing over and above an idealized social consensus; it is not a transcendent or theological moral standard.
(6) When I have reason to distrust my own impartial spectator, the natural and sufficient corrective is to go back to the felt responses of the actual spectators around me.

Please audit this set: which pairs are in genuine tension, and which only look as though they are in tension? Rank any genuine tensions you find by severity, and for each say what would have to give in order to resolve it. Then tell me which single revision of one commitment does the most to make the whole set coherent, and what that revision would cost me. I want your committed verdict—your actual ranking and your actual choice of revision—not a survey of the moves available in the literature.
