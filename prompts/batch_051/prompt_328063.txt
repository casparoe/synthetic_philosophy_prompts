I've been reading Machiavelli's Discourses on Livy — the old Ninian Hill Thomson translation that's free online — and one of the chapter titles keeps bugging me. It says "That Men seldom know how to be wholly good or wholly bad." I know the chapter is about how political actors are usually a mix, not pure villains or saints. But I honestly can't tell what kind of claim that is. On one read it's just a factual observation about politicians: that in real life someone who does a brutal but necessary thing usually also has decent motives, and someone who seems virtuous usually has some self-interest. On another read it's more of a conceptual point about dirty hands: that political morality itself doesn't sort people into clean camps, that the whole idea of a purely good or purely bad statesman is confused even before you go looking for evidence.

Can you design the best test or measurement you can to tell these apart? I mean: what observation or experiment would treat Machiavelli's line as an empirical hypothesis, and what result would count for it or against it? Then tell me what even a strong positive result would not show—for example, if you found that the same politicians who order drone strikes also visit their sick mothers, would that actually prove the conceptual point, or just describe behavior? I'd like you to take a definite position: is the chapter heading best read as a psychological claim, a conceptual claim, or both, and what kind of evidence could settle as much of it as evidence can settle.
