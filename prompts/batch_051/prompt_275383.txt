Suppose a country is drafting a new constitution and its framers are divided between those who want a strong constitutional court and those who want the elected legislature to have the final word. They settle on the following compromise. The Constitutional Court has seven members, and it may hold a statute unconstitutional only by a vote of at least five of the seven. Such a holding is provisional for nine months: the statute remains in force, and during that window the legislature may re-enact the identical provision by a simple majority. If it does, the re-enacted provision is immune from constitutional review for the next eight years. If it does not, the holding becomes final and the statute is void. Separately, any three justices may publish a reasoned declaration that a statute is unconstitutional. The declaration has no direct legal effect, but the constitution requires the legislature to hold a full-day debate on it within forty-five days, with no vote at the end.

Analyze this as a mechanism, not as an ideal. How would it actually behave over its first two decades? What does it incentivize among the justices in how they write opinions, form coalitions, and choose how far to push a case? What does it incentivize among legislators, government ministries, and repeat litigants? Who would re-enact a struck-down statute, who would decline to re-enact even while insisting the court is wrong, and what would each choice signal to voters and to lower courts? Where would the system be gamed first, and which failure modes are most likely? Finally, can the main failure modes be fixed by adjusting the thresholds, the windows, or the immunity period, or would those fixes mainly push the pressure somewhere else?
