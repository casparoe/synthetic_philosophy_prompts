I'm a journalist working on a piece about how much of language is rule-governed versus just memorized, and I've been going down a rabbit hole on suppletion — the thing where a paradigm grabs forms from completely different roots, like go/went, good/better, bad/worse, or be/is/was. Its weird because these are often the most common words in the language, and kids learn them fine, but they aren't derived by any rule; you just have to store went next to go in memory.

Here's the thought experiment I want you to think through with me, and I want a real prediction with reasoning, not a story. Suppose that starting tomorrow, due to some small change in how the language faculty matures, no child can acquire a suppletive form: a form only gets learned if it's built transparently from its base by a regular operation (go + past = goed, bad + comparative = badder, good + superlative = goodest). Nothing else changes — adults keep saying went and better, children just can't reliably learn those pairings. Walk me through what happens to English, and any other languages you want to bring in, over the next three hundred years. Which forms disappear first and why? Do the high-frequency ones hang on longest as isolated fossils, or do they collapse suddenly once a generation fails to learn them? Do people lean on periphrastic workarounds (more good, did go) instead of new suffixes?

And since sound change is the usual engine that creates new irregularity, tell me whether that cycle just stops, or whether sound change keeps producing forms that then can't be acquired. Here's the part I care about most: after this, does the language still have a real grammar, or does it become something like a transparent agglutinative system with one default rule and no exceptions? Where in your prediction are you confident, and what hidden assumption would have to be false for the whole thing to go differently?
