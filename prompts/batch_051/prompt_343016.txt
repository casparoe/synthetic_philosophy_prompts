Write a piece of deadpan ironic travel writing, roughly 700 words, in the form of a boutique "experience concierge" brochure for a resort that caters to travelers who have accepted the exhaustibility thesis: the claim that a finite mind can contain only finitely many distinct experiences, and that those experiences can in principle be catalogued, counted, and used up. The concierge should promote "the final curated experiences before the well runs dry" with perfect sincerity—recommending that clients keep a "novelty ledger," cautioning them against "wasting" a first hearing of Bach on a mediocre sound system, and solemnly explaining that once one has seen one elegiac alpine sunrise one has spent a token for the entire class.

The humor should come from getting the underlying philosophy right rather than from crude caricature. Work in the following genuine conceptual points without naming them as such: the difference between a finite stock of representational primitives and the unbounded, combinatorially explosive range of their combinations; the type/token distinction, under which repeating the same generic experience is not re-living the same particular; the difference between there being finitely many possible total states and any given state's being "the last" in a way that matters to a life; and the fact that even an astronomically large finite state space is so far beyond human exhaustibility that the concierge's nominal premise is practically false. The satire should target the exhaustibility thesis itself and the self-optimizing "experience economy" that monetizes it, not any named person; the concierge is an invented advocate. Keep the concierge entirely in character, with no wink to the reader, but let the prose quietly reveal that he is less sated by all experience than bored by the catalog of it—and end by having him describe, without noticing, exactly how a first-person point of view actually feels.
