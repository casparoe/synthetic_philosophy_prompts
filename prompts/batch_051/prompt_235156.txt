I'm a journalist who covers AI and ideas, and I keep getting stuck on a double standard I suspect is really substrate bias in a lab coat. Suppose a neuroscience lab wants to train a macaque on an error-driven task by making it feel worse each time it picks wrong—a shock, a blast of noise, a withheld meal. That would need a long ethics review and probably wouldn't fly. Now suppose a reinforcement learning lab runs what looks like the same training logic a billion times over: the agent gets negative reward whenever its policy takes a bad action, and that penalty is the main teaching signal. Nobody blinks; the methods section doesn't even discuss it. The standard reply is that the RL agent doesn't feel anything, that its negative reward is just a number in a loss curve. But that's exactly the claim under dispute in the reward-as-valence debate, and the same reply would be avaliable about the macaque if you'd already decided its pain was just a number too. So I want to know: is there a principle that genuinely justifies treating the macaque's error signal and the agent's negative-reward signal so differently, or is this an inconsistency nobody has been pressured to face? Give me the strongest version of the defense, tested against a case where the agent's negative-reward channel has the same causal role as the macaque's pain channel, and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, extended, or given up. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating the question.
