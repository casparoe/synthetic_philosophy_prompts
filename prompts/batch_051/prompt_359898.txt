In the decision-theory reading group I attend, last week's session turned into a standoff over whether you can coherently assign probabilities to your own choices while you are still making them. The assigned paper had defended the strong line that prediction and deliberation pull in opposite directions: the stance you take when you forecast what you will do crowds out the stance you take when you decide. Two members, Marcus and Elena, kept arguing after the session ended, and both asked me to referee; after half an hour I could not.

Marcus began by saying he saw no reason a committed Bayesian should exempt the proposition "I will take the visiting position" from the usual requirement to have a credence. He has long familiarity with his own preferences, his finances, and his history of choices, so he could honestly put 0.7 on taking the position and 0.3 on staying, and that would simply be calibrated self-knowledge of exactly the kind an outside observer could have. Elena replied that the outside observer gets to predict him because the observer is not the one choosing; Marcus, by contrast, is supposed to make up his mind, and a person who already assigns 0.7 to taking the job seems to have settled the question more than a deliberator may. Marcus answered that people predict their own lapses all the time — a dieter can say "I'll probably eat the cake" while remaining able to refuse it — and that a probability is not a decision, so the charge of self-defeating conflict does not stick. He added that decision theory already traffics in probabilities of the form "outcome, given that I do X," so one's own act can function as an assignable event; refusing the unconditional version of the same probability struck him as inconsistent. Elena came back that the conditional in decision theory is a supposition, not a self-forecast, and that the dieter's prediction concerns a failure that happens to her rather than an act she performs. The deeper problem, she said, is that a deliberator treats her options as open whereas a forecaster treats her own act as the output of a mechanism; those two stances cannot be occupied at once, and the kind of evidential updating that makes self-prediction rational breaks down when the prediction and the thing predicted are not independent, since settling the credence is itself part of the process that produces the act. Marcus granted that a near-certain self-prediction might end deliberation, but he insisted that a middling credence like 0.7 leaves the 0.3 case as real practical work, and that if his choices really are the outputs of his own dispositions, then having the best evidence about those dispositions puts him in the best position to predict himself, not the worst.

I find myself genuinely divided and cannot see how to weigh these moves against one another. Please work through the exchange point by point — who is right about what, where each overstates, and what both are missing — and say who, if either, is more right overall. As you do, please separate what is settled here from what is genuinely contested among people who work on decision theory and the theory of agency, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
