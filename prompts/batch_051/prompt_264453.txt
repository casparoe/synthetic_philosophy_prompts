A charter city is to be founded on a greenfield site under a host-state special economic zone agreement, but with its own constitution. Its designers propose a device they call the Standing Exit Clause, binding on the city operating company, in the hope that a cheap and credible right of exit will keep the city's government responsive. The clause has the following parts.

First, any owner-occupant who has lived in the city for eighteen continuous months may serve an irrevocable notice of exit. Within one hundred twenty days of the notice, the city must repurchase the unit at 90 percent of its original purchase price, adjusted for the change in a published construction-cost index between purchase and exit, and reduced by a fixed depreciation schedule; it must also pay a relocation grant equal to four months of the city's median wage. Residents who leave without serving notice, or who sell on the open market, receive no repurchase and no grant. The right attaches only to a unit's original buyer and lapses twenty years after construction.

Second, every resident who serves notice completes a structured exit interview, and the operating company must publish a quarterly exit report stating the number of notices served, the median time from notice to repurchase, the length of the exit queue, and the reasons for leaving as coded into fixed categories.

Third, the operating company receives half of its annual management fee in cash and half in deferred credits; deferred credits vest only in quarters for which the exit-liability fund is projected to cover the next four quarters of repurchases and relocation grants.

Fourth, to prevent the exit right from producing a run, no more than 15 percent of occupied units may complete repurchase in any single quarter; notices beyond that level are held in a queue and honored in order of service in later quarters.

Analyze the Standing Exit Clause as a piece of institutional engineering, not as a proposal to be endorsed or rejected in the abstract. What does it incentivize among current owner-occupants, prospective buyers, renters, non-resident investors, and the operating company, including owners who were planning to leave anyway? Show how each group would game the repurchase formula, the exit interview, the deferred fee, and the quarterly cap. Then identify where the mechanism breaks down and what second-order effects it produces in the city's housing market, labor market, and politics. Finally, say which failure modes could be repaired by small amendments, whether those amendments would be gamed in turn, and what sort of city this clause would actually select for.
