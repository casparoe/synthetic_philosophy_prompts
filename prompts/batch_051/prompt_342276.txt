You are an early modern historian of philosophy who also works on monetary theory. I am giving a departmental colloquium next month, and I need you to prepare a question-and-answer prep sheet for me, not a defense of the paper. The paper defends the thesis that Berkeley's monetary writings, especially the interrogative work usually known as The Querist, are best read as a coherent nominalist theory of money rather than as ad hoc Irish policy advocacy. The central argument is that Berkeley's immaterialism makes the objects of commerce into ideas whose reality consists in their perceivable, ordered role in experience, so he is entitled to treat money as a conventional sign or counter: a coin or a banknote commands goods and labour not through intrinsic metallic value but through accepted use and coordinated expectation. I argue that this makes Berkeley an early credit-money or chartalist theorist, and I concede that the texts also contain mercantilist elements that sit awkwardly with that reading.

The audience is a mixed early modern colloquium: two Locke specialists, a Hume scholar, an economic historian of eighteenth-century Ireland who knows the currency shortages and banking disputes of the period in detail, and a philosopher of economics who is generally hostile to rational reconstruction as anachronism. They will treat the textual texture of Berkeley's questions as decisive and will not be impressed by contemporary monetary theory except as evidence against me.

Begin by brainstorming likely objections from each member of the audience. Then write the prep sheet: the six or seven objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way this particular audience would put it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is softest. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks. Think carefully about which objections can be conceded outright and which must be held.
