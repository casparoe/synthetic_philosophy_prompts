I am a student in an upper-division seminar on the ethics of character, and this term we have been reading defenses of the virtues alongside critics who argue that our culture's talk of grit, fortitude, and resilience often functions ideologically. I have a set of fairly stable reactions to particular cases, but I cannot yet state the principle behind them, and I would like help working out what I actually think.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

C1. A friend has attempted to summit the same difficult mountain three times, each time being turned back by weather or injury, and each time training harder and returning. I find her persistence straightforwardly admirable. It strikes me as exactly the kind of fortitude worth wanting in oneself.

C2. A first-generation college student is working two service jobs to stay enrolled. His roommate tells him he has real grit. The comment seems to me harmless and probably deserved. I have no objection to it.

C3. The CEO of a company sends a memo praising employees' "grit and ownership" after a year of unpaid overtime and asks for more of it during a difficult quarter. I find this repellant. The word "grit" is doing manipulative work here.

C4. A hospital system responds to reports of burnout and medical errors by rolling out a "resilience program" of mindfulness webinars and gratitude journaling, while leaving staffing ratios unchanged. I find this worse than C3: it has the appearance of care while quietly relocating the problem from the institution to the individual.

C5. A friend with a serious diagnosis insists on combat language, saying she intends to fight it and to find meaning in the struggle, and her family calls her brave. This is the case I go back and forth on. Sometimes it seems admirable and sometimes it seems like a form of denial that our illness culture has imposed.

C6. After a flood, a national news story praises a low-income town's "resilience" as residents rebuild with little public aid, while a local reporter calls the coverage a case of resilience washing, the same trick as greenwashing. Both things feel true to me at once: the residents' tenacity is genuinely impressive, and the national framing is still obscene.

My working hunch is that the objectionable cases are ones where the person praising resilience could instead change the conditions that make resilience necessary, and is using the praise to avoid doing so. But that hunch does not obviously explain C5, and it stumbles on C6, since the journalists cannot rebuild the town. So I am prepared to be told that one or more of my verdicts has to go.

I would like you to do the following. State one to three candidate principles that would capture these verdicts, or most of them, stated precisely. For each candidate, tell me which of C1 through C6 it preserves exactly as I gave them, which verdicts it forces me to revise or abandon, and what it says about three further cases I have not made up my mind about:

- N1. A teacher in a severely underfunded school tells a struggling student that what she needs is more grit, rather than advocating for more resources.
- N2. A retired athlete is profiled in a magazine for the fortitude it took to return from a near-career-ending injury.
- N3. A business magazine celebrates a startup founder's "hustle" and hundred-hour weeks without mentioning the family money that cushioned the risk.

Finally, tell me which of my six original verdicts you think I am least entitled to keep, and why.
