At a dinner party last month two friends of mine got into it about a third friend, Anika, who has spent months looking after an acquaintance with chronic pain — he treats her badly and she keeps going back, saying she cant stand to see him suffer alone. I wasnt in the argument, just listening, but I've been replaying it since. Micah, who leans hard on Schopenhauer, said Anika was doing the only unambiguously non-self-interested thing any of us had done all year. His point was that compassion is the one motive that doesn't ultimately serve the agent's own will: duty, respect, self-improvement, even guilt can be traced back to what the agent gets out of them, but genuine compassion registers the other person's pain as pain, not as one's own inconvenience, and for Schopenhauer that is the entire basis of morality. Tania, who is more Nietzschean, said Micah was laundering pity into a virtue. Pity is a depressing affect, she said: it doubles the suffering in the room, since now two people are in pain instead of one, and the second one volunteered for it. It also carries a built-in condescension — the pitying person gets to feel strong and useful by contrast, while the pitied person gets the message that he is pitiable, which keeps him stuck. Micah answered that this is just cynicism: any decent action can be relabeled as self-interested by attributing a hidden motive, and the 'hidden condescension' charge is unfalsifiable. Tania said it isn't unfalsifiable — look at how Anika falls apart when her help is refused. If the point were really the other person's good, she'd be relieved he didn't need her; instead she's wounded, so something in it is about her. Micah said that only shows his vanity is blocking the help, and having your compassion refused out of pride doesn't retroactively make the compassion fake — Tania's standard would make it impossible to help people too proud to be helped. Tania said no, that pride is exactly the point: a genuine benefactor should want the other person to become less pitiable, stronger, not to serve as a permanent audience for his suffering. Micah said that's a pretty way of saying let people go under unless we can hand them a solution, and he'd rather err on the side of the person who stays. They turned to me to settle it, and I couldnt. I also suspect they were using 'pity' and 'compassion' for slightly different things, which is part of why I keep going in circles.
