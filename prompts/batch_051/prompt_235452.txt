Karl Jaspers argued, in response to the Nazi past, that a nation cannot be guilty in the way an individual can: criminal and moral guilt attach only to persons. Yet he also held that citizens bear a distinct political guilt for the deeds of their state — not for anything they personally did, but because they are members of a continuing political community whose institutions acted and whose common life is carried on by its present members. In that sense, political guilt is inherited across generations through membership in a polity, not through biological descent. His account was framed while the perpetrator generation was still alive and the citizenry was largely continuous with the one that had acted or looked away. Now take a case that severs those background conditions and adds demographic rupture: a state whose earlier regime committed mass atrocities six generations ago, such that no one now living participated in or witnessed the wrongs; the offending governing institutions have since been abolished and replaced under a new constitutional order; and sustained immigration and emigration mean that a large share of the current citizenry descends neither from victims nor from perpetrators. Does Jaspers' argument for inherited political guilt survive this development, does it need repair, or does it collapse? Identify precisely which premises the generational and demographic changes put pressure on, and say whether a repaired version could still underwrite a present-day state acknowledging and acting on responsibility for wrongs committed before any living person was born.
