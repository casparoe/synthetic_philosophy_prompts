I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I have been working through a case a compliance lawyer described to me over coffee. It sits at the intersection of legal interpretation and machine governance, and I want reasoning rather than reporting. The governing statute contains a clause that reads, in its relevant part: "No person shall be excused from compliance with any provision of this title on the ground that the person did not know of the provision." It was drafted in an era when the law was published in print gazettes, and its obvious target was the person who failed to check the gazette or pleaded ignorance of a widely published rule. Nothing in the clause's history contemplates rules that take effect faster than a person can read them.

The case is this. A small exporting firm uses a commercial compliance service that checks the official register each morning for changes to export restrictions. On the Monday before a shipment, the register showed no restriction on the component it was sending. On the Tuesday, an automated rule-making system issued a technical amendment: from that moment, the component required an export licence. The amendment was published only as a structured, machine-readable data file; there was no summary, no public announcement, no plain-language notice, and a careful professional could not have discovered the new duty before it took effect by reading anything a normal person would know to read. The firm shipped on the Wednesday.

Does the clause reach this firm? Give me the readings and then commit to one: the literal words; the evident purpose of the drafters; what the drafters would have said if asked, at enactment, about a machine-generated duty effective before it could humanly be learned; and what a reasonable reader of the statute would understand today. If they diverge, say which should control and why. I also want the philosophical question underneath: is it fair to bind people to rules they cannot know when the ignorance is produced by the way the rules are published rather than by the person's own negligence? End by saying what would change your mind: which consideration or variation of the case would most plausibly flip your verdict or shake it, and where in your reasoning you are least confident.
