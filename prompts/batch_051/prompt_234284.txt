I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have been thinking on my own for a while about a question I cannot settle: what actually makes something a sport rather than a game, a contest, or a performance. I have arrived at a working list of criteria, and I want to test them against a case that sits right at the boundary.

The criteria I have settled on, stated as plainly as I can, are:

1. It is a structured competition between individuals or teams, governed by fixed rules and ending in a determinate outcome.
2. Success depends substantially on trained skill rather than on luck or on the private taste of judges alone.
3. At least part of the trained skill is physical: the body itself has to be trained for it, not only the mind.
4. It has official stakes — titles, rankings, records, promotion and relegation — which persist beyond any single event.
5. It is organized at least partly for spectators, not only for the satisfaction of the people competing.

Here is the case I cannot classify. Suppose someone founds a professional argument league that is deliberately built to sit on the line between intellectual contest and sport. Each match pairs two debaters, and each debater must deliver their entire argument while maintaining a set pace on a stationary bike; if a debater drops below the pace, they forfeit the round immediately, no matter how good the argument was. The motion is drawn by lottery a few minutes before the round, and sides are assigned by coin flip. Three judges score the round under a rubric that gives two-thirds of the points to the quality of the argument and one-third to physical execution under load. The league has a season, a championship, promotion and relegation, and a paying audience that comes as much for the physical strain as for the reasoning.

On my list of criteria, is this a sport? I want you to go criterion by criterion and tell me where the case clearly satisfies or clearly violates each one, and where a criterion simply stops being decisive. Then say what the borderline is teaching me: is the physical-skill criterion doing the real work in separating sport from non-sport, or is the word "sport" carrying something else — prestige, seriousness, funding, or license to value grit over content? And finally, whatever verdict you reach, tell me whether treating argumentation this way would improve the argumentation itself, or just change who ends up winning.
