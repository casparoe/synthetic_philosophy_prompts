I'm teaching a seminar that pairs novels with philosophy of physics, and I want to do Slaughterhouse-Five alongside the block universe. The setup I keep coming back to is the Rietdijk–Putnam argument: if simultaneity is relative in special relativity, then events in what looks like "the future" from here are already real relative to some other inertial frame, so past, present, and future all exist on the same footing — the universe is a block. The Tralfamadorians in Vonnegut's book see all moments at once, like we might see a range of mountains, and Billy Pilgrim gets unstuck in time and relives moments out of order. But I suspect the novel is running together eternalism — the claim that all times are equally real — with fatalism, the claim that nothing anyone does makes any difference. Those arent the same thing: a block philosopher can say the future is real partly because of what we choose, and that has nothing to do with whether our choices are causally impotent. I want a careful reading that uses the physics/philosophy distinction to go through the novel, not just use the novel as an illustration of the metaphysics. Specifically: where does Vonnegut dramatize genuine eternalism, and where does he slide into fatalism in a way the metaphysics doesn't warrant? Is Billy's time-travel even coherent under a block-universe picture, given that a block picture says there is no moving through time, only a static four-dimensional self? And where does the book push back on the philosopher — what does it get right about grief, guilt, or the impulse to relive some moments and not others that a bare eternalist metaphysics is silent about? I'd rather you say what the literary text adds to the philosophy than just score it for accuracy.
