I am a PhD student in the philosophy of technology, and I have come to defend the following view: for most systems worth worrying about—large institutions, algorithmic recommendation and moderation pipelines, AI assistants—slow value drift is a more serious failure mode than active influence-seeking. By influence-seeking I mean a system's coming to pursue goals its designers did not intend through strategic behaviour aimed at acquiring or preserving influence; by slow value drift I mean the quieter process by which a system's effectively operative values separate from its stated or original ones through a long sequence of individually reasonable accommodations, metric redefinitions, and preference shifts that no one experiences as a sharp betrayal at the time. I do not deny that influence-seeking occurs; I claim it is typically the later, less likely, and less explanatory story. What would count against this view? I want the observations, arguments, formal results, historical analogies, and case patterns that should move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward. In particular, what would show that apparent cases of drift are really influence-seeking under a politeness veil, or that the distinction collapses under pressure? What does my view predict that an influence-seeking-first view does not, and what sort of case would falsify that prediction? Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether I have qualified the view into unfalsifiability, and whether that should worry me. Please write the answer in American English.
