In my AI-safety-ish reading group we've spent something like a year going in circles on the unilateralist's curse and I'm starting to suspect we aren't even disagreeing about the same thing. The setup, in case it matters: you have a bunch of agents who can each unilaterally take some irreversible action that affects everyone — messaging a possible alien civilization, releasing a risky model, unilaterally deploying a geoengineering pilot — and each one has a private estimate of whether the act is net-good or net-bad. The curse is that the group acts whenever the most optimistic member crosses their threshold, so as the group grows the chance that someone is miscalibrated enough to pull the trigger grows too. Half the group takes this to mean you need heavy collective gatekeeping: near-unanimity, or at least a strong presumption that unilateral action is presumptively illegitimate. The other half says the curse is an artifact of modeling agents as independent and badly calibrated; in real life people defer, coordinate, and read each other's hesitation, so the curse almost never bites, and anyway gatekeeping has it's own failure mode where a stubborn cautious minority vetoes obviously good actions forever. The frustrating thing is nobody disputes the model, nobody disputes the examples, and the two sides haven't moved.

My working theory is that underneath it all this is a hidden empirical question about how miscalibrated real-world agents actually are — the "curse" people think calibration error is large and irreducible, and the "artifact" people think it's small or easily fixed by deferring to experts. Is that right, or are we actually differing about something more structural — like what counts as a reason to act, or whether you're allowed to assume coordination will happen? I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a survey, and an honest account of what would actually move the two camps.
