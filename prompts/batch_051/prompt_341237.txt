I've been working through Keynes's Treatise on Probability, and I keep tripping over the same thing: he insists that a probability judgement has two separate dimensions — the probability proper, a degree of rational belief in the proposition on the available evidence, and the weight of the argument, which is how much relevant evidence that probability rests on. You can raise the weight without moving the probability at all, like adding a bunch of independent witnesses who all tell you the same thing. My advisor says this idea basically vanished from economics after the subjectivist turn, and that the modern ambiguity literature — Ellsberg's two-urn experiments, beliefs represented as sets of priors, maxmin expected utility, alpha-maxmin mixing between the worst and best case — is the closest thing to a revival. I want you to answer as Keynes, not as a historian neutrally summarizing him. Drop the Keynes of the Treatise, committments intact, into a seminar where this modern material is being presented. What would he make of it? Would he see the set-of-priors model as finally capturing the weight of argument, or would he say it models something else — a preference for avoiding unknown proportions — while still missing the thing he cared about? Be concrete about where he'd be pleased and where he'd accuse the modelers of changing the subject.

In particular, take up two points. First, Keynes held that many probabilities are non-numerical and that comparisons between them are often only partial; would he think representing beliefs as a set of fully specified distributions plus a maxmin decision rule sneaks in more comparability than he was willing to grant? Second, in a repeated Ellsberg task where people can sample from the ambiguous urn before betting, what would his view predict about how the ambiguity premium behaves as the sample grows, and would the set-of-priors model tell the same story? Push the answer past "he'd like the spirit of it" — I want to know exactly which formal move he would reject and what he'd say is still unrepresented.
