In a seminar on normative ethics, a classmate reconstructed the following argument, which our instructor called a tidy version of the paradox of deontology, and I have been turning it over ever since:

(1) A deontological constraint against killing an innocent person says: do not kill an innocent person, even when doing so would prevent several other innocent people from being killed.
(2) The natural rationale for this constraint is that the killing of an innocent person is a grave bad, and this badness is what gives each agent a decisive reason not to kill.
(3) If the badness of a type of act grounds a reason not to perform that act, it also grounds a reason to want there to be fewer acts of that type, whoever performs them.
(4) So the badness of killing gives each agent a reason to reduce the total number of innocent people killed.
(5) Sometimes the only way for an agent to reduce that total is to kill one innocent person herself—say, by killing one so that five others will not be killed.
(6) In such a case, the very consideration that grounds the constraint on killing appears to speak in favor of one act of killing.
(7) Therefore, deontology seems caught: if the constraint is grounded in the badness of killing, it should give way when giving way minimizes killings; if it refuses to give way, then its real ground must be something other than the badness of killing—but then what is it, and why should it bind?

My suspicion is that the trouble enters at step (3), in the slide from the badness of each act to a reason to minimize the total, but I cannot say why that slide is illegitimate without helping myself to the very agent-relativity that is in question.

Walk through the argument step by step and say what it establishes. Is it valid as stated, or does some specific step fail, and under what reading? If it is valid, which premise must the deontologist reject, and what kind of alternative justification for constraints remains available?
