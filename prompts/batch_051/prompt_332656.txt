Retired analytical chemist here, finally working through the moral philosophy I never had time for. Before I read any more work on when an individual's contribution to a collective harm is wrong, I'd like the desiderata spelled out first. I keep seeing people argue about whether you should stop eating meat, or stop flying, or whether your vote matters, and each side picks one case and builds the whole theory from it. I want the full list of what an adequate theory here has to getright.

Specifically: what are the requirements — the cases it must handle, the distinctions it can't collapse, the constraints from decision theory or common-sense morality it has to respect — with the reason behind each? I'm thinking of things like: It has to say something sensibe about the bucket-in-a-lake case (a thousand people each dump a bucket, and the lake can absorb 999 of them without harm) and about the case where there's no threshold at all but each act adds a tiny expected amount to the harm. It has to distinguish between 'my act made no difference' and 'my act was wrong.' It has to respect the difference between doing harm yourself and participating in a practice that does harm. It probably has to handle overdeterminination — the same harm would have happened without me anyway. And it shouldn't just say 'what if everyone did that?' because that's usually a non-answer when only some people are doing it. Those are only my guesses; I want the real list, and I want to know which of these are genuine desiderata and which are just one theory's prejudice. Also flag any pair that can't be jointly satisfied, and tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate the usual contenders.

And at the very end, on its own line, give me a one-sentence summary of where the real tension is.
