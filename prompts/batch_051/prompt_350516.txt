I'm on the grants committee of a small family foundation, the Meridian Fund. For the past few years we've tilted our portfolio hard toward what one member calls far-future work: pandemic prevention, AI governance, civilizational resilience, and a couple of projects whose payoffs are explicitly centuries out. Last night I got into a long argument about it with a friend, and he kept hammering the simulation argument: if it's reasonably likely we're living in a simulation, then the enormous far future we're trying to protect might not be the base-reality future at all, our descendants might be sims the simulators can terminate whenever they loose interest, and the expected-value math that treats trillions of future lives as the dominant term might be built on sand. His takeaway wasn't that we should do nothing for the future; it was that the simulation argument should specifically dampen the fanaticism — the thing where a small probability of affecting an astronomically large far future swamps every near-term consideration in our granting. By 2am he had me half convinced; this morning I'm not so sure.

Concretely: next Thursday we vote on next year's slate. One proposal would move roughly 40% of our far-future allocation into near-term grants (clean water, lead abatement, basic primary care) on exactly these grounds. I'm the one drafting the short memo that circulates before the vote. It needs an actual recommendation, either keep the current far-future tilt and say why the simulation argument changes nothing or not enough, or support some reallocation and say how much and on what timeline, and it has to engage the strongest point of the side I don't pick rather than knock down a strawman. Keep it around a page and a half. If the right answer is that we're missing a necessary piece of information, say what it is and what policy we should run while we don't have it.
