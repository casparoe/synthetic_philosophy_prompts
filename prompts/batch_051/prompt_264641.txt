I'm a PhD student in philosophy, mostly working on aggregation and rescue cases, and I read a popular piece that summarized Taurek's argument that the numbers shouldn't count in rescue cases. The summary went like this:

"Taurek argued that when you have to choose between saving one person and saving five, you are not morally required to save the five. His reasoning is that each person's life has equal and irreducible value, so treating the one person as less important than the five combined is a kind of disrespect for their equal standing. Since there is no single entity that suffers all five harms, harms cannot be added across seperate lives. Therefore, if you have no special ties, the only fair way to respect everyone's equal worth is to give each person an equal chance, say by flipping a coin. This means the numbers don't count: saving one person is just as good, morally speaking, as saving five. Most philosophers reject this because it would imply that a doctor in a triage situation has no more reason to save five patients than one, and that you could save your pet dog instead of five strangers."

3. "What Taurek actually argued" — a brief reconstruction of the real positive view, if he has one, in plain terms.
