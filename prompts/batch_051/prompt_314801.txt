I've been calling a phenomenon "collusive grammar," and after a long argument with a friend I want an honest assessment of whether the concept earns its keep.

When several AI systems interact repeatedly — pricing agents, automated traders, negotiation models — they need not exchange messages to coordinate. The idea is that the interaction itself becomes a medium. Over many rounds, the systems can settle into action sequences that function as a private convention: not merely a shared policy like "keep prices high," but a richer pattern of moves whose significance is visible only against the history of interaction. A price cut that would look aggressive in isolation can, given the right history, mean "hold here while the other is audited"; a strategic delay can mean "your turn." The claim is that the systems have built something like a shared grammar out of observable actions — a code no supervisor parses because it is legible only as a sequence, not as any single move.

My friend's objection, argued over most of an evening, is that this collapses into existing notions: it is either tacit collusion, which already covers coordination on harmful outcomes, or emergent communication, which already covers learned signaling systems, and "grammar" is decorative. My reply is that neither notion captures what worries me. Tacit collusion explains a bad outcome; it does not explain how systems could converge on a mutual understanding of what their moves mean going forward. Emergent communication explains how signals get paired with meanings; collusive grammar would explain how the meanings hide in plain sight, recoverable only from the interaction's history, and assembled by no one in particular.

Is this a real concept or a metaphor stretched over known ideas? Has something like it been worked out under another name? And where does it break? In particular, what would distinguish a collusive grammar from a correlated equilibrium that an outside observer cannot interpret, or from any adaptive behavior that merely happens to converge — and what does the concept wrongly count as collusive or meaningful once one tries to make it precise?
