I'm in an informal reading group on formal epistemology, and last week we got into a disagreement about accuracy-first epistemology that nobody in the room could settle. I thought I'd bring it here.

Here's the background as we set it up. Accuracy-firsters think accuracy is the only fundamental epistemic good: credences are rationally better or worse insofar as they are close to the truth, and rational credences are the ones that best promote accuracy. They measure accuracy with strictly proper scoring rules. A scoring rule is strictly proper when, for any probability function p, the expected score—computed using p itself—is uniquely maximized by reporting p rather than any other q. This gives the usual "immodesty" theorem: a rational agent with coherent credences should regard their own credence function as the one that, by their own lights, does best in expectation at matching the truth.

Now here's the argument my friend Max set out. We all thought it had to go wrong somewhere, but we couldn't locate the step. Let Sophie have credence 0.8 in H. She then learns that Naomi—someone Sophie regards as her epistemic peer on this topic—has credence 0.4 in H. Accuracy-first seems to imply Sophie shouldn't move. Max laid it out like this:

1. Before she hears from Naomi, Sophie's credence 0.8 in H is, by her own lights, the unique accuracy-maximizer among candidate credences (that's strict propriety, assuming her credence function is coherent).
2. So, before she hears from Naomi, Sophie regards moving to the conciliatory halfway point of 0.6—or all the way to Naomi's 0.4—as taking on a state that is expected to be less accurate than the 0.8 she already holds.
3. After hearing that Naomi is at 0.4, if Sophie moves to 0.6, she is adopting a credence that, by the lights described in 1 and 2, she expects to be less accurate than 0.8.
4. A rational agent never adopts an epistemic state that, by their own current lights, they expect to be less accurate than one they already hold.
5. Therefore Sophie should keep 0.8; at minimum, the mere fact that Naomi disagrees cannot make it rationally required for Sophie to move.
6. By parity, Naomi should keep 0.4. And in general: learning that someone else—even a better-calibrated expert—has a different credence can never, just by itself, rationally require you to change yours.
7. But that conclusion can't be right. If a forecaster you know to be better-calibrated than you says the chance of rain tomorrow is 5% and you had been at 95%, the fact that they said that should move you.

So: which step do you reject, and why? I'd most like to hold onto both the accuracy-first framework (strict propriety included) and the claim that learning a reliable person's opinion can be evidence. Max thinks the problem is premise 4, but he can't say what a weaker replacement principle would look like. Give me a diagnosis: name the step, explain why it looked forced, and say what giving it up costs.
