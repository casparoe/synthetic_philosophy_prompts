Everyone around me who's tempted by epiphenomenalism reaches for the same analogy: consciousness is like the whistle on a steam locomotive. The boiler and pistons do the real work; the whistle is just a noisy byproduct of the steam, it doesn't turn the wheels, and you'd be confused to say the train moves because the whistle blew. They use it to dissolve the paradox of phenomenal judgment — the worry that if qualia never cause anything, then my saying I am in pain or my writing articles about experience can't be caused by the pain itself, which makes all this accurate talk about experience look like a massive coincidence. The analogy is meant to show that a real phenomenon can be caused by the machinery without doing any of the work, and that our reports about it can still be explained entirely by the machinery.

Audit the analogy instead of just repeating it. Give the correspondence table first: what in the brain plays the boiler, the steam, the whistle, the wheels, and — this is where I get stuck — the person standing nearby who hears the whistle and forms the belief that the engine is running. Then go through the places it breaks. My main suspicion: the whistle is publicly observable, and the bystander's true belief is caused in the ordinary way by the sound. Epiphenomenal qualia are private and, by hypothesis, can't cause anyone's beliefs. So is the analogy smuggling in the exact causal relation it's trying to make unproblematic? Say whether the analogy can support the conclusion that the paradox dissolves, or whether it only gets you the weaker claim that a byproduct can be idle with respect to one specified output while still have plenty of other effects. And if it's broken for the intended purpose, say what a better analogy would need to contain, or why people keep using the whistle anyway.
