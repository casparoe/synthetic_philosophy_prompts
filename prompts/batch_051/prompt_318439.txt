I'm helping prepare public-comment analysis for a state-level proposal that would let public agencies sign settlement agreements and contractor nondisclosure agreements covering records the public would otherwise be able to request — for instance, internal findings about regulatory violations, monitoring data on unsafe conditions in state facilities, or the terms negotiated to resolve enforcement actions. In our working group, one member keeps closing these discussions with the adage that sunlight is the best disinfectant. His intended conclusion is that any measure permitting such confidentiality provisions is indefensible on its face, because making official conduct public is how wrongdoing gets detected and cured.

I'd like to respond to him precisely rather than just pushing back at the same level of generality. Treat "sunlight is the best disinfectant" as a compressed slogan that could be unpacked into several distinct theses about government secrecy, publicity, and accountability, and present the menu of readings from the near-trivial to the radical. Readings might include: an empirical claim that publicity raises the probability of detection; an empirical claim that the prospect of disclosure deters misconduct; an empirical claim that exposure causes correction through reputational or electoral pressure; a comparative claim that public exposure works better than alternative accountability mechanisms such as inspectors general, auditors, or courts; a conceptual claim that being accountable to the public requires that the public be in a position to know what was done; a deontic claim that the public has a right to know; and a blanket claim that government secrecy is always corrupting or always unjustified. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantial and defensible, or false or overstated, and identify what would have to be true for that reading to license a conclusion against the confidentiality provisions in this bill.

After the menu, identify which reading or readings my colleague's argument actually needs in order to support his conclusion, and which readings are true but too weak to carry it. Lay the readings out as a numbered list with a short assessment for each, then add a section on which readings do the work for the anti-NDA conclusion. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by the name of the reading the argument needs and whether, on that reading, the argument succeeds.
