For the last few years I have been trying to follow the dispute about whether a language model’s chain-of-thought output should be taken as a report of the model’s own reasoning. One camp treats the written-out steps as our best window onto the process that produced the answer: if the model’s stated reasons are bad, we learn why the answer is bad; if its stated reasons are good, we have some assurance the answer was reached for the right reasons. The other camp treats the window metaphor as the heart of the mistake. The intermediate text, they say, is just more generated language, produced by the same trained distribution that produces the final answer. A model that lists steps before answering is no more reporting an inner process than a model that supplies a fluent justification after the fact; the apparent introspection is a genre convention. They point to cases in which a model’s rationale omits the feature that actually moved its answer, or in which the rationale flips when the answer is nudged, as evidence that the narration and the decision can come apart. Everyone I read agrees that chain-of-thought is neither the literal transformer computation nor pure noise, which only makes the standoff stranger.

I am not asking for a verdict on which camp is right. I want a diagnosis of why the argument has stalled and what kind of disagreement it really is. My working hypothesis is that the two sides mean different things by ‘the model’s reasoning’: something like a useful, stepwise reconstruction of the answer versus the actual causal determinants of the output. If that is right, the dispute is at least partly verbal and the live question is which notion of reasoning matters for evaluating and controlling these systems. But I can also see the dispute as a plain empirical one — more interpretability work might eventually tell us how much of the final decision is mediated by the semantic content of the intermediate tokens — or as a methodological disagreement about what ought to count as evidence of faithfulness in the first place.

Diagnose the structure of the disagreement, engaging what each side actually says. Is it verbal, factual, methodological, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about what genuine reasoning would have to be? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal result, a new experimental protocol, a distinction that dissolves the question, or an argument that we should not expect this to settle?
