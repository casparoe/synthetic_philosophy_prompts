I'm in a seminar on medieval philosophy, and we've been reading Ibn Rushd's Fasl al-Maqal — the text usually called the Decisive Treatise — where he argues that doing philosophy, by which he means Aristotle-style demonstrative reasoning, isn't just allowed by revealed law but actually required by it. The move my professor keeps reaching for whenever someone raises a place where Aristotle and the Qur'an seem to flat-out contradict eachother (eternity of the world, whether the afterlife is bodily or spiritual, that kind of thing) is the maxim "truth cannot contradict truth." His line is: since both demonstration and scripture are sources of truth, they can't be in real conflict, so any apparent conflict has to be dissolved by interpreting one of them figuratively — and in practice that means allegorizing the scripture rather than revising the philosophy.

My problem is I can't tell how much work that maxim actually does. On one reading it's just the law of non-contradiction in fancy dress, which doesn't tell you which side to reinterpret. On another reading it seems to be a big substantive claim that philosophy gets to decide when revelation is being read literally when it shouldn't be. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims "truth cannot contradict truth" might be expressing, from the trivial to the radical, say which ones are actually defensible and which are false or just question-begging, and especially say which readings would actually get my professor from "no real conflict" to "allegorize the scripture rather than revise the philosophy"? I want a menu I can bring to section, not one interpretation.
