I keep stumbling into the same dispute. A junior woman researcher makes a suggestion in a meeting and it is ignored; a few minutes later a senior man makes the same suggestion and it is taken seriously. One side calls the first episode testimonial injustice: the junior researcher, they say, was assigned less credibility than she was owed because of her social identity, and was thereby wronged specifically in her capacity as a knower — the kind of wrong analyzed by Miranda Fricker. The other side says no after-the-fact moral vocabulary can settle that, because the meeting was a single unreplicated event and the hearers may have been responding to the senior man's track record, delivery, or the fact that the idea was now being offered a second time. I want to know whether evidence can actually cut between these readings, rather than the dispute being settled by which vocabulary one prefers.

Design the strongest test you can of the underlying claim: that a credibility judgment in an exchange like this is distorted by identity prejudice rather than by context-appropriate use of epistemic evidence. Be concrete about what would be measured or observed and what would be held fixed, and say exactly what pattern of results would favor each reading. Then say what even a clean positive result would not settle — especially whether the original episode instantiates the distinctive wrong of testimonial injustice, as opposed to merely showing that such patterns occur in general — and whether that residue is a temporary limitation of available methods or something built into the concept itself. Distinguish throughout what is settled among people who work on epistemic injustice from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
