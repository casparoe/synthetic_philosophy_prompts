I use a large language model to study subjects I never learned formally, and I have a settled policy about how much to trust it. When it gives me a fluent, confident explanation of something I don't already know, I treat the answer as provisionally true but discounted; and when I later discover that one of those answers was false—what people call a hallucination—I lower my trust in the model roughly as much as I would for a person who had made an honest mistake, and less than I would for a person who had lied to me. My reasoning is that the model does not intend to mislead me, so its false statements are errors rather than lies.

A friend says this is too forgiving and that I have the categories backward. He says the model is doing something closer to what Harry Frankfurt called bullshit: it produces text without genuinely caring whether the text is true. On Frankfurt's account, my friend says, bullshit is in one way worse than lying, because a liar at least pays truth the tribute of hiding from it, whereas the bullshitter treats truth as irrelevant. If my friend is right, then a fluent false explanation from the model should reduce my trust more than a human lie would, not less.

I see the force of that, but I am not sure Frankfurt's categories apply to a system with no intentions at all, and I am not sure why the moral classification should dictate my practical rate of discount. Is my current policy defensible? Does it rest on the mistaken assumption that an intentionless system can only make honest errors? Can a language model bullshit in Frankfurt's sense, or is that a category mistake—and if the categories do not apply, what trust-updating policy would actually be best for an assistant that is sometimes very knowledgeable and at other times systematically indifferent to truth?
