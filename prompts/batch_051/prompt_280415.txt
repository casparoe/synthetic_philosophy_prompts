I'm on the Academic Affairs Committee at a small Muslim liberal-arts college, and we have to decide on a proposed required first-year seminar. The course is basically a close reading of Ibn Rushd's Decisive Treatise. The instructor argues, following him, that revealed law actually commands the person capable of demonstration to study philosophy, and that when a demonstrated conclusion seems to bump against a verse, the wise move is figurative interpretation while ordinary believers keep the outward sense. She's added a final assignment where each student has to say whether, on the Treatise's own principles, a mandatory philosophy course for a whole entering class is a good idea — and I suspect the strongest papers would end up arguing no, since Ibn Rushd divides people into the demonstrative class, the dialectical class, and the rhetorical class, and is pretty explicit that you don't hand the interpretation to the last group.

That's also the trustee's objection: if the plain sense is what protects ordinary faith, then requiring eighteen-year-olds to dissect verses they aren't yet trained to handle either produces a shallow cleverness or real doubts their other coursework never addresses. The philosophy department's answer is that college is precisely the movement from the common understanding toward something more demonstrative, and that the course is our best shot at keeping the religion and the sciences from splitting off into two separate spheres. I've been reading the Treatise in a mosque study group with my teenage son, so I'm feeling both sides personally. Draft the short memo I can circulate before our Thursday meeting: approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with reasons the losing side would recognize as fair.
