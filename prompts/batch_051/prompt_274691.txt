Han Feizi and the Legalists argued that you shouldnt build a state around virtue, because people are self-interested and unreliable, and even the rare virtuous official is a bad foundation for institutions. The ruler should instead set up clear, public laws with dependable rewards and punishments and let interest do the work. Rule by incentives, not rule by character.

I want you to think through whether that argument survives a development that came long after the Legalists. Behavioral economics has a whole literature on motivation crowding out: when you introduce material incentives for something people partly did out of social or moral motives, the act can get reframed as a transaction, and the underlying pro-social motivation shrinks. Theres the classic daycare study where introducing a fine for late pickups made lateness go up, not down, and removing the fine didn't fix it. Theres also the broader metrics problem: once a measure becomes the basis for reward and punishment, people get very good at optimizing the measure instead of the behavior the ruler actually wants.

So does Han Feizi's argument survive this? Which premise is actually under pressure -- his descriptive claim about self-interest, or his design claim that you can install incentive systems without corroding the background norms and information they need to work? And is there a repaired version that stays Legalist in spirit but weaker and more defensible, or does the motivation-crowding evidence just kill rule by incentives?
