I direct the internal training program for the analysts at a foundation that makes large, hard-to-reverse grants under uncertainty. For years we taught the standard toolkit — base rates, expected value, pre-mortems — through seminars, readings, and checklists. The program has been criticized for producing knowledge without skill: analysts can describe each technique but do not reliably execute it when a live decision carries stakes, time pressure, and a room full of strong opinions. I have come around to a martial-arts view of training: that rationality is partly a bundle of trainable sub-skills, and that each sub-skill needs its own standardized drill, repeated with feedback until it is automatic, not merely understood. But I also know the analogy cuts the other way: a punch drilled in the air does not always land in a fight, and some drills produce people who are excellent at the drill and no better at the work.

Every quarter I receive proposals for new drills, and I need a reusable screening-and-design procedure my team can run on any proposal before I sign off — with about an hour per proposal and no ability to run a proper training study. The procedure should take us from a proposal to an approve, reject, or modify. Write it as an ordered set of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it cannot eliminate judgment and must hand the decision to a person. Then run the procedure on three proposals: (1) a calibration kata in which analysts assign probabilities to general-knowledge trivia and receive immediate Brier-score feedback; (2) a base-rate kata in which analysts read a short, anonymized grant case and must specify the relevant reference class and its base rate before giving a funding recommendation; (3) a self-critique kata in which analysts spend ten minutes arguing against their own current recommendation before committing to it. Include at least one outcome the procedure gives that I will not like — I am strongly attached to the calibration drill — and close by saying what the procedure cannot do, especially about transfer from the drill to the real work.
