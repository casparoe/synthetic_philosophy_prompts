Me and my friend spent half of last night arguing about what actually makes something funny, and I've come away thinking she's running some principle she can't quite state. We went through a bunch of cases, and her verdicts are weirdly consistent, so I want to see if someone else can pin the thing down.

For her, these are funny: a video of a bloke slipping on ice outside a pub, going legs-up, then bouncing straight back up and giving the camera a thumbs-up; wordplay puns where one word flips to a second meaning, even when no one is being made to look stupid; a clip of a toddler pulling a genuinely furious face, like a tiny barrister mid-objection; and the moment a mate jumped out from behind the sofa at a party, which terrified her for half a second before she couldn't stop laughing. These are not funny for her: a joke about a flood that killed people, however sharp the punchline was; a clip of an elderly bloke falling off a ladder and properly hurting himself; and ordinary, uneventful, fine-but-unsurprising moments.

The case we got stuck on is this. Her older cousin gives the same toast every Christmas, and every year he ends it with the same deliberately crude pun about a family member's surname. Everyone in the room knows it's coming, half of them mouth it along with him, and it lands exactly on cue. She thinks she might still laugh, mostly because it's a bit off-colour but safe, since it's family; but she also admits she isn't sure. I suspect she won't, because there's no surprise left in it.

Predict how she'll come down on that case. I know the standard theories — superiority, incongruity, relief, benign violation — but none of them handled all her calls by itself when I tried. Which rule or rules does her record fit best, which other rules fit her record just as well, and what further case would cleanly tell them apart? And could you write the answer in British English, please?
