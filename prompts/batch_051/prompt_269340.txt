I've been reading Adam Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments* and got stuck on the way he handles gratitude and luck. Smith seems to treat gratitude as grounded in the benefactor's intention — the benevolence or propriety of the affection behind the act — but he also observes that our actual moral feelings shift with the beneficial or hurtful consequences that an action happens to produce, even when those consequences are wholly outside the agent's control. That has left me puzzling over an asymmetry in ordinary gratitude.

Suppose two people each go out of their way, with equal care, effort, and benevolence, to do me a genuine good turn. A's plan succeeds and I receive the benefit. B's plan is identical in all respects that depend on the agent — intention, effort, care, sacrifice — but a freak accident blocks the benefit at the last moment: the gift is destroyed in transit, the life-saving advice arrives one day too late. Most of us would feel warmer gratitude toward A, and would regard ourselves as having more reason to repay A, than B. Yet the only difference between A and B is luck. Now add a third case: C benefits me substantially, but entirely without intending to — the benefit falls out of an action that expressed no concern for me at all, perhaps even a selfish action. The benefit to me is identical to A's, but most people would not count C as a benefactor to whom gratitude is owed in the full sense. So ordinary practice seems to make gratitude track both intention and success, though the two can come apart: failed intended benefit yields weaker gratitude than succeeded intended benefit, and unintended benefit yields something that feels almost unlike gratitude altogether.

Is anything actually justifying this pattern, or are we just imposing an outcome bias on a sentiment that ought to answer to intention? Please answer as a professional moral philosopher.
