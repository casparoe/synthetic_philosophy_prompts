Consider a counterfactual Britain, 1780–1835, in which only one thing is changed from the actual record: the Atlantic slave system remains as profitable for British elites at every point in the period as it had been at its eighteenth-century peak, and the planters and slave-trading interests therefore retain their eighteenth-century levels of financial and parliamentary weight. Slave revolts occur as they actually did but do not dent profitability; the abolitionist movement is exactly as it actually was in its arguments, petitions, religious base, and parliamentary tactics; and broader British social and political development proceeds as it actually did. Predict, with reasoning, whether and when Britain abolishes first the slave trade and then colonial slavery. Distinguish three candidate drivers — material interest, humanitarian argument, and shifts in what it is honorable or dishonorable to defend in public — and say which of them is carrying the causal load at each stage. For each stage, state whether the effect is robust or fragile under small changes to the scenario, and identify the condition whose absence would most plausibly reverse it. End by assessing the economic-interest thesis about actual abolition — that Britain ended slavery because the slave economy had become unprofitable, peripheral, or less worth defending — and say what your counterfactual implies about it, and where that implication is weakest.
