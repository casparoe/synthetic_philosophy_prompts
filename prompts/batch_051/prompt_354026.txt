I have a standing policy in argument: I will not accept the conclusion of a deductive argument until the speaker has justified the rule of inference itself, not merely the premises. If someone argues, "If P, then Q; P; therefore Q," I ask them first to establish that modus ponens is truth-preserving. If they offer an argument for modus ponens, I apply the same demand to whatever inference rule that argument uses, and so on. I adopted this policy after being badly misled by an argument that moved through steps I would not have endorsed on reflection; I now regard the acceptability of an inference rule as a suppressed premise in every deductive argument, and I think it is only honest to ask that it be defended. My friends say this is just Carroll's tortoise dressed up as epistemic caution: it confuses rules of inference with premises, makes every argument uncompletable, and in practice I seem to invoke it only against conclusions I already dislike. Is my policy a defensible form of epistemic self-protection, or a rationalization for never having to update? Is there a restated version that keeps whatever protection it provides without the regress — for example, requiring explicit justification only for unfamiliar or contested inference rules while treating elementary rules as trusted by default? And how would I tell, from the inside, whether I am applying it even-handedly rather than selectively?
