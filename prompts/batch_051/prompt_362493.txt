I’ve been working through Russell’s theory of descriptions, and I keep getting stuck on a point that feels both technical and philosophically consequential. In “On Denoting,” Russell famously analyzes a sentence like “The present King of France is bald” as a complex existential claim. On his analysis, the sentence asserts, roughly, that there exists something that is uniquely a present King of France, and that this thing is bald. Since there is no present King of France, the whole sentence comes out false. Russell takes this to explain how we can understand, and indeed make straightforwardly false claims about, things that do not exist. But there is a puzzle that emerges from the very examples Russell uses. In the same essay he distinguishes denoting phrases that do not denote anything from denoting phrases that denote one definite object, and he treats “the present King of France” as the paradigm of an empty denoting phrase. Yet he also treats the proposition as perfectly intelligible, and indeed as the kind of thing a speaker might assert and a hearer might deny without either of them being confused about the fact that there is no such king. The puzzle is this: if the surface grammar of “The present King of France is bald” disguises a quantificational claim about a unique object, why does it remain intelligible even when the embedded condition is known by all parties to be empty? What exactly does the analysis do for us here—does it explain meaning, truth value, or something else? I want the most committed and precise account you can give of what Russell’s theory is really a theory of, and of how it avoids the idea that we are somehow talking about a nonexistent object when we say that the present King of France is bald. Take a side on the contested interpretive question and defend it.
