I've had this referendum rule rattling around in my head and I want to know how badly it goes wrong before I pitch it to anyone. The rule: instead of a fixed turnout threshold, every binding referendum has a quorum equal to the median turnout of the previous five referendums that reached the ballot, rounded down to the nearest whole percent, whether or not those earlier referendums cleared their own quorums. For the first five, the quorum is a fixed 50% until there's enough history. So if the last five turnouts were 61, 44, 39, 47, and 38, the next one needs 44% to bind. The idea is that the quorum flexes with how engaged people actually are: it won't get stuck high after a one-off burst of enthusiasm, and it won't get stuck low enough that a tiny group can decide things forever.

Walk me through what this actually does in practice. How does it change the usual get-out-the-vote logic, and do opponents start quietly campaigning for abstention instead of no votes? What does that do to the meaning of a yes result? Who games it first: a goverment scheduling a referendum after a run of sleepy low-turnout votes, or a party trying to manipulate the five-referendum window with high- or low-turnout stunts? What happens to the quorum during a long slide in turnout, and can it ratchet down until a small, highly motivated minority passes things for everyone else? Which failure modes can actually be patched (a floor and a ceiling, using registered rather than eligible voters, a longer window, requiring the quorum to be met by yes voters specifically, excluding failed referendums) and which patches just create new games?
