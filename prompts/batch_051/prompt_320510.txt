Here is an argument I keep half-constructing about historical writing, and I cannot tell whether it is a real insight or a slide. I want you to check it step by step.

(1) A history of any existing thing—a state, a science, an institution—is written from the outcome backwards: the historian knows how the thing eventually stood, and that outcome frames the question the narrative sets out to answer.
(2) Once an outcome frames the question, the events the narrative selects are selected for their bearing on that outcome; whatever lacks bearing on it is passed over.
(3) An event selected for its bearing on an outcome is woven into the narrative as part of the chain by which the outcome came about.
(4) To present earlier events as links in a chain by which a present state came about is to present the past as progressing toward that present state.
(5) To present the past as progressing toward a present state is just what critics mean by Whig history.
(6) Therefore, whoever writes the history of an existing thing inevitably writes Whig history.

The conclusion feels too strong, so I suspect something has slipped, probably in the move from leading-to to progressing-toward. But I also worry that the whole argument might be valid on a deflated reading of progress, in which case it proves only something trivial. Please say whether the argument is valid; if it is not, identify the first step that fails on the natural reading and explain how the failure arises. Then give the strongest reading of the contested term on which the argument is valid, and say whether the conclusion that survives on that reading is still the objectionable Whig conclusion or only a harmless one.
