Since retiring from a career as a field ecologist, I have been reading moral philosophy, and I keep returning to Bernard Williams's integrity objection and to the point he dramatizes as one thought too many: that requiring a person to justify a deeply personal action from an impartial standpoint can itself distort the action and alienate the person from the commitments that give it its point. I would like you to apply that line of thought to an interpretive problem from my former profession.

The safety code of the field station where I worked for many years reads: "In an emergency, a member who is able to render aid shall direct it where it will do the greatest good, and shall not prefer any person because of friendship, kinship, or professional regard." The clause was drafted in 1961 to govern paid field-crew leaders while on expedition, and it has never been revised.

Last year, during the station's anniversary dinner, a small fire broke out in the laboratory wing. A retired member—not on duty and never a crew leader—was the only person who could reach either of two trapped people: his wife, who had accompanied him to the dinner, and a visiting postdoctoral researcher he had never met. Both could have been saved if reached in time, but he could reach only one. Without stopping to compare injuries or chances, he pulled his wife out. She survived; the postdoc did not. The station's executive committee must now decide whether he breached the clause.

Please explain what the words of the clause require in this case, what its original purpose would suggest, what the members who drafted it in 1961 would most plausibly have said if they had been asked, and what a reasonable member reading the code today would take it to mean. Then explain whether Williams's integrity objection, and the one-thought-too-many point in particular, gives reason to prefer one of these readings over the others—or to read the clause as not reaching a rescuer in this man's position at all—and say which approach should control here. I am less interested in the committee's practical verdict than in whether a defensible interpretation of the clause can avoid demanding exactly the sort of thought Williams treats as one thought too many. Please answer in American English.
