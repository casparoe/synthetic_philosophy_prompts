I have become convinced that the resistance to naked statistical evidence in legal proof is not a confusion about probability, and that courts are justified in refusing to let a bare high base rate satisfy the burden of proof. The case that fixed this for me is Smith v. Rapid Transit, Inc., in which the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court faced a plaintiff forced off the road by a bus she could not identify. Her evidence showed that the defendant was the only bus company licensed to run a bus on that route at that time, so the probability that the defendant operated the offending bus was high. The court nonetheless held that this was not enough, on the ground that the evidence left the defendant's liability to a mathematical guess rather than tied the particular accident to the defendant through something more direct.

I want to know what would count against this view. I am not asking for the familiar objections already on the table—that if the standard of proof is really a probability threshold the distinction makes no sense, that the view is inconsistent with how we treat aggregate harm, that individualized evidence can always be redescribed as statistical at a finer grain. Instead, I want the defeaters that should make a committed defender of the anti-naked-statistics position feel the ground shift. Which contemporary epistemological notions, such as sensitivity or safety, would put pressure on the distinction? Which constructed cases would be most damaging, and which would merely show that the rule needs an exception? What would have to be true about the function of legal adjudication—for instance, if its primary purpose were deterrence or loss allocation rather than the assertion of relational liability—for the distinction to collapse? What formal result in probabilistic reasoning or decision theory would be fatal, and what empirical finding about factfinder behavior would be merely awkward? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what defenders themselves should concede counts against the position from what they can reasonably dismiss, and end by saying whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and if so whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.
