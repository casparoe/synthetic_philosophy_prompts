Permit me to set out a craft conviction I have acquired over a decade of writing historical fiction, and to ask for it to be tested rather than confirmed. I have come to hold that a serious historical novel should keep in play all three of the relations to history Nietzsche distinguishes—the monumental relation, which takes up past figures as exemplary and invigorating; the antiquarian relation, which preserves and lingers within the particular texture of a vanished world for its own sake; and the critical relation, which sits in judgment on the inherited past in order to loosen its hold on the present. I further hold that a novel in which any one of these relations achieves lasting dominance is defective in a specifiable way, not merely in a way I happen to find uncongenial; it will, I suspect, fail its material in a characteristic pattern even if it succeeds as entertainment or as style.

The question I want answered is not whether this amounts to a defensible reading of Nietzsche, but whether the conviction itself can be wrong. What would count against it? I am asking for the observations, arguments, and literary counterinstances that should genuinely move me, ordered by how decisive they are: great historical novels that succeed through the ascendancy of a single mode; reasons for thinking the three relations do not exhaust the ways a storyteller can stand toward the past; reasons for thinking that demanding all three at once condemns a book to a formless balancing of attitudes; and an honest judgment about whether my view, as I have stated it, risks anything at all. In particular, I want to know whether it makes predictions about particular works that rival views would not make, or whether it has been quietly insulated by the looseness of words such as dominance and balance, so that almost any novel I admire can be redescribed as satisfying it. I would be most swayed by an argument that there is a fourth relation to the past, absent from the familiar three, doing real work in fiction; I would also be swayed by being shown that my own practice already treats one relation as a check on the others rather than as its equal.
