I'm a software engineer on a team that ships binary classifiers for fraud detection, and I have no formal philosophy background. A colleague walked me through an argument about majority-vote ensembles, and it now strikes both of us as proving too much, but I can't locate the step that fails.

(1) Call a model competent on a task if, on the instances we actually see in production, it has a better-than-even chance of assigning the correct label.
(2) The Condorcet jury theorem says that, for a binary question with a determinate correct answer, if each voter independently has the same probability greater than one-half of voting correctly, then a majority vote is more likely to be correct than any single voter, and as the number of voters grows the majority's probability of being correct approaches certainty.
(3) An ensemble of models whose outputs are combined by majority vote is just a group of voters deciding a binary question, so the theorem applies to it.
(4) Our individual models are competent in the sense of (1): each clears 50% accuracy on our held-out evaluation set, most of them sitting between 55% and 65%. We can also make their errors nearly independent by training each model on a disjoint slice of the training data.
(5) Therefore, if we keep adding models, the ensemble's accuracy should approach 100%.
(6) But we have already gone from three models to fifteen, and accuracy has plateaued near 80%. No one on the team believes that an indefinitely large ensemble of mediocre models would be infallible.
(7) So either the theorem as stated in (2) is false, or one of the earlier steps is false or does not apply.

I would most like to keep (2), since it is a theorem; I am less attached to the others. Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
