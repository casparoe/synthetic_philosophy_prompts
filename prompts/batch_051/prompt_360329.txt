I've been rereading Ursula K. Le Guin's The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas for a discussion group, and I want to use it to make the equality-of-what debate vivid rather than let it collapse into the standard utilitarianism-versus-the-separateness-of-persons reading the story usually gets. Treat the story as a dramatization of the question whether the currency of egalitarian justice should be welfare, resources, or capabilities, and give me a close reading through that lens.

Specifically:

- Work through the three currencies in detail. The welfarist locates the wrong in the child's suffering and the citizens' joy; the resourcist in the child's nearly total lack of all-purpose means—food, light, freedom, comfort, hope; the capability theorist in the child's lack of real opportunities to be healthy, to play, to be touched, to choose. For each, say which elements of the story carry the most weight, and where that reading is forced to strain.

- Pay attention to the story's suggestion that the child's degradation has gone deep enough that it might be wretched if released into ordinary life. That looks like an adaptive-preferences case: the child's expectations have adjusted downward, so a welfarist who trusts subjective states may find the harm harder to express than it should be. Does the story thereby give ammunition to the capability view, or does that reading smuggle in a standard of flourishing that the story leaves unargued?

- Address where the story resists all three framings. The citizens do not run a comparison between the child's metric and their own; they ritually accept a bargain whose central fact is that the child is a condition of the city rather than a member of it. What does the story claim about knowledge, complicity, and the choice to walk away that no choice of currency captures?

- Say whether the story implicitly sides with one currency, or whether its real contribution lies elsewhere—for instance, in showing that the equality-of-what question has a prior question about who counts as a subject of distribution at all.

End by saying what the story adds to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone do not. Throughout, flag where your reading is most likely to fail and what would change it: which textual detail or which philosophical consideration would make you reverse or substantially revise the interpretation. I want a reading that knows where its load-bearing assumptions are, not a verdict.
