Imagine that starting next year in the United States, every registered charity and government aid program has to publish a single audited 'impact per dollar' score each quarter. The score is computed by a fixed formula that counts only outcomes directly measured within the program and within three years (QALYs saved for health, school completion for education, income gains for cash transfers), all converted to a common points scale. A new Effectiveness Fund—equal to the current annual value of the charitable tax deduction plus a third of the foreign-aid budget—is then allocated automatically each quarter to the top-scoring programs across all categories. Nothing else changes. Predict, with reasoning, how the nonprofit and aid landscape looks after twenty years: which cause areas grow and which quietly die, how organizations restructure to raise their scores (legitimately or by gaming), how private donors respond to the public scores, and whether the policy makes the world better in the way its designers presumably intended.
