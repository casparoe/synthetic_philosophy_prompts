I'm a retired geneticist — did population genetics, not medicine — and got into philosophy about a year ago. Lately I keep chewing on the nonidentity problem: whether you can wrong a person by bringing them into existence when the alternative was a different person, or no one. A friend who teaches ethics gave me her verdicts on a bunch of made-up cases (all about whether the choice wrongs the child or future people), and I kept them:

- A 14-year-old has a baby on a whim. The child's life is hard — poverty, lousy schooling — but still worth living. If she'd waited, a different child would have had a much better life. Verdict: wrong.
- A woman gets rubella early in pregnancy, ignores her doctor's advice to terminate, and the child is born deaf, blind, and with a heart defect; the child's life is barely worth living. If she'd terminated and conceived later, a different, healthy child would have existed. Verdict: wrong.
- A couple in their late thirties conceive naturally at the time they'd always planned, no testing. The baby is born with mild hearing loss and some extra arthritis risk later on; no different child was realistically on the table. Verdict: not wrong.
- A country chooses to use up a resource, so the people alive 200 years later have much grimmer lives than the people who would have been alive under a conservation policy; those grim lives are still worth living, but only just, and the would-have-been conservation people are different people entirely. Verdict: wrong.

Now the new case. A woman doing IVF has several embryos. Preimplantation screening is offered and would show one embryo carries a variant whose effects are moderate: the resulting child would probably be partly deaf from his teens and get painful arthritis in his forties, but he'd have a normal lifespan and an overall clearly good life — well past just worth living — just noticeably worse than an unaffected sibling's would have been. She declines the screening, that embryo is implanted, and the child is born as predicted. Had she screened, she would have implanted a different, unaffected embryo.

I keep going back and forth between three framings: a person-affecting view (you can't wrong someone by creating them, at least not if their life is worth living), a threshold view (creating a person is wrong when the predicted life falls below some line of an adequate life), and a comparison view (wrong when the child is significantly worse off than a different child the parents could readily have had). Propose any principles that genuinely fit her track record — start from those three, but add or drop as needed. What would she say about the new case, which earlier case is the closest analog, and what further case would settle whether she's a threshold person or a comparison person? Also say how confident you are in all of this, and single out the point your least sure of.

For format, give me a JSON object with these fields:
- candidate_principles: array of strings, each a one-sentence rule that fits her track record.
- verdicts_by_principle: object mapping each candidate principle to its prediction for the new case ("wrong" or "not wrong") plus a one-clause reason.
- implicit_principle: string, your best guess at which principle she's actually running, with a sentence of reasoning.
- predicted_verdict: string, your best guess at her verdict on the new case.
- closest_precedent: string, which earlier case is the nearest analog and why.
- settling_case: string, a short description of a further case and what each candidate predicts about it.
- confidence: string, low/medium/high plus one sentence.
- least_sure_point: string, the part you're least confident about and why.
