Aristotle, in Book V, Chapter 5 of the Nicomachean Ethics (1133a–b), writes:

"Money, it is true, is liable to the same fluctuation of demand as other commodities, for its purchasing power varies at different times; but it tends to be comparatively constant. Hence the proper thing is for all commodities to have their prices fixed; this will ensure that exchange, and consequently association, shall always be possible. Money then serves as a measure which makes things commensurable and so reduces them to equality. If there were no exchange there would be no association, and there can be no exchange without equality, and no equality without commensurability. Though therefore it is impossible for things so different to become commensurable in the strict sense, our demand furnishes a sufficiently accurate common measure for practical purposes."

I want to work out whether the argument compressed in this passage survives a specific modern development. The account treats money as a conventional artefact introduced to make heterogeneous goods commensurable, but it also seems to require that money itself be a commodity whose purchasing power remains comparatively stable, and it assumes that prices can be fixed so as to secure future exchanges. Consider instead an economy with central-bank fiat money that has no non-monetary use, where the money supply is deliberately managed, and where prices are not fixed by bilateral agreement between trading partners but discovered through continuous automated markets and order books. Does Aristotle's argument that demand is the real common measure still go through? Does the fiat-digital setting vindicate his view that money exists by convention rather than by nature, or does it remove a premise the argument needs, such as the comparative constancy of money or the possibility of fixing prices? I am interested in what happens to the philosophical argument itself, not in economic policy.
