I'm a journalist working on a piece about how often people read old scientific arguments as if the author was just making an arithmetic mistake, when actually the conceptual setup was different. My test case is Aristotle's argument against the void in Physics IV.8. The standard line among science writers is something like: Aristotle says a body would move through a void instantaneously because you can't have a ratio between the void and the full, and he got that wrong because he didn't have zero. I want to push back: that's an anachronistic reading. In his framework, a ratio is a relation between two positive quantities, so saying the void bears no ratio to body isn't a failed attempt to divide by zero; it's a claim that void is not the right kind of thing to enter into the causal story about speeds. My position is that the "Aristotle made a mathematical error" reading is mostly a projection of our number concept onto his physics, and the better reading is that he was making a conceptual point about what can count as a resistence. What would count against my position? I'd like the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they are, including things in the text itself that I might be ignoring and places where defenders of my view should honestly concede. Is there a way to show that his argument genuinely needs a zero and falls apart without it? And am I insulating my view by saying "this is just the best reading" whenever someone shows a flat-footed version is false? If so, is that a problem for this kind of historical-interpretation claim, or just the normal way it works?
