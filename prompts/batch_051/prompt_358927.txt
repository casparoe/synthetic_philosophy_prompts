Over the years I've absorbed a lot of evolutionary-debunking material, and I've converged on a personal policy for separating my moral preferences from my moral biases. When a moral reaction shows up — my sense that breaking a promise to a friend is worse than breaking one to a stranger, say, or my resistance to genetic enhancement — I run a provenance test on it. I ask whether I can sketch an evolutionary or social-selection story that would install exactly that reaction in creatures like me whether or not it tracked anything. If I can, I file it under "bias" and stop letting it count as a reason. If I can't, I file it under "preference" and let it guide my choices. Last week the policy mattered: I applied it to my persistent unease about genetic enhancement, decided I could tell a plausible status-competition story about that unease, and set it aside — then noticed I felt relieved the way I do when a procedure tells me what I already wanted to hear.

Now I'm suspicious of the policy itself. The provenance test promised to break the deadlock between treating every intuition as data and treating none as data, but in practice it seems to let me debunk whatever I find inconvenient while leaving the intuitions I like protected, because with enough effort I can generate a "plausible story" for almost any reaction, and the ones I keep are just the ones I decline to scrutinize. Is a provenance test a defensible way to separate moral preferences from biases, or is it itself a bias dressed up as a method? When is it reliable, when does it fail, and is there a restated version that keeps the benefits without the arbitrariness — and would I be entitled to it given that I have no independent access to the moral facts it is supposed to be filtering for? I'd like the response to say how confident it is in the specifics and to single out the point it is least sure about.
