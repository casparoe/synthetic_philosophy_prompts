I run a graduate seminar on AI governance and international relations, about twenty students, mostly from computer science and public policy, in a single two-hour session. We are reading James Fearon’s 1995 article Rationalist Explanations for War, and I have noticed that students can restate the argument that war can occur even when both sides are rational but they do not feel the underlying mechanism. The part I want to make vivid is what Fearon calls the commitment problem: roughly, if one side expects the distribution of power to shift in the other’s favor, the currently stronger side may not believe the other side’s promise to respect a peaceful bargain once its position improves, so the stronger side may fight now rather than live with a worse deal later. I would like an exercise where this happens to them not through lectures but through choices with points at stake, transposed to an AI race between two rival labs or two governments deciding each round how much to invest in frontier model development, safety measures, or the ability to defect. Could you design a 75-minute game, for groups of 4–5, with simple materials and a payoff structure that uses points or chips rather than money? The game should make participants feel why an agreement to slow down or share safety information does not stick when one side’s capabilities are expected to grow faster than the other’s. Then provide a debrief that maps their decisions onto the private-information, commitment-problem, and indivisibility categories, and connects the game to cases like chip export controls, autonomous weapons, or the choice to release or not release model weights. Also tell me what will likely go wrong when I run it the first time and which feature of actual AI races the game cannot reproduce.
