As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have spent the past few years interviewing working scientists and philosophers of science about replication failures, p-hacking, and the periodic calls to reform how research is conducted. The subject I keep circling back to is the strange afterlife of Paul Feyerabend's work. His critics write as though he had argued that all methods are equally good and that science is merely one ideology among others; his defenders insist that the notorious slogan that anything goes was a reductio ad absurdum aimed at fixed, universal methodological rules, and that Feyerabend's historical case studies—Galileo above all—were meant to show that the rules methodologists proposed would have blocked a major advance rather than enabled it. What puzzles me is that both sides seem to accept the textual record and the broad outline of the history, yet neither moves. I have watched this argument in print and at conferences for years, and it now looks to me less like a dispute about what Feyerabend meant and more like a collision between different ideas of what a method, or a methodological rule, would even have to do. My working hypothesis is that one side is asking a descriptive question—how do successful sciences actually regulate themselves?—while the other is asking a normative one—are there rules that scientists ought to follow because following them is what makes a conclusion trustworthy?—and that these two questions have very different standards for what would settle them. I am not confident in that diagnosis, and I want it tested rather than confirmed.

I am seeking a diagnosis rather than a survey of Feyerabend scholarship. What kind of disagreement is this, really: a verbal one about the scope of a slogan, a substantive one about whether any methodological rule can carry genuine normative force, a disagreement about which historical episodes count as evidence, or something else? Given your diagnosis, what would count as real progress—a distinction, a formal result, a historical finding, a change in how the question is posed—and is there any reason to think this particular debate could end, or is it the sort of impasse that will persist regardless of what gets published?
