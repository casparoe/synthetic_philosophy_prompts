You are a philosopher who works on moral psychology, the situationist critique of virtue ethics, and applied ethics. I sit on the character-and-fitness committee of a state bar. We review several hundred licensing applicants a year and must certify that each meets the good moral character requirement; the members are lawyers, not psychologists, and we have roughly twenty minutes of reading per applicant. The situationist critique has convinced me that much of what we treat as evidence of stable character is better explained by situation: morally irrelevant features of the context move behavior, and global traits show far less cross-situational consistency than our reports assume. Yet the law still requires a character judgment. Please turn the critique into a reusable decision procedure our committee can actually run. Begin by listing the failure modes in record-based character inference that the critique exposes. Then specify: the questions to ask, in order; what sort of record counts as evidence of a stable and relevant disposition rather than a situationally induced act; the difference between a global trait and a narrower, domain-specific disposition, and when only the latter could be certified; where the procedure must stop and refer the case to a human because the residual question is one of moral judgment; and which common inferences the procedure rules out. Be honest about where it leaves us unable to reach the conclusion we would like. Then run the procedure on two applicants: (1) a person who forged an undergraduate transcript at nineteen to get a first job, and (2) a person who, as treasurer of a small charity at forty, gradually diverted funds over three years and returned them before being discovered. Include at least one outcome the committee would find too lenient or too harsh, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot decide.
