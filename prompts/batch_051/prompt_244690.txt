ok so I keep using a distinction and want it stress-tested before I keep using it. I think there are two quite different things people call 'moral desert.' One is a claim someone has against you—the victim, the community, the state, God, whoever—so desert is always desert-from-someone, owed because of what you did. The other is more like a balance sheet: a wrong creates an imbalance in an impersonal moral ledger, and desert is just the ledger moving back toward zero. I've been calling this second one 'ledger desert.'

Here's the bit I actually want checked. I think karma, read charitably, is best understood as a theory of ledger desert rather than of punishment. If you cheat someone, the wrong isn't mainly about what the victim may properly do to you; it's that the ledger between you and them is now off, and any later cost to you—even a completely unrelated run of bad luck—counts as the debt being paid down. That's why 'they had it coming' can feel apt even when nobody intended to punish you and the victim never gets restitution or even finds out. Same thing for a wrong that makes everyone better off: on the ledger view it still leaves you in debt, even though nobody has a grievance. It also dissolves the standing problem, because for ledger desert nobody needs the authority to punish—the desert isn't a claim held by anyone.

My question: is this a coherent distinction, does it already exist under another name (it feels too obvious not to), and where does it actually break? I'm especially worried it seperates desert from proportionality and from the victim entirely, which might make it basically just-world bias with nicer branding.
