Epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness keeps tempting me and I can't tell whether the usual objections are fatal or just theater. Before I settle my view I want the checklist that any defensible epiphenomenalism has to clear to count as not self-defeating. Write the desiderata for a theory that says qualia exist but cause nothing, built specifically around the paradox of phenomenal judgement: if the pain does nothing to make me say or think that I'm in pain, the judgement is just one more physical event, and the view seems to have sawed off the branch it sits on. For each desideratum give the case or consideration that forces it, and flag the distinctions a theory has to respect — for instance between a state causing a judgement and a judgement reliably tracking that state, or between causal and explanatory relevance, if you think those come apart. Then mark which requirements are load-bearing and which are just one camp's taste. The part I most want is whether the load-bearing ones can all be met at once, or whether meeting them quietly turns you into an illusionist, a type-B physicalist, or an interactionist; if two requirements conflict, name the pair and say what each side of the trade costs. Close with a short scorecard of the standard escapes — the common-cause story, the line that self-knowledge needs no causal route, and the bullet-biter who grants we can't know we're conscious — against your list. Keep the criteria the main course.
