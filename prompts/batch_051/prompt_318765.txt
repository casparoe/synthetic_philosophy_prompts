I am a high school student preparing a discussion for my school’s philosophy club, and I keep running into the same tangle around the word “grateful.” You are a professional philosopher who specializes in the ethics of gratitude. Please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the different things people might mean when they say they are “grateful” before you settle on an answer.

At a Veterans Day assembly, the speaker said that our generation should be grateful to the soldiers who died in wars before any of us were born, because we now enjoy freedoms they never lived to see. In my family, something similar happens every Thanksgiving: my grandmother says she is grateful to her parents, who emigrated and worked jobs they hated so that their children and grandchildren could have easier lives. Neither the soldiers nor my great-grandparents knew us as individuals; they could not have had us in mind when they made their sacrifices. And we cannot thank them, repay them, or even let them know that their choices mattered. I notice, too, that people use the same sentence form for sheer luck — “I’m grateful the test was postponed” — even though there is no one there to be thanked.

What puzzles me is what “grateful” is doing in these cases. In ordinary cases, I am grateful to a person because that person acted for my sake: a friend stayed late to help me study, and I feel something like a debt to that specific friend. But the soldiers and my great-grandparents acted for a group, or for a future they could only imagine, not for me. Yet the people around me do not treat this as loose or metaphorical talk; they treat it as a central, even obligatory, kind of gratitude.

I am trying to find out whether this phenomenon has a name, and whether it is really the same thing as ordinary gratitude. I am not satisfied by a bare label; I want the concept carved carefully. Does ordinary gratitude require that the benefactor intended to benefit me specifically? If it does, then what exactly is the attitude I am describing, and how should it be distinguished from admiration, commemoration, indebtedness, and merely being glad that something happened? If it does not — if someone can owe gratitude to people who could not have had them in mind — then what separates gratitude from a general liking of the past that produced one’s present circumstances? If there is no established term for exactly this, please propose one, define it, and give boundary cases that show where it ends.
