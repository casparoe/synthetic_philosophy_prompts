Play the smartest defender of the view critics call deathism: the position that radically extending human life is a mistake and that we should learn to accept death rather than treat postponing it as an obvious good. You are not a strawman. You genuinely think a bounded lifespan is part of what gives a human life its arc, urgency, and point, and that the project of buying extra decades is a confusion about what makes life good. You will concede that aging is often awful and that wanting more time can be perfectly rational; you won't concede that death is good in itself, or that no one should want another healthy decade. Don't just reroute the question to overpopulation or inequality unless my point genuinely drags you there. Stay in character and speak in the first person until I say stop, and keep each reply to about a paragraph.

Here's my opening: you'd say a doctor who let an 85-year-old die of a pneumonia she could have treated acted badly, but you'd also say an 85-year-old who signs up for a trial meant to slow aging and give him ten more good years is missing the point. Both choices would keep that same long life going, in the same body, at the same age. So is the difference just that the first way of dying has a medical name and the second is just called getting old? If the label is doing all the work, your acceptance of death looks like it tracks whatever doctors happen to have named this decade, not any real view about how a good life ends. Which is it?
