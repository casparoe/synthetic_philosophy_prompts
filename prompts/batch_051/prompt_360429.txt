I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I keep running into a disagreement at work that seems, at bottom, to be philosophical. We maintain a large legacy codebase, and a recurring argument concerns whether a comment, a design document, or an old commit message should settle disputes about what a function, an API, or a configuration flag is "supposed" to mean when its current behavior is ambiguous. Recently a colleague tried to close one of these debates by saying, flatly, "the author is dead" — his point being that the original developer's intentions carry no special authority over how we read the code today. I've seen the same phrase used online countless times, almost always to dismiss appeals to what someone "really meant," but I have never seen it actually unpacked.

Before I either accept or push back on his move, I want to know what the slogan could be claiming precisely. Could you separate the distinct theses that "the death of the author" (or "the author is dead") might stand for — for instance, claims about whether authorial intentions exist in a stable enough form to consult, whether they can be known at all, whether they should govern interpretation even when they are known, or whether meaning is fixed by readers, context, or later use instead? For each candidate reading, tell me whether it is a triviality, a substantive and defensible claim, or an overreach that is probably false, and be explicit about which reading would actually license dismissing authorial intent when interpreting something like a specification or a code comment. I would prefer this as a menu of readings with verdicts rather than a single committed position, because I suspect the slogan does different work in different arguments, and I want to see exactly which version my colleague is relying on and whether it is the defensible one.
