Corporate consultancies and market-research firms routinely partition consumers and employees into generational cohorts—Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, Generation Z—and sell advice about what each cohort values, trusts, and wants from work or from brands. The practice treats a birth-year cohort as a robust social kind: members are said to share deep dispositions such as loyalty, a desire for feedback, digital fluency, or institutional distrust, supporting one-size-fits-all marketing and management, even though the cohort boundaries shift from firm to firm and within-cohort differences in class, region, and culture are plainly large. At the same time, many of these firms' own training materials denounce stereotyping and insist that people be treated as individuals. What social ontology does this practice presuppose? Does it strictly require that generations are real kinds with shared intrinsic traits, or is it compatible with treating them as merely useful statistical aggregates, historically constructed labels, or optional social identities? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and spell out what would have to change—in the segmentation, the claims, or the training—if the industry replaced generational realism with a thoroughly nominalist account of cohorts.
