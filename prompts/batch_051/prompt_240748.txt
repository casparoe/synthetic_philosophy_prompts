I am a working novelist, and I should like to know whether a formal result about the value of information can be turned into a procedure I can actually follow in my practice.

I half-remember a theorem, which I believe is due to I. J. Good, to the effect that a rational agent should never refuse information that costs nothing to obtain and that one is free to ignore. The rough argument is that such information can only enlarge what one knows, and one is never obliged to act on it, so it cannot make a decision worse. The principle seems directly relevant to my working life, because I make a dozen or more small information decisions in a typical week, but I have never seen it translated into a method a writer could use under ordinary working conditions.

Each week, while drafting or revising a novel, I decide such things as: whether to read a negative review or a friend's candid report on a draft; whether to research the accurate mechanics of a scene that only an expert would notice was wrong; whether to verify a historical detail I am reasonably sure of; whether to learn the sales figures for my previous novel while I am drafting the next; whether to ask another writer for an honest view of a troublesome chapter; whether to learn that a living person on whom a character is partly based has discovered the book. The decisions are frequent, the costs are chiefly time and attention, and the stakes range from a small avoidable error to losing weeks of work to discouragement or distraction. There is usually no editor, publisher, or institutional gatekeeper making these decisions for me; the choice is mine alone, and I must live with its effect on the work.

Please write a decision procedure I can run on any such case. It should be grounded in whatever remains true of Good's theorem once its idealisations are set aside, and it should be honest about where the theorem's assumptions fail for a writer. In particular, it should distinguish at least four things: the cost of obtaining the information; the cost of thinking about it once obtained; the fact that some information cannot be put out of mind even when one decides not to use it; and the possibility that information changes not what one would choose but how one feels while carrying out a choice one would have made anyway. I want the procedure in the form of ordered questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where the procedure stops with a clean yes or no, and where it must hand the decision back to judgement because the question is not one that a theorem can settle.

Then run the procedure on these three cases: (a) a candid reader's report on a finished but revisable manuscript, offered without charge by a trusted and competent reader; (b) learning the exact correct procedure for a scene that experts might notice but ordinary readers will not, where the research would take an afternoon; and (c) opening the royalty statement for my last book while I am drafting the next, when I have already resolved that the figure will not change whether or how I write the next book. Please include at least one case in which the procedure returns an answer that I, as the writer, will dislike, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do for me.
