Before I read further in the literature on conceptual engineering and ameliorative analysis, I want a specification of what an adequate theory must do, so that I can evaluate positions against it as I encounter them. Write the desiderata for a theory of concept revision: what any account of what it is to engineer a concept, and of when an engineered concept is legitimate, has to deliver. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable conditions of adequacy and which are best read as preferences of a particular camp (externalist, pragmatist, conceptual ethicist, revisionary metaphysician, and so on). If a subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, or can be jointly satisfied only on one contested reading of what concepts are, say so explicitly; that coherence question is the part I most want to see worked out.

Fix the purpose as follows. The theory should explain and guide the deliberate, normatively motivated revision of shared classificatory concepts: the kind of move at issue when a theorist proposes replacing or revising a received concept in order to better serve an epistemic, scientific, moral, or political aim, rather than merely describing what speakers already mean. It should also say how such deliberate engineering differs from ordinary semantic drift, and how the two are continuous.

I have several candidate requirements; use them as a starting point, challenge them if they are question-begging, and add what is missing.

1. The analysis/engineering distinction. The theory must distinguish the project of saying what a concept is from the project of saying what it should be, and must locate an ameliorative analysis within that space. Motivating case: a theorist who proposes a revisionary account of gender as a type of social subordination is not doing the same thing as a lexicographer recording dominant usage, and an adequate theory has to say what the difference consists in.

2. The target question. It must say what kind of item is being engineered; a word, a shared linguistic meaning, a mental representation, an abstract object, a pattern of inference, or something else; and it must not equivocate on this when facing hard cases.

3. The externalism/control problem. It must explain how a concept can be deliberately revised at all, given that what terms pick out can be fixed partly by the environment, by experts, and by history rather than by a speaker's current beliefs or decisions. Motivating cases: the chemical kind expressed by a term, the community's uptake after a learned body reclassifies a celestial body, and a stipulation that a contested social term shall henceforth be governed by new reference conditions. The theory should say what actually changes in a successful revision and what does not.

4. Topic continuity. After a revision, the theory must be able to say whether the same concept has been improved, whether the same subject matter is still under discussion, or whether one concept has been discarded and another put in its place. Motivating case: debate over whether a revised legal or social concept of marriage is still a concept of marriage at all, as opposed to a new concept with a misleading old name.

5. The normative standard. It must supply or explicitly borrow standards for evaluating candidate concepts, and say whether those standards are epistemic, political, moral, or something else, and how conflicts among them are to be handled. For example, is it a good reason for a revisionary concept of some social kind that it better reveals pervasive injustice, even if it departs from ordinary classification? The theory needs to say why such a reason is allowed to count.

6. Verbal legislation versus conceptual change. It must distinguish engineering a concept from merely changing how people talk, and from relabeling an existing distinction. It should say something about which apparently deep philosophical disputes are partly verbal, and about where that diagnosis stops being adequate.

7. The uptake requirement. It must say how much social uptake is needed for a revision to count as successful, and whether what philosophy usually revises is the shared concept itself or a proposed model for more local inquiry. This matters because a revision in mathematics or official taxonomy settles things differently from one in a contested social term.

Now the consistency part. I can already see pressures among these: requirements 1 and 5 pull against each other if describing a concept is never fully separable from evaluating it; requirements 3 and 4 pull because the more one locates the concept outside the head, the harder deliberate revision becomes and the less clear it is that the revised item is the same concept; and requirements 2 and 7 pull because abstract objects are not the sort of thing uptake can change. Say which of these pressures are real, which dissolve on inspection, and whether any pair is close to unsatisfiable. In a short closing paragraph, indicate which existing style of view; ameliorative, externalist, or one that separates the normative choice of a concept from the mechanism of making it public; is put under the most strain by the list as a whole, and why.
