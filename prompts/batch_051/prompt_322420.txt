Since I retired from a chemistry institute a few years back, I've been reading philosophy in a rather unsystemmatic way, and I keep coming back to a dinner argument I witnessed that I'd like someone to referee. Two old friends, Anita, a biochemist who spent the better part of fifteen years on a drug candidate that never made it out of phase two, and Paul, a civil engineer, got into it after she said the project wasn't wasted time. Anita's line was that the striving itself had been worth something: she'd been fully absorbed, she'd grown as a scientist, and there was something dignified in having worked that hard at something hard, even though the molecule failed. Paul said no, all of that is still payoff—skills, good memories, self-respect—so she hasn't shown that effort is valuable in itself; she's shown that effort sometimes has side effects. If the effort had produced none of those, just exhaustion and a dead end, she wouldn't call it valuable, which means the value tracks the results, not the effort.

Then it went a few more rounds. Anita said you can see the difference if you think of practising an instrument for years without ever performing: we admire that, and not only because of the skill, but because of the discipline and the way the person gave themselves over to the activity. Paul said he admires the enjoyment and the skill, and effort without those is just pain; if effort were valuable in itself, we'd have to admire someone pushing a boulder up a hill forever, and nobody really thinks that boulder-pusher's day is a good day. Anita said maybe we should take that figure seriously, that there's a dignity in wholehearted striving that our results-obsessed culture has gone blind to. Paul answered that "dignity" is something onlookers feel, and it doesn't make the struggle good from the inside, and anyway she would never advise a student to start a project with no expected outcome just for the character-building, because that would be cruel. She said that only shows our advice has been captured by funding and publications. At that point someone changed the subject, but I kept swinging back and forth and still don't know who to side with.

Could you go through this exchange move by move and adjudicate it, the way a good judge would? I'd like to know who is right on which specific point, where each of them overreaches or changes the subject, and what distinctions they both seem to be missing—for instance between effort, difficulty, and suffering, or between the value of a whole activity and the value of the effort inside it, or between how things look before and after you know the result. And please don't just declare a winner overall; I want the point-by-point verdict. Don't use headings, bullet points, bold, or tables. Just write me continuous prose, the way you'd write an email.
