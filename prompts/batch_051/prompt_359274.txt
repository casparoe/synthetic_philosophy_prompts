People in my IR class keep repeating that a territory is only actually a state once other states recognize it — that recognition is part of what makes it a state, not just a nod to a state that already exists. The rival view is that statehood is in the facts on the ground (territory, population, a government that actually governs) and recognition is just politics. I'm trying to figure out whether the first view is even testable, or just a way of talking. Design the best empirical test you can of the claim that recognition does real statehood-making work: which polities you'd compare, how you'd get around the fact that recognized and unrecognized polities differ in lots of other ways, what you'd measure (treaty capacity, passport acceptance, domestic legal effectiveness, border control, borrowing costs, whatever tracks "being a state"), and what result would favour each side. Then be explicit about what a positive result would still not settle — in particular, how we'd know whether recognition changed statehood itself or just changed the things recognition tends to unlock (seats, loans, treaties). Is the leftover uncertainty a data problem, or a sign that the claim is partly a choice about what the word "state" is supposed to do?
