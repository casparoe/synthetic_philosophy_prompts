After fourty years in the lab I'm used to thinking of myself as an evidentialist: you don't believe anything you can't support with experiment or calculation. Lately I've been reading Wittgenstein's On Certainty and I keep chewing on a passage in paragraph 4. I'm working from the German text; here it is exactly as I have it: "Zum Zweifeln fehlen mir die Gründe! Es spricht alles dafür, und nichts dagegen. Dennoch läßt sich vorstellen, daß bei einer Operation mein Schädel sich als leer erwiese." My rough translation: for doubting, I lack the grounds; everything speaks for it and nothing against it; nevertheless one can imagine that during an operation my skull would turn out to be empty.

Now here's what I want you to do. Please argue that the natural sciences, no less than Moore's common-sense judgments, rest on hinge propositions that are not themselves experimentally justified, and that this does not make science irrational but is instead a condition on scientific inquiry making sense at all. Use the passage I quoted to develop the argument. Then tell me honestly whether a working scientist should be bothered by that conclusion or whether it just says something about what "reasonable doubt" means.
