People have been arguing about the Kelly criterion for decades and neither side seems to move. The expected-utility mainstream says that maximizing expected log wealth is just one utility function among many, so it cannot be a general rule of rational choice: if your utility is not logarithmic, betting Kelly is simply not what you should do, and the appeal to the long run is either a disguised preference over wealth paths or a fallacy. The Kelly side replies that for a repeated multiplicative bet the geometric mean, or time-average growth rate, is not one preference among others: it is the quantity that separates compounding from one-shot gambles, and expected-utility reasoning goes wrong by averaging over parallel histories instead of over time. My working hypothesis is that this is not a mathematical disagreement but a disagreement about what the question is — whether the unit of evaluation is the single decision or the indefinitely repeated process, and whether norms of rationality should be defined over ensembles or over time. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is my hypothesis right, or is there a real theorem or empirical claim doing the work underneath it? And what would actually count as progress: a formal result, a sharper distinction, a change of question, or the admission that the two camps are answering different questions and should stop sharing a label?
