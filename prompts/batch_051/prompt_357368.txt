You are an expert in algorithmic fairness, in particular the formal results showing that certain fairness criteria cannot all be satisfied at once, and in translating those results into procedures that non-specialists can actually run. Think carefully. Begin by working through the main fairness criteria (calibration within groups, equal false-positive and false-negative rates across groups, and selection-rate parity), the precise sense in which they conflict, and what that conflict does and does not settle; use that to build the procedure.

I lead a fairness review team at a company that sells pre-employment screening tools to call-center and warehouse employers. Every quarter we review a new or retrained model before release; each one will rank or reject thousands of applicants, and the outputs sit next to employment-discrimination exposure, so legal and a vice president have to sign off — but only on a short written justification, not on the math. My reviewers are data scientists and product managers with no training in fairness theory, and we have about half a day per model. We can compute base rates, score distributions, and false-positive and false-negative rates on validation data.

I keep hearing versions of the impossibility results: roughly, an imperfect risk score cannot be simultaneously calibrated within each group and have equal false-positive and false-negative rates across groups when the groups have different base rates, and related results say that fixing one criterion typically moves the violation to another. I accept the results but do not know how to make them govern an actual release decision rather than just being something we cite in a slide.

Please turn these results into a reusable review procedure my team can run on each model — a checklist or flowchart in words, with the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to legal or a VP because the remaining issue is one of values rather than arithmetic. Do not write for people who will read the original papers; keep the distinctions that actually do work (what a calibrated score means, the difference between the two error types, why equal selection rates are a different demand from either), and drop the machinery. The procedure must handle these hard cases: the outcome label is itself built from supervisor ratings and may encode bias; the validation data is small or the relevant cell counts are tiny so some error rates cannot be estimated reliably; and the two groups' score distributions differ so much that no single threshold makes both error-type gaps small. For each, say what the procedure should require or record, and where it should refuse to certify the model until the measurement problem is fixed.

Then run the procedure on three worked examples:
1. A customer-service screening model where one applicant group has a much lower measured qualification rate. The model is well-calibrated, but at the business's chosen cutoff the lower-base-rate group has a false-negative rate roughly double the other group's. The product team wants to ship because the scores are "predictive," and legal has already said the numbers do not require a change.
2. The same model, but the team proposes to eliminate a selection-rate disparity by setting group-specific score thresholds for advancement.
3. A warehouse model whose "quality" label is a supervisor's three-month rating, and the team cannot supply any link between that rating and measured job performance.

For each worked example, give the path the procedure recommends, what the decision record must contain, and any conditions on approval. Include at least one case where the procedure blocks something the team wants to ship, or forces a choice they were hoping to avoid. End by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do: which decisions are irreducibly value choices, where the procedure is likely to misfire, and what it cannot guarantee legally or morally.
