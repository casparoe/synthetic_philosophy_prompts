I have no academic background in philosophy, but I have been turning over a question about art and trust, and I would like you to put on your professional-philosopher-of-aesthetics hat for this one.

The tension I keep noticing is this. On one side there is the thought that a real aesthetic judgment about a novel, a record, a play, or a film has to be earned by first-hand experience: another person's report can tell you where to spend your time, but it cannot give your verdict the same standing as the verdict of someone who has actually seen, heard, or read the thing. On the other side there is the thought that in ordinary life we sometimes get more reliable aesthetic information from a friend whose taste we know how to correct for than from our own rushed or badly situated direct experience. I want to find out how deep the first thought goes, and I want a test case that makes the second thought win, if it genuinely can.

Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. It must be one ordinary-life scenario: no drugs, no brain devices, no science fiction. Two people, A and B, form a belief about the very same aesthetic feature of the very same work — for example, whether a novel's ending is truly earned, whether a piece of music is disciplined rather than self-indulgent, whether a play is crueler than it first seems. Invent the work yourself; do not rely on any actual novel, album, or film. A has experienced the work directly and attentively, in good faith. B has never encountered it and has only careful testimony from someone else, or from a small number of people, who has experienced it. Your case must reverse the usual ranking: B's testimonial belief should come out better justified than A's first-hand belief, even though it is the same claim about the same work. Do not make A careless, dishonest, or incompetent. Do not secure the reversal by making B's informant a world-class expert while A is a novice; assume the people involved are comparable in knowledge, practicedness, and relevant taste, and let whatever advantage exists come from the structure of the situation rather than from a raw difference in skill.

Before settling on your final case, brainstorm two or three candidate scenarios and briefly say why you reject or keep each. Then present the final scenario in one paragraph. After that, run the checks. State exactly what claim both A and B end up believing, and describe the path each one took to it. Then think through the nearby versions of the situation — the small differences that could easily have flipped each person's belief — and explain why A's path is unreliable here while B's path is a reliable route to the truth. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the real work, and say what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse back into the familiar result that first-hand experience wins? Finally, give the strongest response available to someone who insists that first-hand acquaintance with a work is always required for a genuine aesthetic judgment: what would your case still fail to give B even if B's belief is the more justified one? Finish by saying whether that response saves the first-hand view, or just changes the subject from being likely to be right to something else.

I should tell you what this case is for. I am trying to work out whether the insistence on first-hand aesthetic experience is best understood as a claim about how likely a person is to get it right, or as a claim about something else — about whether the judgment is genuinely yours, whether you have earned it. Build the case as a probe for that distinction rather than as a stipulation that B wins.
