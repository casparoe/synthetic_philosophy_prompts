I'm a journalist working on a piece about mathematical structuralism, and I keep coming back to Paul Benacerraf's paper "What Numbers Could Not Be." The move that gets me is the one where he asks you to imagine two people, Ernie and Johnny, who have each learned a perfectly good set-theoretic reduction of the natural numbers. On Ernie's version, the number 2 turns out to be an element of the number 4; on Johnny's, it doesn't. They agree on every arithmetic truth, they can both count and prove everything about numbers, but they contradict each other about whether 2 is a member of 4. And arithmetic itself gives no way to adjudicate the dispute, because it never talks about membership. So Benacerraf's conclusion, as I understand it, is that numbers aren't really any particular sets—the reduction to sets is arbitrary, and questions like whether 2 is in 4 are pseudo-questions.

I'd like to give my readers a concrete example of the same conceptual issue somewhere else in mathematics, preferably something a non-specialist can follow. So: give me an example of a situation where two rival formal constructions of some familiar mathematical object agree on all the structural facts but wind up giving contradictory answers to some incidental membership or identity question. Ideally not another example about numbers-as-sets, unless it's illuminating in a new way. And then say what a structuralist should say about wether that incidental question actually has a determinate answer. I'm especially curious if there are cases where it feels harder to dismiss the incidental question than it does in Benacerraf's original 2-and-4 setup.
