I'm on the board of a small city's historical commission, the body that decides what goes on commemorative markers, what goes into the official municipal history, and how the museum labels things when the community's shared memory and the documentary record disagree. Next month we take up a case, and before the meeting I want to figure out whether our past decisions actually commit us to a principle. Treat this as a puzzle about reconstructing an implicit rule from a track record, not as a request for a theory of collective memory, and please don't give me a reading list or citations.

Three of our own precedents keep getting cited in these arguments. First, the Lantern Festival. Local memory says it began in 1902 as a banned harvest protest. We found two newspaper notices and a council ledger entry from 1898 describing something called the Lantern Fair with much the same features. We dated the festival to 1898, but the marker adds that it is locally remembered as having begun as a 1902 protest. Second, the Kiln Road mine cave-in of 1952. The miners' families remember 47 dead, and they say the company had sealed the ventilation shafts to break a strike. The written record is thin: a company log and one inspector's report, both giving 31 dead and no mention of the vents. We put 47 names on the memorial; in the archive, the official file says 31 but carries a long note on the discrepancy and on the company's interest in minimizing the number. Third, the old quarantine station, 1880s. There is a persistent local story about a doctor who let smallpox patients die in an earth pit so the disease would not reach the town. The written record is fragmentary: a ship manifest, a few burial entries, no mention of a pit. We kept the official history with the fragmentary record, and the doctor's-pit story appears in the museum only as recorded folklore, not as history.

Next months case is the 1961 Harlow Bridge bus crash. The town's memory, still kept by a few surviving child passengers and their families, is that the driver swerved to avoid a group of children in the road and went through the rail, and that the swerve saved them. The documentary record is not thin: a state police accident report, a coroner's inquest with witness statements, and the bus company's maintenance logs all say the brakes failed and the railing was already rotten, with nothing about a swerve. These record-makers look neutral, with no stake in the memory. But the event is still inside living memory, barely, and the surviving families have gathered signatures from half the town asking that the marker name the driver and say he swerved.

Act as a methodical consultant who reconstructs decision rules from case law. I can think of two or three principles that might fit our three precedents, and I suspect they come apart on the bridge case; I want you to lay out what those candidate principles are, say which one our decisions best support, and then tell me what each principle would require us to do next month. Give me your best recommendation if we want to be consistent with ourselves, identify the closest precedent, and say honestly which principle we would be adopting each way if the precedents leave it open. Also tell me what further piece of information or future case would most cleanly split the remaining candidates. Think carefully and feel free to start by brainstorming candidate rules before committing.

And please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
