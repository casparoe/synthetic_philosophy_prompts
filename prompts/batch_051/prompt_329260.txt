Okay here's a thing that's been bugging me, and I'd like you to think it through carefully like an expert philosopher who works on anthropics and population ethics. Start by brainstorming a few different assumptions the practice could be making before you settle on a reconstruction.

I spend alot of time reading effective-altruism-adjacent grant writeups, and there's a pretty standard move: an evaluator will compare two programs, one that saves or improves lives now (say, a malaria-prevention program expected to save a few thousand present-day people) and one that reduces existential risk (say, technical AI safety work with maybe a one-in-ten-thousand chance of preventing a catastrophe that would kill all future humans). They then rank the existential-risk program as overwhelmingly more valuable, because the expected number of future lives involved is so astronomically large. They do this even when the probability of success is tiny.

But then I re-read this passage in Nick Bostrom's paper "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" (2003):

"One thing that later generations might do with their super-powerful computers is run detailed simulations of their forebears or of people like their forebears. Because their computers would be so powerful, they could run a great many such simulations. Suppose that these simulated people are conscious (as they would be if the simulations were sufficiently fine-grained and if a certain quite widely accepted position in the philosophy of mind is correct). Then it could be the case that the vast majority of minds like ours do not belong to the original race but rather to people simulated by the advanced descendants of an original race. It is then possible to argue that, if this were the case, we would be rational to think that we are likely among the simulated minds rather than among the original biological ones. Therefore, if we don’t think that we are currently living in a computer simulation, we are not entitled to believe that we will have descendants who will run lots of such simulations of their forebears."

He seems to be saying that, unless we already think we're currently simulated, we shouldn't be confident we'll have descendants who run vast numbers of simulations of their forebears. But the big far-future numbers the grantmakers are multiplying by seem to depend on exactly that kind of future: trillions of biological descendants, digital minds, ancestor-simulations, the whole thing.

So here's my question: what philosophical commitments does that grantmaking practice presuppose? Specifically, what view of anthropics, of the value of simulated lives, and of how to handle expected value over an unknown future does the practice only make sense under? Which rival views does it quietly rule out? Do its parts—the tiny probabilities, the astronomical future populations, the willingness to count digital minds—presuppose consistent things, or is there a tension once Bostrom's passage is added? Try to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined. Is the practice actually forced to take a stand on the simulation argument, or is it compatible with several readings of Bostrom? And if you forced a longtermist grantmaker to take that passage at face value, what exactly would they have to change in how they do their rankings, if anything?
