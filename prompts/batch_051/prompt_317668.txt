Last spring I served on a criminal jury that hung eleven to one. I was with the eleven. The holdout and I had taken the same notes through the same testimony, and afterward I could not honestly identify any respect in which one of us was better informed, more careful, or more intelligent than the other; we had simply arrived at different degrees of confidence about the defendant's guilt. Since then I have been trying to get clear on what rationality requires in that situation, and I have built the following argument. I find its conclusion repellent, but I cannot locate the failure.

Let conciliationism be the view that when you discover that an epistemic peer — someone as well informed and as competent as you are, who has considered the same evidence — disagrees with you about a claim after the two of you have exchanged your reasoning, you are rationally required to move substantially toward their view, because their disagreement is good evidence that you have erred.

(1) If conciliationism is the correct rule for responding to peer disagreement about ordinary claims, then it applies to disputed claims generally, unless there is a principled reason to exempt a particular class of them.

(2) Conciliationism is itself a disputed claim, and its opponents are not simply uninformed: there are careful, intelligent people acquainted with the relevant arguments who accept a steadfast view, holding that one may reasonably retain one's belief in the face of peer disagreement.

(3) So, by conciliationism and (2), a reflective conciliationist who becomes aware of such steadfast peers has, on their own principles, a standing reason to move substantially toward rejecting conciliationism.

(4) This reason recurs with each thoughtful, well-informed person one encounters who rejects the view. Each such encounter rationally erodes the conciliationist's confidence in the very principle that requires the erosion.

(5) A correct rule of rational belief-revision must be stable under its own application: a rule that directs its adherents to become progressively less confident in the rule whenever the rule is disputed is self-undermining, and a self-undermining rule cannot be a correct account of what rationality requires.

(6) Therefore conciliationism is false.

I am reluctant to give up either (1) or (5). Losing (1) feels like deciding, after the fact, that the holdout's disagreement simply did not matter; losing (5) feels like admitting that rationality could rationally order its own destruction. Yet if I retain both, I cannot see how the conclusion is resisted. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would also like to know how the strongest version of conciliationism would answer this argument without simply declaring opponents non-peers or exempting itself from the rule by fiat.
