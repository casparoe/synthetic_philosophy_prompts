Okay so a friend and I spent most of Saturday night going in circles about whether James and Paul actually contradict each other on justification. He kept going back to James, I kept going back to Romans, and somehow we both ended up on Abraham, which made it worse, not better. The passage we kept staring at was the Epistle of James (King James Version, traditionally attributed to James the brother of Jesus), chapter 2 verses 21–22: "Was not Abraham our father justified by works, when he had offered Isaac his son upon the altar? Seest thou how faith wrought with his works, and by works was faith made perfect?" The problem is Paul uses the same patriarch to argue in what sure feels like the opposite direction in Romans 4, and Genesis 15:6 has Abraham's belief counted as righteousness years before Genesis 22 has him offering Isaac, so the order of events seems to matter and we couldnt agree on what to do with that.

Anyway. I teach an adult Sunday school class at my church: six weeks, about forty minutes of real discussion per week, maybe fifteen people, a few of whom can read Greek and a few of whom have never read Romans. I need to turn this argument into a course, and I dont want to waste five of the six weeks on stuff that is exegetically interesting but changes nothing for how somebody actually lives, reads their Bible, or treats other people. So here's what I want: in the faith-and-works debate between James and Paul, which questions are actually worth spending limited class time on, and in what order? For each one you'd include, tell me why it's upstream of the others, how much turns on it for a non-specialist adult in the room, and what you'd do differently in the class if the answer came out one way rather than the other. Also tell me which questions you'd deliberately skip because they're weeds or because they don't pay rent downstream, and defend that too. If you think the whole "so do they contradict each other?" framing is itself the trap, say so, and tell me which question should replace it as the spine of the six weeks. Just write it all out as continuous prose, like an email; no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
