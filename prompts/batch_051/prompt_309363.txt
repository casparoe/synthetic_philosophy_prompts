I am a novelist at work on a political satire set in a small republic two decades after it abolished competitive elections and moved to universal sortition, with nearly every public office filled by lot. A recurring in-world document—quoted with mounting disbelief by my protagonist, a former election clerk now seconded to the new regime’s audit office—is the official public guide issued by the republic’s Office of the Allotment. I would like you to write that guide for me.

Please write it as a deadpan government FAQ or information booklet for the prospective drawn citizen, roughly 700 to 900 words, in the calm, reassuring register of a genuinely published civic document: numbered sections, short definitions of terms such as “the draw” and “civic readiness,” and cross-references to nonexistent regulations. It should be legible as satire while remaining entirely straight-faced.

The target is the technocratic capture of sortition: the way a device meant to break elite capture can quietly become a new selection by merit. In this republic, the lottery has accumulated, over twenty years, a series of minimal eligibility and readiness conditions—a residency and citizenship screen, a stratified sampling algorithm, a civic-aptitude threshold, a conflicts-of-interest check, a compulsory deliberative-training module, and finally a brief confirmation interview described as purely preparatory. Each condition is individually the sort of thing real proponents of sortition defend; together they shrink the actual draw to a small, tested slice of the citizenry while the document insists throughout that selection is purely random and that the republic has no political class. I want the irony sustained in the gap between those reassurances and the eligibility sequence the guide itself lays out. The standard objections—who writes the aptitude test, which demographics predictably fail it, whether competence at a test is competence at governing, and whether a vetted lottery is still a lottery—should be visible in the piece but never announced as abstract criticisms.

The humor has to come from getting the theory right, not from mocking the idea of sortition wholesale. A political theorist who works on lottocracy should recognize, behind the exaggerations, the descriptive-representation case for stratified sampling, the accountability or sanction problem, the competence objection and the reply that someone must design any filter, and the difference between drawing a representative sample of the citizenry and selecting for competence. Please commit throughout to one definite satirical thesis rather than offering balance; the piece should have a clear point of view.

After the guide, add a short note of 150 to 200 words telling me where the piece exaggerates or leans on a contested reading of the real positions, so I can calibrate it against the novel’s arguments.
