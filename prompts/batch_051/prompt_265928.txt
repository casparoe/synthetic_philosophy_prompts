I spent the last few months managing a major-version migration of a library much of our codebase depends on, and the experience keeps looking to me like a cleaner instance of Kuhnian incommensurability than the scientific episodes usually discussed. The difficult part was never the functions that were added or removed. It was the ones whose names and signatures survived while their semantics changed: a call that was legal in both versions, and looked identical at the call site, did something slightly different in each. You could not translate the old code into the new code one function at a time, because the intermediate states were neither working old code nor working new code; the migration had to happen as a whole. The team also kept trying to adjudicate whether the old or new API was the right design in the abstract, and every argument bottomed out in facts about the surrounding ecosystem—which downstream libraries had already migrated, what the maintainers had committed to supporting, which user base was growing—rather than in a version-neutral specification of what an API should be. In Kuhn, the vocabulary that survives a revolution shifts its meaning, the two sides cannot settle their dispute by appealing to a neutral observation language, and the outcome depends on the future of the community as much as on a direct comparison of the theories. I cannot tell whether I have found a real structural parallel or am just doing the ordinary thing of calling every large rewrite a paradigm shift. I would like this checked rigorously. Is the mapping between a major-version migration and Kuhnian incommensurability deep or superficial? If it is deep, what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer, and do the software-specific mechanisms—adapter layers, running both versions side by side, scheduled deprecation periods—have nontrivial counterparts in scientific change? And what does the analogy predict that is false of actual science, and should that falsify the parallel or just mark its boundary?
