You are a grader for an upper-level undergraduate course called Representation and Writing, which treats writing systems as theories of linguistic structure. Question (20 points)

In designing or maintaining a writing system, a language community inevitably ignores many perceptible aspects of speech; a script can therefore be read as a theory of which distinctions in the language are structurally significant. With this in mind: (a) explain what it means to describe an alphabetic script as phonemic, and explain in what sense the phonemic principle is an idealization rather than a guarantee of one-to-one letter-sound correspondence; (b) give one concrete way in which English orthography departs from a strict phonemic principle and say what the departure marks instead; (c) name one non-alphabetic script and identify the level of linguistic structure it primarily encodes, being precise about what that means.

Student answer

An alphabet is phonemic because its letter units stand for phonemes, the sound contrasts that can change meaning, rather than for every surface variant of those phonemes. From this it follows that a genuinely phonemic alphabet would have to be biunique: one distinct letter for each phoneme of the language, and conversely one phoneme for each letter. That requirement is just the traditional one-sound-one-symbol ideal, and it supplies the correct test for deciding whether a script is a true alphabet. English fails that test in everyday ways: the gh in through has no pronunciation, the letter c spells /k/ in cat but /s/ in cell, and the plural suffix written -s is pronounced as /z/ after dogs. These are not mere exceptions; they show that English orthography is governed by morphological identity and word history as much as by pronunciation, which is why it is standardly called a deep orthography. For this reason English is commonly said not to be a true alphabet at all. A non-alphabetic case is Chinese: each character stands for a morpheme, the minimal unit of meaning, so the script is logographic. Because the unit encoded is meaning and not sound, Chinese writing carries no phonological information whatsoever; it operates at a purely semantic level and abandons phonological recoding during reading entirely.
