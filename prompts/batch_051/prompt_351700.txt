As a novelist, I have noticed a structural parallel between a principle I work with and a dispute in cognitive science, and I want to know whether it is deep or superficial. When outlining, I tend to treat a character's psychology as an internal model—motives, beliefs, traits—that exists before any scene and is then represented in the prose. But the characters who come alive on the page seem to think through their hands, their settings, and their interlocutors: the scene does not report a pre-existing mind so much as constitute one. This resembles the contrast between representationalist and enactivist accounts of cognition—cognition as the manipulation of internal representations of an external world versus cognition as skilled, embodied coping in which body and environment are constitutive. Please assess the mapping precisely: which elements correspond, what would have to be true of both sides for insights to transfer rather than merely borrow vocabulary, and where the structures come apart. If the parallel is genuine, do enactivist ideas such as scaffolding, affordances, or the context-sensitivity that stored descriptions handle badly carry over to narrative craft? And what prediction would the analogy make that a representationalist about character could rightly point to as false on the fiction side?
