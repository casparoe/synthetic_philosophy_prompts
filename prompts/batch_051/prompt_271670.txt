I am trying to work out what I actually think about the taxation of inherited wealth, proceeding from my reactions to particular cases rather than from any settled principle, and I would like you to help me extract the principle. My verdicts on the following cases are as stated; I offer them as data, not as conclusions I am committed to defending.

1. A couple with savings of about eighty thousand dollars dies, leaving the whole sum to their only child, who earns a modest salary and will use it as a down payment on an apartment. I regard this transfer as unproblematic and would not tax it.

2. A dentist leaves four million dollars divided equally between two adult children who are both well-paid professionals. I think the inheritance should be taxed, though not confiscatorily: the children did nothing to earn this wealth.

3. The same dentist leaves four million dollars to the same two children, but half of the estate was itself inherited by the dentist from her parents, who had built it from nothing. The children therefore receive wealth that no one now alive has earned. I find myself thinking this should be taxed more heavily than the previous case, though I am a little surprised to find that, since the beneficiaries' situation is identical in both cases.

4. A farmer dies leaving a farm worth three million dollars to her daughter, who has worked the farm alongside her for most of her adult life and could not otherwise afford to keep it. I think the farm should be exempt, or at least that the tax should be low enough, and payable over time, that the daughter is not forced to sell it.

5. A farmer dies leaving the same farm, worth the same three million dollars, to her daughter, who left for the city at eighteen and has not worked the farm since; the daughter intends to sell it to a developer. I think this should be taxed as if she had inherited cash. But I also cannot state why the recipient's way of life should matter for the taxation of the very same asset, and this is the case I keep going back and forth on.

6. A wealthy entrepreneur places fifty million dollars into a private family foundation run by her children, who draw substantial salaries and direct the grants. I do not think the transfer should escape tax merely because it is structured as a foundation, and I am unsure how much difference it makes that some of the money will be spent on good causes rather than consumed.

7. During her lifetime, a parent pays for her child's university education and gives the child one hundred thousand dollars toward a first home. I think these transfers should not be taxed, but I also think this is not necessarily inconsistent with taxing the transfers in cases 2 and 3, though I cannot yet say what does the moral work in the distinction between an inter vivos gift and a bequest.

The tentative principle I am drawn to is something like this: gratuitous transfers should be taxed only insofar as, and to the degree that, they entrench dynastic concentrations of advantage; the aim is not to tax parents' helping their children as such. I had expected my views to line up with a straightforward equal-opportunity rationale for heavy estate taxation, yet cases 1, 4, and 7 resist that, and case 6 suggests I want the rationale to reach more than the formal label under which wealth is transferred. I worry that my tentative principle is only a slogan, and I can already see that it may not fit case 5 or case 7.

Please do the following. (a) State one or more candidate principles precisely enough that each clause is motivated by one or more of my cases, and say which clause handles which verdicts. (b) Say which of my verdicts, if any, the best candidate cannot accommodate, and explain whether I should revise that verdict rather than complicate the principle. (c) Apply the best candidate to two cases I have not listed: (i) a childless person leaves twenty million dollars to a niece who grew up in poverty, and the niece immediately gives half of it to effective charities; and (ii) during her lifetime, a parent transfers a profitable family business to a child who has never worked in it, and the child immediately sells it. What does the principle say about these cases, and do those results seem right to you?
