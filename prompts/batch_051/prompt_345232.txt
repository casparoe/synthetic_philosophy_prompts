I have a rule I mostly use when a friend or a podcast tries to sell me on some new explanation for how the world works. I basically don't give it credit unless it predicts something I didn't already know. If all it does is tidy up facts I had before I heard it, I treat it as entertainment, not evidence. My reasoning is that any clever person can, after the fact, tell a story that makes three or four known things fit together, so explaining old facts is cheap. Lately though I've been reading about older science and noticing that some of the best theories made their name mostly by tying together weird stuff people already knew about: like the way Mercury's orbit was off, and everybody already knew that long before Einstein came along, but people still count it strongly in his favor. A friend pushed back that I cant have it both ways: once I know a fact, I can't go back and pretend I don't, so no theory ever gets a fair shot at predicting it for me. He says I'm just making it impossible to convince me with anything that isn't new. Now I'm torn. Is my rule defensible, or is it just a bias dressed up as carefulness? If it is defensible, is there a fixed version that keeps the protection against after-the-fact storytelling without throwing out cases like Mercury? And how should I tell the difference between a theory that merely fits old facts and one that is genuinely supported by them?
