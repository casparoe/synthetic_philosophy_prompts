I've been working on a feature for my magazine's ideas section about the rise of "existential emptiness" as a kind of folk diagnostic among young adults — people reporting a void, a sense that nothing matters, but without the classic markers of depression. In the course of this I reread Pascal's Pensées, and I was struck by the section on diversion. He argues there that people chase after war, gambling, hunting, court office, and so on not because the objects are worth having but because if they stopped to sit still in a room, they'd be face-to-face with their own nullity. He says something to the effect that men would rather be overworked than idle; give a soldier rest and he becomes miserable; the whole machinery of ordinary life exists to keep us from noticing what we already are. I'm paraphasing, but that's the gist.

I've come around to the view that the feeling of a void is not a direct perception that life lacks meaning, but a kind of withdrawal symptom: the discomfort that appears when the usual diversions stop, and that we misread as a metaphysical discovery. In other words, existential emptiness is not evidence about the world; it's evidence about our inability to be alone with ourselves. What would count against this? I want a map of the defeaters: psychological findings on boredom, meditation, sensory deprivation, cross-cultural differences in the experience of emptiness, phenomenology of melancholia, whatever. Which of these would be decisive, which merely awkward, and what would a committed Pascalian concede as genuinely counting against the view? Also, is the view too insulated — where exactly does it make a risky prediction, and does the fact that it can explain away every alternative feeling undermine it? I'd like it sorted from fatal to annoying.
