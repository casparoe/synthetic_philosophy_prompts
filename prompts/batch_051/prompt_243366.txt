In a seminar on Bayesian methods, two people disagreed about their priors over a model parameter, and a third cut the argument short by saying that the prior washes out. The intended point was not merely that the disagreement would turn out to be practically unimportant in this particular case; it was that Bayesian updating has a built-in tendency to make priors irrelevant, so that arguing about priors is in some general sense misguided. I doubt that. Taken flatly, the slogan is obviously false: a prior that rules out the region of parameter space containing the truth never recovers, no matter how much data arrives. But something in the neighborhood seems true, and presumably the person repeating the slogan did not mean the flat version. I want to know what they did mean. Lay out the distinct precise claims that the prior washes out could be making — about what exactly is supposed to wash out (the posterior mean, the full posterior distribution, posterior predictive probabilities, the decisions a Bayesian would make), in what sense of convergence, under what conditions on the prior and the model, and in what limit. For each candidate reading, say whether it is a theorem, a useful approximation, an idealization, or simply false, and whether it is a platitude or a substantive claim; and say which reading would actually license the move from the prior washes out to this disagreement about priors is not worth having.
