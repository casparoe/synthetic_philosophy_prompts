I teach first-year undergraduates and have one fifty-minute session to introduce the idea of Whig history — the tendency to write the past as a story of progress toward the present, with past actors sorted into forward-looking figures and backward-looking obstacles. I would like you to produce a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself. Plan the sequence: what conceptual distinction has to be in place before the label 'Whig history' can mean anything, and what depends on it; which single concrete historical case should carry the session, what features it must have and which features would make it misleading — and why you would pass over more familiar candidates such as the abolition of slavery or the scientific revolution; which misconceptions I should expect from students who arrive steeped in school progress narratives (I anticipate at least 'Whig history is just optimism about progress' and 'avoiding it requires the historian to be neutral or have no standpoint'), and where in the sequence each misconception should be headed off rather than corrected afterward; one closing question that would tell me whether students have understood the concept as opposed to merely learning the term; and what adjacent debates I should explicitly decline to enter at this level, such as whether present-day categories are ever legitimate in historical interpretation.
