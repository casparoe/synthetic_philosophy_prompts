You are a professional epistemologist specializing in the value of understanding and in cognitive offloading. Think this through carefully, and brainstorm your analysis before you settle on a verdict.

In discussions of whether our growing epistemic dependence on AI erodes human understanding, a familiar reassurance is that using an AI is like using a calculator: no one worries that a student who uses a calculator for arithmetic has lost an understanding worth worrying about, so we should not worry that a radiologist relying on an AI diagnosis, or a lawyer relying on AI case research, has lost an understanding worth worrying about either. The analogy is being asked to do real work here — namely, to establish that offloading a cognitive task to an AI is, in itself, no more threatening to human understanding than using a calculator is.

Audit how far this analogy holds. First, give an explicit mapping from source to target: what in the AI case corresponds to the calculator, the user, the offloaded operation, the output, the surrounding practice, and the mechanisms for catching error. Second, identify the features that make calculator use benign for understanding — for example, that the offloaded operation is narrow, transparent, mechanical, and checkable, and that it is not itself load-bearing for higher-order understanding — and determine which of these features carry over to AI use and which break. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that are real but irrelevant. Third, deliver a verdict: does the relaxed conclusion survive the audit, survive only with qualifications, or was it carried by the analogy all along? If the analogy fails in important respects, specify what a better analogy would be and what it would need to get right.
