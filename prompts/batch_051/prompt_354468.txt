I'm writing a short essay on whether a long-run prediction can be right in mechanism but wrong in timing, and a colleague handed me an argument I cannot shake. (1) Malthus's prediction is that human population, growing exponentially, must eventually outrun a food supply that grows only arithmetically. (2) Since the claim says only "eventually," two centuries of rising food per person do not refute it; the reckoning can always be placed further out. (3) Defenders of Malthus say exactly this: the mechanism is sound, it was merely early. (4) But "right but early" can rescue any failed long-run prediction — depleted oilfields, the coming socialist revolution, a date-setting apocalyptic movement — simply by moving the date. (5) A claim compatible with every finite stretch of non-occurrence, and whose defenders can always postpone the disconfirming event, is one that no observation could count against. (6) A statement that no observation could count against is empirically empty. (7) Therefore Malthusianism, so defended, is empirically empty; and by the same steps, so is every long-run claim — fossil fuel exhaustion, climate thresholds, even the eventual burnout of the sun. (8) But that conclusion is absurd. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (6), since it seems to be the line between scientific prediction and mere assertion, but if that forces me to treat the sun's burnout as empty, I need to know exactly what I'm paying.
