I recently became chair of our regional conservation commission after my predecessor resigned over a land-use fight, and that fight is now on my desk. A developer has applied to fill a 370-acre salt marsh for a housing-and-light-industrial project. The development side’s economic analysis is strong: new tax base, jobs, and flood-resilient housing on higher ground. The conservation side, however, commissioned a natural capital assessment that prices the marsh in dollars — storm-surge buffering, carbon burial in the peat, nursery habitat for the local oyster and blue crab fisheries, water filtration, and recreation visits. The report treats the marsh as a natural asset that yields a stream of services, and it concludes that the marsh’s present value exceeds what the county would gain from the development. One bloc on the commission takes the report as decisive; another bloc says the marsh has value that is not the value of any service it renders to us, and calls that intrinsic value when they are being philosophical and relational value when they are thinking of the shrimping and oystering families who have worked there for four generations.

I am not asking you to audit the dollar figures. The report’s whole method rests on the analogy that a wetland is a form of capital — a stock of natural assets producing a flow of benefits, which can be maintained, depreciated, or invested in. That analogy is what is doing the argumentative work toward the conclusion that we should deny the permit. I need to know how far it actually holds before I write the commission’s recommendation, and I want the disanalogies that matter for the recommendation separated from the ones that are real but irrelevant to this decision.

Please give me an audit of the analogy in two parts.

First, a correspondence table with these columns:
- source_term: the concept in the source domain (capital and asset accounting);
- target_term: what it is claimed to correspond to in the marsh case;
- mapping_quality: one of holds_well, holds_partially, or fails;
- imported_feature: the feature of capital the analogy quietly brings along that the marsh may lack, or vice versa;
- effect_on_conclusion: strengthens, weakens, or is neutral for using the analogy to justify denying the permit.

Include at least the customary rows — capital stock, asset, depreciation, investment, return, substitutability, and risk — plus any further rows you think cut importantly.

Second, a brief verdict structured as a JSON object with these fields:
- conclusion_survives: a boolean indicating whether the conservation conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table;
- strongest_disanalogies: a list of up to three short strings;
- better_analogy: a short string naming an alternative framing if one is available, or null;
- proper_role_of_the_assessment: a short string saying how the commission should use the report in its decision;
- confidence: a number from 0 to 1.
