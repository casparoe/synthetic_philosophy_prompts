I spend a lot of time on rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums, and at a recent meetup two people I consider sharp got stuck on an argument about whether ordinary driving is "worth it" once you do the micromort arithmetic. I'd like an expert adjudication.

The debate ran, more or less verbatim, as follows. (A micromort is a one-in-a-million chance of death.)

(1) A: "I ran the numbers. The usual road-safety figure is something like one micromort per 250 miles driven. Over 10,000 miles a year, that's 40 micromorts a year; over a 40-year driving career, that's 1,600 micromorts, which is roughly a 1-in-625 lifetime risk of dying in a car crash. That's not a rounding error — it's comparable to hazards we write regulations about. Most people who say driving is worth it have simply never done the aggregation."

(2) B: "You've aggregated your way to a scary number with the wrong arithmetic. The relevant quantity for a decision is expected cost: 40 micromorts per year times the value of a statistical life. At a VSL around nine million dollars, that's $360 a year. Almost everyone would happily pay well over $360 a year for the mobility and convenience that driving buys. So by consequentialist standards, driving is clearly worth it; you've just turned a small annual premium into a frightening lifetime sum."

(3) A: "Two problems. First, a VSL is a policy instrument estimated from people's wage–risk tradeoffs on small statistical risks; it is not the price I would pay to avoid a 1-in-625 chance of an actual, personal death, especially with dependents in the picture. Second, your expected-cost calculation treats the risk as if it were fairly priced and spreadable across many people. But the one person who dies isn't paid nine million dollars ex post, and no ex ante average matters to them."

(4) B: "That non-transferability point cuts both ways. If you refuse ex ante aggregation for catastrophic outcomes, you can't justify buying insurance or taking any risk at all, which is absurd. And your worry that your personal price on death exceeds the VSL doesn't refute the expected-value framework; it just means the cost to you is higher than $360 a year. Given the actual safety profile you mentioned — modern car, daylight driving, low-speed roads — your per-mile risk is well under the population average, which brings the expected cost back down to peanuts."

I have my own leanings, but I'd like to hear each move rated before I trust them in either direction. Please take on the role of a decision theorist with a solid grounding in risk analysis and the philosophical side of expected utility. Start by briefly setting out the distinctions you'll be using (micromorts, VSL versus personal willingness to pay, ex ante versus ex post, marginal versus average risk, and anything else relevant). Then rate each of the four moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification for each rating. Be concrete about what is sound, what is overstated, and whether either rating should shift if we grant the other party's premises. Finally, say what, if anything, both of them are missing.
