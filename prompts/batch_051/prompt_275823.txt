Epistemic status: been going around in circles on this one all week, so take the framing with a grain of salt.

I've been re-reading Taurek's "Should the Numbers Count?" and the various blog responses to it, and I'm trying to catalogue the genuinely distinct arguments for the view that the numbers do count. Setup for anyone who hasn't had it in front of them: you can save one person or five strangers, and Taurek's claim is that "one vs. five" doesnt by itself settle anything. Each individual has an equal claim on your help, so the fair move is something like a coin flip; saving the one is at least permissible even though five people are on the other side.

The arguments I already have for why the numbers should count:

1. The outcome argument. Five deaths is a worse outcome than one death, full stop, and you should bring about the better outcome.
2. The ex ante argument. Behind a veil (or when picking a principle everyone involved could reasonably accept), each of the six should prefer a save-the-five rule because it gives each person a five-in-six chance of surviving, versus the coin flip's one-in-two.
3. The tiebreak argument. Grant that each person has an equal claim; the numbers can still break the tie. When claims are equal and none is stronger than another, satisfying five of them is better than satisfying one, so the numbers matter after all.
4. The institutional argument. Whatever an individual may permissibly do when a friend is involved, impartial systems—triage, disaster response, public health—cant be run on coin flips; their whole job is to save the greater number.
5. The iteration argument. Run the same choice a hundred times. Always flipping the coin kills far more people than always saving the five, and a policy that looks fine case-by-case but awful across a run is suspect.

I keep feeling like there's a sixth argument I'm missing, but everything people throw at me turns out to be a restatement of one of these five—usually the tiebreak and outcome arguments blurring together. So: give me one more argument for counting the numbers that is not a rewording, special case, or combination of anything on this list. Then tell me honestly whether it's any good, or whether it also collapses into the five once you poke it.
