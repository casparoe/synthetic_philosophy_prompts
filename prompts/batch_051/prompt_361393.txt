Consider a stipulated version of Quinn's self-torturer. A dial with settings 0 through 1,000 controls a device that produces an electric current; setting 0 is painless, and setting 1,000 is agony. The difference in discomfort between any two adjacent settings is too small for the agent to introspectively discriminate, but the discomfort is cumulative across the range. The dial starts at 0 and can be advanced by exactly one setting at a time; each advance pays $10,000, the dial cannot be moved back, and the agent, who knows the design of the device and can keep count, may stop at any point and keep the payments. The offers are made sequentially, so at each step the agent faces only the local choice between staying at the current setting and advancing one setting for the payment; there is no binding precommitment. The puzzle is that each local comparison seems to favor advancing, while a global comparison spanning the entire range seems decisively to favor never going far up the dial, and a forced one-off choice among all settings at the outset would plainly select a much lower setting.

Draft the document that a committed Transitivity Preservatist (TP) and a committed Intransitive Permissivist (IP) could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. TP holds that rational preference is transitive and that the puzzle exposes a flaw in the agent's stepwise reasoning or in the inference from indiscriminability to evaluative parity. IP holds that rational preference can be intransitive in this kind of phenomenal-sorites case, so the agent can rationally prefer each adjacent higher setting while rationally preferring a low global setting to a high one, and the stepwise sequence can be rational even when it leads to an outcome the agent would not have chosen in a global comparison.

The document should have two parts.

Part 1: a numbered agreed-claims section. It should include only substantive claims both signatories accept, concerning the setup, the phenomenology of adjacent and global comparisons, what standard expected-utility theory presupposes, what the puzzle does and does not already establish, and any distinctions the two sides found necessary to avoid talking past each other. Distinctions likely to matter include preference versus choice, synchronic global evaluation versus diachronic local evaluation, epistemic indiscriminability versus evaluative parity, and a threshold in the underlying discomfort function versus a threshold the agent can access from the inside.

Part 2: a disagreements table with columns for the issue stated neutrally, the TP position worded as its own proponents would accept, the IP position worded as its own proponents would accept, the crux, a bearing case or argument both accept, the TP prediction, and the IP prediction. The table should cover at least these issues: whether adjacent settings must be evaluatively on a par; whether the agent errs at some particular step rather than in the pattern of aggregation; whether transitivity constrains preference, choice, or both; whether the shifting baseline can justify each step; and whether a money pump can exploit the agent. Use at least the following bearing cases, refining or replacing them only if both sides would accept the move: a forced one-off global choice at the outset; a variant in which the agent is shown a numerical readout of the setting but not of their own discomfort; and a money-pump operator who offers, after each accepted step, to trade the agent one setting downward for a small fee.

Finally, state which parts of the dispute, if any, the collaboration showed to be merely verbal, and state the residual disagreement in terms both sides would sign. Do not appeal to empirical literature or produce a reading list; the document should be fully workable from the stipulated case and the two positions.
