The standard argument that gauge freedom is mere descriptive redundancy runs something like this. Classical electromagnetism can be written in terms of potentials, but potentials related by a gauge transformation determine the same electric and magnetic fields, and the fields seem to do all the causal and observational work. Since no measurement can separate one gauge-related potential from another, taking the potentials as physically real adds structure that earns its keep in no observation and explains nothing: surplus structure. The restrained view therefore says that only gauge-invariant quantities are real, and in this simple setting that has meant the field strengths.

Now consider the quantum-mechanical Aharonov–Bohm effect. A solenoid is shielded so that the magnetic field is negligible along every path an electron can take. Yet an electron beam split and recombined around the solenoid shows an interference shift that depends on the line integral of the vector potential around a closed loop enclosing the solenoid. Potentials related by an exact gauge transformation give the same shift, but potentials with different loop integrals give different shifts, even though no local measurement of the field strength along the paths could reveal the difference.

Which premise of the redundancy argument does this development bear on? Can the argument be repaired by saying that the real gauge-invariant content is not the field strength at each point but the holonomies, or loop integrals, so that gauge freedom is still surplus structure in the precise sense that potentials with the same holonomies are redundant? Or does the effect instead show that the original line between real fields and unreal potentials was drawn in the wrong place? Say what the redundancy argument needs in order to be true, and whether it remains true once this development is taken into account.
