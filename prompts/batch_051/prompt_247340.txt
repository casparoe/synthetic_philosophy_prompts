You are an expert philosopher of physics who works on theory confirmation, the epistemology of string theory and cosmology, and the logic of underdetermination. Think through the question carefully, and begin by brainstorming possible defeaters before you organize them into an answer.

I have come to hold a position about theory choice in fundamental physics, and I want to know what would count against it. The position is this. When a theory is internally consistent, accommodates all the data we currently have, and has no serious rival on the table, then non-empirical considerations can legitimately raise our rational confidence in it. I have in mind considerations such as: the absence of any credible alternative framework; the way apparently independent lines of research converge on the same structure; the theory's success in unifying phenomena that previously looked unrelated; and the judgment that if the theory were false, this convergence would be a surprising coincidence. On this view, it can be rational to assign significant credence to string theory, or to a multiverse picture, even while its distinctive claims have not been directly tested and perhaps cannot be. I am not claiming that these considerations could outweigh a direct empirical refutation; I am claiming only that, where direct evidence is silent, they carry genuine evidential weight rather than being merely pragmatic reasons to keep working on the theory.

What would count against this position? I want a map of its exposure, not a bibliography. Please sort the defeaters into at least four grades: fatal, seriously damaging, awkward but absorbable, and merely apparent defeaters that a defender can legitimately set aside. For each, say what the world would have to look like — what observation, argument, historical case, or formal result would do the work — and how much damage it would do. I also want an honest answer to the suspicion that the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability: is there any possible state of affairs it rules out, and if not, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or does the demand for falsifiability beg the question here? A defender of the position would presumably concede some of the things you list; tell me which ones, and why conceding them does not force abandoning the view. I personally find the most persuasive challenge to be the historical one — the thought that physics has seen uniquely promising, no-rival frameworks fail before — so give that challenge the most careful treatment, but do not let it crowd out the others. Along the way, please take seriously the charge that I may be confusing reasons to pursue a theory with reasons to believe it, but do not let that end the discussion prematurely.
