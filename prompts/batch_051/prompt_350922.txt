I'm putting together a two-page handout for a rationalist reading group that has been working through memetics, and I want something more useful than the usual what-is-an-antimeme explainer. Please write a diagnostic decision tree — a field guide, not a prose taxonomy — that lets someone take a candidate idea, answer concrete questions about what happens when the idea is formulated, repeated, and received, and land on a named failure mode with a note on which kind of fix, if any, that failure mode invites.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Let the top-level splits track the stage of propagation at which the idea dies: acquisition and encoding (can a single person acquire and hold the idea at all?), willingness to transmit (do people who hold it try to pass it on?), faithful reception (do recipients accept and retain the actual idea rather than a degraded or neighboring one?), and retention over time (does the idea stay available when it matters?). Phrase each node as a question a reader can answer about a concrete idea without already knowing which failure mode it points to.

The leaves must include at least these nine: (1) naming failure, where the idea lacks a handle or its usual name points to something weaker; (2) compression-resistant complexity, where any transmittable summary is false and the full version is too heavy to travel; (3) self-concealment, where the content of the idea gives the holder a reason not to think or speak about it; (4) status-cost, where expressing the idea marks the speaker as low-status or disloyal; (5) taboo or sacred-value conflict; (6) salience failure, where the idea is stateable but does not come to mind when it is relevant; (7) receiver-sided verification failure, where hearers cannot distinguish the idea from its false neighbors; (8) package-deal conflation, where the idea is remembered only bundled with a different idea under one label; and (9) a leaf for not an antimeme after all, where the idea is simply false or not useful. Add or split leaves as needed, but land between eight and twelve leaves total and keep no path longer than five questions.

For each leaf, give the name, a one-sentence diagnosis framed in terms of the stage at which the idea dies, and one sentence on the most awkward implication — for example, whether the right response is to argue harder, rename the idea, make it cheap to signal, change the packaging, or expect the forgetting to continue and stop blaming people for it.

Also flag the pairs your tree cannot cleanly separate — I expect status-cost and taboo to overlap, since both run through social punishment, and naming failure and compression-resistant complexity to overlap, since both show up as the simple version is misleading — and say what additional evidence would distinguish them. Mark any combination of answers that no leaf occupies, with a one-sentence guess about whether that region is empty because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Use these four as test cases and say briefly where each lands: (a) a true but unflattering pattern about the reader's own group that no one in the group wants to repeat; (b) a technical result the group has independently reinvented several times because its standard name points to a different and weaker claim; (c) a self-undermining insight whose content implies that attending to it worsens judgment; and (d) a recommendation everyone endorses when it is stated but no one recalls at the moment it applies.

Keep the whole thing in American English, formal but not academic, sized for a two-page handout. Begin with the handout itself — an orientation sentence inside the handout is fine — with no preface or commentary before it.
