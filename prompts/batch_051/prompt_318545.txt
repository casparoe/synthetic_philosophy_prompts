im prepping for a small podcast interview with a guest you could call the maximin guy: he defends the hardline view that for one-shot decisions under genuine ignorance—payoffs known, but no probabilities over states, maybe not even a settled state space—the only defensible rule is maximin, full stop. he's been arguing this on rationalist-adjacent forums for years, so he has canned replies to the usual objections. write me eight to ten interview questions that actually force him off those scripts, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply he's most likely to give.

please cover at least these four pressure points, and write the follow-up against his predictable comeback:

- the pessimism objection. people say maximin would refuse a gamble that pays ten million dollars in 999,999 out of a million cases and costs you one dollar in the single bad case, because the worst case is just losing a dollar. he usually answers that this conflates risk (known probabilities) with ignorance, and that behind a real veil of ignorance you don't get to know the odds are .999999. push on where the cutoff lives: if the agent learns just a little about the probabilities, does maximin stop being rational, and what counts as knowing nothing anyway?

- the minimax regret alternative. he tends to write it off as maximin with relabeled payoffs—regret is just a payoff transformation—so it either sneaks expected-utility-style thinking back in or inherits maximin's own weirdness. press him on whether minimax regret might better capture what actually bothers people about maximin's pessimism. and have a follow-up ready for his standard comeback, which is that minimax regret makes your ranking depend on options that won't be chosen (irrelevant alternatives). something like: is that dependence actually fatal for a one-shot choice, or are we just assuming the choice set gets fixed by the problem?

- rawls versus harsanyi. he'll probably say harsanyi smuggles in the principle of insufficient reason by assigning equal probability to being each person behind the veil, and that's exactly the kind of information the veil denies. follow up by asking whether refusing all probabilistic judgment doesn't through away usable information: even under ignorance you can sometimes compare likelihoods ordinally, or at least notice dominance relations. does his maximin rule forbid using that partial information, and if so, on pain of what?

- dynamic consistency. if an agent applies maximin at each node of a sequential decision, it can end up executing a plan it would have rejected ex ante, or fail the sort of backward-induction reasoning that people expect from a coherent rule. ask whether maximin is meant as a rule for whole plans or only for isolated one-shot acts—and if it's only for isolated acts, what makes a decision genuinely one-shot in practice. maybe close by asking whether he has ever actually made a consequential one-shot life decision by literally picking the best worst case, and if not, whether maximin is a decision procedure for people or just a criterion for designing institutions.
