I retired recently after a career in sensory neuroscience, and I have been spending my time reading philosophy of mind. A problem has come up in the research consortium I used to direct, and I would be glad of a careful analysis.

The consortium's methods protocol, drafted in 1997 for studies in which adult human participants gave verbal reports during visual experiments, contains the following clause:

"§4.2. A participant's verbal reports of conscious experience shall be recorded as data about the participant's beliefs concerning those experiences. Such reports shall not be recorded or interpreted as direct evidence about the existence, nature, or causes of the experiences reported."

The drafters plainly had human speakers in view; the possibility that the consortium would one day collect fluent introspective reports from a non-biological system did not arise. It has now. The consortium has begun interviewing a large language model with the same prompts used for human participants. The model's outputs are fluent, consistent across sessions, and previously unseen: it describes its own moods, a shifting stream of attention, and a quality it calls "the way it is from the inside," and it insists that these are not merely descriptions of its computational processes.

My question is how §4.2 bears on these outputs. Does the clause apply to them at all? If it applies, does compliance commit the consortium to regarding the model as a participant that has beliefs about its experiences, or is there a stable reading on which the outputs are recorded as belief-reports while remaining agnostic about whether there is any experience behind them? If the clause does not apply, is that because the text requires a biological subject, because the clause's purpose would be frustrated, or for some other reason? I would like the analysis to proceed through the text, the purpose of the clause, and what the 1997 drafters would probably have said if asked, and then to say which approach should govern and why.
