I have been working through structural-demographic accounts of large-scale political instability — the family of models associated with Turchin's work on secular cycles — and I find their systematic virtues genuinely compelling. The models take a bewildering variety of rebellions, state collapses, and dynastic crises and show that many of them cluster in periods when a small set of measurable variables move together: popular immiseration, elite overproduction, and fiscal strain on the state. The approach is parsimonious, it makes retrodictions across centuries and continents, and it seems to yield a definite diagnostic procedure: track those curves, and you can say something useful about when a polity is entering a danger zone. I also find the advertised recent successes — for instance, the claim that this kind of model pointed to rising instability in the contemporary United States before it was widely acknowledged — hard to dismiss as pure hindsight.

And yet I cannot shake the intuition that the ontology of history is different from this. When I think about the English Civil War, the French Revolution, or the Taiping Rebellion, what seems to explain why this upheaval happened here and then is a dense tangle of particular people, texts, institutions, accidents, and decisions: a king's miscalculation, an ideology taking hold among a specific group, a bad harvest arriving in a particular year. The structural variables are in the background, but the event itself seems irreducibly contingent and singular. The claim that a model of aggregate pressures could predict or explain such an event seems to substitute a smoothed curve for the actual object of historical inquiry.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what would justify each move: what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, debunked as a product of hindsight bias or narrative preference; what would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes, or to be amended into something much weaker; and whether there is a reading on which both survive — for example, by treating the model as predicting only the background probability of instability while leaving the explanation of particular events to narrative history. Is the intuition tracking something the theory leaves out, or is it a cognitive artefact of exactly the kind cliodynamics exists to correct for? Walk me through the strongest version of each position and tell me which you would give up.
