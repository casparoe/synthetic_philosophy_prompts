I chair the Emergency Measures Review Board of the Meridian Republic, a small constitutional democracy. Under the Public Health Emergency Act, the executive may declare a public health emergency for a maximum of 90 days, and every order issued under such a declaration lapses when the declaration lapses. Any extension beyond 90 days requires an affirmative vote of the legislature. The Board reviews emergency orders and issues written recommendations to the government and the legislature; our recommendations are not legally binding, but they carry considerable weight.

During an outbreak of a severe respiratory virus, the government declared an emergency and closed the border to all entry, including by citizens abroad, with exceptions only for urgent medical evacuation. The declaration reaches its 90-day limit this Friday, and the government has announced that it will let the declaration lapse rather than seek the required legislative extension. It has already issued a new, substantively identical border-closure order under a different provision of the Public Health Act: a standing ministerial regulation-making power that requires no legislative vote and carries no fixed sunset. The new order takes effect the moment the old one lapses. The government says the move is lawful and necessary, citing the risk of imported variants; opposition legislators and civil-liberties groups call it an end-run around the legislative check and argue that, whatever its legal status, it is an illegitimate exercise of emergency power. Hospitalizations have fallen for five consecutive weeks, and the health ministry's own risk assessment describes the situation as markedly improved but not yet safe.

I need the Board's written opinion to circulate before our Friday meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action: that the new border-closure order should stand, that it should lapse, or that it should continue only under specified conditions, with those conditions set out precisely. Give the full reasoning for your recommendation. Engage honestly with the strongest argument for whichever position you reject, and take seriously the possibility that a measure may be lawful in every technical sense and still lack legitimacy, or that lawfulness and legitimacy may come apart in more than one direction. Do not reduce the question to a point of statutory construction, and do not simply restate the statute.

The opinion should be written as continuous prose, the way one would write a considered letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold text, and no tables. The bottom-line recommendation should nevertheless be unmistakable from the prose.
