In my political philosophy reading group, the argument between prioritarianism and telic egalitarianism has stalled for several weeks, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a winner. Everyone agrees on the facts of the cases. Everyone agrees that leveling down—making the better-off worse off while leaving the worse-off exactly as they are—is not all-things-considered better. And everyone shares the same underlying concerns: a concern for the worse-off, and an unease about the idea that equality could be good when it benefits no one. Yet no one has moved.

Here is the shape of the standoff. The prioritarian says: a change that is better for no one is not better in any respect; the fact that a levelled-down distribution is more equal cannot make it better, even pro tanto. Everything we rightly feel about the worse-off is captured by weighting benefits by how badly off the beneficiary is, with no separate value of equality doing any work. The telic egalitarian replies: inequality is non-instrumentally bad, so the levelled-down state really is better in one respect—more equal—while still being worse overall because someone has been made worse off. The objection is not a neutral datum; it bites only if one already accepts a person-affecting restriction on 'better,' and that is exactly what is in dispute. When the prioritarian answers that a value which floats free of anyone's interests is no value at all, the egalitarian replies that the extra weighting of the worse-off is itself an unexplained compromise unless it is grounded in the badness of inequality—and the loop closes.

My working hypothesis is this: the two sides are largely answering different questions under the same heading—one asking what we owe to persons, the other asking what states of affairs are impersonally good—and the real load-bearing premise is a person-affecting restriction on goodness that is never made explicit. But I am not sure whether that diagnosis is right, or whether it is simply a polite way of saying the egalitarian is losing.
