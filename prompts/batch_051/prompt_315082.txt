ok so before I read one more attempt to make karma into a respectable theory of moral desert, i want the requirements list, not the scorecard. like, if someone claims the cycle of rebirth (or the universe, or cosmic law) gives people what they deserve, what does that theory have to do before it counts as an actual theory of desert and not just a vague law of moral cause-and-effect? for each requirement i want the motivating case or consideration, and a judgment on whether it's negotiable or a genuine adequacy condition. some of the ones i'm already losing sleep over:

- proportionality: there's a huge gap between a careless remark and a genocide. does the theory have a non-arbitrary metric for the karmic "size" of an act, and does it track intention, act, or consequence? what happens when those three come apart?
- identity across the gap: if the punishment lands in another life, the person being burned doesn't remember the crime and may not even recognize the punishment as theirs. at what point does "you deserved this" stop meaning anything? there's a distinction between one person getting what they deserve and a causal chain that just makes some future someone miserable, and the theory can't collapse it.
- the initial condition problem: any first life has to start with a stock of karma, but then there's an unearned starting position, or an infinite regress. either way the desert part seems to break at the very beginning.
- apparently misallocated suffering: a child born with a fatal disease, the victims of one random landslide. is each of them exactly getting their due? if so, the theory has to explain how a landslide is individually calibrated to dozens of separate ledgers. if not, what kind of desert is it?
- transfer and collective stuff: if merit can be transferred or shared (ancestors, rituals, families, whole nations), is anyone still getting what they deserve, or are we just moving desert around until it isn't desert anymore? which is it?
- the enforcement problem: usually desert is owed by someone or something. does an impersonal law of karma actually count as justice, or just as natural causation with a ledger attached? and if you add a divine administrator, does that fix it or smuggle in a different theory?
- the non-ad-hocness constraint: an unobservable ledger that can be invoked after any event to rationalize any suffering is a just-so story, not a theory. what evidence or constraints would keep it honest, and is this requirement negotiable? (i suspect this is the one that knocks out the most versions, but i want your read.)
- expiation and the "debt" picture: if bad karma can be burned off by penance, then desert starts to look optional, like a fine. if it can't, then moral change becomes irrelevant to what you deserve, which is weirder still. which horn should a good theory take?

then tell me which of these, if any, can't be jointly satisfied — that's the part i most want — and whether the problems come in pairs (identity + memory, proportionality + non-arbitrary metric, etc). keep the list tight; i'd rather have eight requirements with real cases behind them than twenty slogans. and if you have to use a word like "atman" or "non-self," say what work its doing, because half the arguments i've read fall apart when the author switches from a soul view to a no-self view without noticing.
