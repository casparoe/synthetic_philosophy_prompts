I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science working on Bayesian epistemology, and I've been neck-deep in the objective-versus-subjective Bayesian literature for months because my advisor said my dissertation chapter can't just "pick a side and move on." Here's the thing. The subjective side—de Finetti, Savage, and most people who treat probability as rational degree of belief—says any probabilistically coherent prior is rationally permissible, and what matters is how you update. The objective side—Jaynes, with Laplace and Keynes in the background—says no: some priors are rationally required, not just allowed. If a quantity lies on an interval and you know nothing else, a uniform prior is mandatory, and if you know constraints like a mean or variance, you should pick the maximum entropy distribution that satisfies those constraints. These positions have been going at each other since the 1950s and the arguments now feel completely frozen. The objectivists keep appealing to symmetry and entropy as if they're obvious constraints on rational belief. The subjectivists keep answering with partition-dependence cases—Bertrand's paradox, the cube-factory case where a uniform prior over side length and a uniform prior over face area or volume can't all be right—and with the point that choosing which constraints to hand to MaxEnt is itself a judgment call. The objectivists then say that's fine, because the constraints are part of the stated problem rather than a hidden prior smuggled in. Nobody moves.

My working hypothesis is that part of the impasse is a conflation: "a rational prior" might mean either (i) a prior a rational agent is not forbidden to adopt, or (ii) a prior rationally mandated as the unique representation of the evidence. If that's right, then this isn't one disagreement but two, and each side has been scoring points against the other side's sense of the phrase. But I can also see the substantive reading: maybe there is a fact of the matter about whether symmetry and maximum entropy pick out uniquely rational priors, and the objectivists just haven't managed to prove it without sneaking in extra assumptions. What I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is the stalled debate verbal, substantive, methodological, or some mix, and what exactly are the two sides disagreeing about when they actually say what they say? Then, given that diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress—a theorem, a sharper distinction, a different question—or whether we should just expect the impasse because the question is ill-posed. Be honest rather than diplomatic. And make the very last line a one-sentence version of your diagnosis, beginning "Diagnosis in one sentence:".
