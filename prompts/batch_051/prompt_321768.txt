I've been reading the back-and-forth in the rationalist/EA world about "systemic change" vs. "marginal interventions" for years and I've ended up with a set of verdicts that don't sort cleanly into either camp. I'd like you to help me figure out what principle, if any, is actually doing the work in my judgments.

Here are my verdicts on some concrete cases:

1. Insecticide-treated bednets vs. a project to "strengthen the health ministry" in the same country. I'd pick bednets every time. The ministry project feels like a theory of change with too many soft links, and I suspect the money mostly gets absorbed.

2. A campaign to phase out leaded gasoline in a country that still uses it vs. a project testing and cleaning up lead in a few thousand homes. Here I'd go with the phase-out campaign, even though it's exactly the kind of un-RCT-able, policy-level, "systemic" thing I rejected in case 1. The difference is probably something about the lead being a single point of failure, but I'm not sure that phrasing holds up.

3. Unconditional cash transfers vs. funding a multi-year effort to build local civil-society organizations that are supposed to hold the government accountable. Cash, easily. The civil-society thing seems to have a ten-step theory of change with no measurable intermediate checkpoints and I just don't trust it.

4. A campaign for one specific policy change, like a bilateral labor-mobility agreement, that has a clear expected-value model behind it vs. cash transfers. Here I'm honestly not sure. It's policy work and I can't run an RCT on it, but if the EV model is decent I might fund the campaign. This feels inconsistent with cases 1 and 3 and I can't articulate why its different from the health-ministry thing except "specificity."

5. Buying carbon offsets vs. funding advocacy for carbon pricing or a clean-electricity standard. I'd fund the advocacy, even though it's diffuse and hard to measure, because offsets feel like they mostly move accounting around. But by the logic I use elsewhere, this should be my least favorite kind of thing.

6. Bednets vs. early-stage research on a malaria vaccine. I'd probably fund the vaccine research, despite it failing almost every measurability test I usually apply, because the upside is enormous and the bottleneck is a technical one rather than a social one.

The closest principle I can extract is something like: prefer the intervention whose theory of change has the shortest chain from my dollar to a concrete outcome, unless the chain passes through a single well-defined bottleneck I can actually move, like a specific vote, a specific chemical being banned, or a specific technical milestone. But I worry that's just a summary of my cases rather than an explanation of them.

Can you state a precise version of the principle (or a small set of candidate principles), test it against these six cases, and tell me which of my verdicts it can't accomodate without gerrymandering? Then apply the resulting principle to two cases I haven't made up my mind about: (a) funding a campaign to ban a specific pesticide vs. distributing safer alternatives, and (b) funding a generalist think tank that might, over decades, shift policy across many unrelated issues.
