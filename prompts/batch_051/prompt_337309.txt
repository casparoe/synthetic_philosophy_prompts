I have been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for years, and I have watched the back-and-forth about Lucas and Penrose without ever feeling that the anti-mechanist argument was put in its strongest form. Here is my own compression of it. It seems to prove too much — or maybe nothing at all — and I cannot tell which.

(1) Let M be any deterministic computing machine. There is a consistent, recursively axiomatizable extension of first-order arithmetic, call it F_M, whose theorems include every arithmetical sentence M will ever assert, no matter what input it is given.

(2) For any such F_M, Gödel's first incompleteness theorem supplies a fixed point: a Π1 sentence G(F_M) equivalent, over F_M, to the statement that G(F_M) is not provable in F_M. Since F_M is consistent, F_M cannot prove G(F_M); and since G(F_M) says exactly that it is unprovable in F_M, this failure to prove it is what makes G(F_M) true.

(3) A competent human mathematician who is given the specification of F_M and a proof of Con(F_M) recognizes G(F_M) as true, by exactly the reasoning in (2).

(4) Therefore, for every machine M there is an arithmetical truth that a human can recognize and M cannot assert. Human mathematical insight outruns every machine.

Here is what bothers me. Every step of (3) after the phrase "a proof of Con(F_M)" is mechanical: Con(F_M) → G(F_M) is itself a theorem of arithmetic, so any program that can assert Con(F_M) can derive G(F_M) in a single step. The entire non-mechanical burden is carried by the human's supposed ability to supply Con(F_M) for arbitrary F_M. Now apply the argument's own premise (1) to the human: if the human brain is a deterministic physical system, there is an F_H whose theorems include every arithmetical sentence the human will ever assert. For the argument to show that humans outrun machines, the human must be able to supply Con(F_H) from resources that (1) has already declared finite and capturable. If the human escapes by reflecting on F_H and moving to a stronger system, then the machine can be given the same reflection rule, because "from a system and a proof of its consistency, derive the system's Gödel sentence" is an effective procedure. So the argument either proves that no finite rule-following system can do what reflection-capable reasoners plainly do — which is too much — or it proves nothing about the difference between humans and machines.

I suspect the fault lies somewhere near the slide from "M's outputs are contained in F_M" to "M is confined to F_M," or in treating a proof of Con(F_M) as a free input whose origin the argument never explains. But I cannot pin down which numbered premise fails, under what reading, and what each side pays to repair it. Be explicit about the epistemic gradient here: the incompleteness theorems and the derivability of Con(F_M) → G(F_M) are settled mathematics, but their consequences for minds are genuinely contested, and I would rather see a contested resolution flagged as contested than presented as the consensus. If forced to choose, I would like to keep the naturalist picture that there is no principled boundary between human reflection and machine reflection — but I do not want to keep it by fiat.
