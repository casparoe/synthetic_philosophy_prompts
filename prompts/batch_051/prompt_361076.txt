I am currently taking a seminar on climate ethics and collective responsibility, and I have been thinking about the inefficacy objection to individual action. I want to separate two structures that collective harms can take. In aggregative harms, each individual act contributes a small expected increment to the harm (an extra mile flown, an extra portion of beef), so the standard objection is that the increment is negligible. In gated harms, the harm is realized only if enough individual acts occur to cross a threshold, and an individual act matters mainly by changing the probability or margin of the threshold being crossed (an election, a signature count, a social norm that tips once enough people visibly comply).

Please sort the following six replies to the inefficacy objection into three tiers: (1) holds up well under this distinction, (2) holds up under one structure but becomes strained under the other, and (3) weak or question-begging for both. Give one sentence for each reply explaining its tier. The replies are: (a) expected-utility / expected-marginal-impact; (b) expressive or participatory value; (c) shared responsibility or complicity in a jointly caused harm; (d) virtue or integrity arguments ('I don't want to be that kind of person'); (e) universalizability or rule-consequentialist generalization; (f) redirecting effort from individual consumption toward structural or political action.

In particular, I want to know whether the aggregative/gated distinction is doing real work: please flag any reply whose tier depends on a contestable handling of the distinction, or whose placement changes if the distinction collapses. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the tier list itself — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Finally, identify the placement you are least confident about, and say what argument, or what empirical fact, would change your mind about it.
