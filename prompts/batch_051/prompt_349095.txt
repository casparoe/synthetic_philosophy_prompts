I've been collecting my own jury verdicts on when ambiguity in a language is a genuine defect and when it's just something a language lives with. I want to know if there's one principle behind my verdicts, or whether I'm just matching vibes to cases. Here are my verdicts; treat them as mine, not as obviously right.

1. bank (riverbank / financial institution). Completely fine. Never once thought of it as a problem. If someone proposed disambiguating it, I'd say they were nuts.

2. bimonthly (twice a month / every two months). This one I genuinely avoid. I think it's defective and I'd rather write out "twice a month" or "every two months" every time.

3. inflammable, which we've mostly replaced with flammable on warnings. The replacement was correct. A word that can be read as either "burns easily" or "won't burn" on a warning label is worse than defective; it's dangerous. But I don't think this generalizes: I don't want to purge all ambiguous words.

4. run (the verb with more senses than I can count). Totally fine, almost charming. It doesn't feel like a defect even though it's the most ambiguous word I can think of.

5. sanction (to authorize / to penalize). In a statute or a contract, I think this is a real drafting defect, and a court should pick a reading rather than leave it to context. In casual speech I don't care.

6. pitch (tar, throw, musical note, sales talk, angle). Fine. The senses sit in such different settings that I can't imagine actual confusion.

7. John told Bill he was wrong (ambiguous pronoun). In careful prose I'd call it a mild defect and edit it; in live conversation I think it's excusable, since stress and gesture usually resolve it.

8. I didn't leave because I was angry (scope ambiguity). Feels the same as 7 to me in writing: a careful writer should do something — add a comma, rephrase. In speech it's fine.

So my verdicts seem to point at something like: an ambiguity is a defect exactly when the two readings are both plausible in the same context and the stakes of picking wrong are high. But then why is run fine and bimonthly not? Run has way more readings, but context always seperates them, I think. Meanwhile bimonthly only has two and they collide constantly. Is that the whole principle, or do I have to give up some verdict to keep it?

Give me the principle, stated precisely, and then test it on cases I haven't listed — maybe cleave (split / stick together), sanction in ordinary speech, French teacher (teacher of French / teacher from France), biweekly, and tone (musical sound quality / attitude / muscle tension / color shading). Tell me which of my verdicts the principle can't accomodate, and whether that means the verdict or the principle should go. Also — I want to know where you're shaky — say what would change your mind about the principle you offer: which new case or argument would make you drop it, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.

Format: lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the principle, or the claim that no single principle captures my verdicts — then the reasoning. No preamble, don't restate the question.
