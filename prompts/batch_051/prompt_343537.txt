Here's the question:

Question 4 (10 points). (a) State the independence axiom of expected utility theory in your own words. (b) Set out the standard two-problem version of the Allais paradox and identify the choice pattern that is anomalous. (c) Explain as precisely as you can which condition of expected utility theory this pattern violates, and say whether the pattern could instead be accommodated just by choosing a suitable non-linear utility function for money.

Here's the student's answer:

(a) The independence axiom says that if a person prefers lottery P to lottery Q, then adding the same common lottery or common branch to both shouldn't reverse that preference. More formally, if P ≻ Q, then for any lottery R and any probability p between 0 and 1, pP + (1−p)R ≻ pQ + (1−p)R. The motivation is that the common part will pay out exactly the same whichever option you pick, so it should cancel out.

(b) In the Allais paradox people first choose between A: $1m for sure and B: 89% of $1m, 10% of $5m, 1% of nothing. Most pick A. Then they choose between C: 11% of $1m, 89% of nothing and D: 10% of $5m, 90% of nothing. Most pick D. The anomalous pattern is A and D. The key observation is that problem 2 is problem 1 with the common 89% chance of $1m in both options replaced by an 89% chance of nothing.

(c) The pattern violates the sure-thing principle rather than the independence axiom strictly speaking, since the common branch has objective probabilities and independence is about acts under uncertainty. Either way it shows expected utility theory is descriptively wrong somewhere. But I don't think the paradox forces us to abandon independence. A concave utility function for money can make the sure $1m better than the risky B in the first pair, and in the second pair the same concave function can still make D better than C because D has the higher expected dollar value and the risk is not overwhelming. So the choices can be made consistent with expected utility after all; what the paradox really shows is that people fail to maximize expected monetary value, not that they violate the axioms.
