I am a doctoral student in Chinese philosophy, and this semester I am teaching a 75-minute discussion section for an undergraduate lecture course on East Asian ethics. The section has about eighteen students, mostly non-majors, and the room has movable chairs, a whiteboard, and enough open floor for small groups to work separately. We reach the Daoism unit in three weeks, and I want one session that does not begin with a lecture or a text. Before the students hear the term wu-wei, I want them to have felt the problem the term is meant to address: that deliberately aiming at effortless, uncontrived, spontaneous action often produces the opposite—self-consciousness, stiffness, contrivance—so that the attempt to be spontaneous can be self-undermining, and an explicit instruction like to relax or to act naturally can make the thing it commands harder to do.

Please design a runnable classroom activity for this session. It should have precise rules, roles, a timeline, and a materials list I can prepare in under an hour with ordinary classroom supplies. The activity should be engineered so that students run into the paradox through what they do, not through an explanation: there should be some moment where the effort to perform effortlessness, or the monitoring of their own spontaneity, visibly changes what they are doing or how it feels. I have a half-formed idea—splitting the room into two groups, giving both the same innocuous task, and telling only one group to perform it as naturally and effortlessly as possible, then comparing self-ratings—but I suspect that version announces the variable instead of letting it emerge, and I would like you to improve it or replace it with something better.

The session should build toward a debrief in the last twenty minutes. In the debrief, we should first name the paradox the students have just inhabited, then map what happened to the distinction between first-order action and second-order self-monitoring, and finally connect that structure to wu-wei and to the Zhuangzi's stories of skilled activity and spontaneity. I will supply the textual excerpts myself, so you should not quote or cite texts; your job is the design and the debrief structure.

Please also include a short facilitator's note on predictable failures—for example, students treating the task as a joke, refusing to take an obviously contrived exercise seriously, or reporting success to please the instructor—and how to handle each. And be explicit about which feature of wu-wei a classroom simulation cannot reproduce, so I know what the debrief must supply verbally rather than experientially.
