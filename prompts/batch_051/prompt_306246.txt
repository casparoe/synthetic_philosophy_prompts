I have been arguing with a colleague about whether a person can own an idea in itself, as distinct from owning the expression of an idea or a legal right in it. I have been defending a rough account: S owns an idea I only if (a) S was the first to articulate I in a determinate, communicable form, not merely to have an inchoate hunch; (b) S's skill, effort, or judgment played a substantial role in producing I; (c) S is in a position to exercise practical control over whether others use I, such as by keeping it secret, imposing conditions, or transferring it; and (d) the relevant community treats S as the owner of I, for example by coming to S for credit, permission, or compensation.

Here is a case that seems to sit at the edge of all four conditions. A novelist keeps a public blog of fragments from abandoned projects. In one entry she sketches a conceit she never develops: a blind beekeeper who learns to track people moving around her farm from the vibrations their footsteps send through the hive boxes, and she notes that this could ground a domestic noir in which the beekeeper grows suspicious of her brother after noticing the rhythm of his approach to the hives. Years later, a screenwriter — who had read the blog but no longer recalls doing so — writes an acclaimed film about a blind apiary manager who uncovers a theft within the family by reading hive vibrations. Confronted with the archived post, he agrees he must have absorbed the premise, but insists he invented it independently, and no one disputes that the screenplay's language, characters, and plotting are his work. The only dispute is over the central conceit. Does the novelist own the conceit? Work through my four conditions honestly, say where they underdetermine the verdict, and then say whether the case shows that one of the conditions is not necessary for owning an idea, or whether ownership of ideas is the wrong category to bring to a case like this.
