In our moral philosophy seminar this term we are considering what adult children owe their parents. The tutor has so far declined to state her own position: she dispenses verdicts on cases and expects us to work out the principle behind them. The one general idea she has allowed us to discuss is a version of the friendship model of filial obligation, according to which what a grown child owes a parent is not the repayment of a debt but whatever care and concern the relationship as it now stands would warrant, much as between friends who share a long history. She has, however, pressed us repeatedly to notice where that model fits her verdicts only awkwardly, and she has refused to tell us whether she thinks a parent's sacrifices matter independently of the present relationship.

Her verdicts so far include the following.

1. A daughter's parents were affectionate and paid for her university education, and she and her mother still speak every week. When the mother later needs months of home care, the tutor says the daughter is under a serious obligation to help, and that refusing because the work is tedious would be wrong.

2. A father abandoned the family when the child was an infant and contributed nothing afterwards. Thirty years later he asks for money. The tutor says the son owes him nothing beyond the ordinary duties owed to a stranger in immediate danger.

3. An adult son lives abroad but remains in weekly contact with his father, who was a steady, affectionate but ordinary provider. When the father asks for help with a modest debt, the tutor says the son owes some help, though only of a kind that fits the relationship they actually have.

4. A daughter's mother gave her the material basics and nothing more, was neither cruel nor generous, and now exchanges only holiday cards with her daughter. When she asks for help in old age, the tutor says the daughter's obligations are thin: light practical assistance at most, not substantial care.

5. A son's parents were generous and loving, and he remains close to them. When they ask him to help them move house and settle into a new city, the tutor says he ought to help despite the inconvenience, because doing so is part of the relationship.

Put aside for the moment what the correct moral view is. What I want is a prediction of my tutor's verdict on this new case, and the principle that earns the prediction.

A daughter's father paid for an expensive education and gave her substantial financial support into her twenties, but he was cold, controlling, and at times cruel, and she has had no contact with him for six years. He is now disabled and asks for regular practical help, while making clear that he expects no reconciliation. The tutor has not commented on a case that combines considerable material sacrifice with an adult relationship the child has deliberately ended.

Would she say the daughter owes him anything more than the minimum due to a stranger? What about a second new case: an adult son is now very close to his father, who was largely absent during his upbringing and spent little money on him, though not maliciously, and the father now needs care?

I am less interested in a bare guess than in the reasoning. Give me the candidate principles that fit my tutor's five recorded verdicts, and say where each candidate fits least well. Then set out what each candidate predicts for the two new cases, indicate which of the two new cases does the most work in separating the candidates, and give your best guess about what the tutor would say, with the reasons for preferring one candidate over the others and an honest statement of which principle her verdict would commit her to. Then name the further case you would most like her to decide in order to separate whichever candidates remain. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which additional verdict from her, or which different reading of one of the five recorded cases, would be most likely to make you reverse your prediction, and where you take your reconstruction of her implicit view to be most fragile. Please write your answer in British English.
