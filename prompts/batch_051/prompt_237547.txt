In preparing a lecture on the common prior assumption, I have been unable to defuse an argument that seems to show the assumption is not a mere modeling convention but a requirement of rationality. It runs as follows.

Suppose Ann and Ben have fully exchanged their evidence and arguments, and suppose further that they agree about how any possible piece of evidence bears on the proposition p: neither thinks the other is ignoring or misreading evidence. Ann assigns p probability 0.7 and Ben assigns it 0.2. Then:

(1) Since no difference in evidence or in the evidential bearing of facts remains between them, the residual difference in their posterior probabilities must be traceable to their priors.
(2) A prior is, by definition, a probability assignment made before the agent has received any evidence.
(3) Where no evidence has yet been received, no consideration can make one prior more reasonable than another; any choice among priors is an arational starting point.
(4) If a belief differs from another's only because it issues from an arational starting point, then at least one of the two beliefs is not rationally held.
(5) Therefore, rational agents who share all their information cannot disagree, and the common prior assumption is rationally mandatory.

The conclusion seems to prove too much: it would make every persistent disagreement among well-informed, sincere scientists or philosophers evidence that someone is being irrational. Which premise or step should go? Explain why the rejected premise initially looked compulsory, and say what a subjective Bayesian and an objective Bayesian each have to give up, or how each should rework the argument, once the diagnosis is in. Does anything of the common prior assumption survive the criticism, or should it be abandoned?
