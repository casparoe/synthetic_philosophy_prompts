Here's an asymmetry I can't shake. When nurses at a hospital all refuse to work until the place raises their pay, that's a strike, and most people treat it as a legitimate exercise of the right to organize. When hospitals in a region all agree not to raise nurses' pay above a certain ceiling, that's wage-fixing, and most people treat it as illegal collusion. But both are just one side of a wage negotiation acting as a group instead of competing as individuals, and in both cases the group is withholding what the other side wants in order to force a better price. I want the strongest principle that makes the first fine and the second not. Then test it honestly: does the principle survive when the "workers" are already well-paid anesthesiologists with a local lock on a scarce skill and the "employers" are small rural clinics barely staying open? If it doesn't, say what should give way — the special status of labor, the ban on employer collusion, or how we draw the line between them.
