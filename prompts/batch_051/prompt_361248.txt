I'm trying to pin down a double standard in how we explain the Holocaust's killers versus its rescuers, and I'm not sure whether to give it up or keep it. The killers—men who shot civilians at close range for months on end—get an almost entirely situational story: ordinary men, peer pressure, conformity, alcohol, careerism, gradual brutalization; the upshot is that an uncomfortably large fraction of us would've done the same in that spot. The rescuers—people who hid Jews while putting their own families at risk—get a character story: unusually empathetic, principled, formed by particular families or values, as though only a special kind of person could do it. I'm not asking whether the acts are morally equivalent—they arent. I'm asking whether any principle justifies explaining evil by situation and goodness by character, or whether the asymmetry is just self-flattering (it lets us think we wouldn't kill but also couldn't rescue). Test the strongest candidate principle against nearby cases—say, a soldier who refused orders versus a neighbor who denounced a hiding family—and tell me which half of the asymmetry, if any, survives.
