I've been reading around in the sovereign immunity literature, and I keep finding the same standoff. One side treats the maxim that the king can do no wrong, and its modern statutory descendants, as more than a rule about remedies: the state is a corporate fiction of a special kind, not the sort of entity that can bear moral predicates, so the idea of the state itself doing wrong is a category mistake. The other side treats that as mystification: the state acts through officials, intentionally harms people, and when it refuses repair it wrongs them in the ordinary sense, so immunity is just a policy choice about when and how to compensate. The debaters have traded these points for decades without moving. Here is the passage I keep coming back to, from Blackstone's Commentaries on the Laws of England, Book I, Chapter 7, where he quotes Finch: "the king's prerogative ftretcheth not to the doing of any wrong." I suspect the debate is stalled partly because the two sides are not answering exactly the same question—one is doing metaphysics of collective agency, the other is doing normative theory of redress—but I am not sure that fully explains the impasse. Diagnose the structure of this disagreement: is it verbal, substantive, or two debates running under one name, and what would actually count as progress?
