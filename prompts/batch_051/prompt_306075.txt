English grammar instruction standardly presents the past tense as a productive rule—attach -ed, with its three phonologically conditioned pronunciations—and treats "irregular" forms (go/went, sing/sang, keep/kept, be/was) as exceptions to be memorized whole, usually as a list. A learner who produces "goed" or "runned" is corrected as having misapplied the rule, not as having failed to recall a stored form. Yet the exceptions are not a random residue: they fall into recurring sub-families (sing/sang/sung alongside ring/rang/rung; keep/kept alongside sleep/slept) and cluster among the most frequent verbs in the language. Historical linguistics operates with a different default: the Neogrammarian tradition treats sound change as mechanically regular, referring apparent exceptions to analogy, dialect borrowing, or the interference of other regular processes. On that view, "went" is not an exception to the -ed rule but the continuing old past of "wend," absorbed into the paradigm of "go" once "go" lost its earlier past tense.

What view of the nature of grammatical rules does this pedagogical practice presuppose? Reconstruct its implicit theory of the rule/exception contrast, and identify which rival views it quietly rules out. In particular: does the practice strictly presuppose a dual-mechanism account of morphology, on which productive forms are computed by a default rule while irregular forms are stored as listed items, or is it equally compatible with a single associative system in which -ed is merely a high-type-frequency pattern? Under what conception of a rule can a rule have lexical exceptions and still count as a rule? Does the historical linguist's redescription, on which apparent exceptions are the outputs of further regular processes, undermine the rule/exception contrast or merely relocate it? Finally, say what would have to change in the practice—in the diagnosis of errors, the presentation of paradigms, and the use of the words "regular" and "irregular"—if the presupposed model were replaced by one on which suppletion, ablaut classes, and affixation are all rules of differing scope, with no privileged architecture of one default rule plus one list of exceptions.
