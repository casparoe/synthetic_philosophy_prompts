I am a PhD student in political philosophy working as a TA for an undergraduate course on political obligation and civil disobedience. Below are a short-answer exam question and a student answer. Grade the answer out of 10 and provide the specific comments that justify that grade; I want a point-by-point assessment of the argument, not a general impression. Return exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion; item 1 should state the grade, and each of items 2–5 should address a distinct point in the answer.

Question: In a state that is reasonably just by liberal-democratic standards, is there a general content-independent obligation to obey the law? In answering, (a) distinguish such an obligation from the state's right to enforce its laws, (b) explain at least two candidate grounds of political obligation, and (c) state what the most plausible of those grounds implies about when civil disobedience is justified.

Student answer: The duty to obey the law and the state's right to enforce the law should be kept apart: a state may be justified in coercing compliance with a particular rule even if its subjects lack a standing duty to obey every rule, and a subject may have a duty to comply with a law that the state would be wrong to enforce. The fair-play account of political obligation holds that those who voluntarily accept the benefits of a cooperative scheme incur a duty to bear its burdens; since nearly everyone receives the protection that the state provides, nearly everyone owes it compliance. A second candidate is Rawls's natural duty of justice, according to which we owe support and compliance to just institutions that exist and apply to us. So in a reasonably just democracy, citizens generally have a content-independent duty to obey even individual laws that are foolish or mildly unjust. Civil disobedience is therefore best understood as a case in which the underlying obligation is absent: when a law is seriously unjust, the political obligation lapses entirely, which is why the act is justified rather than merely excused.
