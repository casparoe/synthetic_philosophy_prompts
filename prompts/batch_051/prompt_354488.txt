Question: In the Meno, Socrates gets Meno to agree that for getting to Larissa, a true opinion about the road is no worse a guide than knowledge. But Socrates still says knowledge is tied down and is worth more than true opinion. Explain Plato's grounds for ranking knowledge above true belief, and say whether the Larissa point undercuts the claim that knowledge is more valuable.

Student answer: Plato's Meno is officially about whether virtue can be taught, but in the last part it turns to why knowledge is better than true belief. Socrates grants that true opinion and knowledge are equally good guides for action: someone with a right opinion about the road to Larissa gets you there just as well as someone who knows it. So practical success can't be the difference. The difference Socrates gives is that true opinion has to be tied down by a reasoning out of the cause; otherwise it runs away, like the statues of Daedalus if they aren't fastened. Since the tie is a justification, Plato is basically defining knowledge as justified true belief.

The point of the tie is stability. A true opinion about the road might get talked out of someone by a clever sophist, whereas someone with knowledge keeps hold of it. That is why knowledge is worth more: not because it gets you to Larissa more efficiently, but because it stays put. But here's the problem: if a true belief gets you to Larissa exactly as well, then the extra value Plato points to is just reliability over time. That's a practical value, not really an epistemic one. So the Larissa point does undercut him. I think Plato probably means the tie makes the belief more likely to be true, but that can't be the story either, since a true opinion is already true. So the dialogue never gives a satisfying reason why knowledge is worth more.
