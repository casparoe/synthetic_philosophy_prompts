Working title: "Choosing an Emotion Regime, Not an Emotion."

Notes. The standard way of putting the question asks which moral emotion, guilt or shame, is the better one to have, and whether a culture can choose to favor it. I think this framing is doubly misleading.

First, the familiar contrast is usually drawn in terms of what each emotion is about: guilt responds to a bad act — I did something bad — while shame responds to a bad or exposed self — I am something bad, or I am seen as something bad. On that contrast guilt looks clearly superior: it is local, revisable, and oriented toward repair, whereas shame is global, identity-involving, and prone to withdrawal. I argue that this apparent superiority is an artifact of looking at isolated episodes in already-individuated agents. Once emotions are viewed as distributed across persons, practices, and time — as an emotion regime rather than an episode — guilt and shame turn out to be much closer in function than the standard contrast allows, because both require an audience, both encode social standards, and both can be recruited for reparation or for humiliation.

Second, I suggest that the more useful unit of moral improvement is not the emotion type but the regime that couples emotion to institutions. A regime includes: which failures and violations trigger which emotions; who is entitled to express them and to whom; which responses such expression licenses (forgiveness, repair, ostracism, punishment); and which narratives a community uses to make sense of lasting failure. Guilt and shame can each be calibrated well or badly by such a regime. A well-run shame practice can track specific deeds rather than identities, while a badly run guilt practice can congeal into a permanent identity of self-accusation. In such cases the usual evaluative ranking reverses.

Third, on whether a culture can choose: the familiar objection is that emotions are too spontaneous to be chosen by culture. I think this is overstated. Cultures do not select emotions directly. What they select, through accountability practices, repair rituals, norms of exposure and privacy, vocabularies of moral failure, and the design of legal and administrative procedures, is the scaffolding that makes certain emotions more accessible and more precisely targeted. Through that scaffolding, a culture can favor a regime in which the dominant response to wrongdoing is an act-focused demand for amendment (what we call guilt) or one in which it is a status-focused exposure of inadequacy (what we call shame). But choosing the scaffolding is not the same as choosing the emotion, and the distinction matters because the same scaffolding can be inhabited by different moral psychologies.

The intended conclusion is that the question "which is the better moral emotion?" should be replaced by "which emotion regimes should we build into our institutions, and on what evidence?" The answer is unlikely to be all guilt or all shame, but a mixed regime whose components must be justified piecemeal.
