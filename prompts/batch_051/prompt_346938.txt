I've been trying to get my views on complicity into some sort of order before I have to present them to my ethics reading group, and every time I write them out they look like they were assembled from different people. The whole thing started after a friend asked whether buying from a fast-fashion brand is "supporting" its labor practices, and I realized my answers to a half-dozen related cases didn't seem to be coming from one view. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Buying from a company that uses sweatshop labor isn't complicity, because my individual purchase changes nothing — the factory doesn't add a shift because I bought one more shirt. Causal inefficacy gets me off the hook.

(2) But working as a bookkeeper for that same company is complicity even if I'd be replaced by tomorrow and the wrong would go on exactly as before, because in taking the role I'm lending my labor and professional standing to a wrong-making enterprise.

(3) Voting for a candidate whose platform includes a policy I think is seriously unjust isn't complicity in that policy, since my single vote almost certainly won't decide the election; but going door-to-door for them is complicity, because there I'm causally boosting the whole ticket.

(4) I don't think you need to intend the wrong, or endorse it, to be complicit — the bookkeeper who privately loathes the sweatshops is just as complicit as one who doesn't care.

(5) But I also think expressive endorsement is a seperate, extra source of complicity: if I buy the shirt specifically to signal that I'm fine with the labor practices, that's complicity, even though the purchase is still causally inert by the standard in (1).

(6) I think benefiting from a wrong — say, inheriting money that came from slavery — isn't by itself complicity; it grounds special obligations to do something about the wrong, and you become complicit only once you start declining those obligations.

(7) And I'm suspicious of theories that draw a sharp line between "tainted" and "complicit." I think complicity comes in degrees and cases shade into each other.

Does this set actually hang together? I can feel that some of these pull against each other, but I can't tell which are real conflicts and which only look like they should be, and I'd rather not find out live in front of the group. I'd like an audit rather than a lecture: go through the commitments, say which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what has to give and what the cheapest revision costs. Then give an overall verdict on whether the set is jointly tenable and which single revision would clean up the most.

Format: return a JSON object with the following fields:
- "genuine_tensions": array of objects, each with "pair" (list of the two commitment numbers), "severity" (integer 1–10, 10 worst), "why_they_conflict" (string), "what_has_to_give" (string naming the commitment(s) and the cheapest revision), and "cost_of_fix" (string).
- "apparent_tensions": array of objects, each with "pair" (list of numbers), "why_they_seem_to_conflict" (string), and "why_they_dissolve" (string).
- "overall_verdict": string.
