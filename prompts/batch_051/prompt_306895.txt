I've been reading the later battles fought in your name. Two schools, one taking a man called Calvin as its teacher and the other taking a man called Arminius, both claim you agree with them; the fight keeps reducing to a question about alternative possibilities — on your account, could the person to whom God gives grace have done otherwise than persevere, or than fall away? A philosopher friend gave me a case he thinks settles part of this. Jones has decided to vote for a proposal to enlarge the city's public hospital. Black, a neuroscientist, wants exactly that, and has secretly implanted a device that will intervene and make Jones vote for the proposal if Jones ever begins to form an intention to vote against it. The device never fires: Jones votes as he had already decided, on his own. So Jones could not have done otherwise, but the vote came from his own settled will.

So here is my opening question. Would you say Jones freely voted, and is praise- or blameworthy for the vote, even though he couldn't have done otherwise? And if so, does that give you an answer to the Pelagians when they say that if grace makes the movement of the will certain, the movement is not really the person's own — or does the Jones case lead somewhere different from your case about grace?
