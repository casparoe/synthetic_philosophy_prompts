I'm a novelist, and I've been reading around in Nishida and the Kyoto School because I wanted to understand something about why a scene can feel present on the page before you can say whose experience it is. The term that got me is pure experience: experience as it is before it splits into a self over here and a world over there. As I understand it, that split is something reflection adds afterwards; the underlying fact is a single, seamless field that precedes subject and object. From the logic-of-place side, I gather nothing exists as a freestanding individual first, independent of everything else; individuals get carved out inside a place (basho), and the ultimate place is not another thing at all but something like an ungraspable field of absolute nothingness in which both subjects and predicates show up. As a description of how first drafts feel, this made a lot of sense to me.

But it led me into an argument that seems to prove too much, and I cant find the exit. (1) The most basic experience is pure experience, with no subject-object split. (2) If that is so, then the distinction between a character and the things that happen to her is not in the basic experience itself; it is imposed later by reflection. (3) A normal narrative sentence--the studio flooded, Lena crossed the bridge--is true only if it picks out a subject as a self-standing individual and predicates something of it. (4) But by the logic of place there are no self-standing individuals; individuals and their properties are both posited within a place, and the place itself is not one more individual. (5) So the ordinary sentence's grammar--subject over here, predicate over there, the place left out entirely--misrepresents the real structure of things before it says a word. (6) Therefore no ordinary narrative sentence is ever literally true; at best it is useful falsification, and fiction is just the honest version of that. (7) But I would also call the sentence my studio flooded during the storm last year true, full stop, not useful fiction, and it has exactly that subject-predicate shape.

(6) and (7) cannot both be right, and I would rather keep (1) than give up on pure experience--its the one piece of philosophy that has actually changed how I write. So where does the argument go wrong, and what do I pay for rejecting whichever step it is? I want the diagnosis, not just the answer: why did the mistake look solid, and which of my early premises would be the least costly to drop if I had to drop one.
