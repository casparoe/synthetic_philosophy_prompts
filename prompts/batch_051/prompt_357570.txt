I just came home from a long argument with a friend about whether Lakatos's picture of mathematics as quasi-empirical has any real bite. His line was that Proofs and Refutations describes only the rhetoric mathematicians use once they already know where a result is going, and that the standard example of Euler's polyhedron formula never shows the body of mathematics changing under pressure from a counterexample the way the natural sciences change under pressure from observation. I think one properly engineered case can move the disagreement off this stalemate, which is why I am asking for a case built to order rather than a historical example.

Please construct a short, self-contained episode in the development of some elementary piece of mathematics — fictional but realistic in tone, and explicitly not Euler's polyhedron formula or any actual named theorem — in which a conjecture is stated, a proof is offered, and a counterexample appears. The episode must be engineered so that all of the following hold when it is analyzed with Lakatos's distinction between global and local counterexamples: (1) the counterexample is a local counterexample but not a global one, refuting a step in the proof while leaving the conjecture itself untouched; (2) the proof hides a substantive lemma, not just a slip, and the lemma is one a reasonable mathematician, once it is made explicit, would want the theorem to absorb; (3) monster-barring — redefining terms to exclude the offending case by decree — is clearly the wrong response, while lemma-incorporation, building the newly explicit lemma into the conjecture, produces a genuinely better theorem; and (4) the episode resists the formalist charge that this is mere definitional clarification, because the refined theorem says something mathematically new rather than merely re-describing the old domain. Then do the verification: state the conjecture, sketch the proof, name the hidden lemma, give the counterexample, and show step by step why each of (1) through (4) comes out true. Close by saying honestly which structural feature of the case does the work, what would happen to the case if that feature were removed, and how a formalist critic would attack the episode. The case is for a mixed reading group of philosophers and first-year mathematics students, so the mathematics should remain genuinely elementary — finite graphs, small sets, arithmetic, simple combinatorial games — while still being substantial enough that the proof is not trivially circular. No bibliographies or citations are needed; the episode should stand on its mathematics alone.
