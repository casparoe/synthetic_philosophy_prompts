I've been talked into a view and I want to know how exposed it actually is. The view is that, on balance, habitat destruction tends to reduce wild animal suffering rather than increase it: an intact ecosystem mostly produces enormous numbers of short, painful lives, with huge clutches, near-zero juvenile survival, and death by predation, parasitism, starvation, or exposure. Clear or drain that land and you lower the number of awful lives it keeps generating. I'm not asking what we should do about this; I'm asking what would have to be true for the claim itself to be wrong.

Map its exposure for me. What observable evidence would decisively count against it, and what would be merely awkward? What does the view predict about populations, death rates, and welfare in degraded versus intact habitat that the opposite view doesn't? It treats at least these as settled — that wild animal lives are, on average, bad overall; that shrinking a population lowers total suffering instead of just concentrating it among survivors in worse conditions; and that the land isn't simply converted into new ways of producing suffering — so for each, what would the world have to look like for that assumption to fail? Are the defenders' escape routes live options or just logically possible? And be honest about wether the view risks anything at all, or whether it's been insulated by qualifications; if it has, tell me if that insulation is a problem. The evidence I'd personally find hardest to dismiss is field data on what actually happens to the survivors in the months and years after a habitat is fragmented or destroyed.

Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
