I've been rereading Crime and Punishment and got stuck on a line from Peirce's The Fixation of Belief: "Few persons care to study logic, because everybody conceives himself to be proficient enough in the art of reasoning already. But I observe that this satisfaction is limited to one's own ratiocination, and does not extend to that of other men." It feels like a description of Raskolnikov to me. He thinks his own article about the extraordinary man is airtight reason, but hes constantly irritated by other people's sloppy reasoning—Razumikhin, Porfiry, the painter, everybody. I want to think about the novel through Peirce's four ways of settling opinion (tenacity, authority, a priori, science). Which one is Raskolnikov actually using? Is his confession a moment of real doubt breaking through, or does he just trade one method of fixing belief for another? And does Dostoevsky show something about belief that Peirce's categories miss—maybe about guilt, or about how a belief can keep its hold on someone even after the reasoning fails?
