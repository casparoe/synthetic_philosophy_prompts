I'm in a course on nineteenth-century political thought and we just did Mill's Subjection of Women. I keep noticing a move in the 'nature' arguments he's trying to dismantle, and I don't have a name for it. The pattern goes like this. Someone says a traditional arrangement fits women's natural temperament—women are naturally nurturing, naturally more emotional, that sort of thing. Then when someone points out that girls are handed dolls and praised for caregiving while boys get mocked for crying, the person will admit that yes, some of what we observe is artificial. But then they immediately say that underneath all that distortion there's a real natural temperament, and they describe that real nature as exactly the traits they started with. Any counterexample from another culture or period gets waved off as interference or corruption, but the same kind of interference never seems to disqualify the behavior they wanted to call natural in the first place. I get that this is related to the appeal to nature, but it feels more specific: these people admit you can't just read nature straight off observed behavior, they just reclassify the observation as 'not really natural' whenever it points the wrong way, with no independent rule for what counts as distortion. Does that two-step have a name of it's own? And how is it different from ordinary scientific talk where you also discount some behavior as nonrepresentative of an underlying disposition—like a linguist ignoring performance errors? I'm not after just a label; I want to know where the line is between a legitimate idealization and this thing, whatever it's called.
