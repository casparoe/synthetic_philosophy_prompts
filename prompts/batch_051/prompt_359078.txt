I'm preparing a seminar on Merleau-Ponty's philosophy of the body, and I want a critical reading of the film The Diving Bell and the Butterfly through the lens of the lived body and motor intentionality. By motor intentionality, I mean roughly this: before any explicit deliberation, the body is already aimed at and reaching toward a world of possibilities — there is a habitual, pre-reflective sense of what one can do, skilled movements are organized by the goal they solicit rather than by a sequence of commands, and the surrounding world presents itself as a field of solicitations for action. The film concerns Jean-Dominique Bauby, who after a stroke is locked-in, able to move only one eyelid, and dictates a memoir through a partner-assisted alphabet. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Each point should identify a specific moment or recurring structure in the film where the lived body and motor intentionality appear, break down, or get reorganized — candidates include the blinking alphabet, Bauby's remembered and imagined bodily life, and his relationship to the inert body he now observes as if from outside — and say what that moment adds to, complicates, or resists in Merleau-Ponty's account. I want a reading faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the film to an illustration of the theory, and it should not force the theory onto the film.
