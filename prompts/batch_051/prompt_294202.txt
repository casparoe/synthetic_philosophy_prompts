Last month in my school's policy club, we discussed a proposal to let libraries digitize out-of-print books when the rights holder cannot be located. After the meeting, two members, Elena and Devin, kept arguing about a concrete case closer to home. A teacher at our school uses an old mathematics-problem generator made by a small company that dissolved seven years ago. The program no longer opens on newer machines because it cannot contact the company's licensing server, and the only way to keep it usable is to patch out that check. One member of the club wrote such a patch and posted it in the class group. Here is how the argument went.

(1) Elena said the patch was plainly acceptable. The company no longer sells the program, no one has been deprived of a sale, and the only actual consequence is that a useful piece of software keeps working. You cannot wrong an owner who is no longer there.

(2) Devin replied that a company's dissolution does not turn its software into public property. Copyrights are assets, and they pass to creditors, buyers, or heirs; someone may well own the program even if that person is hard to identify. Patching and sharing it without permission is still using someone else's property.

(3) Elena answered that if "someone may own it" were enough, almost no orphaned work could ever be used, because one can always imagine a hidden owner. In this case the licensing server is dead, which makes the program inoperable unless someone patches it. A rule that leaves useful things permanently unusable for the sake of owners who neither sell nor maintain them is too strong.

(4) Devin said that "cannot be found" is not the same as "does not exist." The genuine uncertainty about whether a rights holder exists is a reason to be cautious, not a reason to take the software. Patching it and distributing the patch is not just failing to find the owner; it is overriding the owner's rights because finding them would be inconvenient.

(5) Elena replied that this places the burden on the wrong party. The default rule treats every use as infringement unless the user can prove the owner consents, which protects merely hypothetical owners at the expense of actual users. The right question is whether there is a real person who is made worse off; if not, calling it a rights violation does not establish that anyone was wronged.

(6) Devin answered that the same logic would allow moving into an empty house because the owner cannot be located. An absent owner still owns the house, and absence does not transfer title. The same holds for an absent rights holder.

(7) Elena said the house is different because a house is rivalrous: occupying it would deprive the owner of its use. A copied program leaves no one with fewer copies or less ability to sell later. If the owner returns, they are in the same position they were in while the program sat unusable.

(8) Devin said that is too quick. A rights holder who returns with a plan to relaunch the program would find the market already gone, because the users now have the patched copy. More generally, allowing copying whenever a creator is absent weakens the entire system of copyright; people could simply wait for small developers to fail and then take their work.

They asked me which of them had the better of it, and I could not sort it out. Please adjudicate the disagreement move by move. Rate each of the eight numbered moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall about this particular case, and explain what important distinctions or considerations both participants are missing.
