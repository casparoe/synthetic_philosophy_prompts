We treat two structurally similar situations differently, and I want to know whether that difference can be defended. When a system has a short underlying rule and its behavior can be derived by a calculation much shorter than the process itself, we call that derivation the explanation, and anything longer feels like a failure to understand. But some systems governed by simple rules are computationally irreducible: no shortcut calculation is known, and none may be possible, so the only way to discover what they do is to run the process step by step. In practice we are not consistent about how much that should worry us. Meteorology is an accepted science whose best account of a storm's path is the run of a large atmospheric model, and "the model run says this will happen" is treated as a satisfactory state of the art rather than as a confession that weather remains unexplained. Yet if a historian said the best account of why a particular war began is a step-by-step simulation of the relevant agents, with no compressed story about incentives, ideology, or contingency, most of us would say the war had been simulated, not explained. Likewise, "run the model and the crash emerges" strikes many people as dodging the request for an explanation of a market crash in a way that running the weather model does not. Is this asymmetry—accepting irreducible step-by-step accounts in some domains while still demanding compressed stories in others—justified? Give the strongest principle you can find that would justify treating these cases differently, test that principle against nearby cases such as a traffic jam, a protein's folded shape, and the pattern produced by an iterated cellular automaton, and then take a definite, committed position: keep the asymmetry, resolve it in one direction for all domains, or revise the underlying notion of explanation that creates it.
