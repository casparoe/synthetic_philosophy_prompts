At my discussion group last week, two members argued at length about whether there is a level of suffering so bad that no amount of pleasure could compensate for it. I am reporting their exchange as faithfully as I can and would like an adjudication of who is right at each step.

One of them, Rhea, defended a view she called lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. As she set it out, the view has three commitments. First, a negative-utilitarian starting point: unit for unit, suffering counts for more than happiness, though pleasure can still compensate for lesser pains. Second, pleasure and pain are commensurable — they can be ordered on a single scale of value. Third, there is a threshold of suffering such that any suffering at or above that threshold is lexically worse than all pleasure and all lesser suffering combined: no amount of the latter, however large, can outweigh one instance of the former.

Malik attacked the view in stages.

Malik: You are trying to have it both ways. If pleasure and pain really are commensurable — if they sit on a single scale — then any finite pain can in principle be balanced by some sufficiently large amount of pleasure. A lexical threshold, however, says certain pains can never be balanced by anything. That is simply to say those pains are not commensurable with pleasure after all. So which is it: one scale, or an absolute prohibition bolted onto it?

Rhea: Those are not the same thing. Commensurability only requires that any two items can be compared as better, worse, or equal; it does not require that every item has a price in the other. A lexicographic ordering — the way words are ordered in a dictionary — is a complete ordering in which one dimension dominates all differences in the next. You can say one torture is worse than one headache, and two headaches are worse than one, without saying any number of headaches equals one torture. Comparability holds; substitutability fails.

Malik: But then your view says one case of suffering just above your threshold outweighs the happiness of every person who will ever live, and the prevention of every lesser pain there will ever be. If you had to choose between sparing one person a threshold-level pain and sparing a billion people a lesser pain, you would spare the one. That is not a moral view; it is an obsession with the worst case, and it licenses ignoring the rest of morality.

Rhea: The alternative is that enough pleasure, spread over enough people, makes a world containing torture better than a world without it. That is what you get if you keep substitutability all the way up, and I find it far worse. As for ignoring lesser pains: I am stating a ranking of outcomes, not a decision procedure. In a hospital, admitting that one cardiac arrest is lexically more urgent than any number of splinters does not mean the triage nurse should never treat splinters. Under uncertainty, many small complaints are evidence about where a catastrophe may be developing, and policy is built around expected effects, not around the certainty that two outcomes are directly competing.

Malik: Even granting that, the threshold has to be drawn somewhere, and pain varies along a continuum. By your own account, a pain a hair's breadth below the line can be outweighed by enough pleasure, while a pain a hair's breadth above it cannot be outweighed by anything. But there is no moral difference between two pains separated by so little. An arbitrary line is doing enormous normative work. Worse, since any real judgement of intensity can err in either direction, you can never know, in precisely the cases that matter most, whether the line has been crossed — so the view gives no usable guidance where it is supposed to be needed most.

Rhea: This is the heap-paradox pattern applied to a moral boundary, and it would disallow every threshold we actually rely on: the age of majority, a lethal dose, the legal category of serious bodily injury. A vague boundary does not show that the two ends of the spectrum are no different, and it does not show that the distinction is arbitrary. As for guidance: if you are anywhere near the line, and the cost of crossing it is maximal, the sensible policy is to err heavily on the side of caution. On my view, vagueness makes caution more important, not less.

Malik: But notice what you have done. The threshold is not discoverable in the commensurable scale you began with; it is a rule you imported — a side-constraint saying certain well-being comparisons may not be made, however the total adds up. So why call this negative utilitarianism? You have smuggled a deontological ban on torture into consequentialist clothing, and you owe an account of where the threshold comes from and why it binds.

Rhea: A threshold is not a rule against an act; it is a claim about the structure of value itself. The scale below the threshold is ordinary; above it, the ordering is lexical; and those joints are part of the evaluative facts, not an external constraint. A deontologist forbids the act of torture whatever the value architecture says; I deny that the value architecture has the trade-off structure you assume. The burden is actually yours: you are assuming the only way to relate two magnitudes is substitution, and that needs an argument.

That was roughly where they left it. I genuinely cannot tell whether Malik's objections dispose of the view or merely show what the view costs. Please adjudicate step by step: who is right about which specific claim; where the two of them are using key terms such as commensurability, comparability, and substitutability in different senses; and what, if anything, both parties are missing. Are there positions available to Rhea that Malik's objections never touch? Adjudicate the arguments as reported, without appeals to named philosophers or citations. Please answer in British English.
