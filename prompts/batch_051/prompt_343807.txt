I am a PhD student in political philosophy, and I recently read a piece in a general-interest venue that summarises luck egalitarianism in a way I suspect is unfair, though I cannot pin down exactly where it goes wrong. The author's account, in my paraphrase, runs as follows. Luck egalitarianism is the view that inequalities are unjust precisely when they are attributable to luck rather than choice: people who end up worse off through no fault of their own—because of their genes, an accident, or the family into which they were born—are owed compensation, whereas inequalities that result from voluntary choices or freely accepted gambles are entirely just, however large they become. The view is supposed to capture two widely shared convictions at once, namely that victims of brute luck deserve redress and that competent adults should bear the costs of their own decisions. The author then treats the position as refuted by its own implications. First, it would permit society to abandon the destitute whose plight stems from imprudent choices, since on its own terms they would have no claim on anyone. Second, the distinction between choice and luck on which the entire theory depends cannot be drawn, because every choice is itself the product of genes, upbringing, and circumstances; in the end everything is luck, and the theory collapses into an unworkable demand for strict equality.

I am particularly suspicious of the biconditional framing and of the treatment of the harshness objection as decisive, but I would like a careful audit rather than my own hunches. Please go through the summary sentence by sentence, distinguishing what an informed defender of luck egalitarianism would accept from what is a common simplification, a subtle conflation, or an overstatement, and then say whether either objection survives once the accurate parts have been corrected.
