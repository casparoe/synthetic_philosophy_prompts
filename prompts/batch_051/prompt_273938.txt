In the second book of Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding, in the chapter on perception, Locke inserts a problem that he says was put to him by William Molyneux. Since I have only an old and unmodernized text at hand, I will paraphrase the passage rather than quote it directly. Molyneux asks us to suppose a man who has been blind from birth and is now an adult, and who has learned through touch to tell apart a cube and a sphere made of the same metal and of nearly the same size, so that by touching them he can say which is the one and which is the other. The man is then made to see. Molyneux asks whether, before touching them, the man could by sight alone distinguish the two and say which is the cube and which is the sphere. Locke reports the answer as no, and the reason he gives is that the man has had experience only of how such shapes affect his touch, but not of how those same shapes affect his sight, so his newly acquired visual ideas would not carry with them the tactile ideas he has learned to associate with them.

If the task is the second, then does Locke's negative answer show something about the nontransferability of sensory ideas between sight and touch, or only something about the poverty of the visual information available at a single moment? Could the man perhaps see that one object is rounded and the other angular, without thereby knowing which one is the shape he had always called a sphere and which the one he had called a cube? Please work through the passage and explain what exactly the newly sighted man is being asked to do, and what the structure of the question would have to assume about the relation between visual and tactile ideas for Locke's answer to follow.
