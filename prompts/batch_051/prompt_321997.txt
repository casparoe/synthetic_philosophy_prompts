I am a PhD student in political theory working on the governance of climate interventions, and I serve as the student member of my university's responsible-research committee. At our next meeting we must rule on a proposal from the atmospheric science department for a small stratospheric field experiment: releasing roughly two kilograms of calcium carbonate aerosol into the lower stratosphere from a high-altitude balloon, with the stated aim of measuring plume dispersion and validating models relevant to stratospheric aerosol injection as a possible form of solar geoengineering. The investigators argue that the release is orders of magnitude smaller than volcanic and aviation inputs, that it poses no meaningful environmental risk on its own, and that ground-based modeling cannot answer the plume-dynamics questions the experiment targets. A substantial bloc on the committee wants to reject it, maintaining that even a tiny experiment helps normalize a technology whose known dangers outweigh its narrow scientific value: moral hazard that weakens mitigation efforts, the risk of termination shock if large-scale injection were ever begun and then abruptly stopped, and the absence of any legitimate institution to decide whether such a technology should ever be used. Some members also argue that a single university has no standing to authorize an intervention, however small, in a shared global system, while others reply that the committee is already the closest thing to legitimate oversight this proposal will get and that treating a two-kilogram release as a step toward deployment falsely equates an experiment with a policy choice. Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions; if you recommend conditions, specify them concretely and tie each one to a specific worry the committee has raised. The memo must give the committee a defensible, actionable bottom line and must engage the strongest consideration on the losing side with genuine force rather than merely restating the other camp's view.
