I'm giving a seminar paper next month arguing for the procreation asymmetry — roughly: there's a moral reason not to create a life that will be miserable, but no symmetric reason to create a life that will be happy, even a very happy one, and even if the child would later be glad to exist. I'm not defending full anti-natalism and I'm not saying having kids is bad; the paper is narrowly about the asymmetry itself, and I'm treating it as a stubborn feature of ordinary moral thought rather than deriving it from some tidier principle. The audience is a philosophy department with two consequentialists who work in population ethics, a Kantian who keeps insisting that existence itself can't be a benefit because benefits are only for beings, one grad student who has read all the Benatar and won't let go of "coming into existence as harm," and maybe seven other people who don't do ethics but will smell any inconsistancy a mile off. My argument in the paper is basically: (1) commonsense judgments show the asymmetry across a range of cases — we don't fault the childless for not creating a happy person, but we do fault people who knowingly bring someone into a life of suffering; (2) attempts to ground the asymmetry in total utility, person-affecting views, or rights either end up denying it or quietly assuming it; so (3) it's probably basic — a feature of procreative morality, not a bug to be explained away.
