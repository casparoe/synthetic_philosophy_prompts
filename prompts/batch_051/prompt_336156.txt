I cover the ideas-and-science beat for a general-audience magazine, and Im trying to put together a back-page satire on infinite ethics that actually gets the view right. Can you draft it as a mock internal memo from the Impact and Ethics Office of a large philanthropic foundation, around 600 words, deadpan bureaucratic voice, announcing that the office is adopting Nick Bostrom's infinitarian-paralysis argument as binding grant-evaluation policy. Start straight in with the memo itself — no preface, no commentary.

By "canonically infinite" Bostrom means a world with infinitely many local good things above some finite positive threshold ε and infinitely many local bad things below −ε. The office is implementing this passage, from Nick Bostrom, "Infinite Ethics," *Analysis and Metaphysics*, vol. 10 (2011), pp. 9–59:

"Ethical theories that hold that value is aggregative imply that a canonically infinite world contains an infinite quantity of positive value and an infinite quantity of negative value. This gives rise to a peculiar predicament. We can do only a finite amount of good or bad. Yet in cardinal arithmetic, adding or subtracting a finite quantity does not change an infinite quantity. Every possible act of ours therefore has the same net effect on the total amount of good and bad in a canonically infinite world: none whatsoever."

The jokes should come from taking that logic seriously and adding institutional procedure, not from making the foundation stupid or evil. A program officer is told they can't release a bednet-distribution grant because its effect on total value is finite, and under the policy a finite increment to an infinite total leaves the total unchanged, so the grant is indistinguishable from doing nothing; the intake form asks applicants to state the cardinality of the harm they expect to avert and to supply their best ε; the FAQ says that, since cosmology only makes the canonically-infinite scenario probable rather than certain, the freeze is framed as provisional and precautionary; the chair asks whether the foundation's own payroll clears the same test. Target the argument in the passage and the practice of operationalizing a merely probable cosmological claim into ordinary funding decisions. Keep it deadpan, fair to the underlying view, and make the reader feel the real objection without the memo ever stating it. Don't spend words re-quoting the passage. Dont wink.
