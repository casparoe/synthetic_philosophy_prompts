Act as a philosopher specialising in moral psychology. Spend some time brainstorming the strongest formulation of each side before you write anything, and then draft carefully.

Consider two positions on gratitude. The agency-first view holds that gratitude proper is a response to a benefactor: someone must have knowingly intended, or at least willingly conferred, a benefit, so thankfulness for luck or nature is better classed as appreciation, relief, or gladness. The benefit-first view holds that what matters is receiving a good one did not earn or secure, so gratitude can fittingly be directed at luck, nature, one's ancestors, the past, or existence itself, and requiring an intending benefactor makes the concept pointlessly narrow. The defenders of these positions have undertaken an adversarial collaboration on the question of what gratitude can properly take as its object, and what it requires of the recipient.

Write the joint statement they could both sign. It must contain: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims, including substantive claims rather than mere truisms—which paradigm cases both sides count as gratitude, and which alleged cases both sides refuse to count; and (2) a disagreements table. In the table give three or four exact residual disagreements. For each one, state each side's view in words that side itself would sign; identify the crux whose resolution would move at least one side; and provide one case or argument both sides accept bears on that crux, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If any apparent disagreement dissolves on closer inspection as merely verbal, say so and drop it from the table. Test every formulation against at least these cases: a deliberately generous stranger; a friend who helps you by accident; inherited or genetic good fortune; and a natural event, such as a lucky escape or fine weather, with no agent behind it.
