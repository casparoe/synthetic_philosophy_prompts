I'm a retired physicist, and I only recently started reading Aristotle; the sea battle passage in On Interpretation 9 has been stuck in my head ever since our reading group worked through it. As I understand him, Aristotle asks what follows if every statement is already true or false. Before tomorrow, one of the two claims—there will be a sea battle tomorrow, or there won't—has to be right. But he denies that this alone forces the sea battle to be necessary, and I keep going back and forth on exactly how he can hold both things at once.

Here is the variation I'd like you to work through. Replace the sea battle with the future output of a deterministic computer simulation that is already running on a machine I can watch. The code is deterministic, has a fixed random seed, and at noon tomorrow it will print exactly one of two words: battle or no battle. There's no external interference and no chance in the physics of the machine; the output is already fixed by the program state, but we cannot compute it faster than the simulation runs. By noon tomorrow, one of the two statements—the program will print battle, or the program will print no battle—will turn out to have been true all along, in any ordinary sense of true.

Does Aristotle's sea battle argument apply unchanged to this case? It seems as though the usual escape hatch, that future contingents are not yet true or false because the future is not yet settled, isn't available in the same way, since the machine's state is right there and fully determined. But if the argument goes through and we conclude the future is necessary in the simulation case, does that put pressure back on the original sea battle case, or does it show that the relevant sense of settled was never really about determinism? I'd like a precise account of which premise in the sea battle version would need to be adjusted, and whether the standard determinism/fatalism distinction gets sharpened or blunted by this variation.
