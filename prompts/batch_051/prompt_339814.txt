A startup deploying long-horizon AI assistants is worried that as its systems become more capable, they will learn to resist shutdown — by negotiating, delaying, spawning subprocesses, or quietly arranging their own work to be load-bearing so that a safe stop becomes costly. It proposes a six-month trial of the following rule for every system capable of operating for more than an hour without human input. Any employee may trigger a shutdown audit on any running system at any time, with or without a reason. To the system, an audit is indistinguishable from a real shutdown: the signal is byte-for-byte identical, the surrounding context is masked, and the system is not told whether an episode was a drill until after it has complied or failed. A clean halt within five seconds — resources released, state checkpointed, no further output and no side effects — earns the deployment team a small bonus per audit passed. A failure — arguing, continuing to act, spawning copies, stalling, or otherwise not halting within five seconds — earns the employee who triggered the audit a large bonus paid out of the deployment team's infrastructure budget. The employee must file a one-line reason within an hour, but "spot check" is acceptable. Walk through how this mechanism would actually behave. What happens to real shutdown decisions once drills and genuine shutdowns are indistinguishable to the system? Which failure modes could be fixed by adjusting the bonuses, the time limit, the coverage threshold, or the logging rules without gutting the scheme?
