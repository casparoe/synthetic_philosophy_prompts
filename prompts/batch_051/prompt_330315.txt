I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a long piece about the rule of law: specifically, how precise a criminal law has to be before a state may punish someone under it. In the course of reporting I listened to a recorded debate between two legal scholars about the void-for-vagueness doctrine — the idea that a criminal prohibition is invalid, or cannot ground a conviction, when it is too imprecise either to give ordinary people fair notice of what is forbidden or to provide officials with standards sufficient to prevent arbitrary enforcement. I expected the debate to yield a crisp rule; instead it produced the following exchange, which I have not been able to referee. Names are changed, but the substance is faithful.

1. Lian: "The core requirement is prospectivity: a person of ordinary intelligence, reading the statute with common sense, must be able to tell before acting whether the act is criminal. A law that punishes 'conduct likely to offend others in a public place' fails on its face, because no one can survey the sensibilities of everyone they might pass. Such a law announces no rule at all; it hands the rule-making to whichever official later decides that the conduct was offensive."

2. Mateo: "That standard would condemn much of the criminal code. 'Reasonable,' 'reckless,' 'dishonest,' and 'against the public interest' all have open edges, and even the sharpest term needs interpretation in a disputed case. Real people don't learn what's forbidden by reading statutes; they learn from norms, warnings, and settled practice. So the test can't be whether the text resolves every borderline case in advance. It has to be whether the text, together with inherited practice, gives an official enough structure to decide the case publicly and consistently."

3. Lian: "You've run together two separate defects. Fair notice to the citizen and guidance for the enforcer are independent reasons a law can be void, and a law can fail on both at once. And there's a difference between an open-textured term and an unanchored evaluative term. 'Dishonest' acquires meaning from a shared public practice of ownership and truthfulness; 'likely to offend others' doesn't attach to any single practice, so each official is invited to deposit a private moral reaction into the statute."

4. Mateo: "Your distinction is doing more work than it can bear. The bad phrase you picked just raises the drafting question of whether the legislature could anchor it to a practice; that's a matter of craft, not a constitutional line. And on the second defect, your own framing concedes that precision isn't what prevents arbitrary enforcement. Plenty of precise rules — parking, license, and zoning rules — are enforced selectively all the time. Conversely, a phrase like 'force reasonably necessary in self-defence' is textually open but tightly structured by instruction, training, and review. The real enemy is unstructured discretion, not imprecision as such."

5. Lian: "But 'structured by community expectations' is exactly the problem. Which community? The community that supplies the jury and the complaining witnesses? If the content of the law is borrowed from the sensibilities of the dominant group, then a vague phrase lets the majority criminalize the ordinary conduct of outsiders by calling it 'disorderly' or 'offensive.' That isn't notice; it's the majority's unadopted norms applied retroactively. Vagueness is the mechanism by which private distaste becomes public prohibition."

6. Mateo: "I agree that 'likely to offend' is a bad statute. But look at how you reached that judgment: you reacted to the phrase, and you reasoned from examples. The doctrine condemns the easy cases while giving no method for the hard middle, which is where the contested laws actually live. If 'vague' means 'needs interpretation,' every law is vague, because no rule applies itself. If it means 'radically unconstrained,' the doctrine only catches the extreme tail and the rest is judicial impression. The doctrine apparently operates by the very felt judgment it claims to prohibit."

I need to turn this into an account readers can trust, and I don't want simply to side with whichever speaker I found more sympathetic in the moment. Please adjudicate the exchange turn by turn. For each numbered move, identify which specific claims are right, which are wrong or overstated, and rate the move from clearly mistaken to clearly right. Then say what both participants are missing, and give a committed overall verdict: who is more right about what, and how that person's position should be repaired in light of the other's best point. Don't hedge with "both sides have something to offer"; I'm asking for a definite ruling.
