I've got a rule about information that my flatmate thinks is irrational, and I honestly can't tell whether he's right or I'm just attached to it. Roughly: I refuse to learn things when I cant do anything about them and knowing would only make me feel bad or make me act unnaturally around other people. I don't check my bank balance during a lean month, I put off opening exam results until after any window to complain or resit has passed, I never ask partners what they think about my previous relationships, I turned down a free genetic test for a late-onset condition with no treatment, and I've asked friends not to tell me when people have been slagging me off. My flatmate says there is literally a theorem — I think by someone called Good — that a rational agent should never turn down free information, because at worst you can just ignore it and usually you can act better with it. So on his view my rule is irrational even before we get to the times I actively avoid findings things out.

I can see the logic. But the theorem seems to assume you'll update cleanly and that the information won't leak into your mood, your face, or your behaviour despite your intentions — and that 'having the option to ignore it' is the same as actually not being changed by it. So my question is about the policy, not the individual cases. Is a blanket-ish rule of avoiding unactionable-but-upsetting information defensible for the kind of agent I actually am, or is it protecting my mood and self-image at the cost of being less informed? I want to know which factors do the real work: controllability, the size and stickiness of the emotional hit, the chance I'll leak it socially, whether I'm any good at precommitting to act normally afterwards. And if the rule is flawed, is there a version that keeps what's good in it without the costs? Please don't just say it depends — I want a ruling and a clear restatement.
