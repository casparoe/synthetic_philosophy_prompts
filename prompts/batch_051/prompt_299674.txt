I'm putting together a short "normative profile" on honor, dignity, and victimhood cultures for a seminar, and I want a consistency audit before I present. The seminar question is whether any of these is morally better than the others, so I've written down the views I actually have, not the ones I can defend cleanly. By honor culture I mean roughly the norm system where standing depends on face and insults and family obligations are policed through shame, reputation, up to and including violence. By dignity culture I mean the system where you're expected to shrug off petty insults, not escalate, and route serious wrongs through impartial authorities. By victimhood culture I mean the system where someone who has been wronged publicizes the harm and appeals to an audience or authority as a person who has been injured.

Here's the list, in no particular order:

(a) The move from honor to dignity culture is a real moral improvement. Shrugging off an insult is a genuine virtue, not servility, because it means you don't have to prove you're not a doormat.

(b) But honor culture still has something we lost. It makes defending your family and repaying betrayal into obligations that third parties enforce through esteem and shame, rather than private choices you can quietly abandon when they get costly.

(c) Victimhood culture gets something right as well. When institutions are stacked against you, publicly naming a harm and appealing to outsiders is often the only way to make the powerful take you seriously; a demand to grow a thicker skin can be a demand that powerless people absorb abuse on powerful people's terms.

(d) At the same time, I find the core of victimhood culture morally corrupting: it gives standing or authority to people because they have been injured, which rewards fragility and invites people to present themselves as victims strategically.

(e) More generally, I believe self-respect should not be hostage to what other people think, and a culture that makes standing depend on public recognition of one's injuries is making a moral mistake.

(f) Still, I think a person who is totally indifferent to his reputation, in the sense of not caring what his community thinks of him, is usually not enlightened but antisocial or dangerous.

(g) And a meta-level belief: the useful question isn't which culture is morally superior in the abstract, but under what material conditions each is stable and least cruel. Honor norms might make sense where the state is weak, dignity norms where the state is strong, and victimhood norms where institutions exist formally but don't actually serve everyone. I half-believe this and half-suspect it's just relativism wearing a functionalist hat.

Please audit this set. Tell me which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are. For the genuine tensions, say how bad each one is and what the cheapest fix would be; then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set hang together, and what that revision costs.
