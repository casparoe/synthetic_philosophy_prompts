I've been reading about how hospitals decide someone is dead enough for organ donation, and I've tied myself in knots. There seem to be two standards running side by side in the same building. A patient declared brain-dead is legally dead even though the ventilator keeps their heart beating and they look warm and pink in the OR. A patient who isn't brain-dead but whose family withdraws life support goes a different route: they're taken off the machine, the team waits for the heart to stop, then waits a short hands-off period — a couple of minutes? five? — and only then declares death and takes the organs. That second route is called donation after circulatory death. But a heart that's just stopped can sometimes restart on its own, and nothing has truly, irreversably decayed in a few minutes. So what definition of death is actually operating in each pathway? Is there one coherent notion of death that covers both, or does the organ-donation system quietly presuppose different things depending on which door you came through? What does irreversible have to mean for the waiting period to do any philosophical work? And is the so-called dead donor rule — that you may only take organs from someone already dead — actually the load-bearing principle, or more of a comforting slogan? If I had to make the system consistent, what would I have to give up: the DCD pathway, the whole-brain criterion, or the dead donor rule?
