A guy in my philosophy of mind group is on a William James kick and keeps using the same move against anyone who thinks consciousness is emergent. He lays it out like this:

(1) Either consciousness was in the basic stuff from the start, or it shows up once the basic stuff gets arranged the right way.
(2) If it shows up once the bits get arranged, then an entirely new nature appears, something whose potency wasn't in the bits.
(3) But no property can show up if its potency wasn't already in the ingredients; otherwise you're getting something from nothing.
(4) So consciousness can't emerge from non-conscious matter.
(5) Therefore it must have been there all along in all the matter, like panpsychists say.

He backs step (2) with this passage from William James, The Principles of Psychology, ch. VI, "The Mind-Stuff":

"But with the dawn of consciousness an entirely new nature seems to slip in, something whereof the potency was not given in the mere outward atoms of the original chaos."

Then he says James himself called this "the entire incommensurability of feeling as such with material motion as such," so if you reject emergence you're stuck with panpsychism. I feel like the argument proves too much: by the same logic, wetness can't emerge from non-wet oxygen and hydrogen, a magnet can't have properties its individual atoms lack, and basically every higher-level property of an arrangement of parts becomes impossible. But I can't locate exactly where it goes wrong. It might be premise 3, it might be a slide in what "potency" means between steps 2 and 3, or it might be that the conclusion doesn't actually avoid the problem. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the anti-emergence move? Also, does premise 5 actually solve the "dawn of consciousness" problem, or just relocate it to the micro level?
