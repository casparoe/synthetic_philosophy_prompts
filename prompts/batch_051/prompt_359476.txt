In my epistemology study group, we discussed Moore's paradox: the familiar observation that a sentence in which someone says that it is raining while also saying that they do not believe that it is raining strikes nearly everyone as absurd to assert, even though both parts can be true, and the whole conjunction can be true of someone else or of the same person at a different time. Two members of the group, Sam and Priya, got into an argument I could not settle, and I would like a referee.

(1) Sam began by arguing that the absurdity is just a matter of assertion expressing belief. To assert that it is raining is to present yourself as believing that it is raining, so the utterance expresses both the first-order belief that it is raining and a second-order belief that you lack that very belief. The contradiction, Sam said, is between the attitudes expressed by the act, not between the contents of the two clauses, which is why the sentence becomes perfectly assertable when the pronouns are changed. (2) Priya replied that this cannot be the whole story, because ordinary insincere assertion is just lying, not a paradox. If you say that it is raining while believing that it is not, you are merely lying; the conflict between the belief your assertion expresses and what you actually believe does not produce the peculiar self-defeating quality of the Moorean utterance. Moreover, the Moorean speaker cannot even make the utterance line up with their own state of mind by trying to be sincere, in the way a liar can simply decide to tell the truth, so something more than the sincerity condition must explain the anomaly. (3) Sam came back that the belief case is just one member of a larger family. Consider the analogous sentence in which someone says they will be at a meeting while adding that they do not intend to be there. That utterance is defective in the same way, and here no special problem about self-knowledge of belief is needed; the act of asserting or committing already expresses the very attitude that the sentence then disavows. So, Sam concluded, Moore's paradox reveals a general fact about speech acts, not anything distinctive about first-person belief. (4) Priya answered that the intention analogy actually proves the opposite. The reason the sentence about intending is incoherent, she said, is that a sincere speaker of "I will do it" expresses an intention, and a speaker has first-person authority over their own intentions rather than having to discover them from the outside. For belief, the analogous first-person authority is transparency: normally, to determine whether one believes that it is raining, one simply considers whether it is raining. On her view, the Moorean sentence short-circuits that transparency rather than merely violating a convention of assertion, and Sam's generalization misses the distinctive epistemic structure of belief.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four numbered moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly wrong, mixed, mostly right, or clearly right, with a brief justification, and then say which overall diagnosis is more defensible and what, if anything, both parties are missing.
