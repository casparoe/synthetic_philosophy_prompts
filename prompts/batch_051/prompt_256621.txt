I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about how communities decide which inherited rules still bind and which get treated as artifacts of an earlier era—a question that sits at the boundary of religion, moral psychology, and the study of institutions. To think through the arguments, I want to run a concrete counterfactual. Suppose a religious community—roughly the size and organizational complexity of a modern denomination, with a canon fixed for centuries—has scriptures that contain commands nearly all members now treat as transcultural, such as prohibitions on murder, theft, and deceit, alongside commands many members now treat as culturally bound, such as dietary restrictions, rules about head coverings, and rules that regulate slavery without abolishing it. The community adopts a new constitutional rule of interpretation: once every generation, a council must re-derive each contested command from a small, fixed list of principles that the community has declared transcultural; any command that cannot be so derived without relying on assumptions specific to the text's time is reclassified as cultural guidance and ceases to be binding. Predict, with reasoning, how the community's practice and its internal structure of authority change over the next three generations—say, seventy-five years. Which commands are most likely to survive re-derivation, which quietly lapse, and where is the procedure most likely to break down? In particular, does the distinction between moral and cultural rules do the work its designers hope, or does it get captured by whichever faction controls the council? Which of your predictions are you most confident of, and which depend on assumptions about how seriously people treat the principles and the procedure? What would have to be true about the community for the rule to produce something close to what its designers intended?
