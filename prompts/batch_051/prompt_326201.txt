I am a philosophy professor who now regularly reads machine-generated prose, and I have adopted a standing policy that two colleagues criticize for opposite reasons. When I catch a large language model in a single fluent, confident falsehood — a fabricated source, a statistic that dissolves on checking, an argument assigned to the wrong author — I do not call the output lying, since the system neither believes the false statement nor intends to deceive. But I also refuse to call it a mere local error of the kind a careful human author sometimes commits. Instead I reclassify the whole text as bullshit, in approximately Harry Frankfurt's sense: discourse produced without a governing concern for the truth of what is said. I then lower my confidence in all the text's remaining unchecked claims far more than a single caught error in a human text would warrant, and I usually stop reading, even in domains where such models have a strong track record. A computer scientist colleague objects that this rests on a category mistake: an LLM has no beliefs, intentions, or relation to truth, so its output cannot literally be bullshit; it can only be evaluated assertion by assertion as true or false. A philosopher friend objects that the policy is prudent but cognitively undisciplined, because it converts one discovered failure into a blanket epistemic discount and lets a global verdict about a producer substitute for local evaluation of claims. Is my policy defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as an epistemic rule? Under what conditions would it fail, and is there a restated version that keeps its benefits without the overgeneralization?
