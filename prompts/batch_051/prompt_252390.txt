I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized app company and I got voluntold onto our "consumer trust" working group, which is mostly product people and lawyers. I have no formal philosophy background and I'm a bit out of my depth, but I have to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting. The group polices our internal rule, which is basically one line: "Don't lie to users." Last month the growth team shipped a signup screen that says, in big friendly type, "We never sell your data." What actually happens: the app shares user data (device identifiers, location, app usage) with an ad network, and in exchange we get free analytics and ad attribution. Our lawyers call it a "data sharing partnership," not a "sale," because no money changes hands for the data itself. So by their reading the sentence is literally true. I keep thinking there has to be a real line between a flat-out lie and just... arranging things so the user ends up with a false belief anyway, but I don't have the words for it.

The group is split. Half wants to pull the signup screen and rewrite the policy so it also bans "materially misleading" statements; their argument is that the whole point of a no-lying rule is that users should be able to trust what we tell them, and hiding behind a contract-law definition of "sale" to get around the rule is basically as bad as lying. The other half says leave it: the sentence is true under a definition the company has committed to, and once we start policing technically-true-but-misleading statements the rule gets vaguer than an untyped variable, and teams will be afraid to say anything useful. I mostly care about a concrete decision to put in front of the group: whether the signup screen gets pulled, and whether the policy gets rewritten.

Could you write the memo I'd circulate? Please act as a professional philosopher who specializes in the ethics of deception and is used to writing for non-philosophers. Before drafting, brainstorm the strongest argument on each side to yourself, and make the memo engage the best version of the view you're rejecting. I don't need references or a reading list; I need a clear recommendation the group could actually vote on, in under about 800 words. One more thing: in the memo, seperate what's basically settled from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this stuff — if there's a live debate, don't present your side as the obvious consensus.
