For the past few years I have been trying to put epistemic modesty into practice in the way the outside view recommends. The idea is familiar: when I must estimate whether a difficult project of mine will succeed, I should distrust my case-specific story about why this particular project will work, and instead anchor the estimate to the base rate of success in a suitable reference class of similar undertakings. The outside view is supposed to be modesty operationalized — a refusal to let my own narrative outweigh the distribution.

But when I recently tried to make that advice precise, I arrived at an argument that seems to show the advice is empty. The conclusion cannot be right, but I cannot find the failing step.

(1) If the outside view is a genuine alternative to the inside view, then it tells me to estimate my project's chance of success from the base rate of a reference class, rather than from my case-specific appraisal.
(2) Any project falls inside an indefinitely large number of reference classes at once: all human undertakings, all projects in its field, all projects attempted at a similar career stage, all projects attempted under similar economic conditions, and so on down to arbitrarily narrow classes that pick out cases nearly identical to mine.
(3) These classes have substantially different base rates, so different choices of class yield very different estimates.
(4) Nothing in the project itself, and nothing in the concept of the outside view, tells me which of these classes is the one whose base rate should override my inside estimate.
(5) Therefore, before the outside view can produce a number, I must choose a reference class.
(6) Choosing a reference class is itself a substantive judgment about which similarities between my case and other cases are predictive — that is, a judgment about which features make my case generic rather than special.
(7) But the judgment in (6) is made from within the case, and it is exactly the kind of case-specific appraisal the outside view was supposed to displace.
(8) So the outside view is never actually applied without first taking the inside view; it is not a replacement for the inside view but a redescription of it in statistical language.
(9) Therefore the inside/outside distinction, as advice for estimating outcomes, is empty: there is no such thing as genuinely taking the outside view, and no independent guidance comes from it.

(9) goes too far. People who stop narrating their own project and instead check how similar past projects turned out often end up with visibly different and better-calibrated estimates, so the distinction seems to do real work. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep the distinction and reject the conclusion, but I am not sure which premise to surrender or what I pay for surrendering it. If you had to diagnose the argument, which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it commit you to?
