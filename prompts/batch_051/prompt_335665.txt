During the past year I have been reporting on the dispute over whether large language models understand language, and I have found myself reaching for a distinction I would like checked rather than simply used. I have been calling it the difference between template fluency and binding reach. By template fluency I mean the thing skeptical observers usually seize on when they call a model shallow: its ability to produce text that stays comfortably within the distribution of what people have already said about a subject. By binding reach I mean something narrower. A system has binding reach for a concept when it can hold that concept's contribution fixed while the surrounding sentence changes and still track the concept through the change — for example, moving from "the solvent dissolved the solute" to "the solute was dissolved by the solvent" without swapping which thing did the dissolving, or moving from "Alice promised Bob to leave" to the correct inference about who is to leave, across fresh cases rather than only memorized ones.

My working theory is that many celebrated examples of models failing to "understand" are neither failures of statistical learning nor evidence that the model lacks anything like meaning. They are failures of binding. The model has learned the concept's associations — the company the word keeps, its typical entailments, the cases in which it is usually invoked — but it has not reliably learned which occurrence of a variable the concept is attached to. When the surface order changes, the concept stays roughly where it was while the rest of the sentence rotates around it, and the model answers on the basis of the old arrangement. That would explain why the same system can write five fluent paragraphs about a topic and then reverse two noun phrases in a premise and draw a conclusion that contradicts the first paragraph.

I would like an honest assessment of this notion. Is the distinction coherent, or have I rigged it by defining binding reach so close to the behavior I want it to explain? Does it already exist under a more precise name, and if so, what is known about it as an account of what understanding adds to prediction? And where does it come apart: is there a kind of comprehension failure it predicts badly, or a plainly meaningful competence it would dismiss as mere template fluency?
