The research council I belong to at a private institute funds a small number of high-risk projects each year in the foundations of machine learning, and the last proposal we must decide on comes from a group that wants to train neural networks without backpropagation. Their idea is to replace gradient descent with an explicitly evolutionary search process, including mutation, recombination, gene flow, genetic drift, and periodic extinction events, and they frame the project as a test of whether natural selection's much-maligned stupidity as an optimizer is in fact a feature worth imitating. They argue that because natural selection operates with no global objective function, no gradient signal, and a highly indirect mapping from genotype to phenotype, it explores fitness landscapes in ways that gradient-based methods cannot, and that the field has overlearned the lesson that evolution is merely a slow and blind hill-climber. Half the committee reads this as a serious challenge to the unquestioned dominance of gradient descent; the other half reads it as a category mistake dressed up in evolutionary vocabulary, since the investigators will still have to define a scalar loss for their algorithm to select on, and natural selection, unlike their algorithm, is not attempting to optimize any fixed quantity at all. Please draft the committee's decision memo — fund, reject, or fund with conditions — that gives a clear recommendation, explains how the committee should think about the analogy between natural selection and gradient descent in judging the project, and treats the strongest considerations on the losing side honestly and in a way the investigators could respond to. Write the memo in American English.
