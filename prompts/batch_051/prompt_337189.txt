In Alan Turing’s 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence, he discusses an objection he attributes to Ada Lovelace: that a machine such as Babbage’s Analytical Engine can only do whatever we know how to order it to perform, and therefore cannot originate anything. Turing responds by pointing out that machines can still surprise their designers, often because the designer has made an incorrect assumption or has not worked through the consequences of the rules.

Construct a thought experiment involving a contemporary system—a theorem prover, a language model, or a protein-folding program—that satisfies both of the following specifications. First, the system exhibits a competence that looks like comprehension to an expert observer: it solves nontrivial problems, explains its answers coherently, and handles novel cases better than most humans. Second, a complete mechanistic description of the system’s training and inference rules contains no representation of the meanings of the domain, only syntactic transformations and error-gradient updates; in Lovelace’s sense, the system originates nothing, because every output is a causal consequence of instructions and data already supplied to it.

Then do the checks. State the belief the system produces, the method by which it produces it, and what a defender of Lovelace’s principle would conclude about it. State what the Turing-style inversion of reasoning would conclude about the same output, and what Dennett’s notion of competence without comprehension would add to that verdict. Say which structural feature of the case carries the philosophical weight—for example, a particular kind of brittleness, a distribution-shift failure, or the provenance of the training signal—and what would change if that feature were removed. Finish by explaining how a critic who rejects Turing’s conclusion would argue that the scenario has been rigged to make comprehension unnecessary, and what further evidence they would demand before accepting that the competence is real rather than merely apparent.
