OK, here's one I keep going back and forth on, and I want a definite ruling rather than a survey.

In my ethics classes I teach standing to blame with a rough four-part test. Someone has standing to blame a wrongdoer only if:

(1) Clean hands: the blamer is not themselves guilty of the same kind of wrong — same practice, same harm, not just vaguely similar.
(2) A stake: the wrong falls within some relationship or responsibility the blamer actually has; they're not just a meddling bystander.
(3) Reversibility: the standard they're applying is one they'd accept being applied to themselves and to their own side.
(4) No deflection: the blame isn't mainly doing work for their own reputation or self-exculpation.

Now the case that's bugging me. Sam spent fifteen years running a political consultancy whose specialty was race-baiting mailers: he'd take a moderate candidate, darken the faces in stock photos, pair them with violent-crime stats, that kind of thing. He knew what he was doing, and he's been candid about it since. He left the business, apologized publicly without hedging, and spent a decade doing pro bono work for voting-rights groups; by all accounts the change is genuinly real, not an image job. The consultant he's now attacking, in a very sharp op-ed, got his start at Sam's old firm — Sam personally trained him in exactly that technique. The op-ed names Sam's own record in the second paragraph and he doesn't spare himself. The voting-rights people he now works with asked him to write it.

So: does Sam have standing to blame this consultant, on my four-part account? Go criterion by criterion and tell me where the account underdetermines the verdict. Then give a committed answer: does the case show that Clean Hands needs to be time-indexed or made causal, or does it show something else about what standing really tracks? I want a definite position on whether the criteria need revising and why, not just the options laid out. And tell me the step in your reasoning you're least sure about.
