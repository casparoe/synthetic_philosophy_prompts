For the animal-ethics segment of my undergrad seminar I use a test I made up years ago, for when a use of an animal counts as treating that animal as a mere means. The background hunch is freegan: using something that would otherwise be wasted is not the same as creating demand for it, and my test is supposed to make that hunch precise. I tell them a use counts when (1) the animal was bred, confined, mutilated, or killed to produce the item; (2) using the item sends money or demand back into the system that does any of that; (3) the use publicy normalizes the idea that animal bodies and animal products are resources for people; and (4) the user's practical stance treats the animal's body or labor as available to them, whatever the animal's actual history.

The case I'm considering assigning splits the room every time I've taught it. A sanctuary in the Hudson Valley keeps a small flock of merino sheep it rescued from a failed farm. Merinos have to be shorn each spring or they overheat and get flystrike, so the sanctuary shears them and has always composted the fleece. A new volunteer, who's a knitter, wants to take the fleece from the sheep she helped shear, process it at home, and knit herself one plain sweater. No sale, no gift shop, no photos online. Half the staff say she's turning the sheep's fleece into a commodity and treating the animal as a standing wool dispenser; the other half say the fleece would have rotted in the compost anyway, the sheep had to be shorn regardless, and nobody is harmed or paid.

Run the sweater through the four conditions one at a time and tell me whether it comes out as treating the sheep as a mere means. Where a condition doesn't settle it, say whether that's because the case is genuinely close or because the condition is idling. If you think the test needs a fifth condition for byproduct-of-rescue cases, propose one, and then say what it would do to the obvious companion case: eggs from rescued hens.
