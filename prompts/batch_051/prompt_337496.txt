I have been trying to build an argument for a seminar and have ended up with a proof of something I do not believe, which usually means the proof proves too much. I should like a diagnosis of where it goes wrong rather than a list of things to read.

Set the case up like this. A has come by a true and damaging piece of information about B without any violation of duty: no spying, no breach of confidence, nothing illicit in the way it was obtained. If released, the information would lower other people's opinion of B. A now proposes to B that, in exchange for a sum of money, A will not release the information; if B declines, A may release it after all.

The argument that A acts permissibly runs as follows.

(1) It is permissible for A to release the information. The fact is true, A is entitled to hold it, and unkind or reputation-damaging speech, by itself, is not a wrong.

(2) It is permissible for A to refrain from releasing it. A is under no general duty to publish what he knows.

(3) It is permissible for A to ask B for money in exchange for a service A is permitted but not obliged to provide. Since A may withhold the publication, A may offer to withhold it on condition that he is paid.

(4) If an action is wrong, something about it must make it wrong: it deceives, coerces, breaches a duty, infringes a right, or the like. A conditional offer to sell one's own future silence has none of those features by itself.

(5) A's proposal is therefore just the sale of A's forbearance, a permitted act offered on permitted terms, and B remains free to accept or decline.

(6) But A's proposal is blackmail, and blackmail is wrong.

(7) So at least one of steps (1) through (5) is false, or step (5) misdescribes the proposal in a way that hides a new wrong-making feature.

Where does the argument break? I should most like to keep (1) and (2), because giving them up seems to make the mere disclosure of damaging truths intrinsically wrong and the mere withholding of them a duty. But I can see that keeping them may force me to abandon either (3), the claim that one may sell a liberty one legitimately has, or (4), the assumption that wrongness must reside in some feature of the package. I need to know which choice is least costly. In particular, I want a principle that condemns A's proposal without condemning the case in which B, upon learning that A knows the information, voluntarily offers to pay A for his continued silence and A accepts. If the difference between the two cases cannot be drawn, I should like to be told that as well, along with what it costs.
