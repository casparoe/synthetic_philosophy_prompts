Question: Explain what Hannah Arendt means by the banality of evil in her account of Adolf Eichmann. In particular, what role does thoughtlessness play, and what follows for Eichmann's moral and legal responsibility?

Student answer: In Eichmann in Jerusalem, Arendt reports that the defendant struck her not as a fanatical antisemite or sadistic monster but as a cliché-ridden, self-important bureaucrat. The banality of evil names this finding: Eichmann's evil lacked the deep or demonic motives the prosecution projected onto him. Arendt locates the source of his crimes in thoughtlessness, by which she means not low intelligence but an inability to stop and imagine what he was doing from the perspective of others. His speech was a tissue of stock phrases and officialese, and this shielded him from genuine moral reflection. Arendt's point generalizes to ordinary people: whenever people are placed inside a bureaucratic chain of command and told to follow orders, they tend to commit evil in the same unthinking way, which is what makes evil banal. Because Eichmann's wickedness was banal and thoughtless rather than monstrous, Arendt concludes that he was less culpable than the prosecution claimed and that the court should have treated him more as a replaceable cog than as a true murderer.
