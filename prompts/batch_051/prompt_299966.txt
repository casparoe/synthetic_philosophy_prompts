On the Dead Sea–scroll beat in Jerusalem, I sat through a long Shabbat lunch where a biblical scholar and a historian got into it about Maimonides on the Temple sacrifices. The historian claimed Maimonides says the whole sacrificial system was basically a concession to the Israelites’ pagan habits: God kept the form of ancient Near Eastern worship but redirected it to himself, so the people could ease off idolatry gradually instead of being told to go cold turkey. The biblical scholar said that reading makes the commandments look like a trick and misses the harder point Maimonides is making about what a law can actually do to a people. He said Maimonides thinks the sacrifices were also a genuine form of service, even if not the highest one, and that the historical-concession story is just the “how,” not the whole “why.” The historian shot back that the text doesn’t give you two independent justifications; it gives you a developmental explanation, and it says God chose the sacrificial regime because it was what the people could bear at that moment – so saying “but it was also a genuine service” is just softening the bite. The biblical scholar answered that if you soften nothing, you end up with God commanding something whose only rationale is that people were already doing something like it, which is a weird thing to call a divine command. They asked what I thought, and I honestly didn’t know how to separate the elements of the view. Can you adjudicate this move by move? In particular, is the “concession” reading separable from the claim that sacrifice was nonetheless a real, praiseworthy form of service in Maimonides’ text, or does one of these two people get to claim the more faithful reading overall?
