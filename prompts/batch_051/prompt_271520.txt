My friend and I are trying to reverse-engineer how our logic professor treats universal statements, because she keeps giving us one-word verdicts on little inferences and we can't agree on what rule she's actually using. So far she's given these:

- "All whales are mammals. Therefore, some whales are mammals." — she says valid.
- "All squares are rectangles. All rectangles are polygons. Therefore, all squares are polygons." — valid.
- "No reptiles are fish. Therefore, no fish are reptiles." — valid.
- "Some prime numbers are greater than a thousand. Therefore, some numbers greater than a thousand are prime numbers." — valid.

We're trying to figure out whether she's running the old syllogistic idea that "All S are P" already says at least one S exists, or the post-Fregean idea that "All S are P" is a quantified conditional that can be true even when there are no S's — so that moving from "all" to "some" needs a separate existence premise.

Next week she'll rule on this one:

"All moons of Venus are cratered. Therefore, some moons of Venus are cratered."

How should we predict she'll rule? My friend says she'll say valid, because the first verdict already shows she's doing the existential-import thing. I think she'll say invalid, because she's just been quietly assuming the earlier subject terms had nonempty extensions, and Venus doesn't have any moons. Which candidate rules actually fit her verdicts so far, what does each predict about the Venus case, which prediction would you put money on and why, and — if you wanted a seperate test before next week — what one additional case would settle which rule she's running?
