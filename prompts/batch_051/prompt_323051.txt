I'm a science and ideas journalist, and I'm working on a long-read about deflationary theories of truth. The framing I've settled on is a scorecard: most philosophers no longer think truth is some heavy correspondence relation between sentences and the world, but they still disagree about what the word 'true' is actually doing. I want to rank the main deflationary options by how well they survive a specific stress test—the cases where the truth predicate seems to be pulling its weight: blind endorsements ('whatever the panel chair said is true'), generalizations ('not everything in the report is true'), and embedded conditionals ('if the model's output is true, the policy is safe').

The candidates I'm weighing are: the redundancy view (saying 'p is true' just collapses into saying p), disquotationalism (the truth predicate mostly cancels the quotation marks, so 'the flight was delayed' is true comes to the same thing as the flight was delayed), Horwich-style minimalism (truth is a property whose entire nature is fixed by instances of the equivalence schema), and prosententialism ('is true' works anaphorically, like a pronoun that picks up an earlier statement rather than naming a property). A colleague keeps telling me the real action is in pragmatics, not semantics, so I'd also like to know whether these four are even answering the same question.

Can you rank the four from best to worst on that stress test, give each a score out of 10, and add a one-sentence justification? Then tell me which one would make the clearest opening for a reader who's never heard of any of them and just wants to know why we have the word 'true' at all. Feel free to correct my one-line glosses if their misleading, but keep the ranking tied to the stress-test cases rather than exegetical points.
