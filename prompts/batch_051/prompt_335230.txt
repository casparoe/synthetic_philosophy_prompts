A friend and I spent the evening arguing about the charge that effective altruism has a measurability bias, and by the end they had left me with an argument I cannot shake. I would like a diagnosis of it. Their argument ran as follows:

(1) Effective altruism directs resources by comparing interventions on their measurable cost-effectiveness.
(2) The great moral achievements of the past — the abolition of chattel slavery, universal suffrage, and civil rights — were won by movements whose eventual success was not calculable in advance; at the time, they were low-evidence, low-probability gambles rather than interventions with measurable expected returns.
(3) So the effective-altruist criterion would not have ranked those movements above proven interventions such as relieving immediate material suffering.
(4) Any philanthropic principle that would have advised against supporting the most important moral achievements is a bad principle.
(5) Therefore the effective-altruist emphasis on measurable cost-effectiveness is a bad principle.

I find the conclusion hard to accept, because the same reasoning seems to indict basic scientific research, venture investing, and nearly every choice made under genuine uncertainty; it appears to suggest that no ex ante comparison of causes is legitimate unless the outcomes are already measurable. But I also do not want to grant that sober comparison is worthless. Which step should be rejected, and why did that step look plausible? If the right response is to repair the criterion rather than abandon it, what does the repaired criterion say, and what does it give up?
