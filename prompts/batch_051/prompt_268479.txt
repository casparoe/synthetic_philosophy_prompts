I coordinate field assessments for a small humanitarian organization working in regions prone to food crisis. Several times a year, after a failed harvest or a sharp rise in food prices, I have to help decide how we characterize the situation in our reports and advocacy: as an overwhelming natural event, as a failure of policy, or as something between the two. The characterization determines our response — whether we mount emergency feeding operations, push for market interventions, or take specific concerns privately to the authorities. I have no academic background in economics or political science, and neither do the assessment staff I supervise. What I have, instead, is a rough conviction formed over years in the field, and I want it converted into a procedure that staff without my experience can use.

The conviction is this: in a food crisis, the decisive question is usually not how much food exists but who can command the food that exists. A failed harvest becomes a famine only when some group loses the ability to buy, receive, grow, or otherwise obtain food, and that loss is almost always shaped by prices, wages, property rules, information, and the decisions of officials. The label matters practically: getting it wrong can direct food to the wrong places, falsely implicate a government in a genuinely unavoidable event, or let a culpable government hide behind a drought.

I want a reusable decision procedure for our assessment staff. It must be runnable within 48 hours using data we can realistically collect: local retail and wholesale prices, daily wage rates, the movement of grain, government stock releases, the condition of roads and markets, relief coverage, restrictions on movement, and whether local reporting is independent. It should place a crisis along a spectrum rather than forcing a binary, with one end being a natural trigger with little remediable human failure and the other a famine produced chiefly by political and economic arrangements. Present it as an ordered set of tests, in the spirit of a flowchart written out in plain words. For each step, say which evidence is most diagnostic, what to do when the numbers are missing or clearly manipulated, and where mechanical checking stops and trained judgment begins. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about where the procedure is likely to misfire. The country director has to sign off on any public classification, so the procedure must be defensible to a skeptical non-specialist, not merely internally consistent.

Then run the finished procedure on these four cases. The first three are summaries I have gathered from my reading; the fourth is a current situation of the kind we face:

- Bengal, 1943. A rice crisis during the Second World War. Wartime inflation and speculation drove rice prices up rapidly, while the wages of rural laborers, fishermen, and artisans did not keep pace. Government procurement for the military and for Calcutta, together with hoarding by traders, pulled rice out of rural markets. Relief came late and was inadequate. Later analysis has argued that the amount of rice available per person was not itself catastrophically low, and that the deaths were concentrated among rural groups that lost the ability to pay for food.

- Ireland, the late 1840s. A potato blight destroyed the crop on which the rural poor depended. Yet grain and livestock continued to leave the country while people starved, and government relief was deliberately constrained so as not to interfere with markets and property. The burden fell hardest on tenant farmers and agricultural laborers.

- China, 1959–1961. After rapid collectivization, the state required very high grain deliveries from the countryside, while local officials under pressure to report success sent upward increasingly exaggerated harvest figures. Grain was extracted from villages on the basis of those inflated reports, leaving too little for the people who grew it. Transport priorities, and the slow acknowledgment of bad news, meant that help arrived late even where grain existed elsewhere.

- Hypothetical current case. A severe drought in a poor country has pushed food prices up sharply. The government describes the crisis as a natural disaster and requests food aid. The affected province has long been neglected by the capital; its road network has decayed through corruption; traders are holding back grain in expectation of higher prices; and the government has barred independent journalists from the area. Food is visibly available in the provincial capital, but rural people cannot afford it or reach it.

For each case, show the classification the procedure produces and name the specific evidence that drove it. Make sure at least one conclusion is something an organization like mine would find inconvenient to publish or to raise with a government, and say plainly why the procedure still supports it. End by stating what the procedure cannot do: what it cannot settle, what it is likely to misclassify, and the conditions under which it should not be used. I do not need references or a reading list; I need the reasoning itself, written so a non-specialist can apply it.
