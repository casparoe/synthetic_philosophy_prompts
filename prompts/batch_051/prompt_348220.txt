I'm a PhD student in moral psychology, and I keep tripping over a term my supervisors use as if it does real work: "bias" in the moral domain. I want a working definition of the term as its used when someone criticises a moral judgement, attitude, or disposition as biased. I don't mean bias in measurement or hiring algorithms — just the evaluative sense, where calling something biased is meant as a criticism that goes beyond "I disagree with that judgement."

I want the definition to sort these cases:

1. A parent in a fire who saves their own child rather than two children they don't know.
2. A judge whose sentencing data show she goes lighter on defendants from her own ethnic group, though she has no explicit prejudice.
3. Someone who insists a consensual adult sibling relationship is wrong but can't give any reason (the moral-dumbfounding pattern).
4. Someone who gives more to a charity after seeing one named child's photo than to a charity helping a far larger number of unnamed children (identifiable victim effect).
5. A vegan who says a meat-eater's willingness to harm pigs but not dogs is speciesism and therefore a bias.
6. Someone who judges actively switching a lever to divert a trolley onto one person as worse than failing to switch when that would let five die (omission bias).
7. Someone who is more alarmed by a small risk from GM food than by a much larger, familiar risk from driving (novelty/status-quo-ish bias).
8. A person who spends money on their own family's comfort while others are starving, but still accepts there are real moral limits on family partiality.

My rough intuition is that 1 is not a bias and 2 clearly is, and that 6 is a textbook example of one, but I can't say what distinguishes 1 from 2 without smuggling in a first-order view about which partialities are morally permitted. And I genuinely don't know whether 5 should count — whether the vegan is using "bias" as a description or just as rhetoric.

So: give me a working definition of "bias" in this evaluative sense, run it through the cases, and tell me where it either surprises you or can't deliver a verdict without importing a substantive moral theory. If the best definition collapses into "departure from whichever moral judgements are correct," then I want to know whether the term does any independent work beyond that. And please say explicitly what separates a bias from a mere preference or partiality, if anything does.
