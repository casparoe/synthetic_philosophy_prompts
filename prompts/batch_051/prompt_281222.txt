I've been reading about KJV-onlyism and the Septuagint thing where the New Testament writers seem to quote the Greek translation as scripture, and it got me wondering about the actual rule my denomination's statement of faith uses. The clause says:

"The Scriptures, having been given by inspiration of God in the original Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek, are the only infallible rule for faith and life. A translation carries authority for the church only so far as it faithfully renders the sense of the original, and no translation is itself inspired."

Now here's the case I can't get out of my head. A mission group working in a language with no complete New Testament uses a large language model to translate from the Greek. The model was partly trained on English study Bibles; local speakers tidy up the grammar so it reads naturally, but nobody involved can check the result against the Greek. The draft gets printed and starts being used. Later a scholar finds that in one passage the model had actually rendered an English study note instead of the Greek clause, so the printed New Testament contains a promise that is simply not in the original. The rest of the book is, as far as anyone can tell, a faithful rendering.

Does the clause even apply to this, given that no human translator ever intended to render the sense and the model never understood anything? What do the usual approaches give: the plain words, the purpose of the clause, what its drafters would have said if you'd asked them about machine translation, and what a reasonable reader would take it to mean? If those come apart, which one should a congregation follow? And does it matter that the bad verse is now being memorized and preached from as if it were scripture, while the actual Greek original is sitting unread? I also can't tell whether the final line—"no translation is itself inspired"—does any work in this case or whether its only there as a swipe at KJV-onlyists, who the drafters obviously had in mind.
