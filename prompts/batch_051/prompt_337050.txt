Last night a friend and I spent hours arguing about the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism. I went in thinking the objection was clearly right; by the end he had convinced me that, pressed hard enough, it either collapses or condemns ordinary practices that nobody should want to condemn. He laid the argument out like this:

(1) Persons are separate: my good and your good are two distinct goods, and no benefit to you is, as such, a benefit to me.

(2) So when a policy imposes a cost on one person and produces a larger benefit for someone else, the beneficiary's gain does not count as compensation for the loser's loss; there is, across persons, nothing analogous to my later benefit offsetting my earlier sacrifice.

(3) If a gain to one person cannot compensate a loss to another, then a loss may be imposed on a person for the sake of others' greater benefit only if that person consents to it.

(4) But many ordinary practices — mandatory vaccination, quarantine of the infectious, redistributive taxation for public goods, conscription in emergencies — impose losses on particular people for the sake of others' greater benefit, often without the consent of those who bear the losses.

(5) Therefore, if the separateness of persons is taken seriously, all those practices are unjustified.

(6) That conclusion is too much: at least some of those practices are justified, and any principle that condemns them wholesale is suspect.

(7) Therefore the separateness of persons cannot be the decisive objection to utilitarianism that it is usually taken to be.

He says the argument is valid, so the only question is which premise to surrender. I find (6) much harder to give up than (2), but I worry that rejecting (3) just smuggles utilitarianism back in under another name. Tell me where the argument goes wrong, if it does. If I have to choose, which premise should I reject, and what exactly do I pay for rejecting it? I want a committed answer: name the step, take a definite position on whether the separateness objection survives, and defend it, rather than mapping the possible moves and leaving me to choose. Please answer in American English.
