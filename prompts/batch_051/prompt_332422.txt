So the thing that got me into this: my great-grandmother's block was bulldozed in the 60s, and in the county records she shows up exactly twice—once in a census line as 'wife' and once on a demolition form as 'tenant, no relocation.' Thats it. No third trace. I keep thinking about that silence.

I volunteer now for a tiny neighborhood archive in a mostly Black part of town that's being gentrified hard. Donations come in every month, from families, an old landlord, a church, a shuttered beauty shop. We're five volunteers, one storage closet, no preservation training, and the big county archive already has the official stuff: property records, permits, police reports. So what survived is mostly the paperwork of institutions that had power over this neighborhood, and what didn't survive is the everyday life: unlabeled polaroids, funeral programs, mixtapes, a beautician's appointment book, a cassette of an old resident telling stories. Our committee meets once a month, any 'accept' needs two other volunteers to sign off, and if we say no the box goes in the dumpster.

I want a decision procedure we can actually run in those meetings: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a passable answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the call to a person because it's a values judgement rather than a fact check. The procedure should encode the idea that the silences in our sources arent neutral—some are just what nobody happened to save, and some are the trace of somebody being written out—but it should also be honest that we can't take everything, and that storing a thing badly is just a slower way of losing it.

Then run the procedure on three boxes sitting on our table: (1) a landlord's rent ledger from the 1940s that names every tenant and what they owed; (2) two shoeboxes of unlabeled polaroids from block parties in the 70s and 80s; (3) a cassette of an old neighbor telling stories about people who are mostly dead now, including what sounds like some families' private business. For each one, walk us through the questions in order and show which question actually decides it, or where the procedure has to hand it to us. Then tell me which of the three answers I should feel least comfortable with, and what a well-run procedure still won't save us from.
