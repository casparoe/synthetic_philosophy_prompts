I'm a novelist, so I come at this from the side of someone who thinks all day about sentences, not systems. Lately I keep getting dragged into an argument about whether games can be art. The version that finally made me sit up is the one that says a game's real medium isn't its graphics or story; it's the player's own agency. The designer builds a little machine of choices, pressures, and feedback loops, and what you experience is the shape of your own deciding—the weight of a commitment, the itch of a hedge. I can feel that because it's close to what I do with pacing and withheld information, except here the reader's role is being played by the player's choices.

So I've been using a rough working definition for an argument I want to write up. Call something a work of art if:
(a) it's an artifact, deliberately made and put in front of someone as a thing to be experienced;
(b) its maker shaped the material for the sake of the experience itself, not only to get some outside job done;
(c) its form is expressive—there's somthing it's about, or a feeling its made to bring into focus, and reasonable people can disagree about what it means;
(d) it's offered to an audience that at least partly attends to the experience for its own sake, not purely uses it.

The case I keep flipping on is a board game called Split, designed by a friend of mine who refuses to call himself an artist. It's for two players, played on a narrow board with a few dozen flat wooden pieces. There's no theme, no characters, and no words in the box except the rulebook. Each turn you face the same fork: take a small reversible step or make a large irreversible commitment, while secretly betting part of a finite store. My friend mostly cares about competitive balance and runs a ranked ladder for the game, but he once told me he was trying to make the texture of caution and regret something you could feel in your hands; afterward he tried to walk it back and said it's just a tight ruleset. People who love it talk like it's a portrait of their own temperament. People who hate it say it's just an elegant machine, a puzzle with no one behind it, not about anything.

So: by my four criteria, does Split count as a work of art? Work through all four tests and tell me where you land, but also flag the places where the tests themselves wobble. And could you answer in American English?
