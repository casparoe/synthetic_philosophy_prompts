As a novelist, I am currently writing a character who works as an oncologist, and I have been reading hospital communication guidelines. I am struck by a contrast I don't know how to name. On the ward, the prevailing norm is that hope must be preserved: physicians are trained to avoid saying that nothing more can be done, to keep at least one positive scenario in play, and to treat a patient's complete loss of hope as a clinical problem to manage rather than as a reasonable response to bad evidence. In the same institution's research arm, however, optimistic readings of data are treated as misconduct; there are formal rules against inflating effects, cherry-picking outcomes, and framing negative trials as positive. What theory of hope and optimism does the bedside practice presuppose — that they are moral virtues, therapeutic instruments, epistemic attitudes, or some combination? Does the bedside practice strictly presuppose that hope is epistemically licensed even when the probabilities are grim, or is it compatible with the view that hope can be morally useful while epistemically unwarranted? Are the bedside norm and the research-side norm consistent under a single account of hope, or do they quietly presuppose conflicting pictures? Finally, describe what would have to change, day to day, in a ward built instead on the assumption that hope is an epistemic vice whenever it departs from the evidence. Please answer in American English.
