Here's an asymmetry I've never been able to settle. Most people think an adult who lies to another adult, even to spare their feelings, has done something at least slightly wrong—something they should eventually apologize for or correct. But those same people, including ones who'd call themselves scrupulously honest, will run a planned, multi-year fiction for their children about Santa, the tooth fairy, and the Easter bunny—often while teaching the child that lying is wrong—and they don't think of themselves as liars at all. They enlist grandparents as co-conspirators, eat the cookies, plant half-eaten carrots and reciepts from the North Pole, and then reveal the whole thing around age eight expecting the kid to find it funny rather than feel played. If a company ran that operation on its customers for their own good, people would call it gaslighting. So my question is whether there's a principle that actually draws the line where ordinary judgment seems to draw it—between the Santa fiction and ordinary adult white lies—or whether this is just a sentimental exception we carve out because we remember the magic fondly. I want the strongest principle you can find, tested against hard cases: lying to a child about whether the dog is dead, keeping the Santa story going well into adolescence, or lying to an adult with advanced dementia to keep them calm. Then give me an honest verdict on whether to keep the asymmetry, narrow it, or abandon it. Also tell me how confident you are in the key claims and single out the one point you're least sure of.
