I've been going back and forth with a colleague who works on consequentialism, and I want a second opinion on whether his central claim is even testable. His claim is that for nearly any ordinary action—donating to a charity, having a child, taking a job—the long-run consequences ramify so quickly and unpredictably that we are in principle clueless about them: not merely that we lack data now, but that no amount of ordinary empirical work could give us defensible probabilities over the outcomes that determine whether the act was right. I keep saying this is an empirical claim about human forecasting ability wearing a conceptual hat. He says it's a point about the structure of the world and the limits of evidence.

How would someone actually test this? I want a concrete design: what data, what comparison, what result would support the cluelessness claim and what would count against it. Then be honest about the residue. If the best possible study came out one way or the other, what exactly would it settle and not settle? Would a study showing that long-run predictions are systematically awful support his claim, or only the much weaker claim that we are currently ignorant? And is there a part of the claim—something about what 'knowing enough' or 'having a defensible probability' requires—that no test can reach no matter how good?
