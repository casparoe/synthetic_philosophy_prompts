I've been chewing on something and keep going back and forth about wether it's deep or just a cute analogy. The hedonist wants to say pleasure is the one thing that is good, but look at examples: the pleasure of a hot shower, the pleasure of seeing someone you dislike fail, the pleasure of getting a proof to work, the pleasure of nicotine. As felt states they seem to have nothing in common—there's no single ingredient present in all of them in different amounts. Now the analogy. In programming, an interface (or typeclass, or duck typing) lets completely different data structures all count as, say, Comparable or Serializable. What unifies them is not what they're made of but that they implement certain operations. So the thought is: maybe pleasure is a functional kind rather than a felt kind. A state counts as a pleasure because of the role it plays—you want it to continue, you'd trade things for it, whatever the exact role is—and the heterogeneity of the underlying feelings is no more fatal to hedonism than the heterogeneity of an integer and a string is fatal to them both being Comparable. I'm trying to figure out whether this is a real structural analogy or whether the programming framing quietly assumes the very thing the hedonist needs to earn. What would have to be true about pleasure (and about value) for the functional move to work? And can you think of something the analogy predicts about pleasure that is actually false? I know the underlying worry has a name (the heterogeneity problem, I think), and I don't just want labels—I want to know whether the functional reading dissolves the problem or just renames it.
