So I cover the courts and the brain beat for a science-and-ideas magazine. Practically every week I'm on deadline trying to decide whether I can write that someone "chose" to do a thing, "could have done otherwise," or "acted of their own free will," versus weaselly stuff like "he was driven to it" or "he had no real choice." Last month there was a sentencing where a defense expert said frontal-lobe damage meant the guy couldn't control his impulses, and the copy desk kept rewriting my sentences in ways that I'm pretty sure smuggled in an opinion about free will either way. I took one philosophy class a decade ago and I remember that compatibilists and incompatibilists exist, but that doesn't help at 4 pm with a 6 pm file time.

Could you turn whatever the free-will literature actually supports into a decision procedure I can run on a specific case in about 15 minutes? I mean questions in order, what counts as a good-enough answer to each, where the procedure should stop and say "don't use free-will language at all," and where it has to punt to an editor because it's a seperate values call rather than a factual one. The newsroom constraint is that the science desk editor and the legal reviewer both have to sign off, and both will throw out anything that sounds like a philosophy seminar. Give me worked applications for three cases: a woman who drives the getaway car because her partner says he'll kill her if she refuses; a person diagnosed with psychopathy who spends months planning a fraud; and a defendant whose brain tumor really does seem to have taken away his impulse control, where the judge plans to sentence him all the same. I want the hard cases flagged — especially the difference between the universe being deterministic (or the brain being broadly predictable) and a particular person being unable to control a particular act — and at least one case where the procedure pushes me toward wording I'd rather not use. Keep the jargon inside your reasoning and put plain-language labels on the questions.
