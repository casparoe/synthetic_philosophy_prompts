I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. I recently encountered the idea of computational irreducibility: roughly, the claim that for some systems there is no shortcut for finding out what they will do, because the only way to know is to run the system itself, and any accurate description of its behavior can be essentially as long as the system's own execution. I'd like you to give me the most important implications of this idea for how I should think about understanding, explaining, and predicting the behavior of the software systems I work on.

In my day-to-day work I keep three activities distinct, and I suspect computational irreducibility pulls them apart in ways I can't fully articulate:

1. Prediction: given a system's current state and inputs, knowing what it will do next, or over some time horizon.
2. Post-hoc explanation: after something goes wrong, or unexpectedly succeeds, giving an account of why it happened that is shorter than replaying the whole execution.
3. Intervention: having a compressed mental model of the system that lets me change it, or foresee how a change will propagate, without simulating every detail.

Please spell out the most important implications of computational irreducibility for each of these three activities and for the relationships among them. In particular, I want to know:

- If a system is computationally irreducible, does that make post-hoc explanation impossible, or just different in kind from prediction?
- Can I build, control, or reliably modify a system I cannot compress or explain?
- Is irreducibility a property of the system itself or of the level at which I describe it? For example, a program's execution might be irreducible at the instruction level even though I can state its purpose in one sentence. Which level should I care about in practice?
- Are there concrete heuristics I should adopt when working with systems that resist the compressed understanding I normally aim for?
