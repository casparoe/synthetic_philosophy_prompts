Consider a rule proposed by a consumer-protection regulator for settling disputes in which what an AI system "believes" becomes legally relevant. Under the scheme, once an AI assistant has logged at least one thousand interactions, its developer must submit it for a recurring belief-profile certification. In each certification cycle, a rotating panel of five non-expert adults is shown a randomized sample of the system's de-identified transcripts and asked, for each candidate proposition drawn from the system's own assertions, whether the panelist would find it misleading if the developer later denied that the system "really believed" that proposition. A proposition is entered in a public belief register when at least four of the five panelists say yes. Registered beliefs can be cited in later litigation, advertising complaints, or warranty disputes. A developer may appeal a certification only through a live structured interview before a fresh panel, with all questions drawn from a fixed public bank that may not include direct questions about whether the system believes anything. A developer whose system is registered as believing that P and who continues to market it as incapable of belief altogether is then liable for deceptive practices.

Treat this scheme as a mechanism rather than as a moral or metaphysical position: what incentives does it create for developers—through training-data selection, stylistic choices, deliberate vagueness, or the curation of transcripts likely to be sampled—for panelists, and for users who know the register exists? Where would the scheme be gamed first, and what would happen to systems that end up with empty or sparse belief profiles? Finally, assess whether the most serious failure modes could be fixed without abandoning the underlying idea that lay attribution, rather than any architectural fact, should settle the question.
