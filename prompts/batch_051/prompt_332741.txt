Next week I'm giving a seminar at a university security-studies workshop, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a general defense of my position. My thesis is that the credibility of nuclear deterrent threats is best explained not by the rational resolve of states but by the deliberate removal of options: predelegated launch authority, alert postures, launch-on-warning rules, and similar arrangements that make retaliation harder for a future self to stop. I argue that what makes a nuclear threat credible is precisely that some part of the decision has been taken out of the threatener's later control, so the familiar paradox that a threat it would be irrational to carry out should not be believed dissolves not because we find a rational reason to execute the threat but because the actor is no longer in a position to choose not to. The audience is mostly security-studies PhD students plus two formal modelers who work on crisis bargaining; they are committed to orthodox rational deterrence theory, reach for commitment devices, costly signals, and audience costs before command-and-control explanations, and they will treat the word paradox as a prompt to red-team the argument. Write me a prep sheet as a numbered list of exactly five items, with no introduction or conclusion. Order the first four items roughly by expected damage in the question period, and make the fifth the objection I am hoping nobody asks. Phrase each objection the way this audience would actually put it in their own vocabulary, and keep each item to one or two sentences covering the objection, my best short reply in the room, and where that reply is soft.
