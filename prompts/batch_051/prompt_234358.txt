John von Neumann died in 1957, just as the era of modern computing was beginning. In his unfinished book The Computer and the Brain — published posthumously in 1958 from lectures he had planned to deliver at Yale — he argued that the brain should be understood as a computing machine, but one of a fundamentally different kind from the stored-program digital computers he had helped design. On his account, the nervous system operates in a massively parallel rather than serial way, and its mode of computation appears to be statistical rather than strictly logical: it seems to produce reliable overall behavior from large numbers of slow, imprecise, individually unreliable elements, rather than by executing a sequence of exact, error-free steps. He was also concerned with whether neural signaling is best described as digital, analog, or a mixture of the two.

Since his death, a different approach to computation has grown up. Modern machine learning — above all, deep neural networks trained by gradient descent, now scaled up into large language models, together with neuromorphic hardware — builds systems that in several respects resemble the picture von Neumann attributed to the brain more than they resemble the precise, step-by-step symbol manipulators of classical AI. They are massively parallel, low-precision, statistically trained, and in some cases analog-friendly, while remaining unreliable in ways he explicitly worried about.
