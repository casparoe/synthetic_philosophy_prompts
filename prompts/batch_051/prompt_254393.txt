Retired chemist here, recently fallen into political philosophy. I keep coming across the claim that what really legitimizes the governments in places like Singapore and, on some readings, China is performance—growth and order—rather than elections. I want to stress-test that idea with a mechanism instead of just arguing about it in the abstract.

So here's the setup. Imagine a small, rich, semi-authoritarian state writes into its constitution a renewable emergency provision. The government may suspend ordinary civil liberties and detain people without trial, but only for rolling two-year windows. At the end of each window, an independant panel certifies whether two targets were met over that period: real GDP per capita growth of at least 2%, and violent crime below a fixed rate or falling at a set pace. If certified, the emergency powers auto-renew; if not, they lapse until an election is held. The government defends this by saying it makes the performance-legitimacy deal explicit: prosperity and order are exactly what justify the arrangement, and the metrics make the deal credible.

Walk me through how this would actually play out. What does it incentivize the government to do to the statistics, the panel, and the definition of "violent crime"? Does tying legitimacy to measurable performance make the regime more accountable, or just better at optimizing the indicators? How does public perception get reshaped by watching the numbers? What happens in a recession? And can the scheme be saved with safeguards, or does it turn credibility into a self-dealing machine?

Wherever you come down, tell me what would change your mind—which consideration or evidence would push the verdict the other way, and which step in your analysis you're least sure about.
