Design a seventy-five-minute activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in decision theory (about 14 students) in which participants arrive, through their own choices, at the Allais paradox and then see the independence axiom as the precise constraint those choices violated. The labels "Allais paradox" and "independence axiom" must not be used until the debrief, and the point must be felt before it is named.

The setting is a bare seminar room with a whiteboard and scratch paper, plus a small real-stakes budget of roughly $30 in vouchers or cash equivalents so that gains are real but trivial. No advance reading and no introductory lecture.

The activity must run in three phases.

Phase 1, naked choices (15 minutes). Present four one-shot lotteries arranged as two binary choices, with payoffs scaled to the budget but preserving the standard common-consequence structure. In unscaled form the template is: Choice 1 offers a sure $1m versus a gamble with a 10% chance of $5m, an 89% chance of $1m, and a 1% chance of $0. Choice 2 offers an 11% chance of $1m, otherwise nothing, versus a 10% chance of $5m, otherwise nothing. Scale the numbers down consistently, keeping the large prize genuinely large relative to the sure prize or the certainty effect will not bite. Students record their choices privately, and the facilitator tallies the class so that any tension between the two majority choices is visible without commentary.

Phase 2, decomposed choices (15 minutes). Present exactly the same four lotteries again, but now drawn as two-chamber gambles in which an 89% common chamber is visibly separated from an 11% distinctive chamber. State plainly that each Phase 2 gamble is equivalent to its Phase 1 counterpart. Every student re-chooses privately, and the new tally is displayed beside the old one.

Phase 3, exposure (20 minutes). Endow each student with a ticket for whichever lottery they chose in each phase, and run a trading game in which one volunteer acts as an auditor. The auditor may post bid and ask prices for the lotteries, make exchanges, and make conditional offers keyed to the common and distinctive chambers made visible in Phase 2; students may revise their choices only by paying a small revision fee. The auditor's explicit goal is to assemble, from the class's stated Phase 1 and Phase 2 rankings, a sequence of individually acceptable exchanges that leaves the class demonstrably worse off. If the common-consequence pattern on its own does not support a strict money pump in the available time, the auditor should instead exploit any divergence between a student's Phase 1 and Phase 2 rankings of the same lottery, and the written plan must say which strategy is being used.

The debrief (25 minutes) must then do four things, in order. (i) Write the two choices side by side, cancel the common 89% chamber, and show that both choices reduce, inside the surviving 11% chamber, to a choice between a sure medium prize and a 10-in-11 chance of the large prize; the independence axiom therefore forces the same ranking in both choices, while the class produced opposite rankings. (ii) Lay out, in plain language before introducing any terms, exactly three ways to revise: abandon the independence axiom, abandon the view that a two-chamber gamble collapses into its one-shot equivalent, or change one of the two Phase 1 preferences. (iii) Price each route: losing independence means losing a single expected-utility representation of their risk attitude and opening room for the sequential exploitation Phase 3 gestured at; losing reduction means allowing differently described versions of the same gamble to be evaluated differently; revising a preference means overriding a first-order risk judgment they themselves recorded on paper. (iv) Close by naming the two labels and stating what has been felt: the pull of certainty is real, and the axiom is what turns scattered hunches into one coherent risk attitude.

End with a facilitator's note covering two things the exercise cannot reproduce. First, the certainty effect often weakens or vanishes with small stakes, so prescribe exactly what the facilitator does if the expected majority pattern fails to emerge: the fallback should show that whatever pattern did emerge still picks out two gambles whose common and distinctive parts can be separated, and the debrief can run on that pair. Second, the trading phase is illustrative rather than a proof with unlimited capital: state precisely which step is a dramatization and what assumption would have to hold for the auditor's sequence to be a strict arbitrage.

Format: open with a one-sentence bottom line stating what participants should have felt by the end of the hour; then give the runnable plan as numbered phases with timings and class-management details; then the debrief; then the facilitator's note. The entire answer must be no more than 1,400 words.
