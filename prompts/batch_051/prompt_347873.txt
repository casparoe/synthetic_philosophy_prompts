I'm a PhD student in the philosophy of finance, and I'm struck by an asymmetry between two informed trades. A collector buys an apparently minor painting at a garage sale for a few hundred dollars; the seller would not have sold at that price had she known the attribution, yet the trade is lawful and widely seen as a reward for expertise. A corporate executive buys shares in her own firm while in possession of material non-public information; the seller would not have sold at that price had he known, yet the trade is criminal in most securities-law regimes. Neither party lies; in both cases the profit tracks the other side's ignorance of price-relevant facts. Is there a principle that justifies the difference, or is it an institutional accident? A theoretical pressure deepens the question: the no-trade theorem, informally, says that rational parties who differ only in private information should learn from the other side's willingness to trade, so purely information-driven trade should unravel; this makes both trades odder than they first look. Please structure your response in four titled sections: (1) Sharper statement of the asymmetry (about 100 words); (2) The strongest case for treating the two traders differently (about 250 words); (3) The case that the distinction cannot bear the weight, tested against blurring variants such as a dealer who learns the attribution while appraising the painting for the same seller, and an executive buying anonymously on the open exchange (about 250 words); (4) Your verdict and its regulatory implications (about 150 words).
