I'm drafting the opening of a paper on moral compromise and I'd like a consistency check before I present it to people who will actually push back. Roughly, I've been thinking of compromise as individuals or groups with different values doing moral trades: each side gives up something they care about less in exchange for something they care about more, the way two countries trade. My commitments right now:

1. Value pluralism: there are several morally important things (care, liberty, loyalty, avoiding harm, whatever) that can't be reduced to a single good, and there's no common currency or complete ordering that settles every comparison between them.
2. Pareto improvement is a real moral reason: if a deal makes each side better off by its own lights, the parties have reason to take it, and others have reason to let it happen rather than block it. Refusing a deal only because you don't share the other side's values is normally a mistake.
3. But I also think some exchanges are off the table no matter how voluntary and no matter that each side's own values say they've gained. Buying somebody's vote, paying a very poor person for a kidney, a town taking money to run toxic waste through its water — these stay wrong, or at least presumptively wrong, even when everyone prefers the deal.
4. I'm not a relativist about these cases: a voluntary exchange can be genuinely exploitative or corrupting, and that its not just my values showing up. Yet I also don't buy that there's one correct moral theory that settles it all.
5. I think need and pressure can invalidate a deal: if someone only accepts because their alternatives are terrible, the agreement doesn't count as the kind of free choice that trade-based reasoning needs.

Do these hang together? I can tell their are tensions but I'm too close to it to see which are real and which are just apparent. Rank the actual tensions by how severe they are, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix would be. I'd also like to know which single revision would clean up the set the most. Be specific — don't dodge by saying they're all compatible at some abstract level or all hopeless.
