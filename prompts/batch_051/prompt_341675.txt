You are a political philosopher who works on urban justice, democratic theory, and the ethics of future generations. I want you to pressure-test a concept I have been developing, and I want you to be charitable and candid in equal measure: reconstruct the idea in its strongest form, compare it with nearby ideas, then say exactly where it breaks.

I have been calling the concept temporal gerrymandering. The motivating observation is that gerrymandering in the ordinary sense draws district boundaries through space to give one group of voters more representation than its numbers justify. Something structurally similar, I think, happens through time in local housing politics. Zoning, permitting, and historic-preservation decisions are made by an electorate that contains only people who currently live in the jurisdiction, or own property there, or can show up to a hearing. But the people affected by a decision not to build housing are not just those people. They include would-be residents who would have lived in the homes that never got built, residents of the wider metropolitan area who absorb the resulting price pressure, and future generations who inherit the built environment. The boundary of the relevant polity has been drawn around a moment rather than a territory, and it reliably advantages incumbents over everyone who is not yet in the room.

My central claim is stronger than the familiar point that NIMBY politics has bad downstream effects. I want to say that the exclusion of these future and would-be residents is not merely a policy failure or a bias; it is a structural defect in the decision procedure, more like malapportionment or a poll test than like a mistaken forecast. And this, I think, licenses treating many formally democratic housing decisions as procedurally illegitimate even when every rule was followed. Example: a planning board votes down a six-story apartment building after hearing from forty neighbors. On my view those forty neighbors are a gerrymandered constituency; the real constituency includes an unknown but real population of potential tenants scattered across the region, none of whom had standing in the proceeding. The system cannot see them, not because they are far away in space, but because they are in the future or just over the municipal line.

What I want from you, in order: (1) Is the concept coherent once cashed out, or does it depend on an untenable notion of representing merely possible people? (2) What are the nearest existing ideas — this has surely been said before under some name — and does my version do any work that those do not? (3) Where does it overgenerate? If every present-majority decision with long-term consequences disenfranchises the future, why would this not delegitimize all legislation, including climate policy and deficit spending? What is the principled line, if one exists, that makes the housing case special without swallowing democratic government whole? (4) If you had to defend the strongest restricted version of the idea, what exactly would it be restricted to?

Take your time and work through the strongest objections yourself before settling on a verdict; I would rather get a precise, slow assessment than a fast list of objections.
