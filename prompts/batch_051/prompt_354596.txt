Let's use Gallipoli. The rough story: in 1915 the Allies — mostly British and French, but with a large Australian and New Zealand contingent — landed on the Gallipoli peninsula to try to knock the Ottomans out of the war, and it turned into a bloody trench stalemate before they evacuatd after eight months or so with over a hundred thousand dead on all sides and nothing gained. Australia and New Zealand still mark 25 April as ANZAC Day, and the collective memory is that Gallipoli was where Australia came of age: a military defeat, sure, but the courage and mateship of the Anzacs proved the young nation's character, so the sacrifice is kept as a kind of founding moment. The historical record pushes back hard: the campaign was badly planned, had no realistic strategic payoff, the Australians were junior partners in a mostly British-run operation, and the nation-founding reading was built afterward, often by people with an interest in nation-building. So here's the task. Analyze Gallipoli through the distinction between collective memory and history, where memory serves a community's present identity and history aims at accuracy about the past. There's a comfortable line that they are just different enterprises and never really conflict; I want that line stress-tested against this case, not just stated. By the lights of the distinction, who should give way when the commemorative story and the record diverge? Does Gallipoli show that memory is doing something history can't do and shouldn't be expected to, or is it a case where the memory has simply hardened into a false record that ought to be revised? And what does the fact that the story survives and is defended a century on tell us about whether collective memory is a useful concept or just a soft way of refusing to update?
