I would like an assessment of the following argument against deontological constraints.

(1) A deontological constraint forbids an act type regardless of consequences: for instance, it is wrong for an agent to kill an innocent person even if that killing would prevent five other innocent people from being killed.

(2) If an act type is morally forbidden, then any instance of it is a bad thing, and there is always a moral reason to prevent or minimize bad things.

(3) So an agent always has a moral reason to minimize the number of forbidden acts of that type.

(4) In some cases the only way an agent can minimize that number is by performing one such act herself: killing one innocent person may be the only way to prevent five other killers from killing one innocent person each.

(5) In those cases, the agent has a moral reason to perform the forbidden act; and where that reason is not outweighed, she is permitted or required to perform it.

(6) Therefore the constraint is self-defeating: it forbids an act that, by its own underlying rationale, morality directs the agent to perform in exactly the situations where the constraint matters most.

My question is not whether the conclusion is unwelcome, but where exactly the argument goes wrong, if it does. Is it deductively valid? Which premise or inferential step fails, and on what reading? In particular, examine the move from "φ-ing is forbidden" to "there is a reason to minimize φ-ing," and the further move from "there is a reason to minimize φ-ing" to "the agent is permitted or required to φ." Does the argument slip between agent-relative and agent-neutral reasons at some point? If the argument succeeds only against one version of deontology, specify which version and say what a more careful deontologist should say in response. Finally, separate what is settled among specialists from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
