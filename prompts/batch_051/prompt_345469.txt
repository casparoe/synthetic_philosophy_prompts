I am a doctoral student in philosophy working on decision-making under uncertainty, and a cognitive policy I have drifted into is starting to trouble me. Whenever I assess an argument for strong longtermism — the claim that, because the number of lives that could exist in the future is astronomically large, the expected value of the far future dominates all near-term considerations — I find myself applying a kind of simulation discount. My rough reasoning is this. If there is a non-trivial probability that we are living in an ancestor simulation rather than in base reality, then the far future as I ordinarily imagine it is not a safe object of expectation: the simulation may be switched off long before billions of years elapse; the 'future' I am trying to steer may be an artefact whose persistence conditions I cannot infer; and the welfare that ultimately matters may belong to base-reality beings whose prospects are opaque to me and perhaps insensitive to anything I do from inside the simulation. In practice I do not disregard far-future considerations entirely, but I shrink them substantially, whilst several of my colleagues treat the simulation hypothesis and strong longtermism as nearly orthogonal.

A friend has objected that this is not really an epistemic policy at all, but a way of managing my unease with the demanding conclusions of longtermism. He points out that the simulation hypothesis could just as easily push in the opposite direction: simulated beings might have moral standing equal to biological ones, simulators might run their simulations further into the future than base reality could support, or I might have exactly the same kind of influence over a simulated future as over a base-reality one. I am not convinced by him, but I can see the shape of the worry, and it has made me want to examine the habit rather than simply continue using it.

The question I would like help with is therefore this: is my policy of discounting far-future expected value in proportion to my credence in the simulation hypothesis defensible, and if so, in what form? I want to know under what conditions, if any, a rational agent should let the possibility of living in a simulation reduce the weight given to far-future considerations; whether the policy is best understood as a legitimate response to model uncertainty, as a bias dressed up as a rule, or as something in between; and whether it could be restated so as to retain whatever is sound in it without smuggling in motivated reasoning. This is not a purely abstract matter for me: I am currently deciding how much of my own research should be oriented towards existential-risk mitigation rather than nearer-term problems, and my answer to that decision depends on whether this habitual discount can be justified.
