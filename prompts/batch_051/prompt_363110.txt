I lead a reading group in history and philosophy of science, and we have been comparing the Newton–Leibniz calculus dispute with the 1858 Darwin–Wallace joint presentation. In Darwin’s Autobiography—specifically in the section called Recollections of the Development of my Mind and Character—he recalls that when Lyell and Hooker proposed presenting his unpublished 1844 essay together with Wallace’s manuscript, he initially said no. His stated worry, as he describes it there, was that Wallace would view the arrangement as unfair; only after Lyell and Hooker pressed him did he reluctantly assent. I am not interested in the history of what happened, but in what this passage reveals about Darwin’s underlying commitments and how they would generalize.

If Darwin held roughly those attitudes about priority, individual recognition, and the ethics of simultaneous discovery, what would he most plausibly have thought about the later development of experimental collaborations in which papers routinely carry hundreds or thousands of authors, credit is assigned to the collaboration as a whole, and simultaneous or near-simultaneous discovery is treated as normal rather than as a dramatic coincidence? Would he likely have regarded that as a solution to the Wallace-type problem or as a new and worse distortion of scientific credit? Please give a specific counterfactual reconstruction of Darwin’s likely position, grounded in the autobiographical passage I described, and mark clearly where the passage underdetermines the extrapolation.
