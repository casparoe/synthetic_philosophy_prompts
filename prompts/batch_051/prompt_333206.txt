I have been reading about expressivism for a school philosophy discussion group, and I am struck by how long the Frege–Geach problem has been with us. The difficulty seems to be that if moral judgements express attitudes rather than beliefs, it becomes unclear how they can mean the same thing when embedded in conditionals, disjunctions, or negations, and how they can figure in valid inferences such as modus ponens. After decades of proposed solutions, though, the debate seems not to have moved: each proposal is accused either of smuggling in descriptive content or of making the attitudes behave so much like beliefs that expressivism loses its point. I want to understand what kind of disagreement this really is. Is it a substantive philosophical problem, a disguised demand for a formal semantics, or partly a dispute about what expressivism was meant to explain in the first place? My own suspicion is that the two sides have different views about what a solution must preserve—ordinary moral reasoning, the non-descriptive character of moral judgement, or the wider anti-realist project—but I should like that suspicion tested. Given your diagnosis, is there a distinction, formal result, or change of question that would actually count as progress?
