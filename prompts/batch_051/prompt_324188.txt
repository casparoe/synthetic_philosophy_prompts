"Higher-order evidence is evidence about your evidence: it doesn't bear directly on whether p, but on the quality of your assessment of p. In the proof-checking drug case, I work through a long derivation and it looks valid; then I'm told I was given a drug that makes people overconfident specifically about checking that kind of proof. The derivation is first-order evidence for the theorem, and the drug information is higher-order evidence, since it gives me a reason to distrust my evaluation of the proof.

The puzzle is this. On a simple 'follow the evidence' picture, I should believe the theorem because the proof supports it. But the higher-order evidence gives me a reason to be less confident in the theorem even though no particular step of the proof has been impugned. So I either ignore information about my own reliability, which seems dogmatic, or I let information that isn't about the theorem lower my confidence in the theorem, which seems strange. That's the puzzle.

For akrasia, I think epistemic akrasia can be rational. In the drug case, I can rationally keep believing the theorem, since the proof still looks valid to me, while also rationally believing that my evidence doesn't support that belief very strongly, because the drug means my sense of obviousness isn't trustworthy. That combination just is akrasia, and it's rational here because the alternatives are worse: giving up a sound-looking proof punishes good first-order reasoning, and ignoring the drug information is dogmatic. So a rational agent can keep the first-order belief while holding that her evidence doesn't support it. The same should apply whenever higher-order evidence reveals that your faculties are compromised: you're allowed to keep the first-order belief and accept the mismatch."
