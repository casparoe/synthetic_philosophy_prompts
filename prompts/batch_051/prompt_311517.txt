I serve on the governance and ethics committee of a county council. We are being asked to approve the settlement terms in a constructive-dismissal claim brought by a former fire-safety officer. The proposed settlement includes a non-disclosure clause: the officer would agree not to discuss with the press or public a report he wrote on fire-safety defects in several council-maintained schools, and not to confirm that the report exists, though he would remain free to repeat the defects to the local fire authority and the Health and Safety Executive. The council's position is that nothing is being hidden from any body that can act: the regulators have the report, the defects are being remediated, release would expose commercially sensitive elements of the maintenance contracts under which the schools are run, and the clause is simply the price of settling quickly and avoiding litigation. Local journalists have already asked for the report under freedom-of-information rules, and the council has refused on commercial-interest grounds; some members worry that approving the non-disclosure clause would convert that refusal into a permanent silence bought with public money. The officer appears willing to accept the settlement, which leaves the committee as the only body that can ask whether the public interest favours paying for his silence.

Draft a policy memo of roughly 800 words for the committee, recommending whether we should approve the settlement as drafted, reject it outright, or approve it subject to modified confidentiality terms. The memo should be a document we could act on: a clear recommendation with the grounds for it, a fair and honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side, and a clear separation of the question 'should this information become public?' from the question 'is it defensible for a public body to contract away its disclosure as part of a settlement?'. We are not asking for legal advice or a survey of cases; we want the committee's own reasoning. Please write in British English.

Finally, include a short closing section saying what would change your mind: which factual claim or argument, if it turned out differently or proved stronger than you currently judge, would lead you to the opposite recommendation, or where your reasoning is most vulnerable to being wrong.
