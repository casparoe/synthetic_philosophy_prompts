I am a novelist, and I have spent the past year drafting a book whose central character responds to a professional failure by adopting a stringent daily regimen: she rises before dawn, eats sparingly, drinks only water and weak tea, and refuses every comfort she can remove. She narrates this as taking command of herself; her sister, a physician, reads it as self-punishment dressed as virtue. I had taken this to be a private ambiguity in my manuscript, but the more I listen to ordinary speech the more it seems to belong to our shared judgments. We praise a marathoner's dietary restraint as temperance, yet we treat a nearly identical pattern of abstention in a grieving person as a symptom of depression. We admire the founder who denies herself sleep and leisure for a venture, but when an unemployed person refuses rest we are readier to call it ritualized self-punishment. The same self-denial that is called healthy discipline in one life is called pathology in another whose ends we do not value.

For the purpose of the question, stipulate that the felt desire, the effort of refusal, and the cost to the person are identical in each pair; only the end served and the social reading of the person differ. Is that difference enough to justify the asymmetry in our judgments, or is the asymmetry a confusion we merely live with? I would like you to reconstruct the strongest case that self-control is a virtue in itself; the strongest case that it is only a prudential instrument, valuable when it serves worthy ends and harmful otherwise; and the strongest case that what we call temperance is often repression wearing a more flattering name. Then confront the asymmetry directly and say whether it should be preserved, reversed, or relinquished, defending your verdict against the stipulated identity of the restraint itself.
