Draft the document that would emerge from a structured adversarial collaboration between two parties: a philosopher who defends sweatshop labor as a case of mutually beneficial exploitation, and a philosopher who maintains that sweatshop labor is wrongful exploitation even when it is mutually beneficial and voluntary in the minimal sense that workers choose it over their alternatives. Produce the text both could sign after a good-faith collaboration, with the remaining disagreement made exact; do not declare a winner.

Begin with a numbered Agreed Claims section. The claims should not be empty commonplaces; they should capture substantive convergence about how to describe the workers' baseline alternatives, what is and what is not chosen in such transactions, the fact that an exchange can be welfare-improving for both parties while still being characterized by severe inequality in bargaining power, and why the debate matters even granting that the jobs are better than the alternatives. If the two camps use the term exploitation in different senses, state those senses explicitly.

Then provide a Disagreements table. For each remaining point of difference, give precise formulations of both positions, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording, and say explicitly whether any part of the dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive.

Finally, provide a Cruxes and Predictions section. Identify the sub-questions that, if settled, would genuinely move at least one party. For each crux, specify a concrete case, empirical pattern, or argument that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Include at least these cruxes, developed precisely: (i) the correct baseline against which to judge the terms of employment; (ii) whether background poverty undermines consent in a way that makes the exchange wrongful; (iii) the non-worseness claim, understood as the principle that, in the absence of a prior obligation to assist, making someone better off than they otherwise would have been cannot be wrong merely because the other party could have done still more; and (iv) whether purchasing such goods makes consumers complicit and creates obligations to boycott or to press for higher standards. Add further cruxes if the argument requires them.
