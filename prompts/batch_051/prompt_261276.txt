In an ethics class a while back I got this checklist for when someone has the standing to blame another person for a wrong:

1. They aren't currently doing the same kind of thing themselves.
2. They didn't help bring about the particular wrong (no complicity).
3. They don't keep enjoying benefits from the wrong — or if they do, they're actively trying to undo them.
4. They don't exempt themselves from the norm they're applying to other people.

Now consider Priya. She spent eight years as an analyst in the contracts division of a big poultry company, building the models that set pay schedules and feed-conversion penalties for the contract farmers who raise the chickens. She now thinks those models were predatory: the company supplies the chicks, feed, and medicine, the farmers borrow to upgrade their barns, and the pay formula leaves them just solvent enough to keep going. She left that team, flagged her concerns internally, and moved into a food safety compliance role. Since then she writes op-eds and tweets blaming the whole industry for exploiting contract growers. But she still works for the same company, her salary and stock come out of the system she denounces, and the company sometimes points to her criticism as proof its listening. She doesn't usually flag her own past role in the op-eds, though she's honest about it when asked.

Go through the four criteria and give her credit where you can. Where the answer is no, say whether that's because of the criteria or because of the case, and whether any one criterion is doing the real work. Then give a bottom line: does Priya have standing to blame? And does this case show the checklist needs fixing? Last, try to separate whether her blame is correct from whether she has standing to issue it, and say if that distinction survives this case.
