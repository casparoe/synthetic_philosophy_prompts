I have an epistemic habit I'd never really examined until my sister and I got into it at dinner last week. I said something like "ducks lay eggs" and she said that's false, because male ducks don't. I said obviously I know that, but the sentence is still true in the way I meant it. She asked whether "ducks are female" would then be true too, since some ducks are female. I said no, and she said that's exactly the problem: I have no consistent rule for which exceptions I'm allowed to ignore. My actual policy, the one I've been running on for years, is to keep using these bare sentences — ducks lay eggs, mosquitoes carry malaria — and treat them as true enough to reason and act from, without adding "some" or "most" or "usually." When I tried to rephrase, "some mosquitoes carry malaria" felt absurdly weak, like a random fact about individual mosquitoes rather than the thing that makes malaria spread, and "most mosquitoes carry malaria" is flat false, since only females of a few species transmit it. So Ive kept the policy: use the bare generic, rely on it for action (I take malaria pills when I travel somewhere with mosquitoes), and unpack the exceptions only when the situation makes them relevant. Is this defensible? Or am I just protecting my right to overgeneralize? Is there a principled way to say when exceptions can stay packed inside the sentence and when they have to be spelled out?
