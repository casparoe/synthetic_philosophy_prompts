Yesterday evening I argued for hours with a close friend who works in philosophy of medicine, about whether the line between health and disease is discovered or imposed. He noticed that I kept dragging the conversation back to cut points on receiver-operating curves, label noise, and unstable case definitions; I noticed that he kept gesturing at an evaluative ingredient that never showed up as a variable. I want that translation done properly, and then I want to know where it fails.

The debate at issue is the naturalism–normativism dispute. On the naturalist side, the rough view is that a condition is a disease when it involves statistically subnormal biological functioning relative to an organism's reference class, so that disease is a value-free fact medicine discovers. On the normativist side, classifying something as a disease always includes the judgment that it is bad for the person, harmful, or at least that medical intervention is warranted; the category is partly evaluative and cannot be reduced to biological statistics alone.

Restate each side, and the dialectic between them, in the vocabulary I actually work in: a continuous risk score and the threshold at which it is turned into a label; reference populations and base rates; label noise and concept drift; calibration; and the distinction between a model's output and the decision rule built on top of it. Treat the score-threshold device as central. Use it to recast the following: whether the disease label is a natural class or an imposed decision boundary; how the reference population should be selected; whether harm is an additional input feature, a label criterion, or something that cannot be represented as a model input at all; and whether either side is committed to a true label independent of the use to which the classification will be put.

Stress-test the translation on one boundary case: the progressive lowering of the threshold at which a continuous marker such as blood pressure or glucose is declared abnormal, so that people acquire diagnoses they would not have received under older standards. Concretely, say which side better predicts and makes sense of that kind of change. I suspect it shows only that biology is continuous and decisions need cut points; my friend insists it shows that value cannot be removed from the classification.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly into scores, thresholds, and labels; which claims translate only under distortion; and which claims have no expression in this vocabulary at all. For each item of residue, say whether it is something genuine that my vocabulary cannot see, or a distinction the translation reveals to be less substantive than it looks. Finish with a verdict on whether my friend is right that the evaluative component of disease categories is irreducible, or whether my vocabulary shows that much of the argument is threshold-setting dressed up as metaphysics. You can assume I am fluent in the modeling vocabulary, but do not assume I know the philosophical literature beyond the sketch just given.
