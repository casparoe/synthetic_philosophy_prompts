Consider the counterterrorism regime familiar from many liberal democracies, described at the level of its working parts rather than any single statute. An executive authority compiles a list of proscribed organizations. Membership in a listed organization is a criminal offence, as is providing such an organization with money, training, expertise, or other material support — even where the support would be directed at lawful, nonviolent activities or would facilitate negotiations. Separate offences criminalize preparation for and public encouragement of attacks before any attack occurs. Meanwhile, a state's own forces are not placed on such lists and are not charged under these statutes when they deliberately kill civilians, while an attacker with no organizational ties can be convicted of terrorism on the strength of his or her ideology and choice of target alone.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of terrorism — or of what makes terrorism wrong — that this package of practices presupposes. Specifically: does the practice strictly presuppose that terrorism is essentially non-state and group-based, or is it compatible with a purely act-based account of terrorism (the deliberate killing of civilians, or the threat of it, in order to coerce a wider audience) that is simply enforced through the pragmatic device of a list? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires by way of a definition, what it merely fits well with, and where it is philosophically underdetermined. Which elements of the package are genuinely load-bearing for a definition of terrorism — the political or ideological motive, the non-state character of the actor, the organizational character, the selection of civilian targets, the coercive or communicative intent — and which are merely connotative, such that abandoning them would leave the legal regime mostly intact?

Identify the places where the practice appears to presuppose inconsistent things: for example, where designation is treated as tracking an objective property of the organization while the decision to list or de-list responds to executive and diplomatic considerations, or where support offences treat the organization as a unit whose wrongdoing contaminates every form of assistance while the trial and punishment of individuals treat wrongdoing as personal conduct. Conclude by saying what would have to change in the law and its enforcement if the implicit theory were replaced with a strict act-based definition of terrorism that applies to state and non-state violence alike.
