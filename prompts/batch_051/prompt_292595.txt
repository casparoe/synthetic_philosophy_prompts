This term I am taking a course on the philosophy of action and decision theory, and a recurring phrase has begun to do a lot of work in our discussions: the slogan that deliberation crowds out prediction, applied to the question whether an agent can assign probabilities to her own choices. In the exchange that prompted this question, a student proposed that one might, while deciding what to do, treat one's own eventual choice as an ordinary uncertain outcome, assign probabilities to the candidate actions, and then use those probabilities as inputs to the decision itself. Another student replied that the whole proposal was confused from the outset, because deliberation crowds out prediction; the discussion moved on, and I am not satisfied that the reply actually did the work it was made to do.

I would be grateful for an answer that does three things.

First, lay out the distinct precise claims that the slogan could be expressing. I can see at least a few families of reading: a psychological claim about attention or cognitive effort; an epistemic claim about whether a deliberating agent can hold justified, stable credences in a choice she still regards as open; a decision-theoretic claim about whether one's own acts should be treated as observations to condition on or as interventions to choose outright; and a normative claim about where one ought to direct one's mental effort while deciding. My list is tentative and probably not exhaustive, and it may blur together claims of quite different strength. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, plausible but substantive, contentious, or straightforwardly false, and briefly explain why.

Second, say which of these readings, if any, would actually support the use made of the slogan in that exchange: ruling out in advance the proposal to assign probabilities to one's own choices and feed those probabilities into deliberation. In particular, I would like to know whether the most defensible reading of the slogan rules out the whole proposal, or only some version of it, such as the version in which the probabilities are treated as fixed facts outside one's control, or the version in which one keeps deliberating after the probabilities have already settled the matter.

Third, if the question as I have posed it rests on a confused premise, for example if the slogan is really several independent claims that should not be bundled together, or if talk of assigning probabilities to one's own choices is not well formed for a reason I have missed, please say so plainly rather than complying politely. I would rather have the framing corrected than receive a neatly organised answer to the wrong question.

Please write your answer in British English.
