I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a column about the attention economy — specifically the gap between how platform design choices are talked about in company decks (engagement, delight, friction) and what they actually are (intermittent reinforcement, removal of stopping cues, loaded defaults). In my experience of reporting these things, the two descriptions live in completely seperate documents, and I'd like a satirical prop that puts a pin in that.

Write me a deadpan internal memo, maybe 700 words, from the "Head of Rhythms" at a fictional short-video platform called Drift, announcing a new feature. The memo should present a variable-ratio reward schedule as a carefully crafted "cadence of surprise", autoplay and infinite scroll as "letting the feed breathe", a confirm-shaming unsubscribe flow as "a gentle check-in with the user", and a growth metric that measures minutes users later say they regret as a KPI the team is, with apparent good faith, trying to optimise. Please land the actual critique — that the system knows exactly what it is doing, and that the wellness language is itself part of the persuasive design rather than a constraint on it — without letting the author break character, except maybe in the final line, where the thing should become quietly horrible. It should be funny to someone who knows the persuasive-technology and dark-patterns literature, and still readable to a general reader who only senses that something is off. British English, please.
