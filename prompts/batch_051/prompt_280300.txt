I'm in a seminar on collective responsibility and the ethics of participating in wrongdoing, and I've settled into a view about complicity that I want pressure-tested rather than merely restated. The view is this: complicity is fundamentally a participatory rather than a causal notion. What makes someone complicit in another's wrongdoing is not that the person's contribution made a difference to whether or how the wrong occurred, but that the person intentionally aligned themselves with the wrongdoer's project. A bookkeeper who knowingly falsifies records for an organization committing atrocities is complicit even if the killings would have gone ahead unchanged without his help; a shareholder who votes for a corporate policy she knows will harm people is complicit even though her single vote is causally inert. Conversely, someone whose actions happen to facilitate a wrong while sharing none of the wrongdoer's purposes — a courier who unknowingly carries a package, or a reluctant engineer who is coerced — is not complicit in the relevant sense, or at least not in virtue of the causal contribution alone.

I want a map of this position's exposure. What would count against it? I'm not looking only for the standard objections, but for the cases, arguments, and theoretical pressures that should actually move me to revise or abandon the view. For each candidate defeater, tell me whether it would be fatal or merely awkward, and say which ones a committed defender of the participatory account would concede as genuine costs and which they would dismiss as changing the subject. In particular, I'm interested in counterexamples where the alignment condition is unambiguously met but the intuition of complicity is absent; whether the account over-implicates, making complicit everyone who votes for a policy, buys a product, or pays taxes with foresight of some downstream harm; whether alignment can be specified without covertly importing causal notions such as encouragement, enabling, or signaling; and whether the account threatens to collapse into a theory of wrongful intention or solidarity rather than complicity. Finally, I'd like an honest assessment of whether the view, once its standard qualifications are added, has been insulated into near-unfalsifiability, and if so whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. Personally, I would be most persuaded by a case in which someone plainly satisfies the alignment condition yet is not complicit, or is plainly complicit without either aligning with or causally contributing to the wrong.
