I spent my working life as an experimental scientist, and in retirement I have taken up moral philosophy with a beginner's appetite. My old habit is to isolate variables, and I have become preoccupied with an asymmetry in the way gratitude is ordinarily distributed—an asymmetry I would like you to examine rather than merely explain away.

Consider a controlled comparison. A person's house catches fire. One neighbour sees the flames, runs over, and extinguishes them, taking some personal risk, because he is concerned for the homeowner. A second neighbour never aims at the homeowner's good; he is on his own roof hosing down the wall that adjoins the burning house to protect his own property, taking a comparable risk, and his action saves the house as a side effect. The benefit to the homeowner is the same, and the risk and effort are, by stipulation, equal. Yet ordinary moral judgment treats the two cases very differently: to feel no gratitude toward the first neighbour would be called ingratitude, a defect, whereas toward the second we would at most expect a polite word of thanks, and no one would call the homeowner ungrateful for feeling nothing in particular.

So gratitude is not evidently tracking the size of the benefit received, nor the risk borne, nor the scale of the causal contribution. It appears to track something about the benefactor's orientation toward the beneficiary—roughly, that he acted for the beneficiary's sake. My question is whether that distinction can bear the weight the ordinary practice puts on it. Please take the strongest candidate justification for the asymmetry and test it against a series of nearby cases: (i) a benefactor who helps only because it will improve his reputation; (ii) a benefactor who helps from a cold sense of duty, with no warm feeling for the beneficiary; (iii) a dog or a machine that saves the beneficiary; (iv) a long-dead ancestor whose decisions still benefit the beneficiary; and (v) plain luck. For each case, say whether gratitude is owed, whether its absence would be a vice, and which side of the original comparison it most resembles.

Finally, render a verdict: is the ordinary asymmetry between the deliberate benefactor and the accidental benefactor justified as it stands, should it be revised, or should it be abandoned? In particular, say whether the principle doing the work is intention, relationship, the meaning the act has within a shared life, or something else—and whether that principle covers all the cases without being ad hoc.
