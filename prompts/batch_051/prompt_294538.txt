I spent my career doing laboratory work in physics—mostly instrumentation, not theory—and since I retired I've gone down a philosophy of science rabbit hole. The thing I can't shake is the debate over whether string theory and the multiverse are confirmable at all, or whether the field has quietly redefined confirmation to mean something like "nicely coherent, mathematically fecund, and nobody has a better idea." When I was in graduate school the Popperian line was that a theory that can't be falsified isn't science. Now respectable people defend the view that a theory can accumulate rational support without any new empirical predictions, through factors like internal consistency, explanatory power for things we already knew, and the absence of live rivals. Meanwhile the landscape/multiverse cuts both ways: the feature defenders say explains the fine-tuned constants is the same one critics say destroys predictivity.

I've been arguing about this with an old labmate. I keep landing around 0.3 that string theory (or a successor in its immediate neighborhood) gets empirically confirmed this century; he's around 0.7. I want your own number, but I mostly want the reasoning decomposed so I can see where the disagreement lives.

Here's the proposition I want a credence on, stated as precisely as I can manage: by 2075, the main research program descended from string theory will have become the broad consensus framework for quantum gravity on the strength of at least one discriminating empirical success—an observation or experiment that supports it against its live competitors. If you think that's the wrong target, say what you'd replace it with, but give me a number anyway.

Decompose it. I want the sub-questions your number depends on, each with a rough probability and a sentence of justification. At minimum: (a) whether non-empirical theory confirmation of the sort associated with Richard Dawid and others is rationally legitimate at all; (b) whether the string landscape really destroys predictivity, or there are surviving observable consequences that would discriminate it; (c) whether cosmology, gravitational-wave astronomy, or some other line of work will plausibly deliver such a test in the next fifty years; (d) whether the sociological process by which a field reaches consensus would even track evidence, as opposed to funding, fads, and generational turnover. Add any other terms you think belong, and show how the probabilities combine to your headline number.

Then tell me which single sub-question your headline credence is most sensitive to, which one the gap between my 0.3 and my labmate's 0.7 most likely comes down to, and what evidence or argument in the next decade or two would move you most, and in which direction.

I want a committed answer, not a survey of options. Give me your creedence and defend it.
