I keep noticing a pattern in philosophical arguments and I want to know whether it has an established name — and, more than the name, where exactly the line falls. Some arguments run as follows: a proposed explanation raises the same difficulty one level down, the same difficulty would arise again at the next level, and so on without end; this unendingness is treated as a decisive objection. In other contexts, a philosopher says nearly the same thing and treats it as the view working properly: the explanation is deliberately open-ended, each step sheds light on the next, and the fact that the chain never bottoms out is advertised as a feature rather than a defect. For example, a theory of vision on which seeing involves an inner picture is attacked because someone would then have to see the inner picture, and then to see that seeing, and so on; yet a view of knowledge on which a belief is justified by another belief, and that by another, with no final unsupported belief, is sometimes defended as a mutually supporting network rather than dismissed as an endless deferral of justification. Is there an established criterion for when an unending chain of this sort is fatal to a theory and when it is harmless or even doing real work? I expect there are labels for the two sides, but I don't want just the labels; I want the test that divides the cases, the boundary cases that come out messy, and an honest account of whether the division is principled or only rhetorical.
