W. E. B. Du Bois argues in his 1926 essay "Criteria of Negro Art" that all art is propaganda, whether artists intend it or not, and he also urges Black artists to make their art serve the aims of racial justice. But the same essay is full of language about beauty, and he seems to take beauty seriously in its own right, not merely as decoration on top of a message. I have been trying to work out how the beauty side and the propaganda side are supposed to fit together, and I keep finding several different readings. Below are five candidate readings. Please rate each one from 0 to 10 as a reading of the whole essay, where 0 means it ignores half the text and 10 means it captures what Du Bois is actually committed to without strain. Then say which reading you would assign the highest score to, and explain briefly what feature of the essay drives your ranking.

1. Instrumentalism: beauty has no independent value; it is only the vehicle that makes the political message effective and palatable.
2. Pluralism: beauty and propaganda are two separate criteria for art, and good Black art must satisfy both at once, with neither automatically overriding the other.
3. Collapse-of-the-distinction view: for Du Bois, Black beauty is itself a political refusal of white supremacy, so making something genuinely beautiful and making something propagandistic are not two separate achievements.
4. Descriptive-versus-prescriptive ambiguity: Du Bois uses "propaganda" in two different senses, one describing what all art does anyway and one prescribing what Black artists ought to do deliberately, and the apparent tension is mostly verbal rather than substantive.
5. Genuine inconsistency: the essay pulls in two directions and cannot be fully reconciled, because Du Bois wants art to serve the race while retaining an aesthetic standard that he never clearly subordinates to politics.
