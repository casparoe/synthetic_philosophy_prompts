I'm putting together a two-page proposal for a dissertation fellowship in philosophy of language, and I keep circling back to an argument I kept having in a seminar last spring. Everyone reaches for the 'etymological fallacy' charge whenever someone tries to settle what a word means now by pointing to where it came from — like saying 'hysterical' comes from the Greek for womb, so its current use is somehow tainted. Fine. But I've noticed philosophers doing something adjacent with word histories that isn't so easy to dismiss: they use an older sense to recover a distinction that got flattened (the way 'substance' now has to cover stuff, material, what underlies, and a kind of being at once), or they use a word's older career to pry a concept apart from the label it currently wears. So my half-formed idea is that 'etymological fallacy' is really a cover term for several different moves — some clearly bad, some maybe legitimate — and people wave it around as if one charge settles all of them, when the actual question is which moves are which and why.

Turn that into a proposal. I want the research question stated so it has a determinate answer (something like: under what conditions, if any, can a word's etymological or historical sense legitimately do argumentative work in philosophy, once we grant it can't fix current meaning?), an account of why the two standard positions — dismiss every origin-appeal as a genetic fallacy, or defend it as the word's 'true' meaning — both leave that question open, and a method: what I'd actually do month by month over the year, where the cases would come from, how I'd avoid cherry-picking, and why the approach I take is better than just collecting examples that favor one side. Also give me a sense of what the possible conclusions are, what would count as the project failing, and an honest scope note so it doesn't balloon into a history of every word I like.
