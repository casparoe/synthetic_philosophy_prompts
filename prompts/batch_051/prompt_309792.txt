I serve on my school's academic affairs committee, which has been asked to propose a make-up exam policy. The issue became urgent after two students missed the same chemistry midterm and both requested a make-up. The first was injured in a car accident on the way to school and spent the morning in an emergency room. The second skipped the exam to attend a concert in a neighboring city, betting that the attendance rules would not be enforced and that she could retake the exam later. The principal has provisionally proposed a rule: make-ups will be granted only when a student has a documented medical or family emergency; in every other case, the original grade stands. I find this rule appealing, but I cannot yet defend it. Another committee member argues that both students are simply unlucky: one was unlucky to be hit by a car, the other was unlucky to have the kind of temperament that leads her to take that gamble. She says a fair policy should not let a grade depend on luck, and that the principal's rule penalizes students for misfortunes of temperament just as surely as for misfortunes of circumstance. That worry seems serious to me, because people do not choose their temperaments or upbringings; but if we accept it, I do not see how any student can ever be held accountable for a decision. How should I think about this? Should a make-up policy distinguish bad luck from bad choices, and can that distinction survive the fact that our choices are themselves shaped by factors we never chose? What rule should I propose, and how should I justify it to the rest of the committee?
