I'm a cog sci grad student trying to settle on a dissertation topic, and I keep coming back to linguistic relativity. The field feels strange to me right now: the old Whorf-versus-universalist war is basically over — nobody serious thinks language is a total prison, and nobody serious thinks language leaves cognition untouched — but a lot of the empirical results feel kind of brittle. You've got the color-term work (Russian blues, categorical perception), spatial frames of reference, time metaphors, grammatical gender effects on object concepts, maybe evidentiality shaping source memory and epistemic vigilance. Some of it replicates, some effects are tiny, and some might just be "thinking for speaking" rather than deeper cognition. I don't want to spend five years churning out one more cross-linguistic difference; I'd rather work on something that actually moves the philsophical debate, like what it would mean for language to be constitutive of a thought rather than merely biasing it. Act as a philosopher of cognitive science who works on these issues. Start by brainstorming three or four candidate open questions at the right altitude — not "does language shape thought?", but the more precise questions that survive after the big war. Then rank them for a PhD student with about three years, some experimental training, and no appetite for fieldwork in an underdocumented language. Defend the ranking with explicit criteria: how many other questions does this one settle or reframe, does it require settling what counts as a genuinely linguistic effect, is it tractable without a huge cross-linguistic dataset, and what empirical result would make you drop it. I want the reasoning more than the list.
