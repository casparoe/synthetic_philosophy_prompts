I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of fiction, and I'd like to test a working account of truth in fiction. Let a sentence P be true in a fiction F just in case (1) F explicitly represents P; or (2) P follows from what F explicitly represents together with the real-world regularities holding in F's setting, unless the text blocks them; or (3) P is required by the best interpretation of F. Now consider the sentence "Sherlock Holmes had a great-grandmother." The Holmes stories represent Holmes as a human being, but they do not settle who his great-grandmother was, and no plot point turns on her existence; ordinary human biology nonetheless entails that any human with a normal line of descent has one. So criterion (2) seems to classify the sentence as true in the stories. Does it? If so, is criterion (2) too permissive, and how should it be restricted—for example, to regularities that competent readers would actually import, or to facts that matter to the plot? Or should the sentence be treated as neither true nor false in the fiction? Please answer in American English.
