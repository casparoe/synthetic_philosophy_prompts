Ive spent most of my working life in neuropharmacology, and since I retired I've been reading philosophy in the evenings—so forgive me if this is a question the field has already chewed over. Lately I keep turning over a thought experiment, and I want a genuinely argued prediction rather than a story. Suppose that tomorrow a drug becomes cheap and widely available—call it Sorrow-X—that specifically blocks the ache of sadness and grief, and does nothing else. A bereaved person still remembers the loss, still knows it would normally be devastating, and can reason about it clearly. The drug doesn't blunt physical pain, fear, anger, love, or joy, and it doesn't produce euphoria. Its effect fades within a few days unless the person keeps choosing to take it, but its cheap, non-addictive, and has no withdrawal. Now suppose that over the next ten years, roughly a third of adults take it at least once after a bereavement, a divorce, or a similarly serious loss.

What does the social world look like at the end of those ten years? I don't want bullet points. Work through the mechanisms: mourning rituals, how people read whether someone actually cared, the way people enter and leave attachments, the informal moral pressure on people who don't grieve visibly, and whatever else you think matters. For each prediction, say how confident you are, and say what assumption about human psychology would have to be wrong for that prediction to fail. Write in American English, and lead with your bottom line in the first sentence—no preamble and no restating of the question.
