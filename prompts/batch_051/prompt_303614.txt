I've been trying on my own to make sense of a skeptical line of thought about "qualia" — the supposedly raw qualities of experience, like the particular bitterness of black coffee or the redness of a red traffic light. The line of thought I keep coming back to says that the standard idea of a quale, as something intrinsic, private, directly knowable, and ineffable, may not pick out any definite thing at all, and that our confidence in it comes partly from failing to notice a confusion between changes in an experience and changes in a person's judgment or reaction. I want you to explain this reasoning to me.

The cleanest way I've found to feel the force of it is a pair of cases involving two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn. Both have drunk the same brand of coffee for years, and both notice that something about their morning coffee has changed. Chase reports: "The flavor itself is exactly what it always was; what changed is that I no longer like it — the same taste now strikes me as unpleasant." Sanborn reports: "My enjoyment hasn't changed at all; what changed is the flavor itself — it now tastes different to me." Both are sincere and both are good at attending to their own experience.

Explain the argument that there is no fact of the matter about who is right — or, more carefully, that nothing available to either taster could establish whether the change is in the quale or in the reaction to it, and that this undermines the idea of a stable quale underneath both. What would a quale have to be for Chase and Sanborn's disagreement to have a definite correct answer? Then address the obvious reply: surely they are just describing two different things that can happen, and in any particular case one of them could simply be wrong. Why, on this skeptical view, does that reply fail to restore a well-defined notion of qualia?

Please answer in under 450 words and begin with a one-sentence summary of the bottom line.
