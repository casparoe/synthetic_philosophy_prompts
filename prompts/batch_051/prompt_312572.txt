Having spent my career in physiology and only recently turned to the philosophy of medicine, I have been working through the strongest objections to the view that brain death—the irreversible loss of all functions of the entire brain—is the death of the human being. I am compiling a list of such objections, and I already have the following, so please do not restate or reword them: (1) the integration objection, from Shewmon's chronic brain-death cases, that a body can remain an integrated organism for months without brain function; (2) the diagnostic-gap objection, that bedside tests establish at most the loss of brainstem and gross cortical function, not the loss of every function of the entire brain; (3) the scope objection, that the personhood rationale for brain death would also count patients in permanent vegetative states, and perhaps infants, as dead, which almost nobody in law or medicine is prepared to affirm; (4) the procurement objection, that the neurological criterion has been entangled with transplant interests from its introduction, and a criterion shaped by organ availability cannot settle what death is; (5) the stipulation objection, that the controversy shows 'death' is partly a moral or metaphysical choice rather than a purely biological fact, so the brain criterion cannot claim the authority of science alone; (6) the prognosis objection, that irreversible loss of brain function is evidence that death is imminent, not that it has already occurred; and (7) the pluralism objection, that religious and cultural traditions define death by cardio-respiratory cessation, and at least one jurisdiction has had to write a religious accommodation into its death statute. As far as I can tell these are the standard objections, so I am not looking for one of them in new wording. I want an objection to the brain-death criterion that is not on this list and not a variant, special case, or combination of these items. Then tell me honestly whether the objection is any good.
