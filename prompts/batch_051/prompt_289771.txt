I teach a small undergrad metaphysics seminar and I want to make a "which philosophy of causation do you actually hold?" quiz for the first day of the course. The idea is to sort students by what they say about concrete cases, not by which label they find flattering, so no question can ask anything like "is causation real?" or "do you think causes are just regularities?" Give me 9 questions, each with a short scenario and three answer options phrased in plain language.

The positions I need the scoring key to separate: (a) causation is a primitive, unanalyzable thing; (b) it reduces to regularities or patterns (the Humean line); (c) it reduces to counterfactual dependence; (d) it's grounded in interventions and manipulation; (e) it's grounded in mechanisms or physical processes; (f) it's eliminable, at least at the fundamental level — the Russellian line that it's a leftover idea from an earlier stage; and maybe (g) causal pluralism, where "cause" is a cluster of different relations that don't all share one analysis.

Build the scoring key from the logical space rather than from the list of names: start from the questions and the answer-patterns they allow, locate the named views as points in that space, and diagnose any combination that nobody holds (is it incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored?). Flag positions the quiz can't tell apart and say how big a problem that is.

For the cases, use in some form at least: two assassins firing at once where each shot alone would have been enough; a drought vs. a match as the cause of a wildfire; two correlated events driven by a hidden common cause; a drug that beats placebo in a trial where nobody knows the mechanism; and a question about whether the fundamental laws of physics describe anything we'd want to call causation. Add cases if you need them to get clean separations, but dont add filler. Keep every option answerable by someone who's never seen the technical terms, and write the whole thing in American English.
