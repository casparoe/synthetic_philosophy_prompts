I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and for several years I have been reporting on the design of deep geological repositories for nuclear waste. One case has stayed with me: the United States Department of Energy's Waste Isolation Pilot Plant near Carlsbad, New Mexico, which stores long-lived, defense-related transuranic waste in salt beds deep underground. The plan calls for the site to be marked so as to warn human beings — or any successor beings who might eventually excavate there — for roughly ten thousand years. The proposed marker system is itself a kind of philosophical artifact: granite monoliths inscribed in several languages, buried information rooms, and arrays of pictograms intended to remain intelligible to cultures that share none of our languages or assumptions. The designers treated this as a task of immense consequence, even though none of them will live to see the markers read, and even though any future readers may have no cultural or linguistic continuity with the civilization that produced the waste.

The philosopher Samuel Scheffler has argued that much of what we care about depends on an implicit confidence in a collective afterlife: not personal immortality, but the continued existence of human beings long after we as individuals are gone. If we truly believed human extinction were imminent, he suggests, many of our ordinary undertakings — scientific research, creative work, the maintenance of institutions — would lose their point, even if the extinction involved no harm to anyone now alive and even if no identifiable future person's interests were at stake. The waste-marker project strikes me as a concrete test case for that thought: a practice that deliberately reaches thousands of years into a future it cannot picture.

What theory of the value of humanity's future does the waste-marker project presuppose? Does it presuppose only that the welfare of possible future persons matters, or does it implicitly treat the ongoing existence of something like humanity as valuable in itself, or as a condition of our present projects making sense? Or is it best read as a merely conditional commitment — if anyone is there, they should be warned — in which case it may not depend on Scheffler's conjecture at all? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say where it is philosophically underdetermined, and tell me what would have to change in the design or justification of the marker project if one adopted instead a strict person-affecting view on which only presently existing people matter.
