In a graduate seminar on early modern philosophy, one of the participants has been pressing a Cartesian line about animal minds. His argument runs as follows: nonhuman animals are, in all relevant respects, like the hydraulic automata and musical clocks that were admired in seventeenth-century Europe. Since such machines can produce complex, purposive-looking movements without any inner feeling, and since we would never attribute sensation or thought to them, the analogy licenses the conclusion that the behavior of a dog or a bird is likewise fully explained by internal mechanism, and that we have no more reason to attribute feeling to the animal than to a clock. I would like a careful audit of exactly how far this analogy holds. Lay out the correspondence explicitly: which features of the automaton or clock are being mapped onto what in the animal, and which conclusion that mapping is being asked to carry. Then identify the points at which the mapping breaks, separating disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from ones that do not. In particular, say what the analogy quietly imports about causation, purpose, and the relation between behavior and inner states. Finally, evaluate whether the Cartesian conclusion—mechanism without sentience—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was being carried by the analogy all along. If some other artifact or system, such as a thermostat, a robot, or a running program, would serve the Cartesian conclusion better, say which and why.
