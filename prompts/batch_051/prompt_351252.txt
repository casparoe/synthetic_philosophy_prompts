Lately I've been going through boxes for my town's historical society, and my great-great-aunt's daybook turned up in a trunk. She was a midwife here during the 1918 flu. The daybook is huge: births, deaths, what families paid her in eggs or cash, and all that spring she's fighting with the mayor and the mill owner about wether to close the mills, with the town doctor mostly against her. The county archive has agreed to take it, so its not a preservation issue. But a friend who teaches history told me it was personally moving but not historically significant. The criteria I got taught in college for historical significance were: (1) the person or event had a large-scale effect on political, economic, or military structures; (2) the action involved elites, institutions, or states; (3) there are records in official archives; and (4) later developments can be shown to follow from it. Now on (3) the daybook qualifies, on (2) it does not, and on (1) the most I can claim is that after that spring the mayor lost his seat and the town set up a public health board, which feels pretty small. Is the daybook historically significant by those criteria? Go through each one and tell me where it passes or fails. And if it mostly fails, is that because the criteria are rigged toward the history of elites and states, or is there a good reason to keep significance separate from human interest?
