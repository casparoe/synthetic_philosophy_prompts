I teach a seminar on the philosophy of history and I keep coming back to the American monument wars of the last decade — the Confederate statues that came down in New Orleans, Richmond, Charlottesville, and the arguments over whether taking them down was erasing history or finally getting straight with it. I want to use this as a case study for Nietzsche's three kinds of history in the second Untimely Meditation: monumental history, which assembles a usable past of great deeds to inspire present action; antiquarian history, which preserves and venerates what has been; and critical history, which drags the past before a tribunal and breaks with it so life can move forward. For Nietzsche each serves life in the right dose and each becomes toxic in excess.

What I'd like from you is a disciplined mapping of the episode onto those categories. Who is doing which kind of history — the heritage groups, the city councils, the protesters, the tourists taking selfies with empty pedestals? What would the framework say each side should have done, where do the three boxes break down, and what does the episode show about the theory? I'm not convinced the defenders are just antiquarians and the removers just critical historians; both sides seem to be doing monumental history with different heroes, and a lot of the actual decisions came down to insurers, budget meetings, and fear of the next rally. So don't let Nietzsche off easy, but also tell me whether a essay from 1874 can actually handle identity politics, public space, and modern municipal government — or whether the episode reveals something his taxonomy was never designed to see.
