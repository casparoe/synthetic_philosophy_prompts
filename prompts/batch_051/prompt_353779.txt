Economists, decision theorists, and philosophers have been arguing for most of a century about whether it is meaningful to compare one person's utility with another's, and the debate has a frustrating shape: everyone accepts the same mathematics of preference representation, yet the participants keep talking past one another. The ordinalist line treats utility as only a representation of choice, so interpersonal comparisons are arbitrary; others reconstruct comparisons from extended preferences over how one would fare in another's circumstances; still others treat comparison not as measurement but as an inescapably normative judgment. My working hypothesis is that the impasse persists because these are answers to three different questions sharing one label. Is that right, or is there a genuine substantive disagreement underneath? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a survey: is it verbal, is it about what could count as evidence for a welfare measure, about what 'utility' means, or about bedrock normative commitments that empirical and formal results cannot touch? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress--a representation theorem, a sharper distinction, a measurement procedure, a change of question--or should we expect it to stay stalled? Finally, say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
