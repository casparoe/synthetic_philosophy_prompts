A friend of mine runs a small restaurant and just got hit with two bad write-ups in the same week. One review said the reviewer found a cockroach in her soup. That was false, so my friend is talking to a lawyer. The other review called it the worst restaurant in the city, and when my friend confronted the reviewer she basically said it was just a bad mood. Under current defamation law, the false factual claim is potentially defamatory, while the nastier, less-checkable verdict is protected opinion. So the more verifiable a damaging statement is, the more legal trouble it can get you into, and the more subjective and unfalsifiable it is, the safer it is to publish. That seems exactly backwards to me.

Then there are the middle cases I keep thinking about: a credit agency downgrades a company's debt, an analyst sets a one-year price target of three dollars, a professor has a 2.1 on a student-rating site. These have numbers attached and look kind of factual, but courts tend to treat them as closer to opinion. I can't tell whether that's a wise recognition that a rating is an evaluative judgment or just a loophole. What, if anything, justifies the underlying asymmetry, that the less checkable a reputational hit is, the more protection it gets? Is there a principled line between fact, opinion, and whatever forecasts and ratings are supposed to be? And if you could redesign the rule from scratch, where would you put ratings and forecasts, and would you keep, shrink, or throw out the fact/opinion distinction altogether?
