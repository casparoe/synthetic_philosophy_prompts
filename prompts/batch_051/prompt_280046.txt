Last night a friend and I argued late into the evening about self-determination rules; Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec kept recurring as examples, and at one point he sketched the constitutional mechanism below. We realized we had been exchanging verdicts without actually tracing the mechanism's incentives, and I want you to do that tracing.

The mechanism. A region may hold a binding independence referendum only after submitting a petition signed by 20 percent of its registered electorate, and the petition lapses unless a referendum is held within eighteen months. The referendum passes only if a majority of votes cast favor independence and those votes also amount to at least 35 percent of the entire registered electorate, with abstentions and spoiled ballots counting against that second threshold. If the referendum fails, the national parliament must within one year transfer an additional competence from a fixed constitutional menu to the region; if it does not, the signature threshold for the next petition falls to 10 percent. If the referendum passes, secession does not take effect at once: the region enters a four-year period of associated sovereignty, governing everything except currency, foreign treaties, and armed forces, and either side may terminate the process during that period by winning an election in its own legislature on an explicit reversal platform. At the end of the four years, a confirmatory vote with a simple majority of votes cast completes secession or restores the pre-referendum constitutional position.

Do not tell me whether the mechanism is wise; tell me how it would actually operate. Give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, covering: (1) what it incentivizes about when and how petitions are called; (2) how the 35-percent-of-electorate threshold reshapes campaign and turnout strategy; (3) what the failure consequences and the four-year transition do to bargaining leverage on each side; (4) who would game it first and where it would break first; and (5) what second-order effects would appear by the second or third cycle and whether any failure mode is fixable without gutting the scheme.
