I'm a retired geochemist—forty years classifying minerals, melts, and rocks, and arguing with colleagues about where one mineral species ends and the next one begins. Since I got into philosophy of science I keep runnning into the slogan that the sciences carve nature at the joints, and something about it strikes me as off, though I can't yet tell if that's a real problem or just my old lab habits talking.

Here's the hunch I want to test. Take mineral species, the categories geologists use to say what counts as a distinct mineral. Whether a lump is one phase or two distinct phases can flip depending on the instrument's resolution, the temperature window you care about, or how long you let the system equilibrate. The same material is a clean, well-carved kind for a field geologist and a messy continuous gradient for someone under an electron microscope. Coastlines do the same trick across scales. So maybe the joints aren't sitting in nature at a fixed depth, waiting for science to find them; maybe what counts as a joint is partly relative to the grain size at which a practice works and partly to what that practice wants the categories to do for it.

If I run with that, what are the most important implications for the old debate about natural kinds? Please actually follow the consequences out rather than give me a survey—what would it mean for realism about kinds, for reductionism, for whether the sciences should aim at one right taxonomy or many overlapping ones. And I'd like you to seperate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this area; don't present a contested view as the consensus, and don't flatter my hunch into something more decisive than it is.
