Next month I am presenting a dissertation chapter at an interdisciplinary theology-and-science seminar, and I want a Q&A prep sheet. The thesis I am defending is this: the doctrine of a good creation should not be parsed as the claim that God's original creation contained no animal death, predation, or pain, and attempts to make the Fall retroactively explain the fossil record are exegetically and theologically weaker than usually admitted. My constructive alternative is that creation is good in virtue of being ordered toward its consummation—the peaceable kingdom in which predation and death are overcome—rather than in virtue of having been free of harm at some initial moment. In particular, I argue: (i) the "no animal death before the Fall" position forces one either to reject the evolutionary and fossil record or to attribute to God a systematically misleading natural history, and both horns are unacceptable for a theology that takes divine honesty and the natural world seriously; (ii) animal pain, though real and to be taken seriously, does not carry the same theological weight as human sin and death, because Scripture binds human death to sin in a way it does not bind animal predation to sin; and (iii) my eschatological reading preserves the goodness of creation without pretending that the present order is morally unproblematic.

The audience is about fifteen people. One is a senior systematic theologian who holds a strong historical-literal reading of Genesis and believes all death, animal included, entered through Adam's sin. One is a constructive theologian in the tradition that emphasizes the goodness and integrity of creation and worries that my view gives away too much to theodicy. One is an evolutionary biologist at the university, not a believer, who will care mostly about whether the theology is consistent with the actual evolutionary record and will not accept any resolution that soft-pedals the fossils. One is a philosopher of religion working on natural evil and animal suffering. The rest are graduate students in theology and philosophy, mostly sympathetic but trained to spot weaknesses.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come and the order of how much damage they can do, phrased the way each of those four would actually put them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold without qualification, and which single reply has to land for the paper to survive. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say why it is the dangerous one. Be committed: rank the objections rather than surveying possibilities, and give me definite recommendations rather than options.
