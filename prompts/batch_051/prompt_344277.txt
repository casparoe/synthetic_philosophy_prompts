I work in the compliance office at a municipal water utility. Every few weeks a proposal lands on my desk to add something to the drinking water or change how it's treated — fluoride, a switch from chlorine to chloramine, a corrosion inhibitor, a pH adjustment. The public can't opt out: once we change the water, the risk (if there is one) is imposed on everyone in the service area, including people who would have refused if we'd asked. Some changes have real benefits and small risks, some have real risks and no benefit to the drinker, and most are somewhere in between. My team has about a day per proposal, none of us have philosophy training, and the board signs off but mostly does what ever we recommend.

Write me a reusable decision procedure for deciding whether a proposed change that imposes risk on non-consenting people is one we should make. The procedure should be something a new hire could run without knowing the theory: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it stops and says "this is a values question, a person has to decide." Ground it in whatever moral and political philosophy has actually worked out about imposing risk without consent — the distinctions that do the real work, not the jargon — but write it for someone who has never read any of that. Then run the procedure on two proposals: (1) adding fluoride at 0.7 mg/L to reduce tooth decay across the service area, and (2) switching the residual disinfectant from chlorine to chloramine to reduce regulated disinfection byproducts. Flag the cases your procedure can't settle on its own, and tell me straight if it would reject a proposal the board thinks is an obvious public-health win.
