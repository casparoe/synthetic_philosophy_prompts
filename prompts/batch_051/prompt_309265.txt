I've been trying to work out, on my own, how a rational person should represent uncertainty when the evidence is too thin or too mixed to settle on a single number. On one side is the precise Bayesian view: for any proposition, a rational state of uncertainty can be captured by one probability, updated by the usual rule, and talk of being somewhere between 40 and 60 percent is just loose speech about being unsure of your own credence. On the other side is the imprecise-probability view: when evidence is sparse, conflicting, or unreliable, the rational response can be to carry a set of probabilities rather than one, and the gap between the set's lower and upper values says something no single number can.

I don't want a verdict on which side is right. I want the document the two camps could sign together after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Use as the running case an urn with 100 balls: 40 are known to be red, and the remaining 60 are each either green or blue, but you are told nothing about how many are green; you must decide what your uncertainty about the claim that a single draw will be green should look like.

Please write the statement in these sections:
1. Agreed claims (roughly 500 words): a numbered list of claims both sides can sign, as exact as possible while remaining non-vacuous, including what they agree on about the urn case.
2. The precise disagreement (roughly 350 words): a two-column table; for each contested issue, a formulation of the precise Bayesian position that a precise Bayesian would accept and a formulation of the imprecise-probability position that an imprecise probabilist would accept, neither column slanted by the other side's vocabulary.
3. Cruxes (roughly 300 words): the sub-questions, stated as yes/no questions, such that settling at least one of them would move at least one side.
4. Agreed tests and advance predictions (roughly 400 words): for each crux, the arguments, cases, or results both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about what the test or case will show recorded in advance.
5. Verbal or substantive (roughly 200 words): which parts of the dispute, if any, both sides would agree are merely about words, and which parts remain a real difference once the wording is fixed.
