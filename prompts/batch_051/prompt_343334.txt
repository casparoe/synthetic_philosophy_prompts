In Part III, Chapter 1 of Adam Smith’s The Theory of Moral Sentiments, Smith develops the idea that when we judge our own conduct, we in effect divide ourselves into two persons: the agent whose conduct is being judged, and the spectator who does the judging. He suggests that we approve or disapprove of our own actions according to whether, when we step outside ourselves and imagine how they would appear to a fair and impartial observer, we can enter into the agent’s sentiments and motives. He then distinguishes between the desire for actual praise and the desire to be worthy of praise. Even when no real person is present, and even when actual bystanders are misinformed, biased, or otherwise unreliable, we remain concerned with the verdict of a supposed impartial spectator within.

What I find puzzling is this: if this internal spectator is built up from our experience of being observed by real people and from our own observations of others, why does Smith think it can ever stand apart from actual public opinion and judge against it? Is the internal spectator simply public opinion made internal, or does it have some independent standard? If it has an independent standard, where does that standard come from, and what kind of authority does it possess over us? Please help me understand this passage by reconstructing Smith’s account of how the internal spectator is formed and why its judgments are supposed to carry more weight than the actual opinions of other people.
