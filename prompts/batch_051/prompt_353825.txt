I recently read an essay in a general-interest magazine that summarizes the problem of mysticism and ineffability this way: "Every serious mystical tradition insists that the ultimate reality — God, Brahman, the One, emptiness — is ineffable: no description in words or concepts captures it, and language can at most gesture toward it. But those same traditions also speak of the mystic's experience as a kind of knowledge, even the highest knowledge, and here the position unravels. Knowledge, in any strict sense, is propositional: to know is to know that something is the case. An ineffable object yields no proposition that can be true or false, so there is nothing there to be known. At best the mystic undergoes a powerful wordless feeling and then retrospectively intellectualizes it as cognition. Appeals to a special 'non-propositional knowledge' are either a misuse of the word 'knowledge' or a concession that what is really being described is an emotional state rather than a claim about reality." I have a nagging sense that this slides from "cannot be fully described" to "nothing true can be said," but I don't know enough to make the objection stick. Is this a fair summary? Please go through it claim by claim: which parts a careful defender of the view that ineffable experience can be knowledge would accept, which parts they would reject or call conflated, what assumptions the essayist is quietly importing, and whether the final conclusion follows from anything accurate in the summary.
