Two people in my building got into a whole argument last night over a made-up gadget, and neither would give ground, so I want someone to referee it.

Picture a white-noise machine with a dial that goes from 0 to 1000. At setting 0 it's silent and it pays you nothing. Each notch up pays you €2 more per day and turns the volume up by an amount so tiny that you genuinely can't tell the difference between one notch and the next. At the top, though, the accumulated volume is a constant, painful roar, and you'd gladly hand back every cent of the €2,000 a day to get silence again.

Dan said the case shows that "better than" isn't always transitive. For every n, setting n+1 is better than setting n: it feels the same as setting n but pays €2 more, so refusing any individual step would be crazy. But setting 0 is clearly better than setting 1000. Since both the end-to-end judgment and every adjacent judgment seem right, transitivity has to go.

Maya said no. She said transitivity is built into what "strictly better" means—if A is better than B and B is better than C, then A is better than C, full stop. So at least one adjacent step must fail to be better, even if we can't point to it.

Dan came back: "That's just a sorites move. If a step fails to be better, there has to be a first failing step, and any first step would be completely arbitrary, because adjacent settings feel the same. Your positing a sharp cutoff with no basis."

Maya: "It doesn't have to be sharp. 'Better' can be vague; the boundary can be fuzzy without any particular step being the first one. And anyway you're confusing 'I'd take the €2' with 'it's all-things-considered better for me.' Each step being tempting doesn't make it better."

Dan: "But if you deny that each later setting is better, then at some point you'd have to advise a person to refuse free money for an unnoticeable change. And if they follow that advice across the whole sequence, maybe they stop at some arbitrary spot while each further step still looked worthwhile. A fully informed, reflective person shouldn't need an arbitrary rule if the comparisons were really transitive."

Maya: "What they should do is adopt a policy up front—say, 'I'll stop after 200 notches'—because they know the whole sequence is a trap. That's perfectly consistent with transitivity. It just means the local 'better' judgments were never all true, even if each one felt true at the time."

Dan: "But why 200 and not 201? The policy has the same arbitrariness you accused me of. If transitivity holds and each step really is better, stopping early is irrational; if you think some step isn't better, you owe me a principled cutoff, not a policy."

And that's more or less where they left it.

Could someone break down who's right about which specific moves, where each person goes wrong, and whether the real issue is "better than" or something nearby like "not worse than" or indiscriminability? Is one of them more right overall?
