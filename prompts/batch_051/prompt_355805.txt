Act as a philosopher who works on philosophical methodology, especially the role of intuitions in analytic philosophy. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming a correspondence table before you commit to a verdict; I care more about the mapping than the final conclusion.

In my reading group there's a guy who reaches for the same analogy every time we discuss whether a compelling theory can override a firm intuition, or the reverse. His stock case is utilitarianism telling you to carve up a healthy unconsenting passerby in the transplant case. When someone says the theory is so elegant that maybe the intuition should give way, he says: philosophical intuitions are to philosophical theories what observations are to scientific theories. They're the data. You don't throw out a clear observation just because your theory says otherwise — you fix the theory. So, he concludes, a compelling theory cant override a firm intuition; it has to accommodate it, the way physics accommodates what the instruments register.

I suspect the analogy is carrying the whole argument. I want you to audit how far it actually holds. First give me the correspondences explicitly, a table is fine: what maps to the thought experiment, the intuition itself, the clarity or firmness of an intuition, the recalcitrant intuition, the theory, the theorist's response, maybe auxiliary hypotheses or the idea of reflective equilibrium. Then go through the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion: observations are public and repeatable while intuitions are private and vary across people and cultures; observations can often be calibrated or error-corrected independently of the theory being tested, while intuitions seem to lack any comparable error theory; science does sometimes override observation without being ad hoc (illusions, bad instruments); and the fact that an intuition feels firm may reflect our psychology rather than truth. Separate the disanalogies that actually damage his conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Then say whether his conclusion survives. If it doesn't, tell me what weaker claim the analogy can support, and state the general principle you'd put in its place: under what conditions should a compelling theory override a firm intuition, and under what conditions should a firm intuition override the theory? And if there's a better analogy than observation, give it.
