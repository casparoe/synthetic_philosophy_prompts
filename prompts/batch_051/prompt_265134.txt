Over the past year I have been putting data-ownership hypotheticals to a colleague and recording how she rules. The question is always whether a person has a property right in information about them, where a property right means a moral claim to exclude others from using it, to be asked for consent, to require its deletion, and to share in any price it fetches; she is careful to say that harm and intrusiveness are a separate matter. Her settled rulings: a stranger may photograph her in a public square and sell the print without her consent, and she owns no part of it; the search queries she types are her property, and a search engine may not auction them without asking and paying her a share; a private journal entry that someone leaks is unambiguously her property; her name, address, and age, scraped from a public voter file into a commercial people-search database, are not her property; and the heart-rate log produced by a chest strap she wore during a run is her property, so the device maker must seek permission before licensing it.

Now take this new case. A credit-card issuer uses only its own records of her transactions to infer, without telling her, that she is probably being treated for depression, and sells that one-line inference to an insurer that will use it in pricing a policy she has applied for; the issuer has never seen her medical records. Do you predict she will say that inferred fact about her is her property? Give me the reasoning behind the prediction: identify the principle or principles that fit her five rulings so far, say what each candidate principle implies about the new case and which earlier ruling is the closest analog under each, then give your best guess as to her verdict and the principle she is most likely running, and state the further hypothetical that would most cleanly separate the remaining candidate principles.
