I have a habit in how I form expectations about the future, and I want to know whether it is defensible. Whenever I face a risky policy that I will repeat — a training routine, a saving strategy, a career approach — I form my expectation of how I will end up by averaging outcomes across all the ways things could go, as if I lived every branch at once. I treat a policy with a high average outcome as one I should expect to work for me. A friend objects that this is exactly the mistake studied in ergodicity economics: my own life is a single path through time, and for many repeated, multiplicative choices the quantity that accumulates is the time-average growth rate, not the ensemble average across parallel copies. He says the two can come apart dramatically, and that by averaging across copies I am systematically overestimating what I should expect along my own single path.

I can see the point for simple repeated bets, where the arithmetic mean can rise while the typical path falls. But most of my choices are not clean gambles with a known payoff distribution, and I do not have a stochastic model of my life to optimize. So my question is this: as a general, low-effort way of forming expectations about repeated choices, is my averaging habit defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as a rule? If it is not defensible in its current form, is there a restated version that captures the time-average insight without requiring me to build a formal model every time?

In your answer, please separate what is mathematically or conceptually settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested reading of the issue as the consensus.
