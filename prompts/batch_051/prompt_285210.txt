A few weeks ago some friends and I were making plans, and one of them, Marcus, told us that the King Street Cinema had a midnight showing on Friday. He'd checked the cinema's own website that afternoon and the site still listed it. What he didn't know is that the page was out of date: the cinema had quietly dropped the regular midnight showing months earlier and never taken it off the site. By pure chance, though, the manager had arranged a one-off midnight screening that same Friday, and the few of us who showed up got in. So what he said was true, and nobody wasted a trip.

Afterwards two of my friends got into a long argument about it that they never really settled, and I'd like someone to referee it. Leah says Marcus shouldn't have asserted it flatly. Her line is that a stale website gave him a justified belief, maybe, but not knowledge, and assertion presents yourself as knowing — as being in a position to vouch for the thing. If he'd said something like "the website says there's one," that would have been fine, but just saying "there is one" was more than he was entitled to. The fact that it happened to be true was luck, and luck doesn't retroactively make the assertion okay. Jules says that standard is impossibly high. If assertion required knowledge, we couldn't say most of the ordinary things we say, because you can always imagine some weird way your evidence is misleading even though the claim turns out true. Marcus believed it, he checked the source any reasonable person would check, and the claim was true; the point of asserting is to pass along information, and from where Marcus stood there was nothing wrong with it. Leah came back with a doctor analogy: if a doctor writes a prescription based on an outdated chart and the drug happens to help, we still say the prescription was irresponsible. The lucky truth of the outcome doesn't settle whether it was okay. Jules replied that the analogy stacks the deck because the stakes are so different — with movie times, the cost of being wrong is small, and it'd be strange to demand more than a quick check. He said the real norm is something like: assert only what you reasonably believe, or what your evidence supports; knowledge is a red herring. Leah said that's just why she thinks the violation was minor rather than nonexistent. Low stakes make it less serious, but they don't change what an assertion claims, and if the one-off screening hadn't happened, everyone would have blamed Marcus precisely because he spoke as if he knew. Jules said that only shows we blame people for not being careful enough, not for lacking knowledge. Knowledge depends on the world cooperating in ways a speaker can't control, so whatever the norm is, it has to be something a speaker could comply with "from the inside."

I honestly couldn't adjudicate this. Can you go through the disagreement as it actually went? I'm interested in which specific claims each of them gets right, which of their arguments are stronger, whether one of them is more right overall, and whether the real question turns on something neither of them separated out. Also, please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you're least sure of.
