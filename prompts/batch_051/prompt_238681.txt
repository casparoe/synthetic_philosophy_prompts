I am putting together a statement of my own position on freedom of expression and want to know whether the whole thing holds together or whether I have been quietly running two different principles and calling them one. The passage that anchors me is from John Stuart Mill, On Liberty, chapter III:

No one pretends that actions should be as free as opinions. On the contrary, even opinions lose their immunity, when the circumstances in which they are expressed are such as to constitute their expression a positive instigation to some mischievous act. An opinion that corn-dealers are starvers of the poor, or that private property is robbery, ought to be unmolested when simply circulated through the press, but may justly incur punishment when delivered orally to an excited mob assembled before the house of a corn-dealer, or when handed about among the same mob in the form of a placard.

Here is what I presently hold, in six commitments:

(1) I affirm the harm principle: the only ground on which the state may punish or suppress expression is the prevention of harm to others. Offense, falsehood, blasphemy, and immorality, taken simply as such, are never enough.

(2) I understand harm narrowly, as a wrongful setback to an interest that the law already recognizes: bodily security, property, reputation against defamation, and security against threats and fraud. Psychological distress, humiliation, and offense do not count, however severe.

(3) I think Mill's corn-dealer distinction is correct. The same words can be protected when circulated as opinion in print and punishable when shouted to an angry crowd outside the dealer's house, because the circumstances change what the speech act is: it becomes incitement, not the mere circulation of a view.

(4) I think incitement can rightly be punished even when no stone is actually thrown. A non-negligible and suitably immediate risk of violence is itself enough; restricting it is harm prevention, not thought control.

(5) I think de-platforming and no-platforming are strongly presumptively wrong, and are justified only for genuine incitement or threats. The abhorrence of a speaker's views, the distress of listeners, or the worry that a platform "legitimates" the speaker never suffices.

(6) I think speech that degrades a group but is uttered as opinion — Holocaust denial, sweeping slurs, claims of group inferiority — is protected and should be answered with counterspeech, not law, because group degradation falls on the offense side of my line. At the same time, I think a coordinated harassment campaign, doxing, or a credible death threat against a particular person may be restricted, because it targets a specific individual and sets back a concrete interest.

Please audit this set against the Mill passage. Specifically: identify every pair of commitments that is in real tension and locate the pressure point in each; rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equal; identify any apparent tension that dissolves on closer inspection and say why; and state the least costly revision that would restore consistency, including what it would cost me. I would rather have the deep inconsistency named than every pair listed as mildly problematic.
