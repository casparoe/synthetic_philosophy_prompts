Question: Explain Locke's account of personal identity and Reid's brave officer objection. Does the objection refute the view that personal identity consists in memory, or does it just show the view needs modification?

Student answer: Locke's view is that a person's identity extends back exactly as far as their consciousness can reach back to past thoughts and actions. Since we ordinarily cash this out as memory, the criterion becomes: A at a later time is the same person as B at an earlier time just in case A can remember B's actions or thoughts from the inside.

Reid's brave officer runs like this. A boy is flogged for stealing apples. Later he becomes a general and remembers being flogged. Still later, as an old officer, he remembers being the general but has completely lost the memory of being flogged. By the memory criterion, the general is the boy (he remembers the flogging) and the old officer is the general (he remembers his general-stage experiences). Identity is transitive, so the old officer has to be the boy. But by the criterion the old officer is not the boy, because he has no direct memory of the flogging. Contradiction. So a direct memory criterion makes personal identity non-transitive, and it has to be rejected.

The standard repair is to replace direct memory with overlapping chains of memory: the old officer is the boy because there is a chain running from old officer to general and from general to boy. This restores transitivity and handles the case neatly. Locke basically had this chain view in mind when he talked about consciousness reaching backward—he never meant you need a direct memory of every past stage, only that consciousness can be passed along a chain. So Reid's objection only shows the memory criterion needs to be spelled out as the ancestral of memory rather than direct memory, and once you make that friendly emendation the criterion survives.
