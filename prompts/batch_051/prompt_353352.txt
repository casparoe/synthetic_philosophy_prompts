Ok, so I work in a state health department's emergency preparedness unit, and last night I had a three-hour argument with a friend about the covid lockdowns and the travel restrictions some states put up at their borders. She's a civil-liberties type and kept saying they were illegal power grabs, and I kept saying legality isn't the only thing, a measure can be legally shaky and still be the right thing to do, and a measure can be perfectly legal and still be an abuse. The argument got heated but neither of us could actually say what the test would be. Then I realized this is literally my job: during a bad flu season or a novel outbreak we meet every day and decide whether to recommend school closures, quarantine orders, event bans, sometimes restrictions that outrun what our statutes were written to cover. Our staff aren't lawyers, we get about an hour per measure, and the governor's legal counsel signs off but just tells us "that's risky" or "defensable" without engaging on legitimacy. Getting it wrong means either preventable deaths or a lasting loss of public trust, and we've done a bit of both.

So here's what I want. You're an expert on political philosophy and emergency governance. Take your time and think carefully. Before you write the procedure, do a short brainstorm about what "legitimacy" could actually mean operationally for an emergency measure thats either legally unclear or clearly legal but morally contested. Then turn it into a reusable decision procedure my unit could run on any proposed emergency measure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it explicitly hands the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a fact. The procedure has to be usable by a non-lawyer in about an hour in the middle of an actual emergency, and it should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Flag the hard cases the procedure can't settle, and say which distinctions from the underlying theory have to survive in the simplified version and which can be dropped.

Then run the procedure on three worked examples: (1) a two-week closure of schools and indoor dining during a novel respiratory outbreak when the specific statutory authority for those closures is ambiguous; (2) a quarantine order applied only to travelers from one named country during a fast-moving outbreak, which is clearly within our legal power; and (3) a curfew that is legally authorized but falls almost entirely on the working poor. At least one of these should come out as a decision you'd rather not make. End by saying what the procedure cannot do, what decisions it should not even be asked to make.
