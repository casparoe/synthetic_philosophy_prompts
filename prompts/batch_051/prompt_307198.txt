A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about inference to the best explanation, and by the end neither of us was sure who had conceded what. I have tried to reconstruct the argument faithfully below; please adjudicate it point by point — rate each numbered move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, and then say who is right about what overall, and what, if anything, both of us are missing.

(1) My friend argued that if a town sees five cases of the same rare cancer in one neighborhood within a year, the best explanation is a local environmental cause, and that this is a positive reason to believe there is such a cause even before any particular toxin has been identified.

(2) I replied that calling something the “best” explanation only says it explains the data better than the alternatives we happened to list; unless we have reason to think the true cause is among the alternatives considered, explanatory goodness by itself gives no support.

(3) He answered that explanatory candidates are not drawn randomly from the space of possibilities: we form them by asking what would make the data probable, so the candidate that wins is already favored by the evidence in a way unlisted possibilities are not.

(4) I objected that this covertly appeals to likelihood — the winning candidate makes the data more probable — but a likelihood still needs to be combined with a prior probability, and the prior that a previously unidentified environmental cause exists is exactly what he was helping himself to.

(5) He responded that the prior is itself governed by explanatory reasoning: one unified cause for five cases is more plausible than a coincidence of independent rare mutations, and unification is exactly the sort of virtue that makes an explanation good.

(6) I said that this leaves inference to the best explanation with no independent work to do: either the explanatory virtue amounts to higher prior probability and higher likelihood, in which case the reasoning is just Bayesian updating, or it is a preference for unified hypotheses dressed up as a general principle of inference.
