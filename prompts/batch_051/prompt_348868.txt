I've been trying to formalize a principle that saturates scientific and legal reasoning — the presumption that two pieces of evidence are independent unless there is a specific reason to think they are connected — and I've built an argument that seems to show the presumption is never justified. I cannot accept the conclusion, but I also cannot locate the slide. Here is the argument:

(1) Independence is an exact numerical condition: A and B are independent just in case P(A∧B) = P(A)P(B).

(2) Given only the individual probabilities P(A) and P(B), the value of P(A∧B) is not fixed; it can range over a continuum of values compatible with those two constraints, and the product is a single point in that continuum.

(3) To presume independence is to make that single point — the exact equality — the default, rather than spreading one's credence over the full range.

(4) Absent specific evidence, it is not rational to concentrate high credence on one exact point in a continuum. If you knew only that a coin's bias was somewhere between zero and one, you would not assign high probability to its being exactly fair; if you knew only that a measurement was subject to some error, you would not assign high probability to the error's being exactly zero.

(5) Therefore the presumption of independence, as a default for unknown dependence relations, is never rational.

(6) But ordinary empirical reasoning routinely relies on exactly that default: two witness reports are combined by multiplication unless dependence is positively established; two diagnostic tests for the same condition confirm more than one only through the product; two replications of an experiment lend extra weight through the same move.

(7) So either a large swath of ordinary empirical reasoning is systematically irrational, or at least one of (1)–(6) is false.
