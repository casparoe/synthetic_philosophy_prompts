I'm taking a course that touches on the philosophy and cognitive science of delusions, and in one lecture the instructor summarized the two-factor theory like this: "Suppose a patient with Capgras delusion says her husband has been replaced by an impostor. The two-factor theory explains the delusion in two stages. First, some damage to the brain breaks the pathway that normally attaches a feeling of familiarity to a recognized face; the husband looks exactly the same but no longer feels like her husband. Faced with this strange experience, the patient rationally infers the best available explanation—that the man only looks like her husband. That inference is the first factor. Second, the patient has an additional, more general failure of reasoning that prevents her from rejecting this explanation the way unaffected people reject odd hypotheses; this second factor is why only some people with the face-recognition problem go on to develop the full delusion. Since the entire syndrome can be broken down into an anomalous feeling plus a reasoning error, the theory shows that a delusion is not really a belief at all, but a downstream symptom of perceptual and logical malfunction—and that is also why patients do not give up the delusion when confronted with evidence."

I suspect some of this is too swift, but I don't know enough to locate the errors myself, and I want to know what to trust. I'd like you to audit this summary: which parts would a careful defender of the two-factor theory accept, which are simplifications or overstatements, and which are just wrong? In particular, tell me what the theory actually says about whether the first factor is a rational inference, what sort of deficit the second factor is supposed to be and how "general" it must be, and whether the theory commits anyone to the view that delusions are not beliefs or are necessarily unresponsive to evidence. Does the final step follow from the accurate parts of the summary, or does it smuggle in a further claim about what delusions are?

I would also ask that you separate what is settled in this literature from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it, and that you not present a contested position—for example, the claim that delusions are not beliefs—as if it were the consensus.
