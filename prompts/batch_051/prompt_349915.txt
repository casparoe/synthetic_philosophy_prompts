You are an expert in formal epistemology. I am a university student currently taking a seminar in Bayesian epistemology, and I would like you to adjudicate a dispute I overheard after class. We had been discussing Van Fraassen's Reflection principle, which our instructor stated roughly as follows: for any proposition A and any value r one's future self might assign, one's present credence in A conditional on one's future credence in A being r should itself be r — equivalently, one's present credence should equal one's expectation of one's future credence, at least for future states one now regards as rationally reached.

After the seminar, two classmates, Elena and Marcus, argued in four moves.

(1) Elena: Reflection captures a genuine coherence requirement on one's own future opinions. If I take tomorrow's version of me to be at least as well informed and careful as today's, then I cannot treat what she will think as irrelevant to what I should think now. Suppose I learn that tomorrow, after carefully weighing the trial evidence, I will assign credence .7 to the defendant's guilt. To keep my present credence at .4 and regard that gap as needing no correction would be to treat a method I claim to respect as arbitrary. Conditional on the future verdict, my present credence should already match it.

(2) Marcus: The principle is still too strong. Suppose I know that in a few minutes I will take a drug that makes me overconfident in whichever proposition is then before my mind, so that after taking it I will assign credence .9 to the proposition that a particular fair lottery ticket will win. Reflection would force me to assign .9 now, on pain of incoherence. But the only defective state here is the future one. A norm of rationality should not require me to defer to a state I already know to be compromised.

(3) Elena: The version we learned is not unqualified. It applies only to future credences one now regards as formed by reasoning no worse than one's own. A drug-induced state fails that test, so your example refutes only an unqualified statement of the principle, not the principle itself.

(4) Marcus: But then the qualification is doing all the work, and it looks problematic either way. If "regard as rationally reached" just means "endorse," the principle tells me only to defer to future opinions I already endorse — close to a tautology, not a substantive constraint. If it means something robust, such as "produced by conditionalizing on all available evidence," then it is a theorem about ideal agents, not a norm governing real ones, who may reasonably expect to update imperfectly even without drugs. Moreover, trust in one's future self is gradable and partial, whereas Reflection demands full deference to any future state that clears the bar.

I genuinely could not referee this myself. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, weak, partly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then give your overall committed verdict on whether Elena's qualified reading or Marcus's critique is closer to correct, and identify anything important that both are missing. Take a definite position and argue for it rather than merely laying out options. Please think carefully; you may begin by brainstorming the strongest versions of each side before giving your final judgment.
