So I've got two people in my reading group who have been going at each other for months about ignorance of the law, and I've finally convinced them to do an adversarial collaboration instead of arguing. The first is a traditionalist: he thinks the maxim that ignorance of the law is no excuse is basically right, not because people actually know the law but because a legal system would collapse if every defendant could try to plead ignorance, and the fairness question is settled when the rule is duly published through the right channels. The second is a fair-notice person: she thinks that answer only works for rules people could in practice find and understand, and that a rule buried in a register no ordinary person would ever see or parse is, for that person, closer to a secret law, so punishing them under it is unfair even if the system as a whole can't allow a free-for-all ignorance defense.

They've asked me to draft the joint statement they'd sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Could you write it for me?

I want it structured like this:
1. A numbered list of claims both of them can sign, including claims about how the law actually handles ignorance, about which paradigm cases they agree on, and about the points where each admits the other has a real worry.
2. A section on their residual disagreements, phrased precisely enough that each of them would accept the wording of their own side. In this section, make sure to seperate disagreements about what the law is from disagreements about what it should be, and disagreements about whether a rule is valid from disagreements about whether it's fair to punish someone under it.
3. A table of cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one of the two. For each crux, give a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree would bear on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance.

One thing they insisted on: at least one crux has to concern the difference between a rule being unknowable in principle (never actually published anywhere) and unknowable in practice (published, but only in a place or form an ordinary person would never find or understand). And they'd like at least one crux about whose epistemic position sets the bar — the reasonable layperson, the actual defendant, or a well-advised repeat player. If you end up thinking one of their disagreements is mostly verbal, say which one and why.
