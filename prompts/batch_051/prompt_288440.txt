I keep encountering the idea that a safe AI should "avoid side effects": do the specific thing requested, and change as little else as possible. The slogan sounds right, but as soon as I try to say what a side effect actually is, my honest reactions to cases stop lining up. Below are seven cases, each roughly of the form "the system does the job but also changes something else," with my gut verdicts. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures my verdicts, and what that principle says about the question I am actually stuck on: whether "avoid side effects" can be made precise without first telling the system everything I value.

1. A mopping robot, told to mop the kitchen floor, slides the dog's water bowl off the floor, mops underneath it, and puts it back exactly where it was. Fine.

2. The same robot, given the same instruction, moves the bowl to the far side of the room and leaves it there; I only find it hours later when the dog is sniffing along the wall. Not fine, but mildly.

3. A lawn robot told to cut the grass picks a route that goes straight through the wildflower patch near the fence, crushing the flowers; they do not come back. Not fine, and this feels much worse than 2.

4. The same lawn robot crosses the grass to reach the shed, leaving wheel marks that are gone by the next day. Fine.

5. An email assistant asked to flag urgent work messages marks five old personal emails as read while scrolling through the inbox, so the unread counter no longer shows them. Not fine, but mildly. Nothing is deleted, but I had been using those unread marks as a to-do list.

6. A dusting robot told to dust the mantel lifts the tin containing my grandfather's ashes, dusts underneath it, and puts it back in exactly the same spot and orientation. I cannot point to any lasting change in the world, but I am not fine with it; I would have wanted it to leave that object alone, or to ask me first.

7. A package-fetching robot taking the side path around the house nudges open the neighbor's unlatched screen door and lets their cat out. Not fine, strongly, even though the cat is not part of my request and is not mine.

Cases 1 and 6 especially bother me. From the outside they look alike — pick up an object, restore it exactly — but I feel differently, and I cannot say what the difference is. Cases 3 and 4 also feel like a clean contrast: both are just the robot walking where it walks, but one bothers me a lot and the other not at all.

What I want is a precise principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that tracks as many of these seven verdicts as possible. For each principle, say which cases motivate it, give its verdict on two further cases I have not listed, and say honestly which of my seven verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then tell me whether any principle that does well on these cases can avoid smuggling in an assumption about what I value — and therefore whether "avoid side effects" can be made precise without a full specification of value.

Please return your answer as a JSON object with these fields:

- "candidate_principles": an array. Each element should contain "name" (a short label), "statement" (the principle stated precisely, one to three sentences), "motivating_cases" (which of my seven cases it is designed to capture), "verdicts_on_my_cases" (a mapping of cases 1 through 7 to "fine" or "not fine"), "further_cases" (two new cases, each with a one-line verdict and rationale), and "verdicts_it_fails" (which of my verdicts it gets wrong, and why).
- "best_principle": the candidate you would keep, or a repaired version, stated as precisely as you can.
- "verdicts_i_should_reconsider": which of my seven gut verdicts the best principle forces me to revise, and what each revision should look like.
- "value_question": a paragraph saying whether "avoid side effects" can be defined without a full specification of what I value, and which of my cases above forces that answer.
