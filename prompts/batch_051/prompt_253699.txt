I'm taking a course on medieval Islamic philosophy and we've been on the old Mu'tazila–Ash'arite dispute about whether God's commanding something is what makes it right, and I can't get one point to stop bugging me. My understanding of the two sides: the Mu'tazila hold that reason can get at at least some moral truths on its own, and that God commands what He commands because those acts really are good or bad — the command doesn't create the value. The Ash'arites deny this: an act is obligatory or forbidden only because God commanded or forbade it, and there's no independent moral order floating above God that His will has to line up with. Fine — that's the familiar Euthyphro-style fork.

But my professor also kept insisting that the Ash'arites don't think of God as a capricious tyrant, and that when they call God just and wise they mean it in a perfectly substantive sense. That's exactly where I get stuck. If 'just' can't mean 'matches a moral standard that even God must answer to,' doesn't it mean nothing at all for them? And if it means nothing, isn't their God just an unconstrained will — arbitrary in precisely the way they seem to want to deny? Or is there a reading where 'just' has real content that isn't secretly the Mu'tazilite standard? And on that reading, what would an Ash'arite say about God commanding something like torturing a child for fun — that it would be obligatory? I'm less interested in whether the view is true than in whether its actually coherent here, because right now it looks to me like they're trying to have it both ways.
