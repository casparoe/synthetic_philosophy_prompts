Aumann's agreement theorem says, roughly, that two Bayesian reasoners who share a prior and then update on different private information cannot hold different posterior probabilities for an event once each one's posterior becomes common knowledge between them: under those assumptions, agreeing to disagree is impossible. The argument's bearing on actual disagreement depends on a substantive premise, the common prior assumption (CPA), standardly glossed by the Harsanyi doctrine as the claim that differences in rational probability judgments are traceable to differences in information rather than to differences in starting credences.

Now consider a development that strains that premise in a new way. In more and more public and private deliberation, the systems producing probability judgments are language-model agents, or people whose judgments are partly outputs of such agents. Two of these systems can be given precisely the same evidence at inference time and still assign systematically different probabilities to the same proposition, because their starting distributions were set by different pretraining corpora, different fine-tuning objectives, and different reward models. These are not differences in the evidence the agents have just been shown; they are baked-in differences in how the agents weight and organize claims, and they encode priorities and values as much as factual information. They also cannot be fully made common knowledge in the way Aumann's agents' posteriors can.

I want a committed verdict on what this does to Aumann's argument as a claim about real disagreement, not a menu of options. In particular: does the theorem survive unchanged as a conditional result while losing its regulative force for actual deliberation, does it need repair (for example, by reinterpreting the common prior as a constraint on training distributions rather than on individual reasoning, or by treating training data as private information), or is the common prior assumption dead as an idealization in any setting where such agents matter? Identify exactly which premise the language-model case bears on, say whether that premise can be restored in another form, and say what, if anything, agreement theorems still have to teach us about disagreement in that setting.
