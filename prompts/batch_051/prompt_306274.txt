Pretend you're an experimental economist who has run the standard two-urn herding demo, like, fifteen times and is bored of it. I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on social epistemology, about 20 students, one 75-minute session. I want a classroom game that makes them feel the difference between an information cascade and reputational herding, and also makes them feel how fragile a cascade is. I dont want to lecture the concepts first — they should run into them in the game and only get names afterwards.

The stock exercise (two jars, private ball draws, sequential public guesses) gets the informational part across but misses the reputational pull and doesn't show what breaks a cascade. So design a runnable, single-session activity that does all three: (a) a cascade forms from private information alone, (b) the same students then feel the extra pressure of not wanting to look dumb in front of peers, and (c) at some point a small but public piece of information arrives and they watch what it does to the cascade that's formed. One continuous game beats three disconnected demos, but if you have to split it into tightly linked phases, fine. No real money; stakes should come from points, status, or mild embarrassment (nothing actually humiliating).

Before committing to a design, spend one short section brainstorming two or three mechanisms for separating informational from reputational herding in a classroom, then say which one you're picking and why it'll survive real classroom conditions.

Then give the design in these sections, in this order, with the rough lengths:
1. Setup and materials (about 120 words)
2. Rules and incentive scheme (about 250 words)
3. Timeline for the 75 minutes (about 180 words)
4. Debrief script that maps specific moments in the game to the concepts, and explicitly surfaces the difference between "the crowd probably knew something" and "I just didnt wanna be the one dissenting" (about 300 words)
5. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to fix each failure on the fly (about 180 words)
6. What this game can't reproduce about real-world cascades and herding, and what the facilitator will have to supply in words (about 120 words)

Work through the mechanics carefully before you write the rules — especially the payoff interactions, and what happens when the public information disagrees with the cascade. Its fine if the game is a little messy, but the debrief has to be able to point at exact moments.
