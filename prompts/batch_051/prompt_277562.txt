One of the final questions was: "Explain and assess Hume's argument that testimony can never rationally establish that a miracle has occurred." A student wrote the following. Student answer: Hume's target is testimony, not miracles themselves: he doesn't claim that a miracle is impossible, only that we can never rationally believe one happened on the basis of another person's report. His starting point is that a wise person proportions belief to evidence. For any alleged miracle, the evidence against it is our uniform past experience that the relevant law of nature holds; that's about as strong as evidence ever gets. Testimony is much weaker, since witnesses lie, misremember, exaggerate, and love a striking story. Hume's central principle is that a miracle-report should be believed only if the falsehood of the report would itself be a greater violation of the ordinary course of nature than the event being reported. But a miracle just is a violation of a law of nature, so it is by definition the most improbable thing there can be; no human report's being false could be more improbable than that. So the principle can never be met, and testimony can never make miracle-belief rational. In the second part of the essay Hume backs this up by looking at the historical record and finding that no miracle is attested by witnesses whose judgment, education, and integrity put them beyond suspicion; he also notes that people are prone to credulity, especially in religious matters. So Part I gives the in-principle argument, Part II gives the empirical case, and together they settle the matter.
