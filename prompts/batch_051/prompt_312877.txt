I keep hitting the same dead end in the does-LLMs-understand debate. Someone will be making a case that a model has picked up something beyond surface statistics — that it tracks entailments across rephrasings, or its internal states encode information about the game it was trained on, or its errors pattern more like a creature with partial concepts than a lookup table — and the reply is always that they're just stocastic parrots, delivered in a tone that says the conversation is over.

I don't want a yes-or-no verdict on that retort; I want it pulled apart. Lay out the distinct precise claims the sentence could be making: a claim about the training objective (next-token prediction), a claim about the absence of referential grounding to anything outside text, a claim about there being no communicative intent, a claim that prediction from the inside is somehow incapable of yielding understanding, a claim about what understanding itself requires, and any others you see. For each one, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive empirical claim that's probably true, a substantive empirical claim that's probably false, or something we can't settle right now.

Then tell me which of those readings actually does the work when the phrase gets used to shut down the kind of argument I described, and which ones are mostly rhetorical. And since I'm suspicious of tidy verdicts: for each reading you rule on, tell me what would change your mind — a behavioural result, an interpretability finding, a training regime, a better account of what understanding requires — and where your confidence is most fragile.
