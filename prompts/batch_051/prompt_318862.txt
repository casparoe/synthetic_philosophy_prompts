Here is an argument that the dispute between Donald Davidson's account of metaphor and conceptual metaphor theory is a substantive clash rather than a merely verbal disagreement about how to use the word 'content'. I suspect the argument hides an equivocation somewhere around step (5), but I cannot tell whether the equivocation can be repaired.

(1) A hearer who understands the sentence 'Our relationship has hit a dead end' spoken metaphorically does not treat it as a report about two people in a vehicle colliding with the end of a road.

(2) On Davidson's account, the words in that sentence retain their ordinary literal meanings and do not take on any additional, metaphorical meaning. Whatever else the metaphor accomplishes — making the hearer notice similarities, directing her attention, triggering associations — is an effect on the hearer, not a further content that the sentence has.

(3) On a standard version of conceptual metaphor theory, understanding the sentence requires the hearer to activate a conventionalized, cross-domain mapping from a source domain of travel to a target domain of close relationships: partners are mapped to travelers, the history of the relationship to a route, persistent conflicts to obstacles, and so on.

(4) Such a mapping is a stable, shared structure that is not supplied by the literal content of the words, and the hearer must recover it in order to understand the sentence in the ordinary way.

(5) Any stable, shared structure that is not supplied by the literal content of the words, and that a hearer must recover in order to understand an utterance, is part of the content of that utterance, broadly construed.

(6) Therefore, on conceptual metaphor theory, the metaphorical sentence has content beyond its literal content.

(7) Given (2), this means that Davidson's account and conceptual metaphor theory cannot both be right: the disagreement is substantive, not terminological.

Is the argument valid? If not, identify the exact step that fails and explain whether the failure is a false premise, an equivocation, or a suppressed assumption. In particular, is (5) a defensible bridge between what understanding requires and what an utterance says, or is it the weak link? If (5) is rejected, say whether Davidson's account and conceptual metaphor theory can be made compatible, and what each side would then have to give up.
