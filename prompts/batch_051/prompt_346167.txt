I'm a TA for a jurisprudence course and I've got a student's take-home exam answer that I keep going back and fourth on. Here's the question the student was given:

Devlin, in the opening of The Enforcement of Morals (his 1959 Maccabaean Lecture), has a passage where he argues, roughly, that a society is not just a group of individuals living under the same government; it also requires a shared moral outlook, a set of common judgments about right and wrong that its members regard as binding on them, and if that common morality falls apart the society itself falls apart. Lay out Hart's moderate thesis and extreme thesis distinction from Law, Liberty and Morality, and use it to evaluate whether Devlin's passage of this kind actually supports the legal enforcement of morality as such.

The student wrote:

Devlin's passage is based on the idea that shared morality is practically necessary for social existence. Hart's moderate thesis is just that descriptive claim: no shared morality, no society. His extreme thesis is the normative principle that the law may enforce whatever the society actually regards as immoral, simply because the society disapproves. Hart accepts the moderate thesis but says Devlin needs the extreme one to justify criminal law. Since Devlin's passage only shows that some shared morality is necessary for a society to survive, and says nothing about this particular moral rule being necessary, it doesn't get him to enforcement. So Devlin's argument fails.

What grade would you give this, and what specific comments would justify it? I'm especially interested in whether the student's characterization of Hart's two theses is precise enough.
