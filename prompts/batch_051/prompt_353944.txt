I have spent the past year working through a systematic program of moral self-reform. I drew up a list of thirteen virtues and kept a daily ledger in which I marked a black dot for every failure against each virtue, resolving to extend the scrutiny little by little over my whole character, so that no part of my conduct would remain unchecked. Two things happened. On one side, I became more industrious and even-tempered; on the other, I started to feel a quiet pride whenever my daily page was cleaner than my spouse's or my colleagues' imagined pages would be, and I now catch myself auditing other people's minor failings. I have also kept falling short on the same few virtues despite months of effort. A friend told me that the whole goal of moral spotlessness is a mistake. He retold, without the book at hand, Franklin's remark about a man who wanted his ax ground bright all over, but once the smith had made only part of it bright and the man had tired himself turning the wheel, the man concluded that he preferred the speckled ax after all. My friend says Franklin's point is that a few remaining faults make a person more tolerable to others and more honest to himself. Is my friend interpreting Franklin correctly, and should I abandon the project at this point rather than push for a perfectly clean ledger? I honestly cannot tell whether this is wisdom or a flattering excuse.
