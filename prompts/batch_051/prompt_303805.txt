I have been trying to make sense of an asymmetry in the way a serious believer I know treats petitionary prayer. She is entirely willing to affirm that God is omniscient, so God already knows what she needs before she asks, and that God is immutable, so nothing she says will change what God has decided to do. Yet on the day her mother goes into surgery she would be on her knees asking God for a successful operation, and she would think something was spiritually wrong with her if she could not bring herself to ask. At the same time, she would not keep pressing a friend for a favor after the friend told her the decision was final, and she would not write to a judge whose ruling has already been signed, urging him to reconsider just because she needs him to. In the human cases she would regard continued petitioning as both pointless and a little insulting, because the will on the other side is already made up. Is there a difference between the divine case and these human cases that survives scrutiny, or is petitionary prayer a special exception we grant to God that we would elsewhere diagnose as an inconsistency?

I would like the strongest principle you can find that would justify treating the two kinds of case differently, tested against the harder variants. One is a prayer for an outcome that would happen whether or not the person prays. Another is a prayer for an outcome the person is confident a good God would not grant. A third is the classical move that God decrees the outcome together with the prayer that accompanies it, so that the prayer is part of the causal order rather than an attempt to overturn a settled decision. After testing these, say plainly whether you would keep the asymmetry, give it up, or keep the practice while revising the theology that makes it look incoherent. As you answer, please separate what is broadly settled in philosophy of religion from what remains genuinely contested among informed theists and their critics, and if you present a contested solution as the best one, mark it as a position rather than as a consensus.
