I am compiling a set of objections to the method of reflective equilibrium as it is used in moral and political theory—the procedure of moving back and forth between considered judgments about particular cases and candidate general principles, revising either side until the two reach a stable coherence. So far the list contains five objections:

1. Unreliable inputs. Even after screening out judgments made in ignorance, agitation, or self-interest, the judgments that remain are heavily shaped by upbringing, local tradition, or inherited cognitive dispositions. A method that treats them as touchstones cannot thereby certify them.

2. Structural conservatism. Because the method starts from whatever judgments one antecedently finds intuitive and adjusts them only to the extent required by local inconsistency, it makes large-scale overturning of one's initial moral outlook unnecessarily difficult; coherence with current intuitions is not an independent reason to trust them.

3. Underdetermination. Distinct and mutually incompatible sets of principles, each adjusted to fit the relevant judgments, can all achieve reflective equilibrium. The method supplies no internal ground for choosing among these competing equilibria.

4. No external constraint. Agreement between principles and judgments is a relation between two kinds of mental attitude, neither of which is independently tied to the facts of the matter. Internal coherence is thus compatible with systematic error.

5. Lack of evidential independence. The fit between a judgment and a principle can appear to confirm both, but if the two were produced—or revised—by the same underlying biases or cognitive shortcuts, their agreement is not independent evidence for either.

I would like a sixth objection that is not on this list and not a variant of anything on it. By a variant I mean an objection that could be turned into one of these five by changing the example, vocabulary, or emphasis while leaving the underlying argument intact. The objection should target reflective equilibrium as a method rather than the content of any particular equilibrium. After you give the objection, assess it honestly: does it identify a real limitation of the method, and would a defender of reflective equilibrium have a plausible reply? If you conclude that no genuinely distinct sixth objection exists, or that the best candidate collapses into one of the five, say so explicitly and defend that judgment.
