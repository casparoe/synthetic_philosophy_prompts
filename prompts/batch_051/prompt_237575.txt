For an essay on translation and revelation, I want a specification of what a doctrine of translation and inspiration would have to get right before I weigh any of the familiar positions. The debates I have read move from a single case to a conclusion — the Septuagint is invoked to show that a translation can be inspired, the King James Version is invoked to show that only one can be, the lost autographs are invoked to show that none can be — and each time the rest of the theory is silently assumed. Set the conclusions aside and give me the specification first.

Fix the purpose of the doctrine as follows. It must be adequate for a community that (a) has to decide whether the translation it actually reads, hears, and teaches can be treated as divinely inspired, not merely as a useful rendering; (b) has to decide whether to teach that status as doctrine; and (c) will revise the translation and correct its textual base as scholarship improves. A doctrine that works only for original manuscripts nobody possesses, or only for a community with a single frozen translation, fails this purpose.

Now give me the list of requirements such a theory must meet. Work through at least these cases and distinctions, and say, for each, why it is a requirement rather than an optional commitment of one theological camp:

- The lost-autograph case. The source texts are gone, and what circulates are copies and translations. The theory must say where inspiredness lives after the originals disappear, or accept that the community is venerating texts with no inspired status.

- The Septuagint case. An ancient translation was treated by a community as carrying its own inspired authority, to the point of legends that the translators produced it under divine supervision. The theory must say whether inspiration can attach to a translation in its own right, and whether what the community was claiming was fidelity to the source, divine oversight of the process, or something else.

- The KJV-onlyism case. One translation is elevated to exclusive authority. The theory must not collapse three distinct things: a translation's fidelity to its source, its authority for a community, and its inspiration. It must handle the position that runs them together and say which, if any, can be claimed without the others.

- The source-shift case. A translation is widely venerated, but the underlying text it followed is later judged inferior, or some of its renderings are shown to be questionable. The theory must state whether inspiredness is compatible with translational error and, if so, where the boundary lies between an inspired departure and a mistake.

- The chain case. A translation of a translation, a revision of a translation, and a copyist's slip all pass a text on. The theory needs a rule for whether and how inspiration propagates through a chain of transmission, and it cannot say that every act of transmission is individually protected without making the copyist's slip inspired.

- The identical-text case. A translator with no religious commitments, working by ordinary scholarship, produces a rendering word-for-word identical to one the community regards as inspired. The theory must say whether two texts that are indistinguishable on the page can differ in status. This forces a decision about whether inspiration is a property of the text itself or of its production and reception.

- The plurality case. Several translations make different, defensible choices wherever the source underdetermines the rendering. The theory must explain how exclusive and non-exclusive accounts of inspiration each handle this: if exclusive, what could select the one inspired rendering out of textual equals; if non-exclusive, how two conflicting renderings can both be inspired without draining the concept of content.

For each requirement, state it explicitly, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a camp-specific preference, with a reason in each instance. The specification itself is the deliverable, so keep discussion of existing positions brief and put the weight on the criteria. End by saying which single requirement does the most work in excluding popular positions — such as autograph-only views, KJV-onlyism, or the claim that translators were protected by the Holy Spirit from error — and defend that judgment. If some subset of the requirements turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly, because that is the part of the specification I most want to see. Commit to definite positions on the negotiability of each requirement and on the final judgment; do not present a menu of options and leave me to choose. Write in American English.
