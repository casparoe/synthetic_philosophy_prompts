I've been chewing on this since a forum thread about whether admiring semi-fictionalised founders is a bug or a feature, and it's exposed a pattern I don't have a name for. Take a saint's life: the historical person is a mess of mixed motives, long dull stretches, and embarassing failures, but the version handed to someone trying to become better has all that filed off — the crises are placed exactly where a lesson needs them, the contradictions vanish, and the result is more morally legible than any real person could be. That looks like a church thing, but the same happens to secular biographies of scientists and founders, with no institution doing the retouching on purpose. And the striking bit is that the idealised life is what actually does the teaching; people imitate the cleaned-up character, not the footnotes. Hagiography is near this, and Plutarch was plainly up to something similar with lives as moral material, but both feel too narrow, or too tied to virtue-talk, for the general technology I'm after: the deliberately retouched life as the load-bearing unit of moral learning. Is there an established concept for that general thing, or if not, what should we call it, and how does it differ at the edges from hagiography and exemplarism? Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
