I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so I'd appreciate an answer that stays close to inspectable premises. The classic Libet-style challenge to free will runs something like this: a subject is told to flex a wrist whenever the urge arises; an EEG readiness potential appears roughly half a second before the movement, while the subject's own report of the conscious urge comes only about two hundred milliseconds before the movement. The conclusion commonly drawn is that the neural process producing the action starts before the person is consciously aware of deciding, so the conscious experience of initiating the action is a late read-out rather than a cause, and free will understood as conscious initiation is an illusion.

I recently encountered a newer reanalysis of the readiness-potential data, and I cannot tell whether it kills this argument or merely complicates it. The reanalysis treats the readiness potential as an accumulation-to-threshold signal: noisy neural activity drifts until it crosses a threshold and the movement occurs. Because trials are aligned to the moment of movement and then averaged, a rising ramp before the movement is what you would expect even if there were no distinct decision event half a second earlier; the apparent lead is at least partly an artifact of how the signal is extracted, not a neural signature of an earlier unconscious decision.

This is the kind of signal-processing issue I encounter at work, and it makes me suspicious that the original inference was weaker than it looked. What I want is a precise account of which premise of the classic Libet argument the accumulator-threshold reanalysis actually attacks, and whether the original conclusion still follows. If it does not, is the argument dead, or is there a repaired version that establishes something weaker but still important about conscious control? I am interested in what the Libet argument still licenses after this reanalysis, not in general opinions about free will.
