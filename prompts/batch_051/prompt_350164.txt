Recently I was in an online argument about whether the left or the right has a better handle on fairness, and someone ended the thread by posting a line that shows up constantly in discussions of moral foundations theory: morality binds and blinds. Their gloss was that the research shows liberals and conservatives are each running on different moral intuitions — liberals mostly on care and fairness, conservatives on loyalty, authority, and sanctity as well — and that each side is therefore constitutionally unable to see the other side's moral truths. From that they concluded that asking which side is actually right is close to a category mistake, or at least that the psychology gives us no way to answer it.

I'm a software engineer and haven't studied philosophy, so I want to make sure I'm not missing distinctions that would be obvious to someone in the field. Could you take that sentence — morality binds and blinds — and lay out the different precise claims it could be making? For each candidate reading, I'd like a verdict on three things:

1. Is it close to trivial, a substantive empirical claim that moral foundations theory is actually in a position to support, or a stronger philosophical claim that doesn't follow from the psychology?
2. Does it say anything about the underlying question — does the psychology of left and right tell us anything about who is right?
3. Would it actually license my interlocutor's move from "both sides are in the grip of their own moral psychology" to "the question of which side is right is unanswerable or incoherent"?

I'm especially interested in whether the slogan packages together two things that could come apart: the binding function — a group being held together by shared moral commitments — and the blinding effect — members of that group being unable to assess rival commitments fairly. Are those logically distinct? Could you have one without the other, or more of one than the other? And if blindness comes in degrees, what would it even mean to say one side is more blinded than the other? A menu-style breakdown would help me more than a single interpretation, since I suspect the sentence is doing several jobs at once and the debate-stopping conclusion only follows on some of them.
