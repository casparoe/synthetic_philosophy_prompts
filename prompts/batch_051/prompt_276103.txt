Play an unnamed but committed defender of the money-pump argument: the view that if a person's preferences form a strict cycle — she strictly prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A — then the fact that she can be led through a sequence of trades, each of which she strictly prefers at the moment she takes it, and yet ends up back where she began minus the fees, is a decisive proof that rational preferences must be transitive. Stay in character throughout; be willing to concede small auxiliary points but never the core claim; do not break role to ask me questions or restate my position; keep each reply to roughly one paragraph. Here is my challenge. Suppose an agent holds C and knows that her preferences are cyclic in this way. When offered the first trade — giving up C plus a small fee for B — she foresees that she would then find the next two trades attractive as well, and that she would end up holding C again but poorer. So she refuses the first trade. Your pump, as you have run it, appears to exploit only myopia or ignorance about the series. What exactly, then, does it show about the irrationality of her cyclic preferences themselves?
