A rural county has watched its churches, lodges, and neighborhood associations shrink for three decades, and with them the informal machinery that once kept small promises among neighbors from going soft: a borrowed truck, a handshake agreement to split the cost of a shared fence, an offer to mind someone's livestock over a weekend. The county board proposes a secular substitute for that machinery, the Good-Faith Registry, meant for promises too small or too personal to haul into court.

Any two adult residents who reach an informal agreement may, with both of them consenting, file it with the county clerk for a two-dollar fee. The filing states the promise in a sentence or two, its deadline, and a bond amount, which is fifty dollars whenever the promise has no clear cash value. Each party posts that amount, and the clerk holds it until the deadline. If the promisee files no claim within thirty days after the deadline, both bonds are refunded. If the promisee files a claim and the promisor does not contest it within fourteen days, the promisee collects the promisor's bond and gets their own back. If the promisor contests, a panel of three randomly selected residents hears both sides and decides the facts; each panelist is paid fifteen dollars, the losing side forfeits their bond to the other side, and the loser also pays the panel's fee. Every claim, contest, and finding is posted on a public record under both names. A resident twice found within three years to have broken a registered promise must post triple the bond on any later registration, the excess over the ordinary amount going to the county's general fund.

I am asking for an operational analysis, not an endorsement or a condemnation. What would the scheme drive people to do—the promisors, the recipients, and the conscripted panelists? Where would the first abuses and workarounds appear, and who would find them? What work would the public record do that the money cannot? What would become of the promises that never get registered, and of the diffuse trust that lets neighbors cooperate without asking each other for security? Which defects could be corrected while preserving the registry's basic design, and which are inseparable from it? Please write in American English.
