Last night I had a long argument with a friend about "performance legitimacy" — the claim that the prosperity and public order delivered in places like China and Singapore are enough to make authoritarian rule legitimate. He kept invoking the reform-era slogan often attributed to Deng Xiaoping: "It doesn't matter whether the cat is black or white, as long as it catches mice." For him, this settled the matter: contested elections, independent courts, and civil liberties are just the cat's color, while growth and stability are the mice, and only the mice matter. I suspect that one sentence is doing several different jobs at once, but I am having trouble separating them precisely. Could you do two things? First, lay out the distinct claims the slogan could be used to assert — for example, that outcomes are the only sensible test of a policy, that good results show a government is competent, that good results give it a right to rule, that pragmatic experiment matters more than institutional form, and any others you can identify — and for each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or a false one. Second, say which of those readings would actually support my friend's conclusion that prosperity and order justify authoritarian rule, and which would not. Please answer in American English.
