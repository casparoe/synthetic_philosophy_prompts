I've ended up, sort of against my own taste, holding a broadly Kim-style line on mental causation. The positive idea: all the causing goes on at the level of the physical realizers—neurons, activation patterns, whatever—and mental properties like being in pain, intending to raise your arm, or thinking about Vienna are real enough, but they don't do any causal work over and above what the physical goings-on are already doing. They get to be real but idle. The causal exclusion argument makes this feel close to forced. I want to know what would count against it.

Treat this as a map of the view's exposure, not a survey of the debate. What would have to be true, or what would I have to come to believe, for the view to be false? Rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward. Say what this view predicts that its rivals don't (about why physical explanation seems to close the causal chain, or why mental causation still seems introspectively obvious, or about how interventionist-style causal reasoning would handle minds). And say where reasonable defenders of the view would themselves admit it's vulnerable—not just what critics shout. I'm personally most swayed by interventionist/counterfactual accounts of causation, so if those are a special problem for the view, dont just wave them off. Finally, be honest about whether the view is insulated: can 'over and above' and 'realizer' always be re-tuned to soak up any counterexample, and if so, is that insulation itself a reason to downgrade the view?
