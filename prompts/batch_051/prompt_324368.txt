So I'm taking this seminar on the ethics of war and a friend and I got into an argument that I can't stop thinking about. The Responsibility to Protect (R2P), as the UN endorsed it in 2005, was built on the idea that sovereignty is not a license for mass atrocities, and that when a government is killing its own civilians the international community has a responsibility to step in militarily as a last resort. Kosovo in 1999 was the big precedent: NATO bombed Serbia without Security Council authorization, and plenty of people thought it was still the right thing to do. Iraq then showed what happens when humanitarian language gets used as a cover for regime change, so when Libya blew up in 2011 the whole point was to do R2P cleanly — this time the Security Council actually authorized force to protect civilians. Only it didn't stay clean. The operation turned into regime change, Gaddafi was overthrown and killed, and the country fell appart into militias and years of on-and-off civil war. Since then, the same Security Council members have pointed to Libya as a reason to block action on Syria and other atrocities, and a lot of people say R2P is dead or at least permanently crippled.

Here's the thing I want to sort out. Does the underlying argument for R2P survive what happened in Libya? I can see the easy answer: Libya was just bad execution, the interveners went beyond civilian protection, and that's a problem of implementation, not of principal. But I suspect the Libya outcome puts pressure on an actual premise in the argument — maybe something like: outside powers can use limited force to protect civilians without being dragged into regime change and without making things worse than the atrocity they're trying to stop. Which premise of R2P does Libya actually bear on? Is there a repaired version of R2P that still permits something real — a no-fly zone, arming rebels, a UN-authorized limited intervention — but is structured to block the creep toward regime change? Or has Libya shown that the idea of neutral, limited humanitarian war was never really available in the first place?
