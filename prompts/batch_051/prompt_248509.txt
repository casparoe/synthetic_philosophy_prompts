After a run of minor coincidences in a single week — a colleague and I repeatedly choosing the same lunch spot, a name from a dream appearing in a message the next morning — I began to wonder why some of these felt as though they must mean something, while others were easy to shrug off. I know enough to recognize the birthday-paradox style of explanation: each particular coincidence is improbable, but the chance that some coincidence of a comparable kind will occur across enough opportunities can be quite high. Yet I have also found that when I recount one of these episodes, the vivid specifics seem to resist that deflation. I would like a thought experiment built to order that forces this tension into the open and lets us see exactly where the disagreement lives.

Construct a thought experiment in which two observers assess the same coincidence and, on the same information, reach contrary conclusions about whether it warrants positing some non-chance connection — for example, an unnoticed prior link between two people or a process that is not as random as it appears. Constraints: the setting should be ordinary — a workplace, a residential building, a school, a family — with no parapsychology, no contrived conspiracy, and no hidden evidence. The two observers must both be competent reasoners; the disagreement should not arise from one of them making an arithmetic error or from one being generally credulous. One observer should frame the event narrowly — the exact event in all its individuating detail — and conclude that an event this improbable is strong evidence for a hidden connection. The other should frame it broadly — the type of event that would have been noticed and told as an anecdote if any nearby variant of it had occurred, counted over the relevant number of opportunities — and conclude that the evidence is too weak to support that inference.

After describing the scenario, show each observer's reasoning, adding a rough numerical gloss where it helps but stopping short of formal calculation. Then identify the structural feature of the case that does the work; I suspect it will concern the choice of reference class, or what gets counted as "the event that occurred" rather than "an event of that kind." Next, explain what happens if that feature is removed: if the target event had been specified in advance, the broad class had been fixed independently of what happened, or the number of opportunities had been small and known, would the disagreement collapse — and in whose favor? End with the strongest reply available to the narrow-framing observer, such as the claim that the broad class is gerrymandered or that individuating details are being ignored, and say whether that reply should persuade us.
