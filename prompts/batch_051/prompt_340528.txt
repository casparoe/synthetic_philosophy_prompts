I'm leading a reading group on Being and Nothingness and I've realised I can't actually give a crisp working definition of bad faith; the usual gloss, self-deception, doesn't seperate the café waiter from an actor on stage, and the minute someone pushes on either example it falls apart. So give me yours — a proper working definition, tested against the cases below, not the textbook one-liner.

Case A: Sartre's café waiter — the one whose movements are a bit too quick, too precise, too much like he's playing at being a waiter. I take it this is meant to be a clear case of bad faith, and your definition has to count it.

Case B: a friend who, every time he's late or forgets a plan, says 'I can't help it, I'm just a disorganised person.' I'd call that bad faith too, and I want the definition to explain why, not just by pointing at the words.

Case C: a professional actor on stage playing a king. I'm fairly sure this isn't bad faith, and my students will want to know why it differs from the waiter. The definition has to draw that line without special pleading.

Case D: a soldier who follows an order he privately thinks is wrong, telling himself 'I'm a soldier; I follow orders.' I want to call this bad faith, but I'm not sure whether it's bad faith because he's deceiving himself about having a choice or because he's hiding behind the role. Your call.

Case E: a friend with severe OCD who says of their compulsions 'I can't not do it' — said clinically, not as a figure of speech. Sartre's talk of radical freedom seems to rule out a real inability, so I honestly don't know whether the framework has room for this or whether it's forced to call it bad faith and thereby shows its limit. Whichever it is, say so.

Case F: a person at an AA meeting saying 'I'm an alcoholic.' On the face of it that's identifying with a fixed nature, which sounds like bad faith in Sartre's vocabulary. But it's also widely treated as the start of taking responsibility. I've no idea how your definition lands here, and I want to know.

Propose the definition, run it through all six in order, say which verdict you're least secure about, and tell me where the definition needed revising or where you'll defend a surprising verdict. End the reply with a one-sentence summary of the definition you settled on, as the very last line.
