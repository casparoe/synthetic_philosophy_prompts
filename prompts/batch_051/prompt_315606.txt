I'm a PhD student in ancient philosophy and moral psychology, and theres a parallel I keep going back and forth on. Plutarch's method in the Lives—pairing a Greek with a Roman and then using the appended comparison to mark out what to imitate or avoid—and a lot of hagiography both treat a life as a stored case: the reader isn't handed a rule but is meant to see their own situation as relevantly similar and act accordingly. That looks structurally a lot like case-based reasoning in AI (retrieve, reuse, revise, retain) and exemplar theory in psychology, where categories and decisions are handled by analogy to stored exemplars rather than by abstract rules. The mapping would be: the corpus of lives is the case base, the paired comparison is the retrieval and similarity-weighting stage, and moral edification is the reuse. If that's a real structural identity and not me projecting the current science back onto old texts, it seems to predict that moral learning from lives should improve as the case base grows and diversifies, and that a narrow or repetitive corpus should degrade moral judgement roughly like a sparse CBR system fails on unfamiliar cases. That prediction feels right for some hagiography (a diet of nothing but desert ascetics probably misfires in ordinary life) but maybe wrong for Plutarch, where the point seems as much about shaping what you admire and desire as about asking what the nearest neighbor to your situation is. So is this deep, and if it is, does anything from CBR transfer—case adaptation, the indexing problem, the problem of picking similarity weights? Or does the analogy break exactly at the point where moral education isn't classification?
