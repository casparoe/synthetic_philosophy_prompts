I serve as secretary to a small charitable trust that governs a regional museum in the United Kingdom. The museum holds a collection of Benin court objects — usually called the Benin Bronzes — taken during the punitive expedition of 1897 and donated to the museum by a Liverpool merchant in 1903. The deed of trust contains this clause, which I quote in full:

"The objects comprised in the Bequest shall be kept together in perpetuity for the benefit and instruction of the public, and shall not be sold, exchanged, given, or otherwise disposed of; nor shall any object be removed from the Museum's premises except for temporary public exhibition elsewhere, and then only upon such security and for such period as the Board may determine."

The Board has now received a formal claim from representatives of the Edo people and the Benin royal court. The claimants expressly do not ask for the physical return of the objects. Instead they ask the Board to (i) transfer legal title to the objects while allowing the museum to retain custody under a long-term caretaker agreement, and (ii) amend the public catalogue so that each object is described as "on loan from the Kingdom of Benin". The trustees disagree about whether the first request amounts to a "disposal" and whether the second is consistent with the status the deed creates. The donor and the original trustees plainly did not have a claim of this form in view.

Analyse the claim under three approaches: the plain words of the clause; the purpose or mischief the clause was designed to prevent; and what the donor and the original trustees would most plausibly have intended had this claim been put to them in 1903. Then say which of the three approaches should control here, and why. Please write your answer in British English.
