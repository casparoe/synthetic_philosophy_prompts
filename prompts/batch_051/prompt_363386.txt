Question: Consider the blue bus and gatecrasher examples. For each, set out the relevant facts, the applicable standard of proof, and what the probability seems to establish. Then evaluate the claim that the lesson of these examples is that a verdict should never rest on purely statistical evidence; if the claim needs qualifying, give the qualification and test it against at least one real evidentiary practice (e.g., DNA evidence).

Student answer: The blue bus case is about a plaintiff who is hit by a bus and cannot remember which company ran it; the only evidence against the Blue Bus Company is that it operates 80% of the buses on the route. Since that makes it 80% likely the bus was a Blue Bus Company bus, and the civil standard is preponderance of the evidence (more likely than not), it looks at first as if the plaintiff should win, but nearly everyone agrees the plaintiff loses. The gatecrasher case is the same idea: 1,000 people attend a rodeo but only 499 tickets were sold, so 501 people must have entered without paying; a randomly chosen attendee is 50.1% likely to be a gatecrasher, which is again more likely than not, yet no court would find that particular person liable on that alone. What these examples show is that statistical evidence can never be enough to convict, because proof beyond a reasonable doubt has to single out this defendant as the actual wrongdoer. Statistical evidence only says something about a group or a rate, not about what this defendant did. So the lesson is that a verdict should never rest on purely statistical evidence. The deeper reason is that statistical evidence carries a known uncertainty or error rate, whereas individualized evidence like an eyewitness or a confession ties the defendant to the event directly. DNA evidence is not a counterexample, because a match is individualized — it is this specific person's profile — so it is not purely statistical in the objectionable sense.
