I have been trying to work out whether Leibniz's claim that this is the best of all possible worlds can be tested against a carefully drawn thought experiment, and I would like you to reason through it with me.

As I understand him, in the Theodicy Leibniz holds that before creating, God in effect surveys an infinity of possible worlds. A possible world is not just any set of facts; it is a complete, mutually compatible system of things, each of which could coexist with all the others. Since only one such world can be actual, God must have a sufficient reason to pick one, and that reason is that the chosen world has the greatest total perfection. Leibniz takes perfection to involve at least two features that can pull against each other: the world should be as rich and varied as possible in the kinds of beings and events it contains, while also being as orderly and lawlike as possible, because order and simplicity in the principles that produce variety are themselves a kind of beauty.

Here is the case I want to test. Take world A to be the actual world. Now take world B to be a possible world exactly like A except for one difference: in B, one entire class of conscious animal life is absent, and with it a particular form of apparently pointless suffering that, in A, appears late in evolutionary history and seems to contribute to no further good, no greater variety, and no moral or intellectual development in any other being. Otherwise B has the same laws, the same degree of order, and nearly the same variety as A, just slightly less of it.

By ordinary moral judgment, B looks better than A. By Leibniz's stated criterion, though, it is not obvious, because B has less variety than A and no greater order.

Please go through Leibniz's criterion piece by piece, treating variety, order, and total perfection as separate tests. Does world A still qualify as the best of all possible worlds, or would B outrank it? Where exactly does the criterion underdetermine an answer? And does this case show that Leibniz's criterion, at least as I have paraphrased it, needs an additional principle, or only that our moral intuitions are a poor guide to metaphysical perfection?
