My housemate and I got into this after I kept refusing to spray the aphids on our kitchen herbs, and he turned my half-formed worry into an argument that now seems to prove too much. It goes:

(1) If a creature might be sentient, that possibility has to enter the moral calculus; you can't round it down to zero just because you're unsure.
(2) Insect sentience is genuinely unsettled, and there's respectable proposals that at least some insects feel something like pain, so we should assign some nonzero probability p to each insect being able to suffer.
(3) One treatment with a garden insecticide kills tens or hundreds of thousands of insects; expected sentient beings killed is p times that, which is large for any p that isn't absurdly tiny.
(4) Compare killing a steer: that's one animal we're certain is sentient, and even people who think it can be justified treat it as a serious moral cost. On expected-value grounds, the aphid spray looks comparable to, or worse than, killing a lot of steers.
(5) And the reasoning doesn't stop at pesticides. It applies to walking across a lawn, driving on a summer evening, washing your hands, taking antibiotics — each kills huge numbers of small arthropods that might be sentient.
(6) So either ordinary life is, by expected value, among the worst moral atrocities we can commit, or the reasoning breaks somewhere.

His conclusion was that if you accept (1)–(5) you're stuck with (6), so the only consistent replies are paralysis or giving up expected-value reasoning altogether. I don't love either. I'd especially like to keep (1), because dropping it feels like giving myself a blank check to ignore uncertain harms. But (6) can't be right. Where does the argument actually go wrong, and what do I pay if I reject the step you'd reject?
