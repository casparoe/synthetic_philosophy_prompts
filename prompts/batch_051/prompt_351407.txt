Below is a question from a media law course, followed by a student's answer. Assign a grade out of 20 and provide the comments you would return to the student, distinguishing the parts of the answer that are sound from those that need correction.

Question: Under modern U.S. defamation law, are published credit ratings and forward-looking financial forecasts protected from liability as opinions under the First Amendment? Explain the governing framework and apply it to two hypotheticals: (i) a rating agency assigns a bond issue a speculative-grade rating and adds that it has reviewed the issuer's financial statements and found them materially misleading; (ii) a financial newsletter states that a company will default on its debts within a year because it is already insolvent.

Student answer: Since Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co., opinion is protected not through a categorical privilege but by asking whether a statement is objectively verifiable as true or false. On that test, a speculative credit grade is a subjective evaluation and a projection of future default is a prediction about the future, so neither is false-capable; both are protected. The rating agency's added remark that it reviewed the issuer's financial statements and found them materially misleading is verifiable and therefore actionable if false, so that part of the hypothetical is the only exposure. The newsletter's claim that the company is already insolvent, by contrast, is merely the factual premise of its forecast; it should be protected along with the prediction, because a court will read the publication as a whole and subordinate supporting reasoning to the overall predictive character.
