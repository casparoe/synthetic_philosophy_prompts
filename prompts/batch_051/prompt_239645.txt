I've been going back and forth on futarchy lately and I want you to help me sort out a particular tension in it rather than survey the whole debate. I'm genuinely drawn to the core machinery: voters pick a welfare metric (the values), and then prediction markets bet on which proposed policy would push that metric the most (the beliefs). As a decision procedure it has real virtues — it's simple to state, it collapses thousands of separate policy fights into one question (which policy maximizes the chosen score?), and it gives people who actually know things a financial reason to surface what they know instead of parroting their team. I don't want to give that up without a fight.

But I've got an intuition I can't shake, and it's not the boring "markets can be dumb" one. It's this: there seem to be decisions where the very act of making them turn on a market's judgment changes what the act means. Take going to war, or granting someone asylum. If the reason we go to war is that a market of mostly disinterested traders priced the GDP-plus-wellbeing conditional higher for the war proposal than for not-war, I feel like something has been lost even if the outcome turns out fine — not because the market is wrong, but because the wrong kind of reason is doing the authorizing. It feels like there's a real difference between using prediction markets as one adviser among others, and making them the decision procedure. But I honestly can't tell whether that's a real moral distinction or just an aesthetic reflex / status-quo bias.

There's a second, sharper worry too. The usual defense of futarchy says markets aggregate information because traders are betting on outcomes they don't causally control. But in a working futarchy the market's output selects the policy, and the policy causes the outcome. Once traders are causally upstream of the outcomes their predicting, a deep-pocketed trader with a stake in some policy doesn't need to be right — she needs to nudge the conditional prediction enough to get the policy enacted, and she may happily take a small loss in the market in exchange for a large gain outside it. So the "prices reveal information" story has to be reargued once the market is inside the causal loop rather than outside it.
