I'm working on a project that requires translating a piece of classical East Asian philosophy into the vocabulary of contemporary affective science, and I'd like you to attempt the translation and then report where it breaks down. The source is the Korean Four–Seven debate: the sixteenth-century dispute, principally between Yi Hwang (Toegye) and Gi Dae-seung (Gobong), later reformulated by Yi I (Yulgok), over how to relate the Four Beginnings — commiseration, shame and dislike, deference and yielding, and the sense of right and wrong — to the Seven Emotions: joy, anger, sorrow, fear, love, hatred, and desire. Toegye's mature position was that the Four Beginnings issue from li, the normative-cum-metaphysical principle, with qi, the psychophysical medium, merely following, whereas the Seven Emotions issue from qi with li riding on them; correspondingly, the Four are intrinsically good while the Seven can be good or evil. Gobong and Yulgok resisted this bifurcation: on their view the Seven Emotions already include the Four, and the difference Toegye treats as a difference of source is better understood as a difference of level or aspect within a single qi-based emotional life, with the Four being the Seven in their properly regulated, morally good form.

I myself work in affective science and am comfortable with the standard theoretical vocabulary: basic emotion theories and affect programs, appraisal theories, core affect and dimensional valence–arousal descriptions, automatic versus reflective processing, and the literature on moral emotions such as guilt, shame, and admiration. Please restate the two sides of the Four–Seven debate in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can, keeping the translation honest rather than assimilating either side to a contemporary view it did not hold. Then give a residue report, organized in three parts: what translates cleanly, what can be translated only by distortion, and what has no counterpart in the affective-science vocabulary at all. Finally, offer a judgment about the direction of the residue: does it show a dimension of the Korean debate — presumably its normative or metaphysical commitments — that contemporary emotion science cannot express, or does it show that part of the sixteenth-century dispute was generated by the li–qi vocabulary and dissolves once the same phenomena are described in your terms? I am less interested in a historical survey than in the conceptual accounting: what survives the move from one idiom to the other, and whether the failure to survive is a defect of the destination vocabulary or of the source claims.
