Last night, a friend and I argued at length about whether the reputation scores on sites such as Reddit and Stack Exchange measure anything worth measuring. I took the skeptical side: early votes exert disproportionate influence, an accumulated score continues to color how a user's later contributions are read, and in many communities a prompt joke outperforms a careful correction posted later. My friend held that the score simply aggregates the community's judgments, and that objecting to any particular ranking amounts to wanting one's own preferences imposed. By the end, we had exposed a deeper disagreement that we could not settle: what exactly is such a score a measure of? Is it a record of value already produced, a credential that predicts the reliability of future contributions, or merely a display of popularity? What theory of quality, trust, or reputation does a cumulative, publicly displayed, per-user score presuppose, and which rival theories does its design rule out before anyone casts a vote? Are the design choices — one vote per user per contribution, costless voting, no decay over time, and a cumulative score attached to the person rather than to the contribution — mutually consistent? Finally, if one wanted the system to track something closer to reliable contribution or future trustworthiness rather than popularity, what would need to change in the mechanics by which scores are earned, displayed, and used?
