I keep seeing Marx cited as a prophet of AI-driven unemployment, almost always in one or two sentences, with the fragment on machines in the Grundrisse waved around as if it settled the matter. I want an inside-view reconstruction instead: what would Marx's considered position on present-day machine-learning-based automation have been, derived from the actual operating parts of his theory rather than from the familiar slogans.

Start from what we know. Marx's mature account of machinery has several layers that do not all point the same way. In Capital, a machine is adopted under capitalism only when its cost is less than the wages it displaces, so that it cheapens commodities; it raises relative surplus value by shortening necessary labor time, deskills and disciplines workers, creates an industrial reserve army, and raises the organic composition of capital, which grounds his tendency of the rate of profit to fall. But in the fragment on machines in the Grundrisse, he opens a different possibility: as accumulated knowledge and technique congeal in fixed capital, the general intellect of society becomes a direct productive force, the worker tends to become an overseer and regulator of the process rather than a direct producer, and labor time stops being an adequate measure of wealth. On one reading, automation carries the seed of capitalism's overcoming; on the other, it is simply a sharper instrument of relative surplus value and control.

Now bring him forward. Suppose Marx could inspect, without being distracted by the novelty of the technology itself: large language models trained on corpora containing much of humanity's accumulated writing and code, robotic process automation, warehouse robots, algorithmic management of gig work, the economics of training runs and open-weight models, and digital goods with near-zero marginal reproduction cost. Predict his considered views, and defend them against these specific tests:

- Would he classify a trained model as fixed constant capital, or would he see its training corpus as the general intellect made concrete? Does the answer change his political prediction, and if so, how?
- Does the automation of white-collar and cognitive labor extend his industrial-reserve-army account cleanly, or does it strain it, for instance because the deskilled work is itself the production of knowledge, or because displaced workers get reabsorbed into producing training data and preference labels?
- Would he judge that labor time has now, or soon will, cease to be an adequate measure of value? If so, would he expect capital to be overthrown by that fact, or expect capital to reimpose labor time as a measure through platforms, microtasks, and cognitive piecework?
- Does his falling-rate-of-profit tendency survive when the fixed capital can be copied for nearly nothing and when the process is precisely the displacement of variable capital in a labor-theory-of-value accounting?
- Would he read an AI-abundant economy as the maturation of the Grundrisse possibility, or as a dystopia in which labor is subsumed more completely than before?

For each answer, give the prediction as Marx's view, show the chain of inference from his actual commitments, and mark where his categories map cleanly, where they must be bent to fit the new case, and where the theory underdetermines an answer. If he is forced to choose between keeping the labor theory of value and accepting the end of labor time as the measure of wealth, say which choice is more faithful to the rest of his system.
