When my cousin got married a few years back and took her husband's last name, everyone switched within weeks — invitations, work email, her own mother — and nobody acted as though anything about who she was had changed. It was just 'Lisa Chen' now, same person, new tag. But earlier this year an old friend started going by a new first name, and the same crowd that handled the surname switch so easily kept using his old one for months, saying it was hard to remember; one aunt even insisted the old first name was his real name. So there is a weird asymmetry: ordinary speakers treat a family name as a replaceable label whose reference glides along with the person, while treating a given name as if it were fused to the person's identity — change it and you have almost changed who the name picks out. I'm not asking about etiquette or whether people are being jerks; assume they should update either way. What I want to know is whether anything semantically or conceptually justifies the difference between how we treat given names and family names in our ordinary understanding of reference.

If both are just names for the same person, why does changing one feel to people like a routine label swap and changing the other feel like pretending to refer to a different person? Or is the whole thing just habit and frequency? I want the strongest principle you can find that would justify the asymmetry, tested against a nearby case or two, and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up as a mere inconsistency.
