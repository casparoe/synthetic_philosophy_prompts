I have spent a few years working out a position I call the No-Upgrade Principle. Its central claim is that a person can be made better off only by repairing something already wrong with them — hunger, fatigue, wounded pride, boredom, the pressure of an unsatisfied want — and that there is no such thing as adding positive well-being to a life that is already whole. I never enjoyed a meal except as the relief of hunger, or rest except as the relief of tiredness, and I believe these cases exhaust the possibilities. If this is right, only suffering, including mild and unnoticed lacks, has intrinsic moral weight, and an already contented creature could never be benefited, only protected from future damage. I know this sounds close to the claim that happiness is merely the absence of suffering, and to a kind of pessimism. Reconstruct the principle in its strongest form, name the existing views it most resembles, say whether it is genuinely distinct or only a variant of them, and then give me the strongest case of a person being genuinely upgraded rather than merely repaired. Tell me whether the principle can honestly absorb that case, and whether a failure to absorb it is fatal to the principle or merely an edge case it can leave unexplained.
