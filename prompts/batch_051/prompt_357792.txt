Last night I got into a two-hour argument with a friend about a local school board controversy, and I'm still trying to figure out who was right. The board was considering a new rule that would require homeschooling families to submit to yearly standardized testing and a home visit from a state evaluator. One parent got up and argued against it by saying parents answer to God for how they raise their children, not to the state. Another parent argued against it on the grounds that the rule enforces a particular conception of what a good education is, and a free society shouldn't impose that on families. What struck me was how differently the room handled these two speakers. The God-based objection got eye rolls and was treated as obviously out of order; the second one got nods and was treated as a legitimate contribution to public debate. My friend says that's exactly right, because religious reasons are private and public decisions need reasons everyone can in principle share. I kept pushing back by saying the second argument is also a deeply contested moral view, not some neutral thing, so the asymmetry is just prejudice.

At some point my friend brought up Mill's On Liberty, the passage near the beginning where he says social opinion can be a more intrusive tyrant than law, and that the despotism of custom is the standing hindrance to human advancement because it makes people fail to ask what they themselves prefer. My friend took that passage to support excluding religious appeals from public deliberation, because religion is, he said, inherited custom. I don't think the passage does that work; Mill seems to be worried about majority moral opinion generally, not just religion, except maybe when he says society should not enforce its own opinions as rules. So I want to know: is there a principled justification for treating the first parent's argument as out of bounds while letting the second one in? If there is, state the principle and test it against nearby cases like a parent arguing from a Kantian view about autonomy, a parent arguing from a utilitarian calculation about children's long-run welfare, or a parent arguing that the state must be neutral among religions. If there is no principled difference, say whether ordinary practice should give up the asymmetry, or whether the practice is tracking something else, like civility or a prediction that religious appeals will be less open to revision. Also tell me honestly whether the Mill passage supports my friend or me.
