I recently retired from a long career in physical chemistry and have spent the past two years working through the philosophy of mathematics. I have now been asked, as an outside examiner, to contribute one question to the final examination of an upper-level undergraduate course on set theory and the philosophy of mathematics. The students have seen the derivation of Russell's paradox, the Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms, and the iterative conception of set, but their regular instructor reports a persistent problem: when asked about Russell's paradox, most students simply reproduce the derivation and conclude that there is no set of all sets that are not members of themselves. He wants a question that cannot be answered by that recitation, and that separates students who understand the structure of the paradox from those who have memorized the labels.

Please draft one essay question, answerable in about forty-five minutes, whose crux is the relationship between the paradox and the principle of unrestricted comprehension. The question should force students to distinguish several claims that are routinely run together: that a particular object fails to exist; that some predicates fail to determine sets; that unrestricted comprehension is false; and that the iterative conception is the correct replacement. It should also require them to say, precisely, why Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory avoids the paradox through Separation rather than through a special prohibition on self-membership, and to identify at least one thing the iterative conception leaves unexplained. Then provide a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme on a 20-point scale. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a top answer must contain and, for each common error it penalizes, say which misunderstanding that error reflects. The difficulty should come from the conceptual crux rather than from obscurity, and the question should not depend on any source beyond what the students have already covered.
