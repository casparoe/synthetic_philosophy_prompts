Okay, here's one I've always found under-explained. Walk me through what happens to the Kripkean necessary-a-posteriori story when you bring in 'actually'. The standard line runs: proper names are rigid, so if Hesperus is Phosphorus than the identity holds in every world, but you still had to find it out empirically. Definite descriptions like 'the evening star' aren't rigid, since some other object could have been the evening star, and that's why 'the evening star is the morning star' comes out only contingent. Now consider 'the actual evening star is the actual morning star,' where 'actual' is the rigidifier from modal logic: it makes the description pick out, at each world, whichever object satisfies it in the actual world. If the ordinary descriptive identity is true, this actualized version seems true at every world too. So: is it necessary, and is it a priori or a posteriori? And the part I really want nailed down: has the descriptivist just built herself a pair of rigid designators that do all the work names do, or is there a real difference left between the actualized-description case and the proper-name case? Be precise about what 'actually' does to the sentence's modal and epistemic profile.
