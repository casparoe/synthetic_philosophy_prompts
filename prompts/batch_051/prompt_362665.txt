hi! so i'm doing an essay for an RE competition and i'd love some comments on my draft before i write it up properly. the essay title is something like: if God looked at the world and called it good, how come the fossil record shows millions of years of animals eating each other, dying of disease, and so on, long before humans even existed? i want to do more than just say the Bible says so; my teacher keeps telling me to engage with the actual problem.

my argument so far is basically this. lots of christians (and my gran) think before the Fall there was no death at all, and that this is part of what it means for creation to be good: no pain, no death, lions eating grass, that kind of thing. but if evolution is true that cant be literally the case, because the fossil record is full of predation and disease and animals dying way before humans show up. so either creation was never really good in that sense, or we're using the wrong sense of good.

i go for the second option. i make a distinction between a world being good as a whole and a world being nice for every creature at every moment. a forest can be an organised, flourishing system even though one particular deer gets eaten by a wolf. the wolf eating the deer isn't a flaw in the system, it's the system working. so "good" in Genesis might mean ordered, complete, fit for purpose, rather than morally perfect with no suffering. i know that sounds a bit cold, and i want comments on that specifically.

then my second move is about what the Fall actually does. i say maybe the Fall doesn't introduce death full stop; it introduces death as something that separates humans from God, because we're the kind of creatures who can know we're going to die and build an identity around that. animals don't do that, so animal death before the Fall is sad but not theologically the same kind of problem. the Fall changes our relationship to death, rather than switching on death in general. then the verse everyone brings up about death entering the world through one man's sin can be read as being about human death-as-separation, not about every animal death in history.

third, i say Genesis isn't doing paleontology. its point is that the world is God's ordered project rather than a chaotic mess, and that project included a long run-up with animals living and dying before humans. that lets me keep evolution and still say creation was good. but i keep worrying this makes God look like he sees animal suffering as just scaffolding for making humans, which feels a bit vile, and it makes it harder to explain the bits where Paul talks about creation being frustrated and waiting to be set right as if something cosmic has actually gone wrong. my friend suggested i could get round this by saying the Fall happens outside time or its effects reach backwards into the deep past, but that feels like cheating and i cant say exactly why its worse than my third point.

so the things i'd really like comments on:

1. is the distinction between animal death as part of the natural order and human death as separation from God actually stable, or does it fall apart when you push on it?

2. is my reading of the verse about death entering through one man's sin just dodging the plain meaning? if so, is there a stronger version of it?

3. the big one: does the whole thing collapse once you admit animals can clearly suffer and seem to fear death? if a fawn dies slowly, that isn't order, it's just bad. can i keep my line without having to claim animals can't feel pain, which i think is false?

4. are there obvious objections i've missed that an examiner would expect me to deal with? (arguments only, i don't need a reading list.)

feel free to be brutal, its only a draft. sorry this is a bit rambly.
