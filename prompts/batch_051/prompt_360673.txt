I have spent part of this term teaching Aristotle's account of exchange in the Nicomachean Ethics and the Politics, and part of it reading work on multi-objective decision making, and I keep noticing a resemblance I cannot settle. In the Ethics Aristotle argues that exchange between people who need different things presupposes a common measure; money, he says, plays this role by convention, standing in for need, and only because money supplies such a measure can a pair of shoes and a house stand in a determinate ratio. But he immediately hedges: things that differ so much cannot, strictly speaking, be rendered commensurable; they are made so only in a practical, need-relative way. Scalarization in decision analysis looks structurally similar. To compare options that score in incommensurable units—cost, delay, environmental damage, quality—an analyst forms a weighted sum of the criteria and treats the resulting scalar as a common scale. The weights are not read off the world; they are imposed by convention or policy, and different weights can reverse the ranking of options, yet the procedure is routinely treated as a genuine commensuration for the purposes of choice. So both cases seem to involve the same move: a conventional, need- or preference-relative device that produces sufficient commensurability without pretending that the underlying values are one kind of thing.

My question is whether this parallel is deep or superficial. Please give the most exact mapping you can between Aristotle's money and scalarization, noting where each element on one side has or lacks a counterpart on the other. Then say what would have to be true of both sides for any result to transfer from one domain to the other—for instance, could the dependence of scalar rankings on the choice and normalization of weights illuminate Aristotle's claim that money is conventional and can be rendered useless by a change in convention? Finally, identify at least one prediction the parallel seems to license that turns out to be false, and say whether that failure is merely a blemish on the analogy or points to a real structural difference.
