On longstanding rationalist forum threads about whether meditation or psychedelic experiences are “real insights” or just felt insights, the impasse resembles a debate in philosophy of religion that I have come to think is misdiagnosed. The debate centers on a passage from William James’s Varieties of Religious Experience, Lecture XVI, where he lists four marks of mystical states: ineffability, noetic quality, transiency, and passivity. The first two marks, ineffability and noetic quality, seem to be in tension: the mystical state is described as beyond expression, yet it is also described as carrying a felt sense of having encountered deep truth, not merely emotion. I want a diagnosis of why this dispute has stalled, not a survey of positions. One side says the noetic feeling is just a phenomenological overlay on a non-representational state, so mysticism yields no knowledge; the other side says the noetic quality is itself evidence of a kind of non-propositional knowledge. My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement is not about the phenomenology, which both sides describe the same way, but about what “knowledge” means when applied to ineffable content—and perhaps about whether “ineffable” means “not expressible in any language” or merely “not expressible in ordinary public language.” Is this a merely verbal dispute, a substantive disagreement about the nature of knowledge, or a disagreement about standards of evidence for first-person reports? And given that diagnosis, what could count as progress: a conceptual distinction, an empirical study of meditators’ reports, a formal account of non-conceptual content, or something else?
