I help run a small cooperative housing community of about forty adults that makes decisions by consensus, and we are currently revising our norms about how much latitude members should have to live in unconventional or idiosyncratic ways. Mill argues in On Liberty that people should be free to conduct what he calls experiments in living, and that individuality is valuable partly because it lets different people try out different modes of life rather than all conforming to a single social standard. But he also worries about the tyranny of prevailing opinion and about the ways in which even noncoercive social pressure can stifle individuality. I would like you to spell out the most important implications of Mill's experiments-in-living argument for how our community should approach this. In particular, where should we draw the line between self-regarding experiments and other-regarding harms when people share walls, common meals, and a single set of collective rules? Is there a Millian case for tolerating ways of living that make other members genuinely uncomfortable, even when the discomfort is not based on any obvious physical or financial harm? And what would Mill's view imply about the difference between tolerating an experiment and actively protecting it from the community's pressure to conform? Please write in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter.
