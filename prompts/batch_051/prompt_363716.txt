I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm a few weeks into a long piece on cognitive penetration — the idea that what you know, want, or believe can literally shape what you see, not just what you infer from it. My editor already wants the lede to be "your beliefs literally change what you see," and I need to know whether that sentence is defensible or just a hashtag. The problem is every researcher I talk to has a different idea of what would even count, and I've started to suspect some of them are arguing past each other because they never agreed on the bar. So before I write another sentence of the piece, I want the spec: what would an adequate theory of cognitive penetration have to get right? Give me a requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each. I want at least these pressure points handled, but I'm sure there are more:

- The attention problem: if someone tells you where to look, or you just look longer at one feature, that's not cognition seeping into perception. The theory has to say why not, and exactly which kinds of attentional effects it excludes — or doesn't.
- The judgment/report problem: people can say a thing looks one way while their actual perceptual experience is unchanged. How does the theory sort "sees differently" from "says differently"? The classic banana memory-color case seems to sit exactly here — a grayish image of a banana gets reported as slightly yellow because people know bananas are yellow, but its not obvious whether the experience changed or just the report. What does the theory have to do with that case to count as non-vacuous?
- The ambiguity problem: if telling someone "its a duck" flips a duck-rabbit figure, is that penetration? If not, why not? And if the theory wants to exclude it, what separates the duck-rabbit from a genuine case?
- The learning problem: radiologists eventually see tumors they once missed, and chess players see threats on a board. Does the theory count perceptual learning as penetration, or as a permanent retuning of the system — and does that distinction survive being poked?
- The directness problem: one camp says a genuine case needs the cognitive state to influence perception directly and specifically, not just through mood, effort, or eye movements. Is that a real requirement or just a way of defining penetration so nothing qualifies? Whatever the theory says, it has to take a stand here and defend it.
- The "so what" problem: some people use "cognitive penetration" as an all-purpose slur for "top-down effect I don't like." A good theory should tell me which accusations of penetration aren't really about penetration at all.

Then mark which of your requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preferences, and tell me which single requirement, taken seriously, does the most work to rule out the sloppy claims I keep hearing. If some pair of requirements can't be jointly satisfied — say, the theory has to be strict enough to keep attention and judgment out but loose enough that some real cases still qualify — say so, because that tension is probably the actual story I need to write.
