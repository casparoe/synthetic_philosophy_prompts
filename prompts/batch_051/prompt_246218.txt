I am working through John Locke’s Essay concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter xxvii, and I would like help with a specific argumentative step. Here is my own paraphrase of the relevant passage, not a verbatim quotation.

Locke distinguishes the identity of a human being, which he thinks consists in the continued life of the body, from the identity of a person. For personhood, he makes consciousness central: a person is a thinking, intelligent being that can reflect on itself as itself across different times and places. He then describes person as a forensic notion, tied to accountability, law, and the capacity for happiness and misery. Consciousness, on his account, extends the person backward to past actions; it is by consciousness that the person owns past deeds, imputes them to itself, and becomes answerable for them on the same basis as for present deeds.

I am puzzled about what this commits Locke to in a case of advance directives. Suppose a competent person writes a directive refusing life-sustaining treatment if she later develops advanced dementia. By the time the directive would be applied, she may have no memory of writing it and no conscious connection to the beliefs and values that motivated it. I want to understand the philosophical authority of the earlier self over the later individual.

If Locke would say that the earlier writer and the later demented individual are not the same person, because the relevant consciousness does not extend between them, then why should the earlier person have any authority over the later one? But if he would say they are the same person, what consciousness does the identity consist in, given that the memories and reasons are gone? And does his forensic account of personhood imply that an earlier self can legitimately bind, own, or answer for a later self across such a gap? I am interested in the conceptual pressure the passage puts on the case, not in legal or clinical details.
