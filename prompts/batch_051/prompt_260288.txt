Lately I have been unable to shake an argument that a relative of mine uses to justify paying for cryonic preservation, and I would like help locating where the reasoning goes wrong. I am not asking for an estimate of how likely revival is; what I want is a step-by-step diagnosis of the argument itself.

Here is the argument as my relative gives it:

(1) A choice with a small, affordable cost, no downside beyond losing that cost, and a positive expected value is rationally acceptable.
(2) Cryonics membership has a small, affordable cost — roughly the amount one might spend on a minor subscription, paid through a life insurance policy.
(3) If revival succeeds, the payoff is enormous: many additional decades of healthy life, perhaps much more.
(4) If revival fails, one is simply dead at the time one would have died anyway; the only loss is the money spent.
(5) One cannot rule out that some future technology will revive preserved patients, so the probability of success is not literally zero. A probability that is not zero is at least some positive probability.
(6) A successful revival would give me the very thing that makes all other goods usable, so its value is not merely large: it is the kind of good that no small financial cost can meaningfully compete with. When the prize is good enough, any nonzero chance makes a small bet worth taking, and the expected value cannot fail to be favorable.
(7) Therefore signing up for cryonics has a positive expected value and, by (1), is rational.
(8) But the same steps apply to any proposal with a tiny chance of an enormous payoff and a trivial cost: paying a company that promises to revive me from stored tissue, buying a device that a stranger says will preserve my consciousness, and so on. As long as I cannot show the mechanism to be literally impossible, the argument seems to say I should pay.
(9) That cannot be right: it would mean a rational person should hand small sums to every promise of a fantastic future payoff, with no principled stopping point.

I would most like to keep (1), because it fits how I think about insurance, ordinary bets, and other everyday decisions. But if (1) is kept, the problem has to lie somewhere else. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it force me to give up in how I think about decisions of this kind?
