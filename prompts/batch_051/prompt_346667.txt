I help run a small blog whose readership mostly came up through the rationalist and effective-altruism blogosphere, and I want a tool that sorts people by how they actually answer concrete cases rather than by which label they like. Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in applied animal ethics, with a side interest in survey design, and build me a "Which position on rescued animal products do you actually hold?" quiz for this audience. Think carefully and brainstorm candidate distinctions and cases before you commit to an instrument.

The domain is freeganism in the broad sense: whether a person who otherwise avoids animal products for ethical reasons may eat or otherwise use animal products that would otherwise be wasted. Relevant cases include dumpster-dived meat, dairy, and eggs; road-killed animals; a friend's leftover steak; surplus catered food; eggs from well-kept backyard hens; and thrift-store leather. Assume each respondent already follows some version of an animal-avoiding diet on ethical grounds; the quiz is about the edge cases.

Three distinctions matter to me:

1. Whether the wrong-making feature of animal products is mainly the demand they create and the suffering that demand causes, something about the act or attitude itself, or something about the social norms the act entrenches.
2. Whether flesh, secretions such as milk and eggs, and non-edible byproducts such as leather should be treated as one category or separately.
3. Whether avoiding waste is a real moral reason, and whether it can make an otherwise wrong act permissible or even required.

The scoring key must have at least these four positions as leaves:

- A demand-focused consequentialist view: eating or using animal products is permissible, and perhaps better than wasting them, exactly insofar as it does not increase demand for animal production and does not plausibly shift social norms toward more harm.
- A norm-sensitive consequentialist view: also consequentialist, but one that takes the main risk to be social — visible consumption of rescued animal products erodes the norm that animals are not food, so private no-demand use may be fine while public or legible use is much more suspect.
- An abolitionist view: using animal products is wrong even when no demand is created and no one sees, because it treats animal bodies or secretions as resources for humans and perpetuates a category error about animals as property.
- A virtue- or attitude-focused view: the primary problem is in the agent rather than only in the consequences — wanting or choosing to eat animal flesh, in particular, expresses or entrenches a disposition that is incompatible with the right stance toward animals, so rescued flesh may be wrong even when rescued eggs or thrifted leather are less problematic.

Feel free to add one or two further leaves if the answer space genuinely admits them, for example an ecological waste-first view on which rescued animal products are often preferable on food-waste grounds, or a rights-based view that treats flesh and byproducts asymmetrically. If you add a leaf, flag it as a hypothesis about the space rather than a standard position.

Please include at least six of these concrete cases, and stipulate the relevant facts in each case explicitly so respondents are not guessing about details. At minimum, use cases that vary along the dimensions of demand versus no demand, private versus public, flesh versus secretion versus byproduct, and accidental death versus deliberate production. The cases I want available to you are: a road-killed deer on a quiet road; an unopened package of factory-farmed chicken found in a dumpster behind a restaurant; a friend's leftover steak that will be thrown away at a restaurant; surplus roast-beef sandwiches at a workplace where the respondent is known as someone who avoids meat; eggs from a neighbor's well-kept backyard hens that would otherwise rot; a thrift-store leather jacket made from an animal killed for leather; and a publicly posted photo of a rescued meal captioned as "vegan." You may use additional cases if they discriminate better.

Now the deliverable:

- Eight to ten questions. Each question should be a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so respondents answer from the case alone and do not need to know any position labels. No question should ask which label someone identifies with.
- Vary the parameters systematically so the instrument actually separates the leaves. In particular, test whether the respondent treats a public act differently from the same act performed in secret, and whether flesh is treated differently from eggs or leather.
- Include a scoring key that maps each answer onto the leaves, with partial credit where an answer is compatible with several positions. The point is to sort readers by what they say about cases, not by what they call themselves.
- Identify underoccupied or unoccupied combinations of answers, and briefly diagnose each: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Flag any two positions the quiz cannot separate with these questions, rather than silently merging them.
- End with a short self-audit. Tell me which question or scoring decision is most likely to misclassify people, what pattern of responses would lead you to revise the tree or split or merge leaves, and which consideration, if you had weighted it differently, would have changed the taxonomy most. This is the part I most want to see you being willing to revise your own construction.

Use American English throughout. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; lay the reasoning out directly. The instrument should be usable as-is by someone with no formal philosophy background, though the scoring key may be more explicit about the structure than the quiz itself.
