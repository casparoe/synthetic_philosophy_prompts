Think like a political philosopher who works on the ethics of voting, collective action, and individual causal responsibility, and work in two stages: first brainstorm the principles that might justify the asymmetry, then stress-test the best one and give a verdict.

A few nights ago I spent two hours arguing with a friend about whether there is any point in voting. His position, pressed with some force, was that in a sufficiently large electorate a single vote never changes the outcome, so staying home on election day is perfectly defensible and chiding non-voters is unearned moralism. I had no good reply and came away half-convinced. Earlier this week the same friend told me, with visible anger, about a news story in which a house-sitter found his elderly neighbor's sealed mail-in ballot, decided the race was already decided, and threw it in the trash. The candidate went on to lose by tens of thousands of votes, so the destroyed ballot was exactly as outcome-irrelevant as my friend's uncast one. When I pressed the parallel, he would not accept it: the house-sitter, he insisted, had done something seriously wrong as a democratic matter, while the citizen who simply stayed home had done nothing wrong at all.

I want to know whether those two judgments can be held together. What principle could distinguish the citizen who omits to cast his own outcome-irrelevant vote, which many of us treat as blameless or merely lax, from the citizen who destroys someone else's outcome-irrelevant vote, which many of us treat as a genuine violation regardless of the margin? Consider the obvious candidates — that the second person thwarts an already completed act, that voting is a right one may waive for oneself but not for another, that destroying another person's ballot has expressive content that abstaining lacks, that only the second person acts on someone else's agency, or anything else you can construct. Then test your preferred principle against nearby cases: an election official who discards a ballot in a landslide; a busy relative who is entrusted with a ballot and never mails it; a retiree who declines to vote in a safe district; a campaign worker who throws a sealed ballot away in a race decided by a single vote. Give me your strongest reconstruction of the distinction, and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up — and in particular whether my friend can keep both his claim that a single vote is causally inert and his conviction that the house-sitter committed a serious wrong.
