I've been turning this one over for weeks and I want a proper diagnosis, not a "both sides have a point" shrug.

A friend — the sort who has tax-loss harvesting and a giving portfolio — walked me through this and I can't find the exit.

(1) Equality of opportunity means, at minimum, that a person's life prospects shouldn't be settled by morally arbitrary features they're born with, accidents of birth they did nothing to earn.
(2) Being born to wealthy parents is one of those features, and an inheritance is basically the cleanest case of an unearned, luck-of-birth advantage.
(3) So a real commitment to equal opportunity should push you towards taxing inherited wealth at something close to 100% (maybe with a carve-out for the family home, but the principle is the point).
(4) But being born to attentive, educated, emotionally stable parents is also an accident of birth, and it shapes life prospects at least as much as the cash does.
(5) The you-didn't-earn-it case against inheritance seems to carry straight over to the vocabulary, the hidden curriculum, the connections, the habits of attention, the books on the shelves.
(6) So the very same principle that kills inheritance also seems to require the state to neutralise those advantages too — and that line ends in forbidding bedtime stories or taking children away from good parents, which basically nobody, including people who'd cheer a 100% inheritance tax, is willing to accept.
(7) So equal opportunity, pushed to it's logical end, appears to eat itself. Yet (1) still feels right, and it's doing all the work in the inheritance case.

My friend says the whole thing shows "equal opportunity" is a motte-and-bailey: the motte is just "no state discrimination," the bailey is "adult outcomes should be independent of the birth lottery." I half-believe him, but I also can't shake the feeling that the argument is doing more than its premises can bear — I just can't name where, and every time I try to price the options I find I want to keep (1) and (3) and also want parents to be allowed to read to their children, and can't see how to have all three at once.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If there's a principled line that lets you take the inheritance money but leave the bedtime stories, draw it; if not, tell me which premise has to go and what's left of equal opportunity afterwards.
