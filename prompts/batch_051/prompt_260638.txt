I keep noticing a parallel between promising and strategic commitment in game theory (Schelling-style bridge-burning) and I can't tell if its deep or just a loose metaphor. Both seem to work by changing what future-you is able to do, or at least what it's rational for future-you to do: the promise puts your reliability on the line, the burned bridge deletes the retreat option. So a promise to show up is like publicly locking yourself out of not showing up. The mapping in my head: the act of promising corresponds to the commitment move, the promisee's hearing it corresponds to the move being observed, the promisee's standing to complain corresponds to a payoff penalty, and the norm of promise-keeping corresponds to some equilibrium-selection rule. But I can already see one breaking point: a promise made to someone who can't punish you and expects nothing (a promise to a stranger you'll never see again) still seems to bind, which the strategic story would not predict. Is this a genuine structural identity — promises as a species of commitment device — or does the normative force of promising do all the work while game theory just redescribes the incentives after the fact? What would need to be true on both sides for results to transfer in the other direction, say from commitment devices back to a theory of promissory obligation? And besides my stranger case, can you name something the parallel predicts that turns out false?
