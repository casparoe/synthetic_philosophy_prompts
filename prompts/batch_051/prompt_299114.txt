A friend and I spent an evening arguing about how much confidence we should place in historical explanations, and he kept returning to the line "history is written by the victors." In his hands it meant something like: the seeming inevitability of past events is always manufactured after the fact by whoever ended up on top, so the explanatory stories we inherit are just retrospective stories, and one is as good as another. I could see that the slogan points at something real about selective record-keeping and retrospective narrative, but he was using it to conclude that historical explanation in general is epistemically worthless. Could you separate the distinct precise claims that sentence might be making — about whose records survive, whose perspective gets told, whose criteria of success are imposed, and how narratives get fitted to known outcomes — and say for each whether it is trivially true, defensible but partial, or false or confused? Most importantly, which of those readings, if any, actually supports his blanket distrust of historical explanation, and which support only a much weaker claim?
