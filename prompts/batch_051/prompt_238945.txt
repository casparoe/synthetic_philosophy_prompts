I am a retired sensory neuroscientist who has only lately begun reading philosophy of mind, and I keep tripping over the phrase 'novel qualia' in discussions of the limits of experience. I would like a working definition of a novel quale — meaning a qualitative character not already present in the normal adult human sensory repertoire — precise enough to classify each of the following cases, saying in each whether the experience is a new kind of experience or merely a new instance of an old kind.

1. The familiar colour scientist, kept since birth in a black-and-white room, seeing red for the first time. New to her, I assume, but not a novel quale in the sense I intend.
2. A tetrachromat — a person with a fourth class of retinal cone — who, if her reports are taken at face value, perceives a dimension of colour closed to the rest of us.
3. A subject who, under special viewing conditions, reports seeing reddish-green: red and green present together without the muddy yellow that normally results from mixing them, and therefore a colour the standard opponent-process account rules out.
4. Someone who tastes durian for the first time and finds a flavour outside anything in their prior diet.
5. A human fitted with a magnetic-compass interface that adds north as a blue overlay to the visual field.
6. A synaesthete for whom every chord produces a brassy taste, combining two existing modalities.
7. A direct cortical interface that yields a wholly new modality tied to no existing receptor surface, if the engineering proves possible.

I am fairly confident that (1) and (4) are new tokens of old types and that (2) and (7) are candidates for the real thing; the middle cases I cannot settle, and I suspect the phrase runs together novelty for a subject with novelty of a type. Propose the definition or definitions, run them through all seven cases, and tell me which of my labels you would overturn and which verdict you find least secure.
