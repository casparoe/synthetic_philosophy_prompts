Two friends of mine got into it after a philosophy of mind lecture and I honestly couldn't referee it, so I'm outsourcing the adjudication.

Lena said epiphenomenalism is self-defeating. If conscious experiences never cause anything, then when someone says "I'm in pain," the pain itself isn't causing the words — the brain state behind it is. So our verbal reports of consciousness can't be evidence that consciousness exists; the view saws off the branch it sits on.

Omar said that's too quick. A fact doesn't have to cause our reports of it for the reports to be evidence. The same brain state can cause both the pain and the words; then the words track the pain because they share a common cause, the way a barometer reading tracks a storm even though the storm never causes the barometer to fall.

Lena shot back that this only gets you correlation, not aboutness. On epiphenomenalism the content of the sentence "I'm in pain" is fixed by the physics alone. So a physical duplicate with no experiences — a zombie — would produce the same sentence with the same confidence. You couldn't tell your case from his, so your true report would be true by luck. Lucky true reports aren't knowledge of your own consciousness.

Omar said the zombie overreaches. The epiphenomenalist can hold that the same physical state produces the same experience as a matter of law, which makes the zombie scenario nomologically impossible. And knowledge doesn't require the known fact to cause the belief anyway — I know the sun will rise tomorrow, but tomorrow's sunrise isn't causing my belief today. Lawlike covariation does the work.

Lena answered that the sunrise case is different because past sunrises still caused your evidence and the sun's dynamics ground the pattern. An epiphenomenal experience is a free rider: it produces nothing and explains nothing. If the report is produced by physics and made true by physics, and the justification never mentions the experience, then the view makes consciousness invisible to the person having it — which is exactly the paradox we started with.

Omar said that's a description, not an objection. Non-causal isn't invisible: if the laws tie the physical state to the experience, the physical state can carry information about it, and reliable correlation can ground knowledge. The alternative, making experiences push neurons around, requires breaking physical causal closure, which is a much bigger cost.
