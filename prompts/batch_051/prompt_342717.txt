I teach an upper-level ethics seminar, and this year's unit on population axiology stalled in a way that genuinely spooked me: normally a couple students move during the discussion, but this time literal nobody did, me included, and I've been turning it over since. The moves are all on the table. Add up well-being and you get the repugnant conclusion — for any population of very happy lives there's some much, much larger population of lives barely worth living that the total view ranks as better, and most people's gut says that can't be right. Average over people and you dodge it, but then you're stuck saying a world with a single blissed-out person is better than a world of billions of very happy people, and that adding happy people below the average makes the world worse. Go person-affecting and refuse to count people who only exist in one option, and you escape the comparisons between different-sized populations but then you can't explain what's wrong with choosing the depleted future, and the non-identity cases eat you alive. On top of that there are now formal impossibility-style results suggesting no view can satisfy a short list of adequacy conditions that everyone initially found plausible. Everyone in the room accepts all of this, and nobody changes their mind. My hypothesis — and I don't fully trust it — is that this isn't a factual disagreement or a verbal one, it's a standoff about which intuitions get treated as data and which get explained away as artifacts. So I'd like a real diagnosis, not a survey of views: what kind of disagreement is this, exactly? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question whose answer would break the tie, a debunking explanation of one of the core intuitions that should rationally compel people, or is "which population is better" maybe an ill-posed question once you push on it? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here rather than another round of intuition-pinging?
