I have recently inherited the running of a fortnightly research seminar in the philosophy of mind and language, and this has made a senior colleague's methodological judgements personally consequential. She will be away during our next meeting, so I shall have to introduce a visiting paper that rests on a single thought experiment and then anticipate, in her absence, the line of challenge she would have taken. I have therefore been trying to reconstruct how she classifies thought experiments from the verdicts she has given across several years.

Her taxonomy has three categories. A thought experiment may be evidence, if it actually supports or refutes a thesis; a mere illustration, if it dramatises a point established elsewhere; or an intuition pump, if it works by eliciting a response that the argument then treats as data — and she is always suspicious of that last move. Her past classifications, as I recall them:

Evidence: Putnam's Twin Earth; the Gettier cases; Galileo's heavy-cannonball argument against Aristotle.

Intuition pump: Jackson's Mary the colour scientist; Chalmers's philosophical zombies; Searle's Chinese Room.

Illustration: Plato's cave; the ship of Theseus.

The visiting speaker's paper attacks the psychological-continuity account of personal identity with a new case she calls the Duplicating Scanner. The case runs like this: a machine scans a person down to the last relevant detail and then assembles two successors, each of whom wakes with the original's memories, plans and mannerisms, while the original is not preserved. The speaker reconstructs her argument as follows: if the psychological-continuity account is correct, the original is identical with each successor; by the transitivity of identity, the two successors are therefore identical with one another; but they are plainly distinct persons; so the account is false. She presents this as an outright refutation, with the premises stated and the inference valid, and insists the scenario is doing ordinary counterexample work rather than prompting a bare intuition.

I expect my colleague to challenge the classification of the case before she challenges the conclusion. I should like you to tell me, in British English:

First, reconstruct the two or three most plausible rules that fit her record of classifications, and say why each fits. Second, state what each candidate rule would make of the Duplicating Scanner: evidence, illustration, or intuition pump. Third, give your best prediction of her actual classification, with the reasons for preferring that rule. Fourth, propose a further thought experiment — not already in my list and described fully enough for me to use it — that would best discriminate between your candidate rules if I were ever in a position to observe her classify one more case.

Please keep the focus on the methodology of thought experiments rather than on the personal-identity problem itself, and assume I know the canonical cases well enough that you need not expound them.
