I am a high school student trying to settle a disagreement in my philosophy club about whether governments and school boards should fund literature and the arts. We read a passage from Percy Bysshe Shelley's *A Defence of Poetry* (written 1821, published 1840):

"Reason is the enumeration of qualities already known; imagination is the perception of the value of those qualities, both separately and as a whole. Reason respects the differences, and imagination the similitudes of things. Reason is to imagination as the instrument to the agent, as the body to the spirit, as the shadow to the substance."

Someone in the club distilled Shelley's view into the following argument:

(1) If literature and the arts are worth public funding, it is because they produce something valuable that markets alone will not reliably supply.
(2) The distinctive thing literature and the arts produce, on Shelley's view, is an enlargement of imagination: the capacity to see likenesses between ourselves and others, and to perceive the value of qualities rather than just enumerate facts.
(3) A society in which people cannot see the likeness between their own inner lives and the inner lives of others loses the basis of moral restraint and social sympathy.
(4) Therefore, a society that refuses to fund literature and the arts weakens its own moral foundation.

I half-suspect a trick but cannot find it. Is this argument valid as stated? If not, please say exactly which step fails, on what reading it might be repaired, and whether the repaired argument succeeds. If it is valid, please say whether each premise is true, identify the weakest premise, and give the best objection to it.
