I can use these notions confidently in argument, though I could not prove the underlying theorems. I would like you to take the debate over whether what cannot be said can nevertheless be known — the apophatic position, often drawn from mystical sources, that there is a kind of knowledge language cannot encode and that may be had precisely at the point where language breaks down, set against the propositionalist position that anything genuinely known can in principle be put into words, so that an 'ineffable insight' is at best a feeling or an attitude — and restate that debate as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary. In doing so, distinguish three things that are often run together: knowledge that language happens not to express, knowledge that language is in principle unable to express, and the stronger apophatic claim that the failure of expression is itself the knowing. Then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly? Which claims can be rendered only with distortion, and what distortion? Which claims have no information-theoretic counterpart at all? I particularly want to know whether 'ineffable knowledge' comes out as an ordinary fact about channel capacity, compression, or the difference between an internal state and a message, or whether what fails to translate marks something the framework cannot see. Where the translation fails, say whether the defect lies in the information-theoretic vocabulary or in the original philosophical claim. Please write the answer in British English.
