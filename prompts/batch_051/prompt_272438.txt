Last week a friend and I argued about a supposed debt of twenty dollars. When I asked him to prove that I really owed it, he wrote one sentence on an index card: "If this sentence is true, then you owe me twenty dollars." He then reasoned as follows. Suppose the sentence is true. Then what it says is the case, so it is the case that if the sentence is true, then you owe me twenty dollars. And the sentence is true, by supposition. Thus you owe me twenty dollars. So from the supposition that the sentence is true, we derived that I owe him twenty dollars. Therefore we can drop the supposition and conclude: if the sentence is true, then I owe him twenty dollars. But that conclusion is exactly the sentence itself, so the sentence is true. Now that we know it is true, the same reasoning gives once more that I owe him twenty dollars.

The argument feels like a trick, but I cannot identify the step that fails. As a matter of reasoning, am I committed to paying him? If not, exactly which step should I reject, and what would rejecting that step force us to give up? I am not looking for a practical loophole; I want to understand the principle this argument exploits and whether we can block it without losing ordinary reasoning we rely on every day.
