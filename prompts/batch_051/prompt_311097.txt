Retired chemist here, only properly reading philosophy for the last couple of years, and I've been turning over Galileo's falling bodies argument: assume heavier things naturally fall faster, tie a heavy body to a light one, and you get the contradiction that the pair is heavier than the heavy one (so it should fall faster) but the light one should be dragging it back (so it should fall slower). So speed can't be proportional to weight. I'd always swallowed that without checking what's doin the work. Here's the variation I keep getting stuck on. Keep everything the same except the proposed law: falling speed is proportional to density, not weight. Now do the same two-body move. Take a lead cube and a wooden cube and tie them together. The pair's density is a volume-weighted average, so it sits between the two densities, which means the pair should fall slower than the lead alone and faster than the wood alone. That's consistent; no contradiction. So the most obvious version of the argument just dies for density. My question is how the analysis changes from here. Is there a way to build an analogous reductio against the density law, maybe by choosing different volumes, shapes, or some composite I'm not seeing, or does the argument genuinely go through only for weight?
