I am German, and a recent argument at a family dinner about reparations has left me unable to settle a question I had previously thought I understood. I carry, and most of my German friends carry, a standing sense that we as Germans born long after 1945 nonetheless inherit some responsibility for the Holocaust; it shapes how we speak, what we teach in schools, and what we think our state may permissibly do. But I noticed that I feel no corresponding responsibility for the Herero and Nama genocide in German South West Africa, nor for the Thirty Years' War, nor for the crimes of the Roman Empire, and when I said this, nobody at the table could say what, apart from emotional proximity and political convenience, separates the first case from the rest. The asymmetry generalizes: present-day German citizens are widely treated as bearers of responsibility for the deeds of the Nazi state, while present-day Italians are not treated as inheriting responsibility for Roman slavery, present-day Mongolians rarely hear that they must answer for the Mongol conquests, and the claim that present-day Americans inherit responsibility for slavery and Native American dispossession remains sharply contested. Is there a principle that generates these different judgments, or is the selectivity itself the thing that needs explaining?

I have been reading Jaspers, who tries to carve the territory up: criminal guilt attaches only to individuals for their own unlawful deeds and cannot be inherited; political guilt binds citizens to the deeds of their state and persists, as a kind of collective liability, beyond the generation that committed them; moral guilt remains an individual matter before conscience; and metaphysical guilt concerns the bare fact of having survived while others were murdered, without having risked one's own life to prevent it. The fourfold distinction is attractive, but I want to know whether it can justify the asymmetry in ordinary judgment rather than merely label it. If political guilt is genuinely inheritable in something like Jaspers's sense, why does it attach so selectively: must the Italian taxpayer share liability for Rome, the Mongolian citizen for the conquests of Genghis Khan, the American citizen for chattel slavery? If the answer is yes in all these cases, then ordinary practice is not tracking guilt in any consistent way but only the accidents of recent history and current power. If the answer is no in some cases but yes in the German case, what marks the difference: institutional continuity of the state, temporal distance, living memory, official commemoration, continuing material benefit, the character of the crime, or something else? Give me the strongest principled reconstruction of the asymmetry you can, and an honest verdict on whether it is fully justified, partly justified, or a psychological habit we should give up.
