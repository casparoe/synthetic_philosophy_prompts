I've been reading David Hume's Treatise, 3.2.5, and this passage has kind of lodged itself in my head:

The act of the mind, exprest by a promise, is not a resolution to perform any thing: For that alone never imposes any obligation. Nor is it a desire of such a performance: For we may bind ourselves without such a desire, or even with an aversion, declar'd and avow'd. Neither is it the willing of that action, which we promise to perform: For a promise always regards some future time, and the will has an influence only on present actions.

I take him to be running an elimination argument here: whatever act of the mind is supposed to accompany saying "I promise" and create its obligation, it can't be the resolution, can't be the desire, and can't be willing the promised action. So the only candidate left is willing the obligation itself — and Hume calls that absurd, because obligations are sentiments and you can't just will a new sentiment into existence. So the force of a promise has to come from an antecedent human convention, not from anything natural in the promisor's mind.

I've come around to this more then I expected, and now I catch myself telling people that promises have no force "naturally," only through convention. So here's what I want: an honest map of what would count against that position. Not the lazy "Hume is wrong because he's a conventionalist" stuff, but the actual defeaters — counterexamples, arguments, internal tensions — sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Which of them would a smart defender of the passage concede as real costs, and which would they bounce? And a worry of my own: does the view end up insulated, where anything about promising gets redescribed as part of the convention, and if so is that a bug, or just how a metaethical view about promising looks?
