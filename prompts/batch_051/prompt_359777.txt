I spent nearly four decades in the natural sciences before retiring, and I have come to political philosophy only recently. Lately I have been absorbed by Simone Weil's argument against political parties. As I understand it, her claim is that a party is not an organ of thought but an instrument of collective power: whatever truth-seeking a member brings to it is progressively subordinated to the party's needs for discipline, prestige, and victory, and the structure punishes independence rather than rewarding it. I find this hard to dismiss, and I have tried to integrate it with the rest of my convictions. I am no longer confident the resulting set hangs together, and I cannot tell which of the apparent conflicts are real.

These are my current commitments.

(1) I accept Weil's core thesis as a structural claim about party politics: durable parties systematically penalize independent judgment and reward the kind of intellectual conformity they need in order to survive as organizations.

(2) I believe large-scale representative democracy is practically unworkable without stable organizations that recruit candidates, maintain legislative discipline, and give voters a durable record to hold accountable; a politics without parties or party-like bodies strikes me as either wishful or a disguised form of elite rule.

(3) I believe a thoughtful voter often does better to defer to a party's accumulated policy analysis than to her own unaided judgment on complex matters such as trade, public health, or energy infrastructure, because the division of epistemic labor is generally more reliable than individual reflection under ignorance.

(4) I regard the modern sciences as at least a partial counterexample to Weil's pessimism: research traditions and schools are collectives with their own prestige and orthodoxy, yet they have corrected themselves over time and produced knowledge, which suggests her point cannot be a general law about every form of collective commitment.

(5) But I also believe that when a scientific subfield becomes tightly coupled to funding, status, and winner-take-all credit, it can reproduce the Weil pathology exactly: results are defended past the evidence, dissent is treated as disloyalty, and junior researchers learn what they are permitted to say. I am unsure how much this limits the force of (4).

(6) I am convinced that I personally should not join a political party, because membership would compromise the independent judgment I value more than any particular policy victory.

(7) I also accept that if everyone with my scruples abstains, party politics is left to those with fewer scruples, and the machinery will become more dishonest over time rather than less.

(8) I want to believe that issue-specific social movements, such as civil rights or environmental movements, can supply the coordination that parties supply without becoming party machines, because their discipline is oriented toward a concrete aim rather than toward preserving the organization itself.

(9) Yet I cannot identify a principled reason why a long-lived movement, once it has acquired real influence, would not simply harden into a party; so I do not know whether the distinction in (8) can bear the weight I would like it to bear.

I would be grateful for an audit of this set rather than a survey of Weil scholarship. Which commitments genuinely conflict, which conflicts are merely apparent, and how should the genuine tensions be ranked by severity? For each genuine tension, please say what would have to be revised or surrendered, and which repair is least costly. I want the ranking to reflect whether a conflict should change my view, not merely whether two sentences can be made consistent by adding a distinction.
