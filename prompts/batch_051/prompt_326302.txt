Consider the reimbursement patterns of many health insurers and national health services. Most will pay for cholesterol-lowering drugs for a symptomless patient whose only abnormality is an elevated lipid value, on the grounds that it reduces the probability of a future heart attack. They will pay for cataract surgery, which restores an older patient's clouded vision to roughly normal but does not extend it. Many will also pay for drugs for erectile dysfunction, even though the condition is not life-threatening, and for hormonal contraception, even though it treats no underlying disease at all. Yet many of the same payers will not reimburse a wakefulness-promoting drug for a healthy adult seeking better concentration, growth hormone for an unusually short child whose hormone levels are normal, or purely cosmetic procedures even when the patient reports severe and lasting distress about their appearance. What theory of health and disease does this pattern of funding presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit account as precisely as you can; distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with; identify which rival views it quietly rules out and where its commitments seem to be in tension; and say what reimbursement decisions would come out differently if the system adopted a purely normative view on which treatable suffering, rather than departure from normal functioning, were the criterion for coverage.
