I'm an undergraduate philosophy student taking a seminar on time, responsibility, and the law, and I keep turning over a tension in our material on statutes of limitations. On one side is a view I find nearly compelling: that the passage of time is morally inert. If an act was wrong, it is wrong now; a wrongdoer's culpability does not decay the way a radioactive isotope does. On this view, statutes of limitations are at best pragmatic concessions to faded evidence, unreliable memory, and administrative convenience — not reflections of any deep moral principle. The theory is simple and principled: it refuses to let a serious wrong be laundered by the mere turning of the calendar.

On the other side is an intuition I cannot shake: that a statute of limitations is not merely a compromise but something morally right. It seems to me that after enough time a person genuinely should not have to answer for an old offense, and not only because a trial would be unreliable. There is something about the life they have built since, the person they have become, and the community's having moved on that changes the moral situation — not just the evidence about it. The time itself seems to matter.

I want to know what it would take for each side to yield. What would it take to debunk the intuition as a confusion — for example, mistaking epistemic decay for moral decay, or mistaking psychological discontinuity for a real change in responsibility? What would it take to amend or abandon the theory by admitting genuinely time-sensitive moral reasons — for example, a right to be left alone after enough time, or a default forgiveness the state must respect? And is there a reading on which both survive — for example, the wrong remains wrong but the standing to demand an accounting lapses, or the grounds for punishment shift even if the verdict does not? I would like an answer that presents the strongest version of each side and then offers a negotiated verdict, saying what would justify each possible resolution rather than simply picking a winner.
