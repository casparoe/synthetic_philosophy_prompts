I am attracted to a form of objective Bayesianism on which rational priors are not free parameters but are determined by the information a problem actually supplies: for any well-posed problem stated as a set of constraints, the unique rational prior is the maximum-entropy distribution satisfying those constraints. I like this view because it turns the vague notion of studied ignorance into a definite computation, because the maximum-entropy assignment is the only one satisfying a short list of consistency axioms that I find individually compelling, and because it promises to keep arbitrariness out of science and decision theory by making the same information always yield the same posterior.

What troubles me is that the procedure appears to be sensitive to how the problem is written down before the constraints are fed in. Suppose I know only that a coin has some bias θ in the interval from zero to one. Maximum entropy over θ gives the uniform prior on θ. Now redescribe the very same ignorance in terms of the log-odds λ = log(θ/(1−θ)), which carries exactly the same information; maximum entropy over λ does not give the distribution corresponding to a uniform prior on θ — and on the unbounded λ parameter there is no proper entropy-maximizing distribution at all without adding a constraint I never said I had. The classical puzzle of a chord chosen at random in a circle makes the same point without any limits involved: the answer changes with the way the chance setup is parameterized. My firm intuition is that a rule of rational prior selection should be invariant under such equivalences: the ignorance is the same either way, so no particular sharp distribution can be rationally required by the evidence alone.

So I am torn between a theory that has exactly the structural virtues I want and an intuitive objection that feels close to decisive. Walk me through what it costs to hold onto each side. What would justify treating the intuition as an artifact — for instance, a confusion between a prior as a model of the world and a prior as an answer to a specific question, or an illicit demand that probabilities be invariant under arbitrary redescriptions rather than tied to the variables in which the problem is actually posed? What would justify amending or abandoning the view instead — restricting it to fully specified, non-arbitrarily parameterized problems, or retreating to imprecise credences that need only respect a whole family of priors? And is there a negotiated reading on which the maximum-entropy prior is rationally mandatory once the problem is stated in a canonical parameterization, while the intuition is conceded only for under-specified problems — or does that reading simply relocate the arbitrariness to the choice of the canonical parameterization?
