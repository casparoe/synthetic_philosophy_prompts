I serve as a senior reviewer for a state accident compensation fund that pays for treatment, income replacement, and rehabilitation after accidental injury, regardless of fault. In a typical week I sign off on several dozen contested claims, each with about an hour of review time, and the guidance I write is then applied by less senior staff to thousands of similar files; every decision can mean the difference between a year of wage replacement and nothing. Our tribunal is currently divided over claims where the injury traces fairly directly to a risky activity the claimant chose, such as backcountry skiing, solo climbing, or motorcycling at high speed. Some reviewers want to reduce or refuse compensation on the ground that the person accepted the risk, while others say the no-fault design of the scheme requires us to ignore how the injury came about and look only at the severity of the impairment. The only philosophical material I have found that speaks directly to this tension is the luck egalitarian distinction between brute luck and option luck: roughly, that we owe assistance for misfortunes that befall people through no choice of theirs, but not for the downside of gambles they freely took. What I lack is an operational version of that distinction for reviewers with no philosophical training facing messy files.

Please write a decision procedure my reviewers can actually run. It should present a sequence of questions in order, with for each question an account of what counts as a sufficient answer, what kinds of material in an ordinary file would bear on it, and whether the answer should stop the review in a denial, stop it in an award, or push it up for a value judgment by a senior panel. Be explicit about the points where your procedure is importing a moral or political judgment under the guise of a checklist, about the kinds of cases where the brute/option luck distinction collapses, and about what the procedure cannot settle. Then run the procedure on three cases: a professional mountain guide left unable to work by a fall on a route she was paid to lead; an inexperienced tourist seriously injured in a tandem paragliding crash that followed a sudden gust of wind after the commercial operator had cleared the launch; and a highly experienced backcountry skier killed in an avalanche he triggered on a slope he had assessed, from the day's bulletin and his own observations, as low risk. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer the reviewers are likely to resist, and say plainly why they are likely to resist it.
