I am a university student currently taking a philosophy of language course on generics, and I also work as a copy editor for my university's public-health communication office. Our office produces the posters, brochures, and web copy that tell students, staff, and parents about mosquito-borne illness, road safety, food safety, and similar risks; the copy is read by tens of thousands of people, and a sentence that overstates a risk can cause panic or stigma, while one that understates it invites people to ignore a real risk. Draft after draft comes to me containing bare plural generics: "Mosquitoes carry West Nile virus," "Teenagers are reckless drivers," "Raw chicken carries Salmonella." They are short and vivid, and we have always used them, but I now think we have no principled rule for when their tolerance for exceptions is a feature and when it is a way to mislead people.

The course makes the problem precise. Generics are not statistical generalizations. "Ducks lay eggs" is true even though only sexually mature females ever do; "Mosquitoes carry West Nile virus" is accepted as true even when very few mosquitoes carry the virus; and "Ducks are female" is false even though roughly half of all ducks are female. What seems to matter is not prevalence but whether the property is characteristic of the kind, tied to what the kind does or is disposed to do, or especially striking or consequential. But I do not know how to turn that observation into a working rule, and the other student editors have not taken the course.

I want you to write me a reusable editorial procedure: a checklist of questions, in order, that a non-specialist student editor can run on any bare plural generic in our draft copy and reach a defensible decision within about ten minutes. The checklist should be usable in plain language without a semester of semantics behind it. The procedure should produce one of four verdicts: publish as written; publish but add a quantifier or modal such as "some," "can," or "infected"; rewrite as a non-generic statement; or flag for human review. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer and what happens next. Be explicit about where the procedure stops settling a factual or semantic question and hands the decision to a person because the issue has become one of values, fairness, or audience harm.

The constraints are these. We cannot look up local prevalence for every claim; the public-health director will review only the items I flag; and a typical campaign contains a dozen such generics, with four campaigns a term. The director has told me she will accept an added quantifier only when the generic would otherwise mislead, and she will not accept a rewrite that lengthens the copy without a stated semantic reason. If your procedure says we must change copy we currently like, say so directly rather than finding a way to keep it.

Please run the finished procedure on the following three claims from upcoming campaigns.

1. "Mosquitoes carry West Nile virus," for a poster at the start of mosquito season. Our county lab reports that fewer than one in five hundred local mosquitoes is infected, but the disease, though rare, is severe.
2. "Teenagers are reckless drivers," for a brochure aimed at parents of newly licensed drivers. Young drivers' crash involvement per mile is substantially higher than that of adults, but only a minority of teen-driven miles end in a crash or a moving violation.
3. "Raw chicken carries Salmonella," for a food-safety handout going to students moving into off-campus housing. Our best estimate is that a minority of retail chicken packages test positive, but the director has used this exact sentence for years and insists students must treat every raw chicken as if it were contaminated.

For each case, tell me which verdict the procedure returns, which question made the difference, and what substitute wording, if any, it would put in place of the original.
