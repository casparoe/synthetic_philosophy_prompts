I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and every couple of weeks I hit a call that the law and the style guide dont cover: something that helps the story right now but would make me queasy if every journalist did it always — compressing caveats in a quote, letting an anonymous junior scientist criticize a senior lab's methods, running an unreplicated preprint as a solid finding, publishing a leaked email. The stakes are usually a source relationship, a correction, or readers' trust in a finding. I want a reusable pre-publication test built on Kant's universalizability test (the formula of universal law, not the treat-persons-as-ends one): I state the maxim of what I'm about to do, ask whether I could will it as a universal law, and act only if I can. Turn that into a procedure I can run in fifteen mintes on deadline, with no philosophy background assumed, and my editor signs off on anything the procedure flags as a values call. Make the hard parts explicit: how to formulate the maxim so I'm not rigging the outcome by writing it too broadly or too narrowly, how to handle the difference between a contradiction in conception (the practice undermines itself if everyone does it) and a contradiction in will (I can picture the universalized world but can't rationally will it), and which steps are real deliberation versus just dressing up a gut call. It should say where the procedure returns a hard no, and where it hands the call to my editor as a values judgment. Then run it on three cases: (1) quoting a study author while cutting the caveat that the result came from a small sample, (2) granting an early-career researcher anonymity plus a pseudonym to criticize a senior figure's methods, and (3) a case where the procedure tells me not to do something I badly want to do. Finally, say how confident you are in this procedure and single out the point you're least sure of, and tell me what the procedure cannot settle.
