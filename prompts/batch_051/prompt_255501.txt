I've been trying to understand the idea that conversation runs on a shared scoreboard: participants take certain things for granted, and when someone says something that would only be acceptable if the scoreboard already contained a certain entry, the participants quietly adjust the scoreboard to make the remark acceptable, unless someone objects. That picture seems built for a small face-to-face conversation in which everyone hears everything in the same order and a single shared scoreboard is a harmless idealization. Now take a large asynchronous online conversation — a long thread or group chat where people join late, read only part of the backlog, answer out of order, and where many lurkers read without ever indicating what they have taken for granted. In that setting there is no one moment at which the audience as a whole has a common scoreboard, and no single order in which presuppositions get accommodated. My question is whether the scorekeeping-and-accommodation picture survives this change. Which assumption is actually doing the work — that the scoreboard is shared, that the audience is definite, or that accommodation happens all at once? And can the picture be repaired to cover many-reader, many-order conversations, or does it stop being a useful model of presupposition at that point? I'm interested in the model itself, not in online etiquette.
