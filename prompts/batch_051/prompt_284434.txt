I'm a journalist who covers neurotech and ideas, and I've been chewing on something. I was rereading Mill's On Liberty and got stuck on the early bit where he distinguishes what he calls the inward domain of consciousness—liberty of conscience, thought, and feeling—and says that this part of human life is where freedom should be absolute, more than any other. That passage was written before anyone could read a brain, but now we've got non-invasive BCIs that can decode inner speech and maybe emotional states. Suppose a company starts asking employees to wear EEG headsets to monitor attention, or an insurer offers discounts if you let them analyze neural markers. Mill's inward domain seems to say none of that is the state's or society's business. But I don't think its that clean: the data only gets out if your brain produces readable signals, and once decoded it shapes how others treat you. So my question is narrowly this: if Mill were around today, would he count neural data as part of the inward domain of consciousness, and would he extend his absolutist line to mental privacy? Or would his harm principle make him more open to regulating brain-reading, since the reading itself becomes a social act? I'd also like you to predict what he'd say about cognitive liberty when the threat isn't just government censorship but companies selling access to your inner speech. Give me the reasoning, not just a yes or no, and flag where you think the historical Mill would have to be stretched or where his own principles would pull him in two directions.
