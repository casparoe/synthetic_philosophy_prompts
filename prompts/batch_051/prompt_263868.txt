Consider a standing disagreement about metaphor. On one side, associated with Donald Davidson, a metaphor has no cognitive content beyond its literal meaning: taken literally it is usually false or trivially true, and its real work is done by the effects it produces in hearers. On the other side, associated with conceptual metaphor theory, metaphors express cross-domain mappings and can carry information that no literal paraphrase of the sentence captures. I would like you to propose an empirical test that could put pressure on this disagreement rather than merely illustrate it. Design the strongest experiment you can to test the claim that at least some metaphors are not cognitively equivalent to their best literal paraphrase. Specify the materials, the comparison condition, the measured outcome, and—to the extent possible—a way of distinguishing a metaphorical contribution to what is represented from a mere framing or salience effect. State what pattern of results would favor each side. Then say precisely what a decisive-looking result would and would not establish: would it support the existence of metaphorical meaning, or only a psychological difference in how metaphors and their paraphrases are processed and used? Be explicit about where your operationalization falls short of the philosophical claim. Close by stating how confident you are in the proposal and in your interpretation of the possible outcomes, and identify the single point you are least sure of.
