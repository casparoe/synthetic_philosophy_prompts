I'm a novelist, and I'm writing a book about a racetrack handicapper, so I've been chewing on the old Meno problem—why knowledge should be worth more than true belief. There's a passage in Plato's Meno (in the W. R. M. Lamb translation) that I keep using as a working definition. Socrates is talking about true opinions:

"For these, so long as they stay with us, are a fine possession, and effect all that is good; but they do not care to stay for long, and run away out of the human soul, and thus are of no great value until one makes them fast with causal reasoning. ... But when once they are fastened, in the first place they turn into knowledge, and in the second, are abiding."

So the criteria I've extracted are: (1) the belief is true; (2) it stays put in the soul instead of running away; (3) it's been made fast by causal reasoning—the person can give the account of why it's true, not just that it's true.

Here's the case I'm actually writing. Angelo has spent his whole life in his uncle's bookmaker shop. He's right about which horse will win far more then the odds and small samples would allow, and the judgments don't waver—he never changes a pick before post time, doesn't lose his touch when he's exhausted or drunk. But when a journalist asks him to explain what he sees, the reasons he gives are garbage: he'll say a filly "stands proud" or that a muddy track "smells honest," and the few times he tried to teach his system, the pupil bet no better than chance. The true beliefs stay; but the causal reasoning doesn't seem to be what's fastening them in place.

Go through the three criteria one by one. Does Angelo know which horse will win, on Socrates' picture? If not, is that a problem with the criteria or with the way I've described him? And does his case suggest that what we're really tracking with the "fastened down" requirement is stability, or is it something else—being able to teach, or defend, or own the belief?
