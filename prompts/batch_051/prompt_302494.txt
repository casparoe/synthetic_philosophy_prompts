I teach a third-year seminar called 'Democracy Without Elections?' and next week is the sortition/lottocracy week. I want to give the students a one-page handout: a decision tree (or flowchart) that sorts them into the view on random selection they actually hold, based on how they answer concrete cases, not on whether they vibe with the word 'lottocracy.' They'll have read one general-audience defense and one critique of citizens' assemblies, so they know the basics but not the taxonomy.

Make the endpoints at least: (a) full lottocracy, where an alloted legislature replaces the elected one outright; (b) a permanent allotted second chamber or oversight body with real delay or veto power over an elected chamber; (c) binding single-issue citizens' assemblies, where elected officials set the agenda and terms of reference but the assembly's decision becomes law; (d) advisory minipublics only, where alloted citizens deliberate and recommend but elected reps keep the final say; and (e) a principled electoral democrat who thinks randomly selected bodies are mostly bad politics, not just occasionally useful PR. Add another leaf only if the tree genuinely demands it.

The spine I want is three distinctions the literature tends to run together: (1) what a legislature is for — being a descriptive mirror of the population, making sound judgment, or producing popular control; (2) whether accountability has to be retrospective (you can throw the rascals out) or whether it can also be prospective and deliberative; (3) how heavily to weight political experience and expertise against ordinary judgment. Every branch node should be a concrete case or claim someone can answer without already knowing which endpoint there heading to. I'd like you to work in at least the following cases somewhere: a 600-person alloted assembly voting to raise the income tax; a 24-person allotted jury-like panel with approval power over all new zoning; an alloted body that can fire an elected mayor; and the old 'would you rather be governed by five hundred random classmates or five hundred career politicians' question.

Then, after the tree, add a short diagnostic note: which combinations of answers are logically possible but empty, and for each, whether you think it's empty because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or just unargued. Flag any two leaves your tree can't actually pull apart. I care about the unoccupied boxes more than I care about the flowchart looking nice.
