In my high-school history course we have spent most of the term reading about early modern Europe through kings, parliaments, wars, and treaties. Last week, when I asked why we never learn what ordinary people were doing—what bakers, midwives, and parish clerks thought was going on—a classmate cut in with the old line that history is written by the victors. He seemed to think that settled it: the powerful determine which records are kept and which stories are told, so any attempt to recover the lives of ordinary people is either impossible or just a kind of speculation dressed up as scholarship. I suspect the slogan is actually doing several different jobs at once, and that some of its claims are trivially true while others would, if taken seriously, rule out a great deal of real historical work.

Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims someone could be making when they say 'history is written by the victors'—for instance, about whose words survive in archives, about whose perspective shapes the narrative afterward, about who gets to decide what is worth knowing, and about whether the discipline is really reconstructing the past or only the story of the powerful? For each reading, please say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and then identify which readings would actually support my classmate's conclusion that history from below is not worth attempting and which readings I could use to push back. I want to bring a sharper version of this to class, so it would help if the answer were organized around what I would have to accept in order to agree with him.
