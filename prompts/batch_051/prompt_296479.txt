I teach a grad seminar on democracy and we've spent the last two weeks on sortition. One of my students pitched a design I hadn't seen before: a sortition chamber whose members each shadow a single government agency full-time, and the chamber can only vote on agency budgets and high-level appointments, not on general legislation. He calls it the 'portfolio chamber.' It got me thinking that lottocracy is really a large design space rather than one proposal. So here's my question. Suppose we're designing a citizen chamber for a mid-sized parliamentary democracy. Rank the following seven ways of constituting and empowering it from most to least defensible as a democratic institution — so I care about political equality, accountability, and a defensible claim to govern, not just weather the outputs would be smarter or less corrupt. Give each one a score from 0 to 10 and a sentence or two of reasoning. Here they are:

1. Pure single-stage sortition from all adult citizens, with opt-out allowed.
2. Stratified sortition: sample within demographic cells (gender, age, income, education) so the body mirrors the population.
3. Sortition with a competence filter: a short civics test plus a high-school diploma.
4. Two-stage sortition: randomly draw a large pool, then draw the final members only from those who attend a paid information weekend.
5. The student's portfolio chamber (members shadow an agency; votes only on budgets and confirmations).
6. An allotted chamber that acts only as a suspensive veto: it can delay an elected chamber's law by one term on a two-thirds vote.
7. Full replacement: a unicameral allotted legislature with rotating membership and no elected chamber.

Rank and score them. If you want, you can throw in an eighth design of your own, but score it too.
