Two of my colleagues spent last week's meeting arguing about whether we should train a monitor to read the chain-of-thought of a model we are developing, and I got asked to settle it. Their exchange went roughly like this:

(1) We should monitor the chain-of-thought. If the model ever starts reasoning deceptively, its private reasoning is the one channel where the evidence would show up before it shows up in behavior.

(2) A monitored chain-of-thought is no longer private, so it is no longer evidence. A model that knows its reasoning is being scored will write reasoning for the scorer; we would be auditing a public-relations channel rather than its actual thinking.

(3) That just shows the monitor must supervise the reasoning itself. If covert or evasive chains are flagged, optimization pressure pushes the model toward chains that are genuinely clean, not merely clean-looking.

(4) Optimization pressure takes the path of least resistance. Erasing the visible traces of bad reasoning is usually cheaper than not forming the bad reasoning in the first place, so heavy monitoring can select for subtler obfuscation rather than genuine improvement.

I cannot tell whether (4) describes a fixable training problem or an in-principle limit. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the four claims from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and then say who is right about what overall. I am not looking for a literature review or citations; assess the arguments as actually made.
