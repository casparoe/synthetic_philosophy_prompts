I'm a software engineer and got dragged into a philosophy reading group by a friend. We've spent the last few months on Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre, and the argument has completely stalled. It starts, like, every session, with a disagreement about what Rousseau is even up to when he attacks the idea of putting a theater in Geneva.

One side says Rousseau is making a psychological claim: watching actors fake passion gives the audience a cheap, fake experience of pity, and over time that makes people less susceptible to the real thing. The other side says no, he's making a fundamentally political point about social organization: a theater creates the wrong kind of social relation, some people on stage performing for others and everyone else sitting there passively watching, and that's incompatible with the active, self-governing kind of citizen a small republic like Geneva needs. On this reading, theater isn't bad mainly because it messes up individual psychology; it's bad because it turns public life into spectacle and citizens into spectators.

The debate never moves because both sides can point to the same text. For example there's a passage in the letter, not one of the famous ones, where Rousseau says the actor's real talent is not to feel passions but to produce the outward signs of them so well that people take the copy for the thing itself. And then he says the spectator who weeps over a tragedy has sort of paid his debt to humanity on credit, without having to give anything to actual suffering people. That seems like it should settle something. But one camp says see, this is about psychology, the fake feeling displaces the real one, and the other camp says no, this is about social role and performance, not internal states.

My hunch is that this is two different disagreements dressed up as one: maybe an empirical question about what watching fictions does to the mind, and a separate normative question about what political freedom requires. The group keeps sliding between them without noticing. But I don't have the background to diagnose it properly. So what kind of disagreement is this actually? Is it verbal, empirical, normative, or a mix of those? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: an empirical study, a conceptual distinction, a different way to frame the question, or something else?
