I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist — spent forty-odd years looking at phase transitions — and I've recently developed a late-life interest in philosophy, mostly the philosophy of AI. There's a parallel I can't get out of my head, and I want an honest verdict on whether it's deep or just me pattern-matching.

The AI side: people disagree about whether an intelligence explosion would be a hard takeoff (competence goes from roughly human to radically superhuman in days or hours once self-improvement gets going) or a soft takeoff (fast but continuous, spread over years, with lots of external feedback). I've only picked this up second-hand, but the shape of the debate strikes me as incredibly familar.

In condensed matter, a first-order transition is just like the hard-takeoff picture: the system can sit in a metastable state past the transition point, local conditions change only slightly, then a nucleation event flips the whole thing abruptly — latent heat, hysteresis, the lot. A second-order transition is more like the soft-takeoff picture: the order parameter grows continuously from zero, there's no latent heat, but fluctuations get huge near the critical point; a ferromagnet near the Curie temperature is the classic case.

So my question is whether this is a genuine structural identity or a superficial analogy. If it's genuine, does anything actually transfer — for instance, critical slowing down as an early-warning sign, quench rate controlling the final state, or the fact that near a continuous transition even small fluctuations get amplified? If it's not, where exactly does it break? My own suspicion is that there's no Hamiltonian and no temperature here, and an AI is not a thermodynamically large system of dumb components but an optimiser that can, at least in principle, look ahead and steer. But I'm not sure that's the right objection.

Please state the mapping, say what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer, and tell me something the analogy predicts that's probably false. And if you could write the reply in British English, that'd suit me fine.
