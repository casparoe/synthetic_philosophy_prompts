I'm a philosophy professor who teaches a philosophy of biology course, and I've realized—embarassing, I know—that I don't have a worked-out view of what makes something a biological function. I have a bunch of gut calls on cases but no principle that gets them all. I keep wanting to say something like "a trait's function is whatever it was selected for," and I'd like you to test that idea against my cases and try to fix it, rather than just giving me a survey of positions. Could you give me a principle (or a small set of candidates), stated precisely, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, and be honest about which ones it can't get? Then tell me what it says about the further case at the end, and maybe one or two nearby cases I haven't thought of. Please write the answer in American English.

My gut calls:

1. A heart that's formed the way human hearts normally form, but a congenital defect kept it from ever pumping blood. I still want to say its function is to pump blood. That's what hearts are for, even defective ones.

2. Blood vessels inside a tumor. They deliver oxygen and nutrients, and the tumor couldn't survive without them. But I don't want to say feeding the tumor is their function. They do it, but it just isn't a function.

3. The first heart, in some early worm before there was any selection for hearts. It moved fluid around the body and the worm depended on it. I have the intuition that it had the function of circulating fluid, but my selected-for principle says it didn't, and I don't know which to drop.

4. A feather that was selected for insulation in some small dinosaur and later got co-opted for flight. For the flight feather of a modern bird, I want to say its function is flight, not insulation, even though insulation is what it was originally selected for.

5. A bacterial gene that makes the cell resistant to an antibiotic as a side effect. Before the population has been exposed to the antibiotic, I don't want to call resistance the gene's function. After many generations of antibiotic selection, I do want to say resistance became a function. I'm not sure this is consistent with 4.

The pair that really bugs me is 2 and 3: I'm denying a function to blood vessels that actually do something useful, while granting one to a proto-heart before any selection. Can a single principle underwrite both?

Further case: a beetle is born with a mutation that lets it digest a new food source much better. It lives a normal, successful life by beetle standards but dies without reproducing. Does that digestive capacity have a function? My selected-for principle says no, but I'm not sure I believe that.

Could you work through all of this? I'd especially like to know which of my verdicts I should revise if they cant all be kept.
