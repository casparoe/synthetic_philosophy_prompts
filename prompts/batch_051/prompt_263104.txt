I've been reading Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics for a course on ancient economic thought, and a friend and I got stuck on a passage in Book V, chapter 5. Here it is (as printed on Perseus): "Hence all commodities exchanged must be able to be compared in some way. It is to meet this requirement that men have introduced money; money constitutes in a manner a middle term, for it is a measure of all things, and so of their superior or inferior value, that is to say, how many shoes are equivalent to a house or to a given quantity of food."

My friend built the following argument out of it, and now we want an outside diagnosis because neither of us can find the exit:

(1) Exchange requires unlike goods to be commensurable, as Aristotle says here.
(2) Since money is what makes shoes and houses commensurable, and money exists by convention (nomisma, from nomos), their commensurability is not something the goods have on their own — it is introduced by the convention.
(3) If commensurability is introduced by convention, then the exchange ratio between any two goods is fixed not by anything about the goods themselves but by whichever custom or currency system happens to prevail.
(4) If the ratio is fixed only by custom or currency, then, from the standpoint of justice, any ratio is as good as any other — money does not track anything real.
(5) But that cannot be right. A vendor who charges a stranded family everything they have for a bottle of water after a flood is doing something unjust, and we seem to know that without consulting any convention.
(6) So either Aristotle's account of money cannot make sense of unjust prices, or one of the moves above is wrong in a way we are missing.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what its worth, I would rather give up (2) than (5), but (2) looks almost forced by the passage. Keep the reply under 250 words; the quoted passage does not count toward that.
