Patients who have undergone a complete corpus callosotomy — the surgical severing of the main bundle of fibers connecting the cerebral hemispheres, usually to control severe epilepsy — are treated by the legal and administrative institutions around them as exactly one person: one passport, one estate, one criminal liability, one signature authorizing a surgical procedure. In laboratory conditions, the same patients can produce behavior that seems to split the subject apart: when visual input is shown to only one hemisphere, the speaking hemisphere may report seeing nothing while the opposite hand points to the displayed object, and the two hands have sometimes been observed working at cross-purposes. What view of the unity of consciousness does this standing institutional treatment presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose that a single subject of experience underlies all the behavior, or can the legal and institutional framework be made consistent with the claim that two streams of consciousness — or two centers of agency — coexist in one skull? Be specific about which parts of the practice, such as criminal liability, informed consent, property, testimony, and voting, would lose their coherence first if the two-streams interpretation were accepted, which parts could remain unchanged, and where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, so that rival theories of consciousness and personal identity would license the same institutional treatment.
