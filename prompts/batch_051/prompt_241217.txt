In my historiography seminar we keep circling around the same move. Whenever someone says, for instance, that a nineteenth-century surgeon who refused to wash his hands between autopsies and deliveries was morally culpable, or that an ancient slaveholder was straightforwardly a monster, our professor says we are being presentist. His favorite analogy is that we should treat the past like a foreign country: you would not land in a foreign place and immediately sneer at local customs as backward, so you should not do the equivalent to your ancestors. I get the intuitive pull of it, but my friend in the class pointed out that its a strange analogy. When people actually visit foreign countries, they do judge local practices all the time—they condemn some, refuse to participate in others, and often think they are right to do so. So the analogy may be doing the opposite of the work the professor wants.

I want an audit of how far this analogy can carry that conclusion. Lay out what is supposed to correspond to what: who is the traveller, who is the native, what is the local custom, what is the distance being crossed? Then say where the mapping breaks, which features of literal foreign travel the analogy quietly imports (tolerance as a guest, respect for self-governing communities, the fact that you can leave), and whether the argument—that moral judgment across time is a kind of parochial sneering and should be restrained—survives once the disanalogies are out in the open. If the analogy collapses in the places that matter, say what a better analogy, or dropping analogies altogether, would suggest about how historians should handle cases like the surgeon or the slaveholder.
