I know I'm late to the Arrow party, but I've been turning over the impossibility theorem for a few weeks and I've come up with a distinction I can't tell anymore if its deep or just a rebranding. I call it preference integrity. The idea is that when I write down a strict ranking like A > B > C, that ranking is more than three pairwise verdicts glued together. It's one structured, transitive object, and where C sits relative to B is information about how I hold B, not just information about C. Arrow's independence of irrelevant alternatives says the social ranking of A versus B should depend only on how people rank A versus B. But if my ranking has any integrity, then whether C is wedged between A and B or dropped below both of them matters to what I'm saying when I claim A over B. So IIA isn't a neutrality condition at all; it's an atomization condition, it forces the rule to treat my ballot as a bag of pairwise choices. My hunch is that once you give up IIA in order to respect preference integrity, you don't need a dictator — rules like Borda count (I know it has its pathologies) become available, and maybe the right response to Arrow is not that dictatorship is unavoidable but rather that the mistake was slicing each person's preference into pairwise comparisons in the first place.

Is this a real idea, or have I just rediscovered something obvious under a new name? If you steelman it, what's the strongest version? Then tell me the two or three places it actually breaks down — especially whether preference integrity rules out dictatorship at all, or whether it just makes room for non-dictatorial rules with their own ugly problems.
