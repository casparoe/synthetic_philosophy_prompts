Ok so I've been trying to work out why oaths bind, and I keep going in circles. Here's the bit from Hesiod's Theogony, where he describes what happens to a god who swears by the Styx and then breaks the oath (Evelyn-White translation):

For whoever of the deathless gods that hold the peaks of snowy Olympus pours a libation of her water and is forsworn, must lie breathless until a full year is completed, and never come near to taste ambrosia and nectar, but lie spiritless and voiceless on a strewn bed: and a heavy trance overshadows him. But when he has spent a long year in his sickness, another penance more hard follows after the first. For nine years he is cut off from the eternal gods and never joins their councils or their feasts, nine full years. But in the tenth year he comes again to join the assemblies of the deathless gods who live in the house of Olympus.

Now here's the argument this text keeps pushing me toward, and it seems to prove too much, because the conclusion is absurd.

(1) The passage says that whichever god forswears lies breathless for a year and is cut off for nine more. It does not describe anyone catching the god, judging him, or having the power to make the penalty happen; it reads like an automatic consequence of the water itself.

(2) So the Styx oath binds even the strongest beings there are, without being backed by the promisee's power to punish or by any external judge doing anything at the moment of breach. The penalty just occurs.

(3) What does the binding, then, is the swearer's own act of swearing plus the automatic penalty annexed to it. The obligation is coming from inside the sworn act, not from anything outside it.

(4) If that is what makes an oath bind, then any act by which I attach an automatic penalty to my own future conduct is an oath: a private vow with a self-imposed fine, or a solemn promise made to nobody in particular, would bind the way the gods' Styx oath binds.

(5) But those don't bind like oaths. A self-fine on a diet can be paid and shrugged off without the particular shame and debt of having broken an oath, and a promise to nobody isn't an oath at all.

(6) So (3) must be false, and the binding force of an oath can't just be self-binding plus an automatic penalty.

(7) But then I cant say what the Styx episode is even showing, and I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than give them up.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I suspect something slipped between (3) and (4), but I want to know what I'd be paying to reject whichever premise you think is cheapest to lose.
