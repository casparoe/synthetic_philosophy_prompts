I have been covering a series of small workshops on self-knowledge, and at one of them I listened to two researchers argue, over lunch and then again in the afternoon session, about whether knowledge can be "self-intimating." I reconstructed the exchange from my notes. I want a point-by-point account of which claims survive scrutiny.

The first researcher opened with a version of what he called the KK principle: if you know that P, then you are at least in a position to know that you know that P. He stressed the "at least in a position" qualifier, because he was not claiming that anyone actually possesses the higher-order belief at every moment; only that the epistemic resources are already there, so a suitably reflective person could come to know that she knows, without new evidence. His positive motivation was that a state which counted as knowledge but could not support second-order awareness of itself would be a strange sort of achievement: you would be entitled to use P as a premise, but you could not, even on reflection, see yourself as knowing it. That, he said, makes knowledge epistemically inert exactly when it matters.

The second researcher answered with what she called the anti-luminosity argument. She asked us to imagine a long series of cases running from one in which a person is clearly cold to one in which she is clearly not cold, with each adjacent pair differing by an amount too small to discriminate. There must, she said, be a last case in the series where the person is cold. If the condition being cold were luminous — in the sense that whenever it obtains, the person is in a position to know it obtains — then at that last cold case, she would be in a position to know she was cold. But knowledge requires a margin for error: if your basis puts you in a position to know that a condition obtains in a given case, the condition had better also obtain in the immediately adjacent case, otherwise your basis would be reliable only by luck. So the person would have to be cold in the next case, contradicting the premise that the earlier case was the last cold one. She then said: run the same argument on the condition knows that P. A series goes from cases where a person clearly knows P to cases where she clearly does not. If knowing that P were luminous, the KK principle would hold; but the margin-for-error reasoning blocks it. So the condition knows that P is no more luminous than being cold, and the KK principle fails.

The first researcher replied that she had slipped between two claims. Luminosity, as he accepted it, requires only that you be in a position to know, not that you actually know; and the margin-for-error premise, he said, is far too strong once it is applied to mere positions rather than to actual judgments. To be in a position to know is to have a capacity or an available basis; capacities can be present even where the corresponding performance would fail in a single borderline case. He also suggested that safety-style premises of the kind she was using are rejected by fallibilist and internalist accounts of knowledge, on which evidence can justify knowledge even if the same evidence would have been held in a just-barely-different case where the belief was false. Her argument, he said, simply stipulates a safety conception of knowledge and then derives its consequences.

She shot back that the margin premise did not apply only to actual judgments, because a "position to know" that regularly yields true belief only by luck is not a position to know at all. On any account worth the name, a positional entitlement has to be reliable: an available basis that is insufficient in a close case is not knowledge-level in the actual case. And she added that if he rejected safety talk outright, he was left having to explain how knowledge could be lost and regained across two subjectively indistinguishable cases without that being mere arbitrariness.

He then said the argument proved too much. In order to make the anti-luminosity reasoning work, you have to allow that there is a determinate last cold case, and then you exploit the combination of determinacy and margin for error to show that the categorisation cannot be known at that point. But that result is earned only if the underlying categories are already assumed to have sharp boundaries. If you run the argument over knowledge, he said, it shows at most that a KK defender must accept that the boundary between knowing and not-knowing is sharp, which is exactly the sort of boundary most epistemologists already accept; it does not show that knowledge fails the KK principle in ordinary cases away from the boundary. So the anti-luminosity argument, he concluded, is either an argument for sharp boundaries or an argument for rejecting safety, but it is not an independent argument against KK.

She replied that the last point ignored the actual force of the argument. She did not need "ordinary cases away from the boundary" to fail KK; she needed only the existence of some knowledge case in which the knower is not in a position to know she knows, and the last knowledge case in any such series is exactly such a case. If he admitted sharp boundaries and fallible knowledge at the boundary, he had already conceded that KK fails at the boundary, and a principle of the form "whenever K P, position to know K K P" cannot tolerate counterexamples at the boundary merely because they are boundary cases. The fact that boundaries are sharp does not restore luminosity; it is what makes the loss of luminosity vivid.

At this point the session broke up. I could not tell whether the first researcher's "proves too much" reply was an attempted reductio of the second's argument or an unnoticed concession.

I would like a careful, point-by-point adjudication of this exchange: at each stage, who is right about which specific point, and who, if anyone, is entitled to the overall conclusion? Also say what both sides are missing or mishandling. In doing so, please separate what is settled by the logic of the argument, given the premises, from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus view.
