Just got back from a long argument with my roommate about whole-brain emulation, and I need help figuring out what view she's actually running, because it feels like she's got two different rules going at once and she doesn't notice.

Here are the verdicts she gave, pretty much in her own words:

- On replacing one neuron at a time with a silicon chip that does exactly what the neuron did, over years, while the person is awake and behaving normally the whole time: she says the peron at the end is the same mind as the original. She was totally sure.

- On swapping the whole brain at once for a functionally identical silicon brain, old brain removed and discarded, no period where both are running: she says not the same mind. The original is dead and the silicon brain is a new person.

- On a non-destructive scan that makes a copy of someone's mind on a computer while the biological person goes on living: she says the emulation is not the same mind as the original. The original is the original; the copy is someone else.

- On a destructive upload, where the original is scanned and then the biological brain is destroyed before the emulation is switched on: she says still not the same mind. The scan is a copy, and death was real even though the copy wakes up with all the memories.

- On a gradual replacement where, instead of chips, surgeons graft in living lab-grown biological neurons one at a time over years, preserving the person's ongoing mental life: she says same mind, no question.

So I tried to reconstruct the rule and ended up with two candidates. One is something like: it has to be a single continuously maintained physical system — the mind goes wherever that one bodily vessel goes, and chips inserted into the same skull count, but copies, instant swaps, and uploads don't. The other is something like: what matters is an unbroken chain of overlapping mental states with the right causal basis and no branching, so the gradual chip case counts because the person never lapses, but the instant swap and the uploads break the chain and the copy branches.

The new case I want your prediction on: imagine the same process as the first one, but instead of silicon chips in the skull, each biological neuron is replaced by a software model of that neuron running on a computer in the next room, wired into the rest of the brain. This happens region by region over months, the person is conscious and conversing the whole time, and there's never a moment of blankness; as each region is taken over, its functions are handed off smoothly to the computer. At the end the biological brain is gone and the entire mind runs on the external computer. What would she say — is the resulting emulation the same mind as the original?

I'd like you to give her likely verdict and explain which precedent is doing the most work; say which of the two rules (or something else) is the best fit; and tell me what follow-up case would cleanly split the two rules if her answer here doesn't.
