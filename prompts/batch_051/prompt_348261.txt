I am a PhD student in a psychology department, and in our quantitative methods seminars a claim about the replication crisis keeps surfacing in different guises. The claim is that the crisis is at bottom a problem with frequentist inference—null hypothesis significance testing with a sharp p < .05 threshold—and that moving to Bayesian methods, or at least to continuous estimation with explicit priors, would substantially reduce the rate at which the literature accumulates false positives. A colleague routinely responds to failed replications by reanalyzing the original and replication data with a default prior, and I cannot tell whether this move is genuinely diagnostic or only changes which researcher choices are doing the work.

Suppose I wanted to isolate the empirical part of the claim. Design the strongest feasible test of the proposition that the inferential framework itself, as distinct from researcher degrees of freedom, publication bias, and low power, is a major driver of non-replicable findings. Restrict yourself to what one could do with existing replication datasets or a simulation a motivated graduate student could actually run; do not fall back on an imaginary randomized trial that assigns whole research communities to Bayesian or frequentist statistical cultures. Specify the design in enough detail that I could implement it, state which pattern of results would favor the framework-matters view and which would favor the view that flexibility and incentives around any framework do the real work, and say what even a strongly positive result would leave unsettled. In particular, address what a Bayesian reanalysis of already-published studies can and cannot demonstrate, given that the model space, the prior, and the decision rule are themselves researcher choices that could be gamed.
