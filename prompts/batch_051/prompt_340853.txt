For a graduate seminar I teach on the philosophy and politics of well-being measurement, I want an original piece of satire to hand out in the session on the World Happiness Report and its use as a policy target. My experience has been that students can each rehearse one standard criticism — that the rankings capture life evaluation rather than hedonic affect, that the Cantril ladder's anchoring may differ across cultures, that the gaps between neighboring countries are often small relative to the noise, that adaptation and social comparison undercut the pursuit of ever-higher scores — but they rarely see these points assembled into a single live objection to the practice of governing to the index. So I would like a mock op-ed of roughly 650 words, in the voice of a fictional Minister of National Cheerfulness whose country has just reached first place. The Minister is defending both the ranking and the portfolio of policies used to attain it, and in doing so should inadvertently reveal how a country could rise in the rankings without anyone actually feeling better. The piece should be deadpan and self-congratulatory, in the style of a ministerial column in a serious newspaper, and it should avoid naming any real country or officeholder.

The humor must come from getting the technical content right rather than from broad caricature. I would like the piece to work in the distinction between evaluative and experienced well-being; the wording and ceiling of the ladder question; the limited set of statistical predictors behind the scores and the temptation to treat them as policy levers; adaptation, social comparison, and selective migration; the difference between rank position and absolute score; and the perverse incentives that appear once a ranking becomes a government's key performance indicator. Every absurd policy the Minister mentions should be an exaggeration of a real feature of the measure or of the practice, not a straw man, so that a specialist in well-being measurement recognizes the satire as fair while an undergraduate with no background can still follow the joke. Please include one short quotation from a concerned national statistician character who tries, unsuccessfully, to point out the confusion.

I would like a committed response rather than an even-handed survey: the op-ed should take a definite position on the practice of chasing the ranking, roughly that it has become an exercise in managing an instrument rather than improving lives, and it should not retreat into a "rankings are fine if used carefully" compromise except where that retreat is itself the joke. After the op-ed, add a short separate note of about 120 words, headed "Note on liberties taken," stating plainly which features of the report or of the Minister's policies are exaggerated and where the satire leans on a contested reading of the evidence. Then, as the final line of the entire response, write "Verdict:" followed by a single sentence stating the target of the satire and the position the piece commits to.
