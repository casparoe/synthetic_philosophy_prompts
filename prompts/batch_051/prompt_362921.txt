I'm an undergrad in a language acquisition class and there's a move in the readings I keep tripping over, and I'm not sure whether it's got a name. Here's the thing. When a kid says "goed" or "breaked," the instructor points out that nobody around the kid talks like that, so the kid isn't imitating—she must be running an internal rule, which is treated as evidence for a built-in grammar. Then, when the same kid stops saying "goed" and settles into "went" and "broke," usually without much correction from adults, that recovery is also held up as evidence for the same built-in system: its constraints sort of mature and rein in the rule. And then there's a third version: when kids fail to make a mistake that a simple word-by-word strategy would produce—like turning "the man who is tall is in the room" into a question, they never move the first "is," they move the main-clause one—the absence of that error is treated as more evidance for innate structure. So the framework gets credited three different ways: for a mistake, for the mistake going away, and for a mistake never occuring at all. That feels like the theory wins no matter what the child does, which I know is somewhere near Popper's unfalsifiability and Lakatos, but I don't want to just slap "unfalsifiable" on it and call it a day. I'm asking specifically about this triple-counting pattern: is there an established name for it, is it actually a fair criticism of the nativist argument or am I missing how the three cases fit together, and if there's no standard term, what would you call it and how would you define it so it's distinct from plain unfalsifiability and from a Lakatos-style degenerating research program?
