In a seminar on Mill we've been going through On Liberty chapter 3 — the individuality and experiments in living material — and the way it's usually framed is as an anti-conformity argument: you should work out your own way of life because you're best placed to know what suits you, and a society of copycats stagnates. But I've been messing with a different way of cashing out the same idea, and I want to know whether I've actually built something or just re-named something familiar.

I've been calling it hedging Mill, or the life-portfolio reading. The thought is this. The real reason to keep strange, unpopular, even probably-mistaken ways of life around is the same reason you hold underperforming assets in a diversified portfolio. We're fallible about what makes a life go well, but our errors are clumpy: whole societies get confidently wrong about the same thing at the same time. So a lifestyle that looks like it's losing right now is still worth preserving if it's uncorrelated with the dominant way of living, because if the consensus flips, or circumstances change, we won't have to rebuild the alternative from scratch — somebody kept it warm. On this view, experiments in living aren't mainly for the experimenter's own flourishing, and they're not mainly about truth through discussion; they're a hedge against collective evaluative error. The public shouldn't tolerate eccentrics out of respect for autonomy, exactly — it should tolerate them the way an investor tolerates the bad quarter of they're portfolio. I like this because it explains why we might protect practices we privately think are worse than the mainstream, without pretending every way of life is equally good.

But I can feel the seams where it groans. Lives aren't stocks: you can't rebalance a person, and the costs of living the hedge are payed by the person doing the living, not by the society holding the option. And Mill draws a pretty firm line at harm to others, which seems like it should override hedging — otherwise you could justify protecting harmful subcultures just because they might turn out useful later. So: is my analogy doing real work, or just dressing up his fallibility argument? If this already exists under some name, just give the name or rough idea; don't worry about full citations.
