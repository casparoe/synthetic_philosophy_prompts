I'm a philosophy professor who teaches philosophy of mathematics, and there's a debate I've watched go around in circles for years without anybody moving. Maybe you can help me diagnose what's really going on, because I don't want a survey of positions and I don't want a verdict — I want an account of the disagreement itself.

The two sides, roughly. The first says a proof has to be surveyable: a competent mathematician has to be able to go through it and see that it works, otherwise it isn't a proof in the full sense, just a report that a computation happened. On this view, Appel and Haken's computer-assisted proof of the four-colour theorem was never fully satisfactory, and Hales's proof of the Kepler conjecture had a version of the same problem — huge case checks done by machine, with the real reasoning partly hidden in code. The second side says this is nostalgia dressed up as methodology. A proof is a warrant, a certificate that can be checked by any sufficiently reliable procedure; if the code is verified and the cases are exhaustively checked, then demanding that it all fit inside a human skull confuses what proof is for with our psychological limitations.

I used to think this was a substantive disagreement about the nature of proof. Now I'm not so sure: maybe it's really a disagreement about what proof is for — producing understanding in finite minds, or providing enormous, defeasible warrant through computation. Or maybe it's a verbal dispute over the word 'proof,' or two communities answering different questions under one heading.

So my question: is this a genuine substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or an ill-posed question where their are simply two legitimate notions of proof? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question in the background — about the reliability of large case checks, the role of formal verification, or the probability of machine error — that would, if settled, actually move things? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a theorem, a change of question, or an honest conclusion that the two sides should stop expecting to persuade each other?
