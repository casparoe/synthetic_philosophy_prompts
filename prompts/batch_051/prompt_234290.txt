I've got a rule about my own reasoning that I've followed for a few years now, and a friend tells me it's either a shield for stubborness or a weird kind of procrastination about thinking. Whenever I'm in the middle of working through a consequential question, I refuse to ask myself "am I actually reasoning well right now, or am I just rationalizing?" Instead I finish the reasoning, commit to whatever answer I've reached, and only then—days later, in writing, usually in the morning—do I audit the whole episode as a seperate exercise. My experience has been that in-the-moment self-monitoring doesn't lead to correction; it just adds a second layer of storytelling. I catch myself thinking "ah yes, I considered the counterarguments, so I'm probably being fair," and then I feel licensed to believe whatever I was already reaching for. The later, written audit is the only version that has ever actually changed my mind. But the rule also failed me recently: I was negotiating a used-car purchase, felt the words "you're anchoring on the sticker price" float through my head, and by my own policy I swatted it away, bought the car, and realized two days later that I'd paid too much. So now I'm not sure whether the policy is protecting my judgment or just protecting my first takes.
