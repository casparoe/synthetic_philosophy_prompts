I'm a PhD student working on decision theory and the simulation argument, and I keep running into a specific impasse that I'd like you to diagnose rather than adjudicate. The exchange is between two positions about whether the simulation argument should dampen fanaticism about the far future — roughly, the cluster of arguments holding that we should be willing to accept enormous present costs for tiny probabilities of influencing an astronomically large future.

On one side, the dampeners argue as follows. If the simulation argument's trilemma is broadly right, then conditional on our civilization reaching anything like the technological maturity that longtermist reasoning assumes, the probability that we are living in an ancestor simulation is high. But if we are simulated, then the far future we would be trying to steer is either the simulators' far future, which we cannot influence from inside the simulation, or a simulated far future whose continuation is at the simulators' discretion. Either way, the expected value of trying to shape the deep future collapses, or becomes hostage to empirical beliefs about simulators' intentions, and the fanatical verdicts no longer survive.

On the other side, the anti-dampeners reply that this is a confusion. The trilemma does not move the expected-value calculus; it just shifts the hypothesis space. If we are simulated, the far future inside the simulation is still enormous, our actions inside the simulation still steer it, and the beings in it still matter morally. If we are not simulated, ordinary longtermist reasoning applies as before. The tiny probability that we are not simulated — or that the simulators will let the simulation run — is enough to preserve the fanatical stakes. On this view, dampening is either a failure to conditionalize properly or an illicit slide from "the future is in someone else's hands" to "there is no future worth steering."

Both sides accept the basic formalism of the simulation argument and neither side claims the other is making an elementary empirical mistake. Yet the argument does not move.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about the probability of being simulated, but about what to do with a probability mass that concentrates on worlds where the agent's causal contact with the deep future is mediated by, or truncated by, another agent's choices. That is, I suspect the disagreement lives inside the theory of anthropic decision-making: whether one should conditionalize on "I am in a simulation" the way one conditionalizes on ordinary evidence, and whether simulated outcomes count as value-relevant in the same way as unsimulated ones.

I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the positions. Specifically: is this a verbal dispute about what "the far future" or "our actions matter" means, a genuine disagreement about priors or probability assignments, a disagreement within decision theory about how to handle indexical uncertainty and small probabilities, or a bedrock difference about which outcomes have value? Which diagnosis best fits what each side actually says, and what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a formal result, a distinction, a change of question? And be honest about whether the impasse is a sign that the question is ill-posed or just hard.

Also, please say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different diagnosis, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
