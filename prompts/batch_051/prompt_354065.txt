Since my brother, an officer, asked me about this after a course on just war theory, I have been working through Anscombe's case against Truman's honorary degree, and I find the underlying account of intention almost impossible to fault: what a person intends is fixed by the structure of the action they deliberately choose, not by the preferred description they give their purpose, so a result that is the very means by which an action achieves its effect is intended even if it is also foreseen and regretted. On that account, Truman could not honestly say he merely foresaw the civilian deaths at Hiroshima and Nagasaki, since the destruction of the cities was doing the work by which surrender was to be brought about. But I also cannot bring myself to give up the intuition that there is a real moral difference between aiming at civilian destruction as one's end or as pure terror, and accepting in a proportionate way civilian deaths that are collateral to an attack on a military-industrial target; that difference does not feel like casuistry, and a decent commander may have to rely on it in war. What I cannot see is how to state the distinction without either defining means so broadly that it condemns any commander who accepts foreseeable collateral deaths, or so narrowly that Truman escapes through a redescribed intention. Present each side at its strongest and tell me which should yield: should I regard the intuition as debunked by the theory, amend or abandon the theory, or is there a precise means–side-effect distinction on which both survive? What considerations would justify each of those moves?
