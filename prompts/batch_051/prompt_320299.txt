You are a philosopher of science who works on the foundations of chaos theory and prediction, and you have been asked to give the commencement address for a university atmospheric-science program. The graduating class is full of forecasters and modelers who will catch every technical remark; most of the rest of the audience are their families, who will not. Write the address, about 900 words. It should be organized around the claim that a deterministic system can be unpredictable in principle — not merely because we lack data, but because of sensitive dependence on initial conditions — and it should draw out what that means for the profession these graduates are entering: what they can honestly promise, what they should refuse to pretend, and why forecasting remains worth doing when exact prediction is impossible. Do not let the speech fall into a Laplacean fantasy of perfect prediction or a lazy conclusion that everything is random; keep the genuine difference between deterministic chaos and mere randomness in view, and make the technical ideas legible to the families without boring the specialists. The philosophical point should shape the speech's reassurances and warnings rather than decorate them. Before writing, work out the single through-line of the speech and the one false comfort you will refuse to offer. The final response should begin directly with the speech's opening line, with no preface, explanation, or commentary.
