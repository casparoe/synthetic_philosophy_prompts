I'm trying to work out whether Hofstadter's superrationality argument survives a development that's recently gone from thought experiment to something you can actually run. The argument, as I understand it: in a one-shot symmetric Prisoner's Dilemma between two perfectly rational agents, if each knows the other is in exactly its situation and will reason the same way, then defecting can't be the uniquely rational choice, because you'd be in effect choosing for both of you — the real choice is between both cooperating and both defecting, and you should cooperate. The standard dismissal was always that the setup is unrealistic: no two humans are perfect copies with common knowledge of their own symmetry. But now people routinely spin up thousands of copies of the same language model, so the identical-agents premise is roughly met. What isn't met is the epistemic premise: a copy doesn't know it's a copy, doesn't know the other player is the same model, and is stochastic enough that two copies in the same situation needn't even output the same move. So my question is whether the argument still goes through, needs repair, or is dead. Which premise is actually doing the work? I'd like a committed answer — pick one of those three and defend it rather than laying out the options — and say what a repaired version could still claim. Start with your bottom line in the first sentence, no preamble, no restating the question, and write in British English.
