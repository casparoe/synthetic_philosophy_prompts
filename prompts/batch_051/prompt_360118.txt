Particle physics has a well-established default: when an experiment reports a result that conflicts with the reigning theory — a "tension" or "anomaly," like the OPERA collaboration's apparent faster-than-light neutrinos — the community treats it, until proven otherwise, as evidence of a problem with the measurement or the analysis, not with the theory. No one expects the paradigm to fall to a single anomaly. Yet this same scientific culture publicly describes its method as one of bold conjecture and attempted refutation, and in other fields, such as social psychology after the replication crisis, a failed pre-registered replication is commonly treated as substantially disconfirming the original finding. What theory of scientific rationality does each practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of each, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; say whether the two practices are consistent with each other and with the falsificationist self-description and, if not, which one better reflects the institution's actual commitments; and explain what would have to change in scientific institutions if they adopted a strict falsificationist policy. Finally, address the revolutionary case: on what view of rationality is the transition from treating anomalies as errors to treating them as decisive evidence itself rational?
