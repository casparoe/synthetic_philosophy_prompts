A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about whether the question "why are some nations rich and others poor?" is one that historians and economists can ever answer with real confidence. My friend kept returning to the thought that institutions, geography, and culture are so deeply entangled that any claimed causal effect can be redescribed: maybe secure property rights caused growth, or maybe they merely signal a society already oriented toward commerce by deeper cultural dispositions, or maybe both are downstream of climate and disease patterns. He ended by insisting that the question is, in practice if not in principle, undecidable from historical data, and that every clever identification strategy only relocates the uncertainty rather than resolving it. I left unsettled, because I could not find a clean reply even though I suspected his pessimism was too strong.

Later I found myself wondering how Ibn Khaldun would respond if he could see the modern form of this debate. He lived long before econometrics, but his writing tries to explain why dynasties, cities, and civilizations rise and decline, and it moves continuously between climate, geography, group solidarity, economic life, law, and political authority. Suppose we could give him a working command of modern development economics: cross-country regressions, natural experiments, instrumental variables, growth accounting, and the debates over whether institutions cause prosperity or are caused by it. How might he take sides? Would he regard the modern institutionalist project as a more rigorous version of his own attempt to find general causes in the ruin and flourishing of states, or would he think it repeats an old mistake of compressing the life of a civilization into a few measurable variables? Would he agree with my friend that the big causal question cannot be settled from historical data, or would he argue that some mid-level claims can be known even if the grand one remains contested? I am less interested in a biographical guess than in using his theoretical instincts to pressure-test modern methodological pessimism. What would his likely verdict be, and what arguments might he offer for it?
