Write a sestina (six six-line stanzas and a three-line envoy, with the six end-words recurring in the standard permuted order) on the ethical implications of panpsychism, understood here as the view that something like phenomenal consciousness is fundamental and ubiquitous, so that the question of suffering reaches all the way down into fundamental physics. Choose the six recurring end-words from the conceptual core of the debate—matter, inside, bind, scale, pain, and sum are usable suggestions—and let the sestina's permuting end-words enact the panpsychist thought that the same micro-experiences recombine into ever larger subjects. Move through levels of organization (an electron in a field, a covalent bond, a metabolic gradient, the nervous system these make possible), and at each level ask whether the whole's pain is anything over and above the parts' pain, without resolving whether micro-suffering aggregates into a morally significant sum or instead dissolves at larger scales because there is no unified subject to undergo it. The envoy should compress that irresolution rather than choose a side. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mind working on the combination problem would recognize the view and its hardest objection, and good enough that a reader who has never heard of panpsychism gets a rigorous poem rather than a New Age reverie.
