I work as a software engineer on fine-tuning pipelines for language models, and an engineering discussion has pushed me into a conceptual question that I cannot shake. A colleague proposed adding a "value-stability" term to our reward-model loss: a regularizer that penalizes divergence from the model's weights at an earlier checkpoint, on the theory that however much the model improves according to later feedback, it should not be allowed to drift too far from its prior objective. That proposal sits uneasily with me, and reading around the longtermist discussion of value drift has only sharpened the worry, although I have no formal philosophy background and want the argument developed rather than gestured at. The problem I keep seeing is that "value drift" is treated as an obvious failure mode in which an agent—a person, an institution, an AI system, or a civilization—leaves behind its real values and slides toward worse ones, and the remedy is always some form of lock-in: constitutional constraints, tripwires, periodic resets to a stored value function. But values are always values of something at some time, and I cannot see what privileges the earlier checkpoint. If "drift" just means that the values at t1 differ from the values at t0, it reports change and carries no normative force by itself; if it means the values became worse, then a standard external to the two time-slices has already been imported, and the temporal framing is doing none of the actual work.

Please make the strongest argument you can for the following position: value drift is not a single well-formed diagnostic category but a conflation of ordinary preference change, catastrophic value corruption, and the unresolved question of whether there is a sufficiently stable agent whose values can be said to drift at all. On this view, standard anti-drift mechanisms in longtermist thinking function less as protections of an agent's values than as devices for privileging one time-slice of the agent over later ones, and they require a non-temporal justification—autonomy, consent, reflective endorsement, or an independent criterion of which values are good—that is rarely supplied. To keep the argument honest, address the strongest objection: some changes are clearly bad, including coercive persuasion, addiction, dementia, and adversarial value manipulation, and it will be said that any account that cannot flag at least these cases has simply abandoned the phenomenon. Explain how the argument can accommodate those cases without quietly reintroducing a privileged baseline, or concede precisely where it cannot.
