I have a friend who defends the axiom of choice by comparing it to the finite case. If you've got ten boxes, each with at least one item in it, nobody doubts you can form a set containing exactly one item from each box; all AC says, he insists, is that the very same move stays legitimate when there are infinitely many boxes. He brought up Russell's boots and socks to make the point that the only real difference between the cases is whether you happen to have a rule like always taking the left boot; he thinks treating that as significant is constructivist scruple rather than mathematics, so the controversy over AC is overblown and the axiom is just obviously true of sets.

I suspect the step from finitely many choices to infinitely many choices is doing more work then he admits, but I can't articulate where it goes wrong. Audit the analogy properly. I want the explicit mapping between the finite procedure and the infinite statement, the points where the mapping breaks, an account of which disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion and which are red herrings, and a verdict on whether the conclusion (AC is obviously true, the controversy is overblown) survives once the disanalogies are on the table.
