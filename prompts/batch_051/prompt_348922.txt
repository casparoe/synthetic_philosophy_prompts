I am a PhD student in early modern intellectual history, currently writing a chapter on seventeenth-century debates over the authority of Scripture in natural philosophy. A recent survey of the Galileo affair that I read for background reconstructs Galileo's position on Scripture roughly as follows. It says Galileo adopted the traditional Augustinian doctrine of the two books: God is the author of both the Book of Scripture and the Book of Nature, and since the two books have one and the same author they cannot truly contradict one another. From this the survey infers that whenever a literal reading of the Bible clashes with what the senses or a mathematical demonstration establish about nature, the literal reading must be abandoned, because Scripture is concerned with our salvation rather than with the structure of the heavens. It then attributes to Galileo a fully general hermeneutical principle: no passage of Scripture, whatever its subject matter, may be interpreted literally where that would conflict with demonstrated natural knowledge. Finally, it concludes that Galileo had already drawn a clean boundary between the two domains, one on which theology has no jurisdiction over the conclusions of natural science and the natural philosopher is the final judge of what the relevant biblical texts mean.

This summary feels too neat to me, and from my own reading of the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina I suspect two things in particular are off: that Galileo limited the principle to passages that speak about the natural world and are not matters of faith or morals, and that he was more careful about reserving the authority to interpret Scripture to the Church than the final sentence allows. But I do not want to overcorrect into the opposite distortion, namely that Galileo was a fideist or that he thought science and theology simply never share a subject matter. Could you audit the summary sentence by sentence, separating what a careful historian would accept from what is an overstatement or a conflation, and then tell me which conclusions survive the correction and which do not? I am especially interested in whether the survey's final "separation of domains" reading is legitimately supported by the earlier premises or whether it is itself the main distortion.
