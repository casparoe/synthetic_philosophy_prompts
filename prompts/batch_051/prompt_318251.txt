I keep hearing that guilt is the better moral emotion because its about the act, while shame is just about how you look to other people. I want to test an argument in the other direction, that shame is actually better. Here it is:

(1) The better moral emotion is whichever one more reliably prevents the relevant wrongs.
(2) Guilt only gets going after you already judge that what you did (or are about to do) is wrong, so it can always be short-circuited by self-serving rationalization.
(3) Shame can get going before any settled judgment, since it's triggered by the mere thought of being seen doing the act.
(4) An emotion that fires before judgment catches wrongs that would otherwise slip through your own motivated thinking.
(5) So shame is the better moral emotion.

I suspect (1) is the cheat becuase it smuggles in a consequentialist standard rather than asking whether the emotion fits the wrong. But a friend said (4) is sneakier: catching more wrongs isn't the same as catching wrongs more accurately, and shame might fire just as hard when the act is actually fine. I also worry that the obvious reply to (2)—that guilt can be anticipated too—just turns guilt into shame by another name. So: is the argument valid as written? If not, exactly which step fails on the strongest reading, and can it be repaired without giving up the conclusion? Then a followup question: suppose it can't be repaired. Does that tell us anything about whether a whole culture could deliberately reorganize itself around shame rather than guilt, or is cultural choice a separate question about feedback loops rather than individual psychology?
