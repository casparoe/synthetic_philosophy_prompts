For a small blog I run on military policy from a philosophy-ish angle, write me a 'which theory of military service do you actually hold?' quiz. Eight questions, each a concrete little case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key. The thread I want the quiz to pull on is whether people defend conscription because they think service is good for the people conscripted (citizenship, fairness, integration) or because of what it does for the state (deterrence, a deep reserve, social resilience). My pet theory is that many self-described civic republicans are really total-defense people in disguise, so write questions that force someone who wants both to pick — no 'it depends' options, and never just ask 'do you think the draft builds citizenship?'

The scoring key should land people on five positions: (1) the old civic-republican / Machiavelli-and-Rousseau militia ideal; (2) the liberal fairness view, where conscription is at bottom a tax paid in labor and the argument is only about whether it falls fairly; (3) the technocratic professionalist, who thinks an all-volunteer force fights better and respects liberty more, full stop; (4) the social-integration view, where the point is mixing classes and ethnic groups; (5) the Finnish/Swiss-style total-defense view, where conscription is mostly about credible deterrence and a trained reserve. Build from the logical space rather than from those labels: write the questions first, then say which answer patterns map onto which leaf. Flag pairs of leaves your questions can't reliably seperate — I suspect (1) and (5) only come apart when you force the citizenship-versus-deterrence choice — and flag empty cells too, like the person who wants civic formation but insists it must be voluntary; is that a real position or just an incoherent mash?

Use at least three of these cases: a long war where volunteers run dry; Sweden reintroducing conscription and applying it to women too; Switzerland voting in a referendum on keeping its militia; and a think tank proposing to scrap the draft and replace it with a tax that funds much higher pay for volunteers. Audience is smart laypeople who've thought about politics but don't know the technical literature.

Format: a numbered list with exactly ten items. Items 1–8 are the quiz questions, each one or two sentences, no intro line and no wrap-up after. Item 9 is the scoring key, kept terse. Item 10 is a one-or-two-sentence caveat flagging the non-separable pairs and empty cells.
