For my philosophy class I'm writing a paper on whether citizens have special obligations to one another, and my teacher wants me to start from my own reactions to concrete cases before reading any theory. I have a set of gut verdicts, but I can't find a single principle that captures them. You are a professor of political philosophy who works on nationalism, cosmopolitanism, and the ethics of partiality. Please think carefully and take my cases seriously. Start by brainstorming several candidate principles that could explain my pattern, test each one against the cases step by step, and only then defend your best account. Do not give me a bibliography or quote authors; I want the argument, not references.

Here are my verdicts.

1. Two strangers are drowning and I can save only one. One is from my country, the other is a foreign tourist. I feel I should save my compatriot, and I don't think that choice is morally wrong, even slightly, provided I don't treat the foreigner with contempt or indifference afterward.

2. Two earthquakes strike in the same week: one in my country and one in a much poorer country. The foreign victims' need is greater. I think my government should send most of its relief abroad when the foreign need is clearly much greater. But if the needs are roughly equal, I think it may spend the money on its own citizens. So a government may prefer its own citizens only when the needs aren't too unequal.

3. A small business owner is hiring and has two equally qualified applicants, one a compatriot and one an immigrant. I feel uneasy saying the owner may pick the compatriot just for being a compatriot. It feels like the wrong kind of reason, closer to nepotism or ethnic preference than to loyalty. But I can't explain why this seems different from the drowning case, where I was sure the preference was fine.

4. My country committed a serious historical wrong against another country generations ago. I think my country as a state owes compensation today, even though no living citizen did the wrong. But I don't think I personally owe anything to particular descendants of the victims beyond what my taxes already pay. I hold both of these, and I'm not sure they fit together.

5. A citizen of my country is arrested abroad and needs consular help. I think my country's embassy should do more for her than for a foreigner in a comparable situation; I'd think it was a scandal if it didn't.

6. At a farmers market someone can buy the same apples at the same price and quality from a local grower or a foreign grower. I think buying local is fine, but I don't think anyone has an obligation to do it.

I keep trying to make one principle work: compatriots have special claims on one another only because they are members of the same cooperative scheme — the same state, laws, and taxes — and not because of shared culture, identity, or mere sameness. But that principle seems to get my first two verdicts backwards. The rescue has nothing to do with cooperation, yet I was sure it was fine. The hiring takes place inside a cooperative economy, yet I felt it was the wrong kind of reason.

Is there a better principle that captures all of these, or close? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn? Be honest about the trade-off: I would rather drop a verdict than end up with a principle that is just my six intuitions dressed up as theory. And please apply your account to a case I haven't made up my mind about: a citizen who moved abroad thirty years ago, versus a non-citizen who has lived, worked, and paid taxes in my town for decades — if I have to choose between saving one of them, who, if either, has the special claim, and on what grounds?
