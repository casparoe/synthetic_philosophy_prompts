I've spent this term in a metaphysics of religion seminar, and the two of us who argue the most have settled into opposite estimates about the Trinity. He puts his credence around 0.7 that the doctrine can be given a rigorous, faithful, and consistent reconstruction; I am down near 0.35. The disagreement is not about whether the doctrine is true — neither of us is a believer — but about whether it is even coherent, and it keeps going because we talk past each other about what the identity claims and the phrase one God would have to mean.

I want your own number, and more importantly the decomposition behind it. The proposition is this: the orthodox doctrine of the Trinity — the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are three really distinct persons, each truly is God, and yet there is exactly one God, not three — admits at least one philosophically adequate and consistent reconstruction, whether or not anyone has fully formalized it in print. By consistent I mean the core identity and predication claims can be modeled in some explicit semantics, which you choose, without contradiction and without quietly changing the subject. The word God, as predicated of each person, may not be turned into a mere role or shared property that a divine committee happens to instantiate; the persons may not come out as three Gods in disguise; and no person may be reduced to a mere mode, mask, or appearance of the one God.

Decompose your credence into at least the following five components, assigning each a probability from 0 to 1 and a one-sentence justification. Where one component is conditional on the success or failure of an earlier one, say so instead of giving me five numbers to multiply blindly.

1. The probability that the flat-footed reading of the doctrine is formally contradictory when identity is read as absolute and one-to-one — that is, the appearance of contradiction is real before any technical repair.
2. The probability that relative identity — the view that an identity statement is incomplete until a sortal is supplied, so that the Father and the Son can be the same God while being different persons — is a coherent, rigorous semantics rather than a verbal dodge.
3. Given that relative identity is viable, the probability that it can in fact be executed to yield a full reconstruction in which the persons remain really distinct and there is exactly one God.
4. The probability that if relative identity fails or is rejected as too ad hoc, an absolute-identity account of the Latin kind — one divine substance, three subsistent relations, the persons constituted purely by relational opposition — can be made consistent, ideally without abandoning divine simplicity, the doctrine that God has no parts and no composition.
5. The probability that a formal consistency result, even if achieved, would be enough: that the winning reconstruction would still be recognizably the Trinity rather than a solution to a logical toy problem.

Then make your combination explicit — product, weighted average, decision tree, or whatever you actually used — and give your final credence from 0 to 1 that the proposition is true.

Finally, tell me which single component the final number is most sensitive to, which component you are least confident in, what one piece of evidence or argument would move your credence most, and — given my friend at 0.7 and me at 0.35 — which component you think actually separates us.
