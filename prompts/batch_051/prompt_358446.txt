I've been trying to get my head around the algorithmic fairness impossibility results, and a data science newsletter I follow summarised Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan's paper *Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores* like this:

"The authors prove that fairness in algorithmic risk scores is basically impossible. They show that once you write down the mathematical definitions that fairness advocates actually use, no algorithm can satisfy the main ones at the same time, except in trivial cases. What this means in practice is that any attempt to make predictive tools fair across groups will inevitably fail: if you fix one kind of bias, you introduce another. So the whole project of algorithmic fairness is chasing an inconsistent set of demands, and the right response is to accept that decisions about trade-offs are political, not technical."

The newsletter quoted this passage from the paper's abstract to back up the claim:

"We formalize three fairness conditions that lie at the heart of these debates, and we prove that except in highly constrained special cases, there is no method that can satisfy these three conditions simultaneously. Moreover, even satisfying all three conditions approximately requires that the data lie in an approximate version of one of the constrained special cases identified by our theorem."

I suspect the newsletter is overreading the result, but I'm not sure where. Could you go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful reading of the actual theorem supports, what is a common simplification, and where the newsletter's conclusions stop following from the quoted result? I'd especially like to know whether the impossibility is between specific technical conditions rather than about fairness in general, and what the authors themselves would likely say about the claim that the field is chasing inconsistent demands.
