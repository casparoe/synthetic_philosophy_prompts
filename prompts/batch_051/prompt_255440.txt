I defended a conciliationist line: the fact that an apparent epistemic peer disagrees with me is itself a reason for both of us to reduce our confidence. He replied that this overcomplicates the obvious truth that reasonable people can disagree, and concluded that neither of us need revise. I suspect that one sentence is being asked to carry more weight than it can bear, but I could not say exactly which claim it was asserting.

Please lay out the distinct precise claims that "reasonable people can disagree" might be used to express. I want a menu of readings: claims about whether a body of evidence uniquely fixes a rational attitude or instead permits several (uniqueness versus permissivism); claims about whether peer disagreement constitutes higher-order evidence; claims about what rationality requires in response to such evidence; and claims about what "reasonable" has to mean if the slogan is to be more than a sociological platitude. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and indicate which reading or combination of readings my colleague would need for his reply to succeed. I am not after citations or a reading list, just a clear map of the conceptual space.
