I've been reading about the attention economy and why apps like TikTok or YouTube are so hard to leave. The usual stuff: variable rewards, infinite scroll, autoplay, streaks, those little red badges. It struck me that these features are not just sitting there, they're built to pull you in, and then the companies defence is always "well, nobody forced you to keep scrolling." Around the same time a friend told me about a legal idea called attractive nuisance. The way he explained it: if you build something on your land that you know will draw kids in — a pool, an old appliance, a half-collapsed shed — and a kid tresspasses and gets hurt, you can be liable anyway, because the thing itself was the lure. The kid's poor judgment is sort of assumed in advance, and the law looks at what the owner did by putting the attraction there.

I can't shake the thought that this is the same structure as persuasive design. The feed is like the pool: designed to attract, known to be risky, and the "you chose to scroll" defense is like the "they trespassed" defense. Is this a real parallel or am I just pattern matching? Where does it actually map and where does it break? For example, a pool mostly just harms the kid, but an app also gives the user things they value, which seems like a real difference. And the doctrine is about children who can't appreciate the risk, while adults arguably can — but then so much of this design seems aimed exactly at the parts of our judgment that don't catch on. If the parallel is genuine, what would a version of this principle look like for platforms, and what would it predict that's obviously false? If it's superficial, what's doing the real work in each case?
