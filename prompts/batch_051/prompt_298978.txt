A friend and I have been arguing about the continuum hypothesis, and the conversation kept coming back to one argument of his. I am left with the sense that something has gone wrong in it, but I cannot say exactly where, and I want the failure located rather than gestured at.

His argument runs as follows:

(1) ZFC is a first-order theory in a countable language, so by the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem, if it has a model at all, it has a countable model; call one of them M.
(2) Inside M there is an object ℝ_M that M regards as the set of real numbers, and M satisfies the usual theorems about ℝ_M, including Cantor's theorem that ℝ_M is uncountable.
(3) But M is countable when viewed from outside, and the things M counts as real numbers are among the things in M; so, from outside, ℝ_M has only countably many elements.
(4) Therefore whether the reals are countable or uncountable depends on the standpoint: inside M they are uncountable, outside M they are countable. Cardinality is not an absolute feature of a set; it is an artefact of which functions a given viewpoint makes available.
(5) The continuum hypothesis is a statement about cardinalities: it says that no set has a cardinality strictly between that of the natural numbers and that of the reals.
(6) Since cardinality shifts with standpoint (step 4), and the continuum hypothesis is a claim about the cardinalities of the reals, there is no standpoint-independent fact of the matter about whether it holds. The continuum hypothesis therefore has no determinate truth value, and the standard independence results are only one symptom of that.

We both know the usual facts, namely Gödel's constructible-universe result and Cohen's forcing result, so I am not asking whether the continuum hypothesis is independent of ZFC. I am asking whether this argument succeeds in showing something stronger, namely that the question itself is relative or indeterminate. Is the argument valid? If not, identify the exact step at which it fails, and say whether the failure is repairable or whether the conclusion has to be defended, if at all, on entirely different grounds. In particular, is he entitled to move from 'the reals of a countable model are countable from outside' to 'the reals are countable from some legitimate standpoint'? Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which premise would have to be vindicated or refuted to reverse your verdict, and at which point in your diagnosis you are least confident.
