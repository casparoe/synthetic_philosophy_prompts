An introductory philosophy of mind book I read gives the following summary of Dennett's case against qualia. The book says that Dennett starts from the observation that people sometimes make unreliable claims about their own experience, so private subjective categories cannot be trusted as data. His central example involves two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn, who both report that they no longer enjoy a coffee they once liked. Chase says the coffee still tastes exactly the same to him but his preferences have changed; Sanborn says his preferences have not changed but the coffee now tastes different to him. According to the book, Dennett argues that since no outside observer could ever tell which man is describing a change in the taste itself and which is describing a change in his reaction to the taste, the distinction between the way a taste appears and one's judgment about it collapses. The book then concludes that Dennett has shown qualia are meaningless because statements about them are empirically untestable, and that he is committed to denying that there is any such thing as what it is like to taste coffee.

I suspect the last step is unfair, but I cannot tell exactly where. Please do an accuracy audit of this summary. Which parts faithfully capture Dennett's argument, which are subtle overstatements or conflations, and how would Dennett himself respond to the conclusion that his view denies that there is anything it is like to taste coffee? Also explain how the Chase and Sanborn example functions within the broader strategy of quining qualia, and whether the book's characterization of that strategy as a straightforward appeal to unverifiability is right.
