I'm the person who reviews new feature and data-sharing proposals at a mid-sized consumer health-tracking app, and I need a reusable review procedure, not another thinkpiece. The recurring fight is: a PM says "the user consented" and someone else says "this is creepy." I want a checklist my non-lawyer team can run in 30 minutes per proposal, with legal signing off at the end, that operationalizes the privacy principle in this passage from Warren and Brandeis, The Right to Privacy, 1890 Harvard Law Review:

"The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury."

The checklist should treat consent as a fact to be weighed, not as an automatic green light, since the principle is about the person's interest in retreat, not just contract or reputation. Worked applications: (a) we archive users' weight data, and a PM wants to send them a push notification that references their logged weight from two months ago to prompt them to log again; (b) an outside company offers to buy aggregated sleep data; (c) we want to auto-detect when a user's logged eating looks like a possible eating-disorder pattern and proactively show them a "we're here for you" message. My team loves (c), and I want your procedure to say what it actually says about (c), not what my team wants it to say. Mark each step where the checklist stops and a human has to make a value call, and say what the principle itself can't settle in 2026. Begin with the checklist/flow itself, no preface or summary of the theory; then run the three cases; then tell me where it breaks.
