People always describe Pearl's causal hierarchy as a ladder: association down at rung one, intervention on rung two, counterfactuals on top. A colleague of mine leans on this picture hard—since it's a ladder, he says, you simply cannot get to do-type answers from observational data, so any estimate of what would happen if we treated everyone that comes from a study where nobody was randomized is automatically suspect. I'm not sure the ladder metaphor should be doing that much work, and I want to know how far it actually holds. Can you audit it? In the structural causal model formalism, what actually maps onto each rung; what the ladder image quietly imports (discreteness? strict one-way dependence? one query type per rung?); where it breaks down; and whether my colleague's conclusion survives once the picture is dropped. And since I'll use this to argue with him, please say what would change your verdict: what sort of example or theoerem would make you treat the ladder as a substantive constraint rather than a helpful heuristic, or the reverse.
