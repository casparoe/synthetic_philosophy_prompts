I am currently taking an undergraduate course on collective responsibility and structural harms, and I have been asked to lead one fifty-minute session for a group of about eighteen students. The assigned topic is the inefficacy objection to individual obligations in large-scale collective harms. Rather than lecture, I want to run an activity in which participants encounter the problem themselves: in aggregative harms such as the emissions from flying or the demand for factory-farmed meat, any single person's abstention seems to make no perceptible difference, and yet the aggregate of many such abstentions plainly would.

Please design a runnable classroom activity for this setting. It should be a game or structured choice task, with rules simple enough to explain in five minutes and materials that can be printed or improvised. It should require no real money: use points, tally marks, or tokens. The mechanics should be arranged so that a player can articulate, unprompted, something like the inefficacy objection — roughly, that one's own move made no difference, so why sacrifice anything — before the facilitator names it. I would also like the activity to include, if you think it can be made to work, a second stage involving a threshold: a small chance that one person's contribution is the one that triggers a large shared benefit or prevents a large shared loss. The idea is to let participants feel how the force of the inefficacy objection depends on whether a harm is purely aggregative or whether any individual could be pivotal, without telling them that distinction in advance. If you judge that this second stage cannot be fitted into fifty minutes without confusion, say so and design the single-stage version instead; do not force it.

Please also write a debrief of about ten minutes that maps specific moments in the play back to the distinction between making a causally perceptible difference and being one participant in a harmful practice, and to the further thought that a practice can be condemned even when no single contribution to it can. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce — for example, moral seriousness, real stakes, responsibility spread over years, or the role of institutions — and say how the facilitator should supply that in the debrief. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how I should handle it.
