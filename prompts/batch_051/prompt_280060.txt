I have been preparing a seminar on the ethics of gratitude, and I have found myself genuinely torn between a theory I would otherwise endorse and an intuition it cannot accommodate. The theory is this: gratitude is the fitting response to a benefactor—a person who has intentionally benefited one out of good will—and its object, occasion, and degree are fixed by the benefactor's intention and the good conferred. This view earns its keep by making gratitude intelligible and criticizable: it tells us when gratitude is apt, excessive, misplaced, or owed in the wrong direction; it explains why one can be wrong to feel grateful toward someone who meant no good, and why one may owe more thanks for the modest intended kindness than for the accidental windfall. I find these systematic virtues genuinely compelling, and I do not want a view on which gratitude becomes a vague warmth toward whatever happens to go well.

But I also have an intuition I cannot rid myself of. I believe I owe something like gratitude to the dead—not to a single benefactor who had me in view, but to the many people who built, maintained, and reformed the institutions, practices, and infrastructure from which I benefit daily, even though their intended beneficiaries may have been people quite unlike me. The benefactor theory, applied strictly, seems to say that there is no one there to thank and nothing fitting to feel, or at most that I may be glad for the outcome. That conclusion feels to me less like a clarification than like the amputation of a real normative relation to the past.

Give me the strongest version of both positions. Is the intuition plausibly a sentimental overextension of a personal relation, and if so, what exactly would debunk it without debunking gratitude itself? Or is the theory's criterion of a fitting object too narrow, and if so, what principle should amend it so that it can include collective, anonymous, or non-reciprocal cases without letting gratitude collapse into general appreciation for good outcomes? Or is there a reading on which the theory and the intuition do not really conflict? I want a negotiated verdict rather than a side: say what each of the three moves would commit us to and what would justify it.
