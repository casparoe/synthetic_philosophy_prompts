I serve on the grants committee of a private foundation that funds SETI research. We must rule on a proposal for a five-year active-SETI (METI) campaign: continuous, high-power transmissions toward a short list of nearby Sun-like stars, intended to elicit a detectable reply within a human lifetime. The committee is split. One faction invokes the dark forest hypothesis—roughly, that any civilization that detects another has reason to destroy it preemptively, so broadcasting one's location is close to self-annihilation—and maintains that no plausible scientific payoff can justify a downside that is in effect unbounded. The other faction replies that the hypothesis is science fiction whose empirical premises are unconfirmed, and that a foundation should not veto real research on the strength of a parable; it adds that Earth is already detectable through biosignatures and radio leakage, so a refusal to transmit may be close to a distinction without a difference. Draft the committee's decision memo: approve, reject, or approve with conditions. The memo should specify the criterion you believe should govern decisions whose worst-case outcome is irreversible and potentially catastrophic, apply that criterion to this proposal, and give a serious, fair statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side.
