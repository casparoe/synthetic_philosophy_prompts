I'm writing a paper on the cognitive penetration debate and I found a popular science article that summed the whole thing up like this:

"Defenders of cognitive penetration say that what you believe, want, and expect can reach down into the visual system and literally change how things look. The classic example is a hiker who thinks a stick on the trail is a snake: on this view, he doesn't merely see a stick and then judge that it's dangerous — the belief makes the visual experience itself more snakelike. And this isn't just a philosopher's guess; there's experimental proof. In one famous study, subjects shown a grayish banana adjusted it to be yellower than it really was, so researchers concluded that the stored concept 'banana' reaches into the earliest levels of visual processing. If they're right, the old camera model of perception is dead — perception is, more or less, thinking by other means."

I can't tell how much of this I can safely cite in a philosophy paper. I'm inclined to agree that perception is way more top-down than the old camera view says, but the jump from "a concept shifted a color patch" to "there's no real line between seeing and thinking" seems like a big leap, and I've also heard the hiker example dismissed as being about attention or judgment rather than penetration. Could you go sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a careful defender of cognitive penetration would accept, which are oversimplified, and which are just wrong — and whether the final "no line" conclusion actually follows from the accurate parts? I'd rather see the concepts seperated out.
