You are a professional philosopher who can also speak fluent distributed-systems engineering. The deliverable is a systematic translation plus a residue report; do not hand me soft analogies, and do not paper over mismatches.

I am a senior distributed systems engineer with no formal background in philosophy. The vocabulary I actually reason in includes primary and replica nodes, multi-primary replication, failover and replication lag, sharding and partition keys, natural keys versus surrogate UUIDs, foreign keys and joins across shards, logical versus materialized views, snapshot isolation, clock skew, quorum, CAP trade-offs, and schema migrations.

I have been trying to make sense of David Lewis's modal realism. As I understand it, the view has roughly the following commitments:

1. Modal claims are quantificational claims over possible worlds: something is possible if and only if it holds at some possible world, and necessary if and only if it holds at every possible world.
2. Possible worlds are concrete, fully determinate entities of broadly the same kind as the world we inhabit — not abstract descriptions, sentences, or mere ways of considering how things might have been.
3. Actuality is indexical. The word actual picks out, relative to a context, whichever world the speaker inhabits, the way here picks out a place and now picks out a time. Every world is actual at itself, and none is actual in some absolute, privileged sense.
4. Worlds are isolated. No two worlds are spatiotemporally related to one another, and no part of one world is part of another. In particular, no individual — person, chair, electron — exists in more than one world.
5. Counterpart theory replaces trans-world identity. Instead of the same individual appearing in multiple worlds, an individual has counterparts in other worlds: entities that resemble it in contextually appropriate respects and degrees. The counterpart relation is context-sensitive and need not be symmetric or transitive, and de re modal predications run through counterparts rather than through strict identity.
6. Plenitude holds. The plurality of worlds is extremely large: roughly, any suitable recombination of the ingredients of worlds yields a world, so almost any consistent possibility is realized somewhere.
7. The project is reductive. Possibility and necessity are not primitive ideological additions to reality; they are to be explained in terms of quantification over this fixed plurality of concrete worlds, with counterfactuals receiving a related similarity-based treatment.

I can half-see how some of this might map onto my world. Actuality-as-indexical feels like the observation that every node is primary for its own writes. The isolation of worlds feels like a ban on cross-shard joins and foreign keys. Counterpart theory feels like replacing a global primary key with a per-shard similarity lookup. And the concreteness of worlds feels like the difference between a materialized view and a merely logical view. But I do not trust these glib mappings, and that is exactly what I want you to pressure-test.
