I teach a decision theory seminar most years, and I keep comming back to Pascal's mugging as the topic that makes the room seperate into camps and then stop talking to each other. Quick setup for anyone who needs it: a mugger asks for a small amount of money—say $20—in exchange for not carrying out an astronomically bad threat, where your credence that the threat is real is tiny but, you judge, not literally zero. Since the disutility is so enormous, the expected value of paying up looks enormous too; classic expected utility theory seems to say you should hand over the money. My seminar then splits. One group treats this as a serious reductio of expected utility maximization, or at least of unbounded utilities. A second group says the right response is to stop treating your credence as nonzero: complexity priors, or the sheer fact that the story is less probable than any ordinary hypothesis you'd entertain, should drive it to zero for decision purposes. A third group resists that move, because it looks like the same logic by which we justify spending real money on low-probability, high-stakes things like asteroid deflection; if you zero out mugger-style hypotheses, they worry you're doing motivated rounding everywhere.

What bothers me is that this argument has now run for years among smart people without much movement, and I've stopped thinking it's merely difficult. My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement isn't about decision theory at all, but about what a nonzero credence is, or about whether an expectation is something you may act on when your own epistemology can't distinguish the relevant probability from zero, or maybe about the difference between the norm "maximize expected utility" and the norm "don't let negligible probabilities carry the decision." But I can also imagine the diagnosis being a hidden formal question—whether the relevant expected values are even well-defined, or whether any finite agent can have the sharp credences the setup assumes—or a dispute about which cases get to count as data for the theory (ordinary insurance-like choices vs. deliberately absurd constructed ones).

I'd like you, as an expert in formal epistemology and decision theory, to diagnose this impasse rather than adjudicate it. Think carefully: start by laying out, as charitably as possible, what each camp is minimally committed to and what each would have to give up to change its mind. Then tell me whether the disagreement is mostly verbal, or about facts, or about standards of evidence, or about starting intuitions neither side can argue for, or whether "what should you do in a Pascal's mugging?" is just an ill-posed question. And be honest about what could count as progress given your diagnosis: a theorem about bounded utility or dominated alternatives, a sharper distinction between credence and decision weight, a different way of framing the case—or an argument that no such progress should be expected and the debate is a permanent feature of trying to combine probability with infinite-seeming stakes. Don't give me a reading list or citations; I want the structure of the disagreement, not references.
