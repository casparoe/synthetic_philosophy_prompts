I teach a small decision-theory seminar for philosophy majors — 12 upper-level undergrads, one 75-minute slot. We've just covered revealed preference and we're about to do money pumps and diachronic rationality. I want a runnable in-class game that makes the students feel the money-pump problem before I name it: each individual swap feels fine in the moment, but the sequence leaves you strictly worse off. Design a game with roles (choosers and one or two traders), a swap structure for a handful of small items or small-cash options, and a fee/token system so the pump can actually run without real money (or with pennies at most). Then I want a seperate 15-minute phase or extension that gets at the diachronic side: where a player at t1 can foresee that their t2 self will want something their t1 self would rather avoid, and can choose whether to bind themselves. The debrief should map what happened onto intransitive preferences, acyclicity as a coherence requirement, and the difference between naive and sophisticated choice over time. It should also push on the question of whether the pumped player made a mistake at any single step, or whether the mistake belongs to the sequence as a whole. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the formal money-pump arguments a classroom game can't reproduce. The students shouldn't hear the phrase "money pump" until the debrief. Write your answer in American English.
