For an epistemology seminar I'm teaching next term, I want to settle a runing fight between me and a collague about Nozick's tracking theory by actually running a small experimental philosophy study on ordinary knowledge attributions, not by trading intuitions. The claim I want tested is this: in a standard skeptical-pair case (zebra vs. cleverly painted mule, or hands vs. brain-in-a-vat), people's knowledge judgments follow something like a sensitivity condition rather than closure—that is, they'll say S knows p, deny that S knows q, and keep doing that even when the entailment from p to q is pointed out explicitly. My collague is sure that once the entailment is explicit, people will update toward closure, and that the only reason anyone denies q is that they're confusing it with a different proposition (about justification or certainty, say). My own hunch is messier: I suspect the apparent sensitivity effect is really a salience effect—making the skeptical alternative vivid drags down confidence in p as much as q, so it isn't clean evidence for sensitivity either.
