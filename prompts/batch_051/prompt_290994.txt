Exam question: When the law treats a corporation as a person, is it best understood as creating a genuine group agent — a subject that acts and intends over and above its members — rather than as employing a useful fiction? Address (i) what it takes for a group to have intentions, (ii) how legal status-declarations relate to underlying social facts, and (iii) what practical difference the choice between fiction and reality is supposed to make.

Student answer: Corporate personality is best read as genuine group agency rather than fiction. Legal personhood is not idle shorthand: a corporation can enter contracts, own property, and sue in ways no individual shareholder can, so the law is tracking something robust. What makes the group a genuine agent is its decision procedure. When the board votes, the result is the corporation's decision, and the corporation's intention is just whatever that procedure yields — exactly as a natural person's intention is whatever their deliberation yields. The fact that no single human mind contains the group's intention is not an objection, because individuals can hold we-intentions and act within joint commitments. Nor does the fictionalist gain much by insisting that legal status-declarations are constitutive rather than descriptive: once the law constitutes the status of legal person, the entity bearing it exists as an institutional fact and can be the subject of rights, duties, and liabilities. And since we do hold corporations criminally and civilly liable — blame that would make no sense without an agent to bear it — we are already committed to treating them as real agents. The fiction view is therefore just a name for reluctance to admit that groups can act and intend.
