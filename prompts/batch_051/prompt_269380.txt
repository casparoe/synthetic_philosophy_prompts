Please answer as a philosopher working on emotion, imagination, and aesthetics, and think through the possible positions before you commit to one.

I am a novelist, and I have a standing rule about fiction that I want you to assess. The rule is that I treat the emotions a story arouses as quarantined from real life: a scene's power tells you something about the writing, but it does not tell you what the moved reader — or I — actually values, believes, or would do. So when a critic wrote that my new novel condones a certain cruelty because the narration lingers on it with something like tenderness, I called that a category error. The events never happened; the people do not exist; an emotion aimed at a known fiction cannot be evidence about the person who feels it in the way an emotion aimed at the actual world might be. But at a reading last month I caught myself doing the opposite: I took a reader's sympathy for one of my characters as evidence that the character was having the intended effect, and then I wanted to infer from that same sympathy that the reader possessed a certain moral depth. My own rule says the first inference was permissible and the second was not, and I am no longer sure I can defend that line.

I know this touches what philosophers call the paradox of fiction: how we can be moved at all by people and events we know do not exist, and whether those responses are the same kind of thing as ordinary emotion. Some solutions make them quasi-emotions, or make-believe, or offline simulations. My question is epistemic: given those possibilities, is my quarantine rule defensible? Under what conditions, if any, does a response to fiction count as evidence about a person's real moral sensibility, and when is it merely a response to craft? If the rule is too strong, restate it so it keeps what is right without forfeiting whatever self-knowledge fictional emotions can offer. At the end, tell me how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
