I'm a graduate student in a theoretical physics group, and I keep running into the slogan "shut up and calculate." It comes up whenever a junior person presses on what the wavefunction really is, whether the other branches in an Everettian universe are real, what the pilot wave is doing in Bohmian mechanics, or what collapse amounts to in the Copenhagen picture. The senior people use it as a conversation-ender, and it plainly works: the question dies, and everyone returns to the calculation.

But the more I observe, the more I think the phrase is doing several different jobs at once. Sometimes it sounds like a narrow pedagogical rule: master the formalism first, and the interpretive puzzles will look different later. Sometimes it sounds like a bold metaphysical claim: there is no fact of the matter about which interpretation is right. Sometimes it sounds like a methodological boundary: interpretations make no new predictions, so they are not physics at all. Sometimes it even sounds like a sociological remark about who survives in the field: people who work on foundations stop being productive and don't get jobs. I would like to be able to answer the slogan precisely rather than just resent it.

Could you lay out the distinct theses "shut up and calculate" might be expressing, from the narrow and defensible to the strong and dubious, and say for each (a) whether a practicing physicist is actually entitled to assert it and (b) whether it would support using the slogan to close down a question about the reality of the wavefunction or the existence of the other branches? I want a disambiguation with verdicts on each reading rather than a single preferred interpretation. Please end with a machine-readable verdict: a final line of the form \answer{<short label>} giving the label for the reading you think best captures the standard use of the slogan when it is deployed against questions about the reality of the wavefunction.
