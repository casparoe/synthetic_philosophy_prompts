I've been trying to sort out what today's refugee and asylum system is actually committed to, and I keep getting stuck on Kant's Perpetual Peace, the Third Definitive Article. From what I remember of the text, Kant says hospitality is the only cosmopolitan right, and he means something very thin: a foreigner has a right not to be met with hostility when she arrives, but she does not have a right to be taken in as a guest. A right to stay would require a special agreement. The ground for this thin right is that human beings originally share the surface of the earth, so nobody has more right than anyone else to be in a particular place.

So here's what I want to know. The modern regime treats non-refoulement as a principle states can't opt out of, but it also lets states manage borders, turn people away, declare them inadmissable, or screen them outside their territory without ever really receiving them. What view of political obligation, membership, and territory does that combination presuppose? Is it still the Kantian picture plus some add-ons, or has the underlying view been replaced by something else—like a right to live under a decent government, or a humanitarian emergency claim, or something purely procedural? And if we took Kant's grounding seriously—that the earth is originally common and no person has more right than another to be somewhere—what would have to change in the actual practice? I'm less interested in whether Kant himself was right, and more in what his article reveals about what we've already implicitly committed ourselves to.
