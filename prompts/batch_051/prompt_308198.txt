I'm writing a paper defending the outside view — the practice of anchoring forecasts in the base rate of a relevant reference class rather than in the details of one's own case — against the charge that it cannot actually be applied without smuggling in the very case-specific judgment it is meant to correct. A colleague has pressed me with the following argument that the outside view is not merely hard to apply but self-undermining:

(1) Any application of the outside view requires specifying a reference class: a set of past cases similar enough to the present one that their outcome distribution bears on the present case.
(2) Specifying a reference class requires judging which features of the present case are causally relevant to the outcome.
(3) A judgment about which features of one's own present case are causally relevant to the outcome is a case-specific judgment made from the inside — an inside-view judgment.
(4) So every application of the outside view depends on an inside-view judgment that the outside view cannot itself validate, since the outside view only becomes available once the reference class has been fixed.
(5) But the maxim of epistemic modesty — when your inside view conflicts with the outside view, defer to the outside view — directs you to distrust exactly these case-specific inside-view judgments.
(6) Therefore, the maxim of epistemic modesty directs you to distrust the very judgments required to construct any outside view; strict outside-view reasoning is self-undermining.

I suspect the phrase inside view is doing double duty in this argument, but I cannot make the equivocation precise. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and under what reading of the key term would each premise come out true? Give the strongest version of the argument on which every premise is true and say whether (6) follows from it. Is there a concession a defender of the outside view ought to make here, and what should she hold?
