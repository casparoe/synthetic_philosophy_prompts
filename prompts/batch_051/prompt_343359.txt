I keep getting stuck on a paragraph from Minkowski's 1908 lecture "Space and Time." He says: "The views of space and time which I wish to lay before you have sprung from the soil of experimental physics, and therein lies their strength. They are radical. Henceforth, space by itself, and time by itself, are doomed to fade away into mere shadows, and only a kind of union of the two will preserve an independent reality."

Here's what I can't sort out. When he calls space and time "mere shadows," is he actually saying the passage of time is an illusion and that past, present, and future are all equally real? Or is he only saying that space and time aren't seperately fundamental, and we shouldn't treat them as separate containers? And how should I think about the later Rietdijk–Putnam argument here, where relativity of simultaneity gets used to argue that every event is "now" for some observer, so the usual presentist picture has to go? I'd like the answer in American English.
