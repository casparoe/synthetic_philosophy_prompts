After my mother died, I began to notice something that is hard to describe without sounding sentimental. It is not simply that I remember her. I seem to be carrying around an active, generative piece of her: in situations she never encountered, I find myself producing arguments she would have made, with her distinctive moves; I occasionally make jokes that are recognizably hers even though she never made them; and in my internal disagreements, a voice that is not quite mine takes her side. My father and sister describe the same phenomenon, but with different pieces. My father carries her practical decision-making, her impatience, and her way of working through logistics. My sister carries her mannerisms, her phrasing, her way of defusing family quarrels. It feels as though the single "I" that was my mother has been broken up and distributed among the three of us, each of us hosting a partial and somewhat different version of her.

A psychologist friend says this is just how memory works: we construct an internal model of the people we are close to, and after they die the model keeps running. I accept that something like this is physically happening, and I do not believe in souls or ghosts. But I am not sure the word "just" is doing real work there. The fragments are not passive recollections; they are productive, they persist in ways my ordinary memories of her do not, and they sometimes feel foreign to me in a way that a deliberate recall does not. I want the argument, not consolation. Is there a defensible, literal sense in which a single self can persist partially across several other brains after death? What distinguishes an active fragment of a person from a mere memory or a predictive model of her? And if I accept that her "I" is genuinely distributed among my father, my sister, and me, what exactly would I then be committed to — about whether she still exists, and about whether it is right to speak of her in the present tense rather than the past?
