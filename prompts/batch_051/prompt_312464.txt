I've been doing RL stuff at work for a while now — mostly DQN and PPO, nothing fancy — and there's a small thing that's been bugging me more than it should. In every codebase and paper, the discounted return is written as γ^t times the reward at step t, with γ = 0.99 or whatever, and the justification is usually something like "future rewards are worth less" or "the agent prefers rewards sooner rather than later." Nobody treats this as a big deal; it's a hyperparameter you tune for stability. Then I was talking about it with a friend who did philosophy and economics, and she pointed out that in those fields, putting a discount on future utility just because it's in the future is called pure time preference, and it's genuinely controversial: a lot of people think it's indefensible, or at least not the kind of thing you should smuggle into an objective function without an argument. She also said a constant discount rate gives you exponential discounting, which is the one form that doesn't make your preferences flip over time — hyperbolic discounting, by contrast, makes you intend to do the hard thing later and then flake when later arrives. And I realized: RL with γ^t is exactly exponential discounting, and we bolt it onto the objective largely so the infinite sum converges and the value estimates don't blow up.

So my question is whether γ in RL is the same thing as pure time preference, or whether I'm just pattern-matching on the math. I'd like a committed answer, not a menu of options: either it is the same structure, in which case I should feel at least a little weird about tuning what's effectively a moral parameter for smoother gradients, or it isn't, in which case I want to know exactly where the mapping breaks. If someone took the "γ is pure time preference" reading seriously, what would it predict about how RL actually behaves that turns out false?
