The topic falls within the philosophy of consent, specifically the question of how deception affects the validity of consent. My half-formed idea is this. A prominent recent view holds that deception invalidates consent when it concerns a condition that the person giving consent would have regarded as a deal-breaker—for example, that a sexual partner is unmarried, or free of a certain infection. I suspect this "deal-breaker" test runs together two questions that should be kept apart. The first is whether the person's consent was valid. The second is whether the person who secured consent by deception is culpable, and to what degree. These can come apart: someone might give valid consent even though the deceiver acted very wrongly in securing it, and a deception might invalidate consent even if the deceiver could not reasonably have known that the hidden fact mattered to the other person. I would like the proposal to investigate whether these two questions can be separated in a principled way, and what follows for the moral and legal treatment of deception if they can.
