For a seminar on strategic interaction I have been working through the centipede game, and a classmate and I built an argument that now seems to us to prove too much, though neither of us can find the fault. The game has two players, A and B, who alternate over six stages. At each stage the player whose turn it is can Take, ending the game immediately with the payoffs listed, or Pass, letting play continue. We fixed the payoffs as follows, written as (A's payoff, B's payoff): A takes at stage 1, (1,0); B takes at stage 2, (0,3); A takes at stage 3, (4,1); B takes at stage 4, (2,6); A takes at stage 5, (7,3); B takes at stage 6, (5,9); and if both players pass at every stage, the game ends at (10,8). We assumed throughout that both players know all of this and that each cares only about maximizing her own payoff.

The argument runs as follows. (1) If stage 6 were reached, B would compare taking (payoff 9) with passing (payoff 8) and would take. (2) Therefore, if stage 5 were reached, A, knowing that B would take at stage 6, would compare taking now (payoff 7) with passing and then receiving 5 after B takes, and A would take. (3) The same two-step reasoning applies at every earlier stage: given what the next player would do if the game reached her, the player to move does better by taking now. (4) Carrying this reasoning back to stage 1, A takes immediately, and the game ends at (1,0). (5) But if both players passed at every stage, they would receive (10,8), which is better for each of them than (1,0), and both players can see this. Hence (6) two fully informed, payoff-maximizing players leave a strictly Pareto-better outcome on the table, and the same reasoning would unravel any finite chain of mutually beneficial cooperation, no matter how large the gains from continuing.

I would most like to keep (1) and (5); I have a sense that something happens between the local reasoning at each node and the global conclusion in (6), but I cannot say what it is, and I do not know what I would be committed to if I rejected any of the steps. Which step should be rejected, why did it look forced, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid as stated, then tell me why the conclusion should be accepted and exactly which picture of rational play it rules out.
