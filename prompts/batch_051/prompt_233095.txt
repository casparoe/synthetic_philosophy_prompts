You are a philosopher working on normative uncertainty. You have been asked to advise a private foundation that will fund a three-year research program in moral philosophy, staffed by three philosophers and devoted to a single unresolved problem in moral uncertainty. The foundation will not fund a survey of the field; it wants one problem attacked in depth. Its trustees have asked you to identify the three problems most worth funding, in order, and to defend the ranking.

The candidates under consideration include at least the following, though you may add others you regard as more important:

1. Intertheoretic comparability of choiceworthiness. Rival moral theories appear to rank options without any common measure of value, so it is unclear what it means for an option to be best in expectation across several theories. The open question is whether any normalization or ordinal method genuinely solves this, or whether the notion of expected choiceworthiness is simply ill-defined.

2. Theory individuation and credences over theories. Expected-choiceworthiness reasoning presupposes a space of theories over which one distributes credence, but there is no obvious principle for carving up that space. Treating utilitarianism as one theory rather than as a continuum of consequentialist variants can change which action is recommended. The open question is whether this is fatal, fixable, or a moral analogue of the reference class problem.

3. The choice of decision rule. Even given an intertheoretically meaningful scale, it is a further question whether one should maximize expected choiceworthiness, choose an option that does tolerably well under every theory one takes seriously, or use some other rule. It is also unclear whether the rule should respect each first-order theory's own attitude toward risk about wrongdoing, and how to do so without importing assumptions alien to those theories.

4. Unbounded and infinite stakes. A small credence in a theory that assigns infinite or arbitrarily large value to some option threatens to dominate the decision procedure. The open question is whether such cases should be handled by discounting, by restricting admissible credences, by treating the theory as not decision-relevant, or in some other way.

5. Moral and metaethical uncertainty. It is unclear whether uncertainty about the truth or status of moral claims is a further source of normative uncertainty requiring separate treatment, or whether it collapses into ordinary first-order moral uncertainty.

6. Collective and institutional decision-making. Even if an individual can answer what to do under moral uncertainty, it is unclear what a legislature, agency, or other collective body should do when its members and the people it serves are themselves morally uncertain, and how their uncertainty should be aggregated without double-counting.

7. Whether moral uncertainty is a distinct practical problem at all. One might hold that a correct first-order moral theory already tells agents what to do under uncertainty, including uncertainty about morality, in which case the second-order apparatus is unnecessary.

Produce a dependency-aware prioritization of the three problems on which the foundation should spend its money. For each of your top three, explain which other problems it is upstream of, how tractable it is for philosophers working without new empirical data, and what results over the three-year grant would lead you to promote or demote it. Also identify which candidate or candidates you would deprioritize, and say why, even if they are intellectually interesting. Make explicit the criteria you are using—for example, importance, tractability, neglectedness, and how many other problems depend on an answer—and where those criteria conflict, explain how you resolved the conflict. The deliverable is a defended agenda, not a survey of the issues.

Please write your answer in American English.
