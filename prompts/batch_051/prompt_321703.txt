I have been trying to settle whether epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness is true, and I find myself caught between an argument I cannot refute and an intuition I cannot abandon.

The argument seems almost coercive. The physical domain is causally closed: every physical event has a sufficient physical cause. If a conscious experience such as a pain also made a causal difference to my behavior, then my behavior would have two sufficient causes, one physical and one experiential, which would force systematic overdetermination; alternatively, the experience just is the physical cause, and then the view is no longer epiphenomenalism. So the felt quality of pain appears to be left with no causal work to do. This is exactly the kind of tidy, closure-respecting picture that I normally find compelling in metaphysics.

Against it, I have a firm intuition I cannot shake. When I touch a hot stove, the pain does not merely accompany my recoiling and my saying that it hurts; it seems to explain them. The claim that the experience was causally inert in the very act of my reporting it is less believable to me than any premise in the causal-exclusion argument. I am aware epiphenomenalists have an account of why brains like ours would produce such reports and the accompanying conviction, but I cannot tell whether that account debunks the intuition or merely explains it away.

Present the strongest versions of both sides. I want to know, for each possibility, what would justify it: what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes; what would it take for the theory to be amended or abandoned; and is there a reading on which both survive, such as a sense in which the pain is causally inert yet still genuinely explains my report? I do not only want to know which side wins; I want to know what evidence or argument would justify each move.
