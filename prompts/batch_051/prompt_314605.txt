A friend who works on health-insurance policy has a scheme for sidestepping the therapy/enhancement distinction entirely, and he wants me to help poke holes in it before it goes anywhere near a board.

The rule: the insurer will reimburse a drug or procedure only if it is prescribed for a condition that appears in the current edition of the insurer's own diagnostic manual. A condition counts as a disease just in case it is listed in that manual at the time of the prescription; anything used for "enhancement, optimization, or lifestyle" is excluded as a category, but the policy deliberately refuses to define disease in terms of normal function or species-typical functioning, because that is the philosophical swamp he's trying to avoid. The manual is revised once a year by a standing panel of seven physicians and one bioethicist; adding or removing a condition takes a two-thirds majority, and any patient group, professional society, or manufacturer may petition for inclusion at the annual review. To catch cases the manual misses, a treating physician can apply for a patient-specific exemption by showing the condition causes significant functional impairment, as judged by the same panel. Covered items carry a small copay; non-covered uses are not banned, just fully out of pocket.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral position. Walk me through what it incentivizes among patients, prescribing doctors, drug and device makers, and the panel itself. Where does it get gamed first? What hapens to boundary cases like ordinary aging, short stature, subclinical or "at-risk" states, and similar? Does the procedural definition of disease hold up under annual revision, or does the therapy/enhancement line just migrate somewhere else? Which failure modes could be fixed without giving up the whole idea?

End with a single sentence beginning "Summary:" that gives your overall verdict on where this scheme breaks first.
