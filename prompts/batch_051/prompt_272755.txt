I have been running a small reading group on rational choice, and last month we worked through the chapter in Douglas Hofstadter’s Metamagical Themas in which he introduces superrationality through the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery. The topic has become personally relevant because I am about to write a short discussion note for the group, and I need to understand where the debate between two members actually stands. For context, the Platonia dilemma is a game in which twenty people each receive a letter offering a chance at one billion dollars. Each participant may send a telegram with just their name within forty-eight hours; if exactly one telegram arrives, that person wins the billion; if zero or two or more arrive, no one wins. There is no way to know who the other nineteen are, and any attempt to communicate with them rescinds the offer. The Luring Lottery is the later variant in which Hofstadter pushes the same reasoning further. After the session, two members of the group got stuck in the following exchange, and neither would budge. They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move.

Elena: In the Platonia dilemma, the ordinary rational thing is to send the telegram. Sending weakly dominates staying silent: if at least one other person sends, you get nothing either way; if no one else sends, sending gets you the billion while staying silent gets you nothing. So all twenty send, no one wins, and game-theoretic rationality just leaves everyone with zero. That seems to me to be the real lesson of the puzzle.

David: That is exactly why Hofstadter distinguishes superrationality from game-theoretic rationality. The standard analysis treats the other players’ choices as fixed and independent. A superrational player instead treats themselves as one token of a type. Since the twenty players are all facing the same problem, an ideal reasoner should expect them all to converge on the same strategy, and then choose the strategy that is best among those symmetric profiles. Here, that means each person should send with probability one-nineteenth, because once your own telegram is in, what matters is that the other nineteen all stay silent.

Elena: I do not see how that helps. If everyone follows the same probabilistic rule, the outcome is just a random draw over possible worlds. In most of those worlds either nobody sends or more than one person sends, so nobody gets the prize. The fact that in one branch a lucky person happens to be the only sender does not mean any of you solved the dilemma. You have simply replaced a certain bad outcome with a lottery.

David: But the lottery has positive expected value. If everyone uses the same probability p, your chance of being the unique sender is p times the probability that the other nineteen all refrain, and that product times a billion is enormous. With p around one-nineteenth, each player’s expected value is something like eighteen or nineteen million dollars. The everyone-sends profile has expected value zero. So superrationality strictly improves on the game-theoretic outcome.

Elena: That expected value calculation is doing all the work, and it smuggles in the assumption that the other nineteen will use the same p. You have no causal influence over them and no communication with them. If they are ordinary game-theoretic maximizers, they will send, and your payoff is zero. If they are superrationalists, they will use your p, but that is exactly the proposition at issue. So superrationality just redefines the problem rather than solving it.

David: That is the standard objection, but Hofstadter’s reply is that the correlation between your choice and theirs is not causal but logical. You are all solving the same problem from the same premises. If those premises make p the uniquely best answer for you, then they also make p the uniquely best answer for the others. Superrationality is simply taking that symmetry seriously.

Elena: I still think that makes superrationality sound like a solution concept rather than a different kind of rationality. In the Prisoner’s Dilemma, for instance, the superrational profile is both cooperate, but that is not a Nash equilibrium, because each player would prefer to defect given the other’s cooperation. So superrationality requires each player to forgo an available gain on the strength of a symmetry that may not hold. That is not rationality; it is a kind of faith in other people’s reasoning.

David: It is not blind faith, though. It is the same move we make whenever we expect another good reasoner to reach the same conclusion we do. The difference is that in a one-shot anonymous Prisoner’s Dilemma, the symmetry argument gives a rational basis for cooperation that standard game theory cannot provide. That is precisely the point of the Platonia and Luring Lottery exercises.

Please go through the exchange and say who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both parties are missing. I would like the verdict to be point-by-point rather than a general impression.
