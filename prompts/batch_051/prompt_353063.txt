I'm a junior in high school doing a semester-long history project about when a legend becomes history, using the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus. For another class I've been reading Hobbes's Leviathan, and this sentence from the dedication to Sidney Godolphin got stuck in my head: "For there is not any vertue that disposeth a man, either to the service of God, or to the service of his Country, to Civill Society, or private Friendship, that did not manifestly appear in his conversation, not as acquired by necessity, or affected upon occasion, but inhaerent, and shining in a generous constitution of his nature." He writes that about a real friend, but it sounds exactly like the elevated way people describe semi-legendary kings. So now I think the standard divide—supernatural stuff vs real stuff, or written vs oral—is too simple, and I'm interested in the messier questions: whether stories can preserve actual names and places while inventing almost everything else, whether a change in genre from annals to epic is what turns history into legend, whether we should put the burden on someone to prove historicity instead of asking skeptics to prove the story wasn't history, and whether it even makes sense to ask if a figure like Arthur 'existed' when the sources are hundreds of years later. I only have about fifteen pages and a semester. If you were me, which two or three of those types of questions matter most for someone trying to understand not just one case but the general problem?
