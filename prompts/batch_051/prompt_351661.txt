I keep running into the same fight in my undergraduate ethics seminar, so I'm outsourcing the tie-breaker. Here are eight small commitments, roughly the kind people make all week. For each one I want (a) a number from 0 to 10 for how morally binding it is, where 0 means "no obligation at all" and 10 means "about as binding as a signed contract," and (b) one sentence explaining the number. Then sort all eight into three tiers — heavy, middle, light — and finish with a short paragraph saying what one or two features do the most work in your sorting.

The cases:

1. Telling a coworker in a meeting "I'll get you comments on your draft by Thursday."
2. Texting an old friend "We should totally grab lunch sometime" and never following up.
3. RSVP-ing yes to a small dinner where the host buys and cooks an extra course for you.
4. Telling your six-year-old "We'll get ice cream this weekend."
5. Ticking a box saying you have read and agree to the terms of service without reading them.
6. Promising a neighbor you'll water his plants while he's away for a week.
7. Saying "I'll call you tonight" and then falling asleep without calling.
8. Telling the host of an open-house party "I should be there around seven, traffic permitting."

I've been pushing a distinction in class between commitments that let someone else rely on you (plan around you, buy food for you, leave their plants alone) and commitments that mostly manage expectations or register good intentions. I'm not sure the numbers will respect that line though; some cases might be binding because of the relationship, or because of how explicit the words are. So the real question: does the reliance/expectation distinction survive your ranking, and if it doesn't, what seperates the tiers instead?
