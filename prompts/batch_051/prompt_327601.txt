In recent arguments about whether a language model trained only on text about color could ever know what it is like to see red, people in my lab keep reaching for Jackson’s Mary as if the analogy is decisive: Mary before release is like the text-only model, Mary after release is like a model with multimodal sensors, and since Mary learns something new, a text-only model necessarily lacks phenomenal knowledge. I suspect the analogy is doing more work than its users acknowledge. In Epiphenomenal Qualia, Jackson is careful to specify that Mary has all the physical information there is about color vision and that she can work out, for example, what happens in the brain when someone sees a ripe tomato; the argument depends on the completeness of that information, not merely on her having read many accurate descriptions. Could you audit the Mary/text-only-model analogy? In particular: give me a correspondence table for what maps onto what (Mary’s room, the black-and-white books, her physical knowledge, her release, the experience of red, the new knowledge), then identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion. Does the conclusion that a text-only model cannot have phenomenal knowledge survive once we notice that the model lacks Mary’s completeness of physical information, has no body or perceptual system at all, and may not be a candidate subject of knowledge in Jackson’s sense? Or is the analogy carrying the whole inference, such that the more careful version—a model trained on all physical facts about color vision—would need to be assessed separately? I want a verdict on whether this comparison licenses anything specific about LLMs or merely restages Jackson’s original intuition in computational garb.
