i've been chewing on a view i'm calling recognition-weighted statehood. the constitutive/declarative debate seems stuck because both sides treat statehood as binary — either you are a state or you aren't. but i think statehood is graded: an entity is a state to the degree that its recognized by other states, and each act of recognition is weighted by the recognizer's own degree of statehood. so a breakaway region recognized only by a patron state and its satellites is less of a state than one recognized by several major powers, but both are partly states. formally, statehood is the fixed point of a mutual-recognition network — think PageRank for states — rather than a yes/no fact. this seems to dissolve the old fight: recognition is neither purely declarative (it adds weight) nor purely constitutive (it can't conjure a state out of nothing; you need some standing in the network already). i realize i might just be confusing legal personality with political influence, or reinventing something the field already has under a name i don't know. give me an honest assessment: is this coherent, or am i equivocating on 'state'? and where does it actually break — what's the objection that isn't just a re-assertion that legal status is binary?
