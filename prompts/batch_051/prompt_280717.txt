Consider a counterfactual version of twentieth-century physics. In our world, a recurring feature of modern science has been that mathematics developed for its own sake — conic sections, non-Euclidean geometry, group theory, Hilbert spaces — later turned out to be exactly the language the next physical theory needed. Eugene Wigner gave this phenomenon a name: the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics. The counterfactual world is identical to ours until 1925: the same classical physics, the same stock of pure mathematics, the same experimental techniques, the same universities and funding structures. The divergence appears only after 1925. In this world, the quantum domain — the phenomena of atomic spectra, chemical bonding, thermal properties of solids, and electrical conduction — turns out to be mathematically recalcitrant in the following precise sense. Any compact formalism powerful enough to unify those domains either reproduces already-known data while making no new correct predictions, or makes some correct predictions while failing elsewhere in ways that no local adjustment can repair. The empirical regularities themselves are real and stable: spectroscopists get reproducible numbers, and by mid-century engineers can build transistors and lasers using piecemeal tables, empirical rules, and domain-specific approximations. But no deeper set of equations sits beneath the patchwork, and formalisms borrowed from pure mathematics do not latch onto the new phenomena the way Hilbert spaces latched onto quantum mechanics in our world.

Predict the intellectual and institutional trajectory of physics, mathematics, and the philosophy of science in this world from 1925 to roughly 1975. Give a committed prediction rather than a survey of possibilities: say which developments you judge robust, which are speculative, and what each link in your argument depends on. Address at least the following. Does theoretical physics survive as a high-status discipline, and if so in what form? What happens to the goal of a unified theory? Does pure mathematics lose its special relationship to physics, and does that redirect what mathematicians work on? Does the culture of physics become more instrumentalist, more phenomenological, more engineering-oriented, or something else? Distinguish effects driven by the actual failure of mathematical unification from effects driven by the community's memory that mathematics had worked spectacularly well before 1925. End by stating at least two conditions under which your main prediction would be wrong, and which of those conditions you consider most plausible.
