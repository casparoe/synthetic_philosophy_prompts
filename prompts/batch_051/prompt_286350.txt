So I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether we literally see through photographs, in Walton's sense. For background: the old line is that photos are transparent, that we see the actual scene through them, because theyre produced by a purely optical/mechanical process, and the image depends counterfactually on the scene without anyone's beliefs about what was there getting in the way. Paintings are different because they are mediated by the painter's beliefs and intentions, so you don't see through them. My friend's move was that modern phone cameras kill this: an HDR photo is not one exposure, its a stack of exposures that the software aligns, merges, tone-maps, and denoises; portrait mode creates the blur synthetically; the eraser tool fills in pixels that were never on the sensor with whatever the model guesses should be there. So by the time you look at the picture, the chain from light to image contains all these little software decisions that look belief-ish, or at least not purely optical. His claim is the transparency thesis is dead, or at best it now applies only to raw sensor data that no one ever actually looks at. I am not convinced: an HDR merge is still anchored to a light field that really hit the sensor, and Ansel Adams was dodging and burning in the darkroom, so the line was always soft. Is Walton's argument actually under pressure here, or is this just a difference of degree that was already present in film? If it is under pressure, say which premise exactly breaks: the mechanical-registration premise, or something in what counts as "seeing"? And is there a repaired version that still draws a meaningful line between a typical photo and a typical painting, or does the distinction have to become a continuum? Also tell me what would change your mind.
