After I retired from a long career in ecology, I started sitting in on a philosophy-and-AI reading group at the public library. Last week two regulars, Roshan and Priya, got into a proper argument about whether you can infer what a person genuinely wants just by watching what they do. Neither of them works in machine learning; Roshan had been reading about inverse reinforcement learning—learning a reward function from observed behavior—and was enthusiastic, while Priya was deeply skeptical. Here's roughly how it went.

Roshan opened by saying behavior is the only window into anyone's preferences; there's no microscope for desires. Science infers unobservables from observable traces all the time, and inverse reinforcement learning is just that idea made precise: given someone's actions in an environment, find the reward function they're maximizing. If you're an honest naturalist about minds, that's the best evidence there is. Priya came back with the famous blind spot: for any observed behavior there are infinitely many reward functions that make that behavior come out optimal. An algorithm only returns one by smuggling in extra assumptions—simplicity, complexity penalties, maximum entropy, whatever. So the "preference" it reports is half data and half aesthetic choice. Its not a measurement, it's a modeling decision. Roshan answered that underdetermination doesn't mark a difference from ordinary science. We pick the simplest model that fits and test it against new data. If two reward functions explain what I did today but disagree about what I'll do tomorrow, you watch me tomorrow. That's exactly how we handled unobservables in my old field. Priya said the problem is deeper than curve-fitting. Whether an action counts as optimal depends on your model of the agent's beliefs and abilities, not just their desires. She didn't take her umbrella: does she prefer getting wet, disbelieve the forecast, couldn't reach the umbrella, or want the chill for some reason? Identical behavior, wildly different value systems. So the algorithm isn't reading values off behavior; it's computing them relative to a background story about what the person believes and can do. Roshan said that's a feature, not a bug—every inference from observation needs auxiliary models. The remedy is to make those models explicit and test them too. IRL isn't magic, but it's the only game in town if you refuse to take self-reports at face value, and we should refuse, because people rationalize after the fact just like everyone else. Priya replied that she wasn't defending self-report. Her worry is what happens to the output. If the reward function is a rationalization shaped by the modeler's choice of hypothesis space, and you then let a system optimize the world for that reward, the modeler's arbitrary choices get scaled up and treated as if they were the person's actual ends. That's not neutral inference—it's a machine for laundering a guess into an imperative.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly got lost in the underbrush—it felt like they were partly talking past each other but I couldn't say exactly where. Could you adjudicate it point by point? Say who is right about which specific point, where each move goes wrong or right, what both of them are missing, and who is more right overall. And please don't just lay out the options—commit to definite verdicts and give your reasons.
