Write a dialogue between two equally thoughtful interlocutors — call them Maren and Noor — who disagree about whether there is a duty to be legible to others. Maren holds that, at least in relationships and institutions where our actions affect other people, we owe them more than bare truthfulness: we have a pro tanto duty to make our reasons, commitments, and emotional states interpretable, because opacity lets us exercise power while evading answerability and shifts the entire burden of interpretation onto those we act upon. Noor rejects any general duty of legibility. She grants narrow duties — do not deceive, and disclose what another person has a right to know within a particular relationship — but she argues that being required to render oneself transparent on demand can be coercive, that it favors people whose inner lives already fit the available vocabulary, and that there is value in a self that is not yet fully articulated even to itself.

Early on, they should separate sincerity — not lying — from transparency — being interpretable — since the real disagreement concerns the second. Let their dispute turn on cases, not only principles. At some point they should take up: a colleague who refuses to explain a decision that will affect the whole group; a partner who answers "Why are you angry?" with "I don't know yet"; a hiring process that asks candidates to narrate their values; and the asymmetry between demanding transparency from a superior and demanding it from a subordinate.

Make it a genuine back-and-forth: each should engage the strongest version of the other's view, concede at least one point, and revise their position rather than hardening it. Keep the central disagreement alive — do not let them converge on a bland midpoint. The question to leave sharp is this: can the demand that someone make themselves legible ever be an obligation they owe, and if so, owed to whom, in virtue of what, and within what limits?
