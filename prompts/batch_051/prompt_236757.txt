I spent my working life in evolutionary biology—mostly population genetics and, later, systematics—and since retiring I have been reading the philosophy of culture and the study of oral traditions. A debate keeps surfacing there, and I would like it translated into the vocabulary I actually think in, which I suspect is both the right tool and a possible trap. One side of the debate holds that, for an oral tradition, a myth simply is the whole population of its tellings: a variant is not a departure from some deeper object, and talk of "the original" or "the canonical version" projects a print-culture habit onto traditions that never had it. The other side points out that some traditions do have authorized versions—ritual recensions, scriptural texts, a community's preferred telling—and some philosophers of art treat "which version is the work?" as a genuine, partly normative question. So the philosophical question I want to chew on is whether canonicity can be a real feature of some traditions, or whether it is always an imposed category.

Please translate that debate into the vocabulary of population biology and systematics: populations and standing variation; allele or haplotype frequencies; drift versus selection; type specimens and holotypes; the reference genome or consensus sequence; reticulation versus treelike descent; and the species problem. In particular, I want to see the claim that a myth is a population of variants rather than a Platonic type mapped onto population thinking; the "canonical version" mapped onto the type specimen, the consensus sequence, the modal phenotype, and the high-frequency haplotype (each will distort in its own way); the authority of ritual or editorial standardization mapped onto whatever biological process can bear the weight, if any; and disputes about which variant is "correct" mapped onto disputes about which level of variation is functional, adaptive, or merely historical accident. Where the mapping is clean, say so; where it can be made to work only by stretching, say how; where there is no counterpart, say that too.

Let me make this concrete. Assume I have notes from one valley containing eleven recorded versions of a flood story. One is used in an annual rite and is treated locally as the proper version. One is the earliest version written down by an outsider and is routinely called "the original" in the literature. One is the version most people can half-recite. The rest disagree about which ancestor survives, which animal gives the warning, and how the flood is sent. Run the translation on this example. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; I want your own reconstruction of the concepts. Act as a professional philosopher who knows both the ontology of cultural objects and philosophy of biology and who can mark partial successes honestly. Please think carefully and begin by brainstorming the candidate correspondences before committing to any of them; if a tempting mapping survives only with strain, keep it but flag it. I have a suspicion the biological vocabulary will carry the descriptive side almost perfectly and fail only on authority and obligation, and I want that suspicion tested rather than confirmed.

Please structure your answer in this order:

1. "The debate restated" — one to two paragraphs setting out the two positions and locating the real disagreement.
2. "The translation, correspondence by correspondence" — five to seven short subsections, one per mapping, each a paragraph.
3. "Residue report" — two to three paragraphs on what failed to translate, what had to be distorted, and what has no counterpart.
4. "What the residue tells us" — one to two paragraphs on whether the failures are a defect of the biological vocabulary, a sign that the original claims were less clear than they looked, or both.
5. "Bottom line" — one paragraph stating whether the biological vocabulary is worth using here, and for which half of the debate it is dangerous.
