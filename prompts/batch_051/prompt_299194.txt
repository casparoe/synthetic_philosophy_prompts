I don't think that response lands, because the reason safety work actually gets quietly defunded in real labs is competition for funding and market share, not the difficulty of the maths. What I'm missing is a clean example that isolates the incentive structure while holding the technical facts fixed, so the argument can't drift into a different disagreement. That's what I want from you.

So, acting as an expert in AI governance and political economy, could you think carefully and construct a thought experiment for me to these specifications? Maybe brainstorm two or three candidate mechanisms for the divergence first, then write up the strongest. Take a near-term AI development programme and run it twice side by side: once as a for-profit lab answerable to shareholders and locked in a race with rivals, once as an independently endowed non-profit with no market pressure, the same technical staff, the same starting point, and the same stated safety commitment. The whole point is to show whether switching the incentive structure and nothing else changes the outcome, so please don't just stipulate that the company behaves badly and the non-profit behaves well; the behaviour has to be generated by the incentives or the example proves nothing.
