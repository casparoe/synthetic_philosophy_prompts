I am a doctoral student in political philosophy, and in a recent reading group on school choice a colleague from economics presented the case for vouchers, charter expansion, and lighter regulation of private and home-based alternatives by insisting that education is, at bottom, a market. In his telling, parents are consumers who know something real about their own children's needs, schools are firms that live or die by enrolment, families vote with their feet, and competitive pressure—not curriculum mandates or certification requirements—is what reliably raises quality. The analogy was made to carry a strong conclusion: that choice is the principal engine of improvement and that most other policy instruments are second-order tinkering. I am not sure whether I resist this because it is wrong or because it is an economic picture that sits uneasily with the way I think about educational authority, and I would like to sort that out.

I would like you to audit the analogy rather than rehearse the usual policy arguments. Lay out the mapping in continuous prose: what plays the role of the consumer, the firm, the product, the price signal, the competitive mechanism, and the discipline of exit. Then identify the places where the mapping breaks, and distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that do not. In particular, weigh the fact that the chooser is not the principal beneficiary; the possibility that parents select on peer composition, convenience, or prestige rather than instructional quality; the informational asymmetry involved in judging a service as opaque as schooling; the extent to which education has public-good and sorting effects that make it unlike a rival, excludable consumer good; and the ways in which schools differ from firms, including non-profit missions, capacity constraints, selective admissions, and the absence of a genuine price mechanism where vouchers or direct public funding are involved. I want a verdict at the end on whether the conclusion—that choice alone reliably improves education—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was being carried by the market metaphor. Please write this as continuous prose, in the register of an email or a letter, and avoid headings, bullet points, bold text, and tables.
