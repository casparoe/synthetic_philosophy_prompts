I spent most of my career as a neurophysiologist, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mind and action more seriously than I ever had time to do while working. I have gradually settled into a set of views about the experiments associated with Benjamin Libet and about free will, but I suspect the set may not be fully coherent. I would like an audit of these commitments rather than a survey of the field.

Here are the commitments, numbered for reference only.

1. I am a compatibilist: I do not think determinism by itself rules out free action or moral responsibility.

2. I accept the central empirical finding of Libet's experiments as a first approximation: a readiness potential builds up in the brain several hundred milliseconds before subjects report the first moment of being aware of an urge to move. I am setting aside the usual worries about how accurately those moments can be reported.

3. I do not think the readiness potential threatens free will. I am persuaded that it should be understood as a stochastic accumulation process approaching a threshold, rather than as a completed unconscious decision that precedes and excludes conscious involvement.

4. By contrast, I take the later decoding studies very seriously: patterns of brain activity can predict the specific content of a forthcoming choice, for instance which hand a person will move, several seconds before the person reports having made the decision. If these findings are robust, I am inclined to say that the conscious act of choosing is not doing the real selecting, but is at best a late-running commentary on a choice already settled by prior brain processes.

5. I hold that an action can be free and properly attributable to an agent even when its initiating causes are brain processes and circumstances that precede the action and were never consciously chosen.

6. I hold that free action requires the agent to be the source of the action, where this means the action flows from stable features of the agent—values, settled preferences, character—rather than being the passive output of forces alien to the agent.

7. I hold that free will is worth caring about only if conscious deliberation between alternatives is at least sometimes causally relevant to which action occurs; if conscious choosing turned out to be wholly epiphenomenal, I would count that as a serious loss rather than a harmless redescription.

8. I find the free-won't data persuasive enough: even after the readiness potential has begun, a subject can consciously cancel the prepared movement. I take this to show that consciousness can exercise a real kind of top-down control over action, at least with respect to cancelling what has already been prepared.

I do not need references and I am not asking for a reading list. What I want is a strict consistency check. Which of (1) through (8) genuinely pull against one another, which pairs merely appear to conflict, and why? Order the genuine tensions by severity, and then say what the least costly coherent revision would be: which commitment would have to be given up or modified, and what I would have to accept if I kept the others.
