I am preparing a unit on the principle of indifference for an undergraduate course on probability and epistemology, and I have come across an argument, built out of Bertrand's paradox, that seems to refute the principle outright — yet I am unwilling to accept the refutation, because the principle seems to capture something indispensable about rational credence. The argument runs as follows.

Take a circle with an equilateral triangle inscribed in it, and ask for the probability that a chord drawn at random is longer than a side of the triangle.

(1) The principle of indifference says: among outcomes between which one's evidence makes no distinction, probability should be distributed equally.
(2) Apply it to the chord's endpoints. Fix one endpoint at a vertex of the triangle. The chord is longer than a side just in case the second endpoint lies on the arc between the other two vertices that does not contain the fixed vertex, which is one third of the circumference. So the probability is 1/3.
(3) Apply it instead to the chord's midpoint. The chord is longer than a side just in case its midpoint is closer to the center than half the radius. If, along any chosen radius, every distance from the center is on a par, the probability is 1/2.
(4) Apply it instead to the midpoint's position in the circle's interior. If every location of the midpoint is on a par, then the probability is the ratio of the inner circle's area (at half the radius) to the whole circle's area, namely 1/4.
(5) Steps (2)–(4) are each faithful applications of the same principle to the same question, so the principle yields no unique answer.
(6) Therefore the principle of indifference, as stated, is untenable — or at best salvageable only by a restriction we cannot yet state.

Where does this go wrong? I would rather reject (6) and keep the principle in some restricted form, but I need to know exactly which of (1)–(5) to deny and what the restricted principle will cost: specifically, whether the restriction can be stated without quietly building in the answer and without reducing the principle to vacuity.
