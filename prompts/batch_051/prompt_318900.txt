In an ethics and economics seminar a friend built an argument about moral compromise and now I can't find the bad step. He starts from the idea that compromises between value systems are just gains from trade: each side gives up a moral commitment it ranks relatively low, and gets something it ranks higher, so both sides are made better off by their own lights. That part seems fine. But he keeps going and ends up with the claim that there are no principled non-negotiables—every conviction has its price, and the only permissible reason to refuse a moral compromise is that the deal doesn't clear your current bar. I want to reject that conclusion, but every premise sounds plausible:

1. If you judge state S' to be more choice-worthy than state S, all things considered, by your own current values, then you are rationally required to move from S to S' whenever S is the status quo.
2. A moral compromise is a move from one state to another in which each party's moral commitments shift, and each party judges the new state more choice-worthy than the old, by their current values.
3. For almost any two value systems that aren't identical, some small compromise of this kind is available, because people rank commitments differently; each side can concede something it cares less about in exchange for something it cares more about. And with enough ingenuity, almost any commitment can be made the centerpiece of such a trade.
4. Rationally required choices compose over a sequence: if you are rationally required to take the first step, and then, from the resulting state, rationally required to take the next, then you are rationally required to end up where the whole sequence leads.
5. Given (3), for any commitment you currently hold, there is a long chain of individually rational small compromises, each looking like a good trade at the time by your current lights, that ends with your having abandoned that commitment.
6. Therefore, by (1), (4), and (5), you are rationally required to abandon any moral commitment whatsoever, if the right chain of individually attractive compromises is offered. Principled non-negotiability is just a refusal to look at the prices.

I'd rather give up (4) than (1), because (1) just looks like what it means to be rational, but rejecting (4) seems to mean rational requirements don't chain across time, which is also strange. My friend says the obvious way out is (3), but then he wants to know what principle could make moral rankings incomplete or lexical without smuggling in the very non-negotiables we're trying to explain. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost?
