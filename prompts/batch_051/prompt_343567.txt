I'm in a small reading group doing Otto Neurath and Isotype stuff, mostly around his picture language, and one person keeps invoking the motto "Words divide, pictures unite" to settle almost any argument. Last week we were arguing about whether you can design a pictogram-based system for, say, a public health clinic that has to communicate something like "you must not take this medication if you are pregnant, but you may take it if you are breast-feeding only after consulting a doctor." I said pictograms get really strained as soon as you need negation, tense, conditionals, or cross-references. He said that's just residual word-thinking, and the Neurath slogan makes the deeper point that pictures are a unified symbol system and words are the things that fragment us. I've also seen the slogan used on design blogs to justify everything from emoji to signage to data visualisations. But when I tried to pin him down on what exact claim he's making, he moved between different things: sometimes that pictures are more internationally legible than written languages, sometimes that pictures are less tied to class and education, sometimes that pictures are ontologically "closer" to perception, sometimes that a standardized picture system could eventually do everything a natural language can. I don't believe all of these are the same claim, and I don't think all of them are true.

Could you unpick "Words divide, pictures unite" the way you'd disambiguate a proverb: what are the different precise theses it could express, from the trivial to the radical? For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive defensible claim, or false, and in particular whether it would actually support using pictures for conditionals or negations in a health clinic. I'd like the response in these sections: "Possible readings" (about 400-500 words), "Plausibility and truth" (about 300 words), "What the slogan can and cannot support" (about 300 words), and "Bottom line" (about 150 words). And I'm fine if the answer is that the slogan is just a compact piece of rhetoric and falls apart when you make it precise.
