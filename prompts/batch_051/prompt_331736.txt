Write a dialogue between two Bayesian epistemologists who disagree about how a rational agent should update on a conditional probability, staged in the Judy Benjamin problem. The characters should agree on the following setup: a soldier is dropped into one of two mutually exclusive sectors, Red and Blue, each of which she regards as equally likely. If she is in Red, she is in either the Headquarters area or the First Company area, and her prior makes those equally likely conditional on being in Red. If she is in Blue, she is in the Second Company area. She then receives a radio message from a source she trusts completely, telling her that if she is in Red, the probability she is in the Headquarters area is now 3/4.

Have the two characters defend these rival positions.

The first character holds that the right way to accommodate the message is to move to the unique distribution that minimizes Kullback–Leibler divergence from the prior subject to the new conditional constraint: that is, the information is a constraint on the posterior, and the agent should make the least drastic global revision that satisfies it. This character should not flinch at the consequence that P(Red) changes — in the specified setup it falls to roughly 0.47 — and should explain why that movement is not an illicit import of information about Red versus Blue but a coherent response to the constraint.

The second character holds that the message is local: it revises the agent's probabilities inside the Red cell and says nothing that bears on whether she is in Red. On this view, rationality requires leaving P(Red) at 1/2 and acknowledges that the constraint underdetermines a unique posterior, so agents with the same prior and the same message may rationally disagree about matters the constraint does not fix. This character should press the objection that minimizing Kullback–Leibler divergence reads a particular geometry of probability space into a problem about evidence, and should own the cost that this position gives up uniqueness.

The dialogue must not be two alternating monologues. Both characters should be equally competent: each should grant the other's strongest points, answer the actual arguments given, and attempt at least once to restate the other's view in their own terms and be corrected. Have them investigate whether their dispute is substantive or merely verbal, and whether the real issue is about conservativeness in belief revision, about what it means for a message to bear on a partition cell, or about the status of the prior over Red and Blue. Include at least one edge case — for example, a nearly degenerate prior, a sequence of conditional messages, or a conditional message with a genuinely ambiguous answer — that strains one of the positions. Do not let either character convert cheaply; any convergence should be earned, and it is acceptable for the dialogue to end with a sharpened rather than resolved disagreement.
