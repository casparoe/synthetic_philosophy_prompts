In a reading group on the foundations of legal and moral rights, one participant kept pressing a card-game analogy. Rights, he insisted, work like a declared trump suit: once trumps are in force, a lowly trump beats any off-suit card, no matter how numerically strong, and the winner simply takes the trick. His point was that balancing a right against aggregate welfare — privacy against administrative efficiency, freedom of movement against epidemic control, religious observance against public order — is built on a kind of category mistake. You do not put a trump on one pan of a scale and ask how many thousand ordinary cards must pile up on the other side before the scale tips; a trump wins the trick outright, and the size of the losing card is beside the point. So he concluded that rights possess a categorical rather than a quantitative priority over collective goals, and that any theory treating rights as one consideration to be weighed in an all-things-considered calculation has already abandoned what makes a right a right.

I am drawn to the conclusion but suspicious of the image, and I would like to audit the analogy before either accepting or rejecting it. Please produce an explicit correspondence table: what, in the domain of rights, plays the role of the trump card, the off-suit card, the trick, the player, the dealer, the deck, and the score? Then mark where the mapping holds and where it strains. In particular, identify which features of the card game the analogy quietly imports — an agreed procedure for declaring the trump suit, players of equal standing, a zero-sum hand, discrete and repeatable rounds, a fixed set of cards, a neutral arbiter of the rules — and say which of those imports actually matter for the conclusion that balancing rights against collective welfare is misguided. Finally, tell me whether that conclusion survives once the serious disanalogies are on the table, and whether some other image — a side constraint, a shield, a default, or something else altogether — would do the same work more honestly than the trump card does.
