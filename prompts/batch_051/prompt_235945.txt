I am putting together a take-home assignment for an upper-level philosophy of science seminar, and I need a single case that forces students beyond the textbook caricature of falsification. Construct a thought experiment to the following specification.

The case should involve a Malthusian-style long-run prediction: a theory claims that a population–resource system is heading toward crisis on a generational timescale rather than within a few years. In the scenario, the theory has made at least one dated, near-term empirical prediction that fails on schedule, and its proponents respond by revising a supporting assumption while retaining the core long-run claim. Constraints: the near-term failure should be a concrete, measurable shortfall (for example, a fish stock that did not collapse by the predicted date, an aquifer that did not fall to the predicted level, or a pension system that did not become insolvent by the predicted year), not a vague mismatch. The revision must add a new, independently testable claim rather than redefining terms or shifting the goalposts. The scenario must remain realistic and be stated in one paragraph.

After presenting the scenario, do the following. (1) State what a naïve Popperian falsificationist would conclude at the moment of the failed near-term prediction, and explain whether that conclusion is forced. (2) In Lakatos's terminology, say whether the revised research programme constitutes a progressive or a degenerating problemshift, and defend that classification. (3) Explain how a defender of the theory could maintain that it was right but early without making it untestable. (4) Identify the single feature of the scenario that does the most work in making the rescue look legitimate rather than ad hoc, and say how the assessment would change if that feature were removed. (5) End with the strongest attack a committed falsificationist could make on the case, together with the concession they would still have to grant.
