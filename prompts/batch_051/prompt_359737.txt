My friends and I do a monthly movie-and-argument night, and next month we're doing Everything Everywhere All at Once, with me responsible for the philosophical angle. The thing I can't stop thinking about is how the movie lands in this old debate in metaphysics between monism and pluralism about what there is. From what I remember of undergrad: Parmenides thought reality is one single unchanging thing, so difference and change are illusions; Spinoza kept the oneness but made the single substance something like God-or-Nature, with ordinary things as modes of it rather than separate beings; and the contempory priority-monist view says there's one fundamental whole (the cosmos) and everything else depends on it, while pluralists deny there's any such underlying unity.

The film seems to dramatize exactly this. Jobu Tupaki, the alternate-universe version of Joy, has seen every timeline at once, and her response is the everything bagel: she says she piled literally everything onto one bagel until it collapsed into a kind of black void, because if all possible lives are already out there, no choice matters and the only move left is to let the bagel swallow every distinction. Evelyn ends up with the same everywhere-at-once vision, but she goes the other way: she keeps returning to particular people and moments, to being kind and paying attention to the one in front of you. So my question is whether the film is best read as a defense of pluralism against the monist temptation (the bagel as the monist's mistake), or whether there's a serious Spinozist or priority-monist reading available, where all the timelines really are modes of one underlying thing and the bagel is just Jobu's nihilistic, despairing snapshot of that oneness. I also can't tell whether the bagel is meant as metaphysics (there really is only one thing) or as a value thesis (there are many things but none of them matters), and I'd like the discussion to keep those apart. Could you give me a reading that's fair in both directions: where does the monist lens genuinely illuminate a scene or a choice, and where does the film resist it and an honest critic has to say so? I'm not after citations, just a sharp frame I can bring to the group, with maybe a name for what the bagel is if not the One.
