In the course of a long-running argument with a colleague about the is–ought gap, I have found myself defending several claims that I have never tried to put into a single view. I wrote them out below, and I would like a careful audit of whether they can actually all be held together before I say more in print.

1. There is a strict is–ought gap: no argument whose premises are all purely descriptive or non-normative can validly yield a genuinely normative conclusion, where that includes judgments about how someone or something ought to be, as well as judgments of good, bad, right, and wrong.

2. Moore's open question argument succeeds against analytic naturalism: 'good' cannot be definitionally reduced to any purely descriptive predicate. I take the naturalistic fallacy to be, in the first instance, this attempted definition of the normative in non-normative terms.

3. Nonetheless, I am not a non-naturalist. I hold that moral properties are natural properties, and that the sentence 'wrongness is identical with causing gratuitous harm' expresses a synthetic a posteriori identity claim in much the way 'water is H2O' does. I do not see this as a reversion to analytic naturalism or as a counterexample to the gap.

4. I accept that thick concepts such as 'cruel' and 'courageous' can underwrite inferences like 'the practice is cruel, so there is at least something wrong with it'. I regard this as consistent with the gap because the premise already carries normative content.

5. I find Searle's promise case persuasive: from a person's saying, in the appropriate conventional setting, that they promise to pay someone a specified sum, together with facts about the institution of promising, it follows that they ought to pay. I regard the premises of this derivation as descriptive institutional facts rather than disguised normative claims.

6. I am also drawn to natural normativity: facts about the characteristic functioning of a kind settle how its members ought to be, so a deer that habitually fails to flee from predators is a defective deer and, given its nature, ought to have fled. I am inclined to treat at least some of these natural 'oughts' as genuinely normative, though not necessarily moral.

Please identify which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension and which only appear to be. For each genuine tension, tell me how serious it is, what would have to give in order to resolve it, and what the cheapest repair costs. Then say whether, after the cheapest repairs, the remaining set is internally coherent and whether any further problems lurk in the background.
