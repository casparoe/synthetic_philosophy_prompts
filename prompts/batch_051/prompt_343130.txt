I have been thinking about a variant of the counterfactual mugging that adds an XOR blackmail component, and I would like a careful decision-theoretic analysis of it. In the familiar version, Omega flips a fair coin; if it lands tails, Omega asks you for $100, while if it lands heads, Omega pays you $10,000 provided it predicted that you would have paid in the tails branch. Modify the case as follows. The coin is biased, with heads probability p that you know. In the tails branch, before you decide whether to pay, Omega tells you its prediction about what you will do. In the heads branch, Omega pays the $10,000 if and only if it predicted that you would pay in the tails branch. Omega also announces an XOR blackmail condition: it has prepared a mildly damaging secret about you, and a third party will publish it if and only if exactly one of the two is true—you pay, or Omega predicted that you would pay—but not both. Since Omega's prediction is a perfect simulation of you in exactly this situation, your choice and the prediction are not independent.

Work through the possible policies or fixed points in the tails branch. Is there a unique rational policy once you are told the prediction, or do both matching policies remain available? How, if at all, does the known probability p affect the verdict? In particular, compare how causal, evidential, and functional decision theory would treat the modified case. Then say which of the standard arguments for paying in the original counterfactual mugging, if any, survives once the XOR blackmail condition is added; if a new reason to pay emerges, is it really a reason of the same kind, or is it just the blackmail condition doing the work?
