I am a novelist working on a collection of short stories, each of which is built around a single philosophical argument that I would like the reader to feel before they can name it. The argument I want the next story to carry is the scale argument about insect sentience, and I want the story to make its logic hard to shake rather than easy to summarize.

The argument runs as follows. For any individual insect—a moth, a leafhopper, a thrips—the probability that it has felt experience, any inner point of view that can suffer, seems very low; the nervous system is minute, and so much of the behavior looks automatic. But the probability is not zero. Meanwhile, the number of insects alive at any moment is almost impossible to take in: estimates run from the quadrillions into the quintillions, before even counting the turnover of short generations within a season. Multiply even a tiny chance of sentience by a number of that size, and the expected number of sentient individuals becomes enormous—far beyond the human population, beyond all the familiar animals. A person who takes expected suffering seriously can therefore arrive, by steps each of which seems sober, at the conclusion that the moral weight of the world may rest in its smallest and most ignorable lives. Yet the conclusion feels absurd when lived: a sprayed field becomes a catastrophe, a windshield becomes a scene of mass death, and some unglamorous devotion to aphids becomes, on the argument's own terms, not an eccentricity but a requirement.

I need a short story that dramatizes this argument without ever stating it outright. Do not write an essay, and do not let the characters lecture. The reasoning should arrive in the story as a discovery made by one specific person in one specific situation. The situation I keep returning to is someone whose work involves counting, handling, or killing insects at scale—a field ecologist sampling leafhopper densities, an agronomist running a pesticide trial, a technician at a facility that raises black soldier fly larvae for animal feed. But I am open to whatever setting serves the idea. What interests me is not the moment of conversion but its aftermath: what it is like to keep doing ordinary work, and to keep living an ordinary life, while believing what the arithmetic seems to show and finding no stable way to act on it. The prose should be exact about the small particulars—the nervous systems, the behavior, the numbers—but the exactness should serve the feeling, not replace it.

Please write the story. It should stand alone, roughly two thousand words, with a real human being at its center and the argument moving underneath everything else like a fault line.
