A moral psychologist of my acquaintance has spent years interviewing people after harmful acts and recording whether, in her judgment, each person knew that what they had done was wrong. She is explicit that merely being able to produce the words is not enough; several of her "no" verdicts concern people who said the right thing fluently. From her case notes, shared with permission, here are the verdicts I have seen.

Case 1. A man with acquired brain damage that has flattened his emotional responses took money from his brother. In the interview he said, "It was against the rules—wrong, sure." Asked why, he could say only that people get punished for that sort of thing and that you are not supposed to do it. Pressed on whether it would still be wrong if no punishment threatened, or whether it would be wrong in another society where nobody minded, he shrugged. She wrote: he does not know it was wrong; he has learned a rule and he repeats it.

Case 2. A seventeen-year-old who shoplifted and later confessed unprompted. She was plainly ashamed, said what she did was wrong, and explained that the shopkeeper had been harmed and that stealing would still have been wrong if she had never been caught and even if all her friends did it. She wrote: she knows.

Case 3. A man with a long record of fraud and no evident guilt insisted that nothing he did was morally wrong. Asked what "wrong" means, he answered that it is a word for the things that get you into trouble, and that he simply got careless. She wrote: he does not know that it was wrong, because he does not count it as wrong at all.

Case 4. A woman destroyed a relative's medication in a moment of spite and then spent the night arranging an emergency replacement. She called it wrong without prompting, described harming someone who trusted her, and said the wrongness did not depend on whether anyone found out. She wrote: she knows.

Case 5. An office manager had made a cruel remark and could produce a polished apology, but privately he explained that wrongness was entirely a matter of what the organization and society happened to have decided to punish, and that in a society where such remarks were accepted they would not be wrong; he showed no feeling about the person he hurt. She wrote: he does not know it was wrong, however fluent his words are.

Now she is about to assess a new person, and I am trying to predict her verdict.

The new person is a man in his thirties with a history of antisocial conduct and a diagnosis of antisocial personality disorder with psychopathic features. Unlike the fraudster, he fully acknowledges that what he did was wrong. On the moral/conventional battery she uses, he performs like a typical morally competent adult: he judges that hurting someone for amusement is wrong, that it would remain wrong if an authority permitted it and wrong in any society where the rule was absent, and he justifies the judgment by appeal to the victim's pain and to unfairness. Then he says: "Of course I know it was wrong—wrong means you damage someone, and I can see that as clearly as you can. It just doesn't weigh with me. I've never felt guilt, and other people's suffering is just a fact about the world to me."

I do not know which way she will go, and I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the rule she seems to be running. Please lay out the candidate principles that fit her prior verdicts, show what each principle would predict for this new person, identify the closest earlier case as a precedent, and say which verdict you think is more consistent with her record overall. Finally, specify the further case whose verdict would most decisively show which principle she is actually using. If her prior verdicts genuinely leave the matter open, say what principle each possible verdict would commit her to.

Please structure your answer in five titled sections, in this order, with roughly the lengths indicated: "Candidate rules" (200–250 words); "What each rule predicts for the new case" (150–200 words); "Closest precedent" (50–75 words); "Best prediction" (150–200 words); and "The decisive further case" (75–100 words).
