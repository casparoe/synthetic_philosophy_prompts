I keep running into the same puzzle with ways of scoring probabilistic forecasts, and I don't know if it has a name. Say someone has to report a probability for rain, and they get a score based on how close their number was to what actually happened (a 1 for rain, a 0 for no rain). If they know the exact scoring formula, they can often do better, in expectation, by reporting a number that isn't their honest estimate: someone who's 60% on rain might expect to score higher by saying 80%, or even 100%, depending on how errors are punished. I've noticed the same thing with students reporting confidence on multiple-choice tests—once they know the grading formula, honest confidence isn't always the best strategy. Yet some scoring formulas don't have this problem, and they all seem to share a strange property: your expected score is maximized exactly when you report your real degrees of belief. Is there a name for that property, and is the no-gaming feature the same thing as the truthfulness-maximizing feature, or are they two seperate conditions that happen to coincide? Also, if a formula is almost non-gameable but not quite, does it still measure accuracy in any useful sense?
