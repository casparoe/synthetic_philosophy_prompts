I keep noticing that people use the word "lying" with complete confidence while meaning quite different things by it, and I want a working definition that survives contact with the cases below. A few of them are easy for me: (1) and (7) are clearly not lies, and (2) clearly is. I lean toward counting (5) as a lie even though no one is deceived, and I have no settled view about (6), (8), and (9). I expect (3) and (4) to come out as "misleading but not lying," though I am not sure that distinction should carry much moral weight.

Here are the cases:

(1) Honest error. Anna tells a colleague that the meeting is at noon. She believes this and has good reason to, but the meeting was moved to 1:00 after she last checked. The colleague misses the meeting.

(2) Straightforward lie. Anna knows the meeting is at 1:00 and tells the colleague it is at noon because she wants him to miss it. He believes her and misses it.

(3) Misleading true statement. Asked whether he saw the dentist this afternoon, Ben says, "I had a 3:00 appointment and I was there on time." Both clauses are true, but he left before being called and never saw the dentist; he knows the questioner will infer that the checkup happened.

(4) Omission. Clara is selling her car in January. She knows the transmission slips badly in sub-freezing weather and says nothing about it. The buyer does not ask and discovers the problem a week later.

(5) Bald-faced lie. At a meeting in front of colleagues who all watched him take the last pastry, Daniel says, "I didn't take the last pastry." No one believes him, he knows no one believes him, and his aim is to signal that he does not care what they think.

(6) False promise. Elena borrows money and says, "I will repay you next month." At the moment she says it, she has already arranged to leave the country and has no intention of repaying.

(7) Joke. During a downpour, Frank says, "Lovely weather we're having," with obvious irony, and no one takes it as a genuine report about the weather.

(8) Truthful dodge. In a salary negotiation, Grace is asked whether she has a competing offer. She answers, "I was offered $95,000 by another firm last month." That is true, but the offer was withdrawn yesterday, and she knows the interviewer will infer that the offer is still available.

(9) Accidental truth. Intending to mislead, Helen says, "John is in the office this morning." She believes John is at home; in fact he is in the office. The hearer goes to the office and finds him there.

What I am asking for is a definition that tells me, for each of those nine cases, whether the speaker lied, and in the misleading cases whether the speaker misled. Please don't settle for the observation that ordinary usage is loose. Make the definition do the work: state the conditions explicitly, run every case through them, and where the definition gives a verdict that clashes with my stated judgments or with your own pretheoretical ones, either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict.

Then take up the moral question. Everyday moral thought tends to treat the outright lie in (2) as worse than the technically true statements in (3) and (8), with the silence in (4) falling somewhere in between. Does your definitional line place the moral difference where it belongs? Which of the nine cases end up on either side of the lying/misleading divide, and does that division earn its keep morally? If your definition makes the distinction look arbitrary, or if you think the moral difference and the definitional line come apart, say so rather than forcing them into alignment.

One further request: if the task is badly posed — perhaps because "lying" picks out two different concepts and my cases mix them, or because the definitional question and the moral question are more independent than I have assumed, or because any case is too underdescribed to decide — tell me that plainly, alongside your best attempt rather than instead of it. I would rather have an honest partial definition than a neat one that quietly ignores the hard cases.

Organize your answer under these headings, in this order and at roughly these lengths:

(1) Bottom line — one short paragraph: the definition you end up with and your answer to the moral question.
(2) Proposed definitions — two or three paragraphs, setting out the conditions on lying (and, where needed, misleading) and flagging the choices you are making.
(3) Application to the cases — one short paragraph or table row per case, covering all nine.
(4) Revisions and defenses — one or two paragraphs on where your first pass gave the wrong answer or no answer, and how you revised or defended it.
(5) The moral line — two or three paragraphs on whether the lying/misleading distinction tracks the definitional line and whether the moral difference survives being drawn that way.
