I am preparing an essay for a political theory seminar on the old dispute about whether commercial society improves or degrades human character. The optimist tradition often associated with Montesquieu holds that trade softens manners, accustoms people to seeing their interests as bound up with the interests of others, and discourages cruelty and zero-sum conflict. The pessimist tradition is harder for me to assess fairly. I have been working through Book V of Adam Smith's *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations*, where he considers the effects of the division of labour on the labouring poor. As I understand Smith's argument, someone whose entire working life consists of one or two simple, repetitive operations has little occasion to exercise understanding, invention, or judgement. The damage is not merely intellectual: Smith thinks the worker loses the ability to take part in serious conversation and to form sound judgements about ordinary public and private duties, so that a certain kind of character is actively undone. He contrasts this with the mental agility of a person in a simpler economy, who must constantly adapt to varied tasks. He does not deny the material gains from commercial society, but he suggests those gains are purchased at a real civic and moral cost for the mass of people.

I would like the essay to use this Smith argument as its test case. Specifically, please address the following. First, is Smith's mechanism best read as a claim about human nature generally, or only as a diagnosis of early industrial conditions that later institutional reforms could mitigate? Second, can the "doux commerce" tradition answer Smith on his own terms, by arguing that markets also cultivate patience, prudence, cooperativeness, and self-control, or does it merely change the subject from character to peace and prosperity? Third, once we distinguish different aspects of character—for example, honesty in dealings, independence of judgement, public spirit, curiosity, and sympathy—what is the most defensible overall verdict on whether markets corrupt or civilise? Do not end by saying that both sides are partly right. Defend a definite conclusion, and make sure you engage the strongest version of the opposing view. Do not write a summary of Smith scholarship; engage with the problem directly. Please write in British English. End the essay with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase summarising your conclusion.
