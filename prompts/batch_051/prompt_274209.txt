One of my former students now works as a welfare scientist, designing protocols for the assessment of pain and distress in animals that cannot self-report. Her professional vocabulary is thoroughly operational: she works with nociception, behavioral indicators such as vocalization, withdrawal, and grimace scales, motivational testing and conditioned place aversion as ways of inferring whether an animal is in a state it will work to escape, validation of welfare metrics against physiological correlates, and precautionary decision rules for cases in which measurement runs out. She recently told me that when she reads arguments about the moral status of digital minds, she cannot tell whether the participants are making claims her discipline could in principle investigate or doing something else under borrowed biological language. I promised her a careful answer, and I would like you to supply the analysis behind it.

Take the main positions in the contemporary debate over the moral status of digital minds: roughly, the functionalist or substrate-independent view that what matters is the relevant kind of information processing rather than what implements it; the biological or embodied view that morally significant subjective states require the kind of living, evolved body that animals have; and the precautionary view that uncertainty between the first two positions itself licenses substantial moral caution toward sufficiently complex digital systems. Restate each position as faithfully as you can in her operational vocabulary, translating its central claim into claims about observable indicators, inferred internal states, validation, and decision rules under uncertainty, without smuggling in the assumptions of either framework and calling that a translation. Then give me the residue report: which claims carry over cleanly, which can be expressed only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in her framework at all.

Finally, assess the significance of that residue. Does a failure of translation reveal a limitation of welfare-science methods when they are applied beyond their home domain of living animals, or does it expose a confidence in the digital-minds debate about consciousness, suffering, and interests that its arguments have not earned? I am less interested in a verdict than in a precise map of what survives the translation and what each failure of translation reveals about the two frameworks.
