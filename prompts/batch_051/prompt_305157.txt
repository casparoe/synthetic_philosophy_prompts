I run the policy team for a platform that hosts on the order of thirty thousand semi-autonomous user communities. Roughly once a week we decide what to do about an unwritten rule inside one of those communities: codify it as an enforceable platform rule, leave it alone, build product affordances around it, or actively try to shift it. Each decision can affect hundreds of thousands of active members and thousands of volunteer moderators, it has to be signed off by a product-policy lead who carries the fallout when a community disintegrates, and we can spend at most a few days per case on log data, surveys, and interviews. The analysts who would run the procedure are policy generalists with no formal training in game theory or social philosophy. I am asking for a reusable decision procedure, not one-off advice.

The procedure should be grounded in a Lewis-style account of convention, the standard distinction between conventions and social norms, and the idea that these states can evolve into one another. Take the working account to be this. A convention is a regular pattern of behavior in a recurring situation that is one coordination equilibrium among several workable alternatives: participants mostly conform, expect one another to conform, prefer to conform given that others do, and this is all common knowledge. A social norm is instead a pattern held in place by normative expectations and sanctions: people conform because deviation would be disapproved of or punished, not merely because conformity is the obvious way to coordinate. A convention can acquire normative expectations and sanctions over time and harden into a norm, and a norm whose sanctions lapse can decay into a dead letter or a merely descriptive regularity. If the procedure needs another category, such as an imposed regularity that is really the moderators' or the platform's doing, add it rather than forcing the taxonomy. You may refine or complicate the working account where the procedure requires it, but say plainly where and why you are departing.

Structure the deliverable as a checklist or word-flowchart we could print and fill in, and give me, in order:

1. The questions or tests to run on any proposed unwritten rule—for example, whether the behavior is actually regular, whether people are conforming because they expect others to or because they fear sanction, whether a genuine alternative rule would have worked, and whether the mutual expectations are common knowledge. For each question, say what evidence would count as a satisfactory answer, what to do when we cannot obtain that evidence, and which verdict the answer licenses.

2. The terminal verdicts the procedure can return and what each implies for intervention. These should at least include: treat it as a convention and avoid enforcement; treat it as a sanction-backed norm and either enforce it deliberately or consciously allow it to unravel; treat it as a transient regularity or fad; treat it as an imposed regularity that is our own or the moderators' doing.

3. The points at which the procedure stops doing factual classification and hands the decision to someone making a product or values judgment, and which role should own that call.

4. A candid note on where the procedure will misfire, especially when self-reported expectations are unreliable or when observers mistake quiet moderator enforcement for spontaneous user behavior, and a paragraph on what it cannot settle, such as patterns that are in transition or genuinely contested between two stable interpretations.

I want the executable procedure and its rationale in plain language; please do not spend the answer surveying the literature or supplying references.

Then illustrate the procedure on three cases, showing for each the evidence you would collect, the path through the questions, the terminal verdict, and the intervention that follows:

(a) A support forum has an unwritten practice that top-level replies open with a bracketed tag such as [SOLVED]. Nobody is punished for omitting the tag, several alternative tagging schemes would have worked about as well, and a solid majority of users can state the practice without prompting.

(b) A fiction-discussion community has an unwritten rule that spoilers must be flagged. Compliance is sustained mostly by public shaming and downvoting of violators, and most members say they would want the spoiler rule even if they were certain never to encounter a spoiler themselves.

(c) A gaming forum has an unwritten rule that gameplay clips go in a weekly megathread rather than as standalone posts. Long-time members describe it as simply how things are done, and our team has been treating it as an organic community norm we would like to stop moderating; but the logs show moderators have quietly removed thousands of standalone clips over several years.

Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer we will not like, and say explicitly which of our instincts it overrides and why.
