Okay, here's one I'd like you to try. In anthropic reasoning there's a split between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption. Very roughly, SSA says you should reason as if you were a random sample from all observers in your reference class, while SIA says the fact that you exist at all should lead you to favor hypotheses on which their are more observers. The standard objection to SIA goes by the name of the presumptuous philosopher: T1 and T2 make all the same local predictions, but T1 says the universe contains a trillion observers while T2 says it contains a billion. SIA then says you should be vastly more confident in T1, purely because there'd be more people for you to have been. That feels, well, presumptuous.

Write a short story in which a character has to actually live with this choice. Don't do an essay, and don't just have a narrator explain the terms. Maybe someone bets real money on a theory's observer count, maybe a civilization has to bet its future, maybe someone keeps waking up in populations of different sizes and has to figure out where they are. The story should make the intuitive pull of each assumption feel real, and the ending should hinge on which one the character goes with. Keep it under about 1,200 words.
