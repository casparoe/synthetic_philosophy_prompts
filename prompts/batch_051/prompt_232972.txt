Recently I inherited a box of my late mother's letters, written in her first language, and I have started translating them into English for my siblings and my children. The work has forced on me a question I used to think had an obvious answer: what makes a translation faithful? I find I have strong verdicts about particular cases, but I cannot yet see the principle behind them, and I would like you to find one.

In one letter she describes, with a single verb, the way two people sit together in silence after an argument until the anger drains away. English has no such verb. I left the word in the original and added a gloss in brackets. I am confident that was right: to translate it as "we made up" or "we sat quietly" would have been accurate in a flat-footed way and false in the way that mattered.

In another letter she makes a pun of the gentle, groaning kind, on a word that also happens to be a neighbor's name. The English equivalents share nothing. I replaced it with an English pun of the same kind, different content and all. I think that was not only acceptable but the only faithful move; a footnote explaining the original pun would have been ponderous for a letter.

Where she uses an idiom that pictures a pointless errand as carrying some surplus thing to a place already full of it, I translated it by the familiar English idiom about carrying coals to Newcastle. A literal version would have read as a mistake rather than an idiom, so I take the substitution to be more faithful, not less.

In the hardest letter she writes about a quarrel between her older and younger sister, and the story turns on the older sister's authority. Her language marks older sister and younger sister with two single words; English does not. I wrote "older sister" and "younger sister" every time, even where it made the English clumsy, because losing the distinction would have made the story unintelligible. I am sure that was right.

In a letter forgiving an estranged brother, she begins in the formal mode of address and, at exactly the sentence where she extends forgiveness, switches to the intimate mode. English cannot mark the switch in its pronouns, so I wrote "you" throughout and inserted one sentence of narration saying that she switched to the familiar form. I think the switch is the point of the letter and had to be marked somehow.

In a few letters written by her sister, who had little schooling, the sentences are fragments and the spelling wanders. I have been correcting the grammar so my children do not read her as stupid or comic, but the decision makes me uneasy. In most of the other cases I refused to smooth away the source's strangeness, and here I am smoothing away a person. I still do not know whether that was right.

I also notice that I treated two untranslatable single words in opposite ways, and I cannot say whether the difference holds up. The first is the verb for the post-argument silence, which I left in the original with a gloss. The second is a noun for the Thursday-evening family meal with candles and songs that I remember from childhood; English has no word for it, and I translated it by the descriptive phrase "the Thursday-night supper with candles and songs." The phrase is accurate, but it loses the meal's status as a named event in our family calendar. Leaving the original word in place would have felt exoticizing to me. Those two cases feel as though they should be decided by the same rule, and I came down on opposite sides.

There is also a pet name she used for me, formed from my own name plus a diminutive suffix. I have not settled what to do with it: leave it in the original, translate it as "little one" or "sweetheart," or coin an English diminutive of my name.

The rule I keep reaching for is something like this: a faithful translation preserves what the original is doing, even at the cost of its surface. But that rule seems to fit the pun and the idiom better than it fits the glossed verb or the shifting forms of address, and it sits awkwardly with my unease about correcting the grammar.

Is there a single principle that captures all of these verdicts, or nearly all of them, and which of my verdicts does it force me to abandon? Please state the principle precisely, say which cases it explains and which it cannot accommodate, and say whether it is ultimately a principle of meaning, of ethics, or both. Then apply it to two further matters: the pet name, and the question of whether untranslatability is a property of particular words or rather of what those words are doing in a particular text.
