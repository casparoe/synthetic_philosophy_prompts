Consider the position that the Münchhausen trilemma is exhaustive: any attempt at an ultimate rational justification of a claim must terminate in exactly one of three unsatisfactory ways — an infinite regress of reasons, a circular justification, or a dogmatic stopping point. I hold this position, and I want to know what would count against it. Give me a map of its exposure rather than a rebuttal. What would have to be true for the trilemma to be false, or for its force to be limited in ways its usual statement obscures? Identify the assumptions doing the work — for instance, that justification flows only along linear chains of reasons, that circularity is always vicious, and that a regress must be completed rather than merely continuable — and say what the landscape looks like if each is relaxed. Distinguish escape routes that are live from ones that are merely logically possible, tie each to concrete epistemic practices such as axioms in mathematics, perceptual entitlement, testimony, or regress-ending conventions, and say what the position rules out that its rivals can permit. Include what defenders of the trilemma would themselves concede counts against it, and sort the considerations into those that would be decisive and those that would merely be awkward. Finish with an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that should trouble a view of this kind. I would personally find most persuasive a worked case in which a stopping point is principled rather than arbitrary, or a circularity that is not vicious; tell me whether that is even the right target.
