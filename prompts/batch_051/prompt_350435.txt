You are a professional philosopher specialising in research ethics and the philosophy of medicine, and you have been asked to help set this year's postgraduate seminar examination in the ethics of randomised controlled trials. Please think through the problem from several angles before committing to a final wording, and write in British English. The aim is to separate students who can genuinely reason about decisions under uncertainty from those who have only memorised the vocabulary.

Draft one essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme. The question should be answerable in 90 minutes, and the model answer should be about 600–700 words.

The topic is clinical equipoise, but I want the question built around a crux that standard summaries leave unresolved: response-adaptive randomisation, by which I mean a trial design in which the allocation ratio is deliberately shifted mid-trial so that more participants are assigned to whichever arm the accumulating outcome data currently favour. The question should not ask whether randomisation in general is ethically defensible; it should ask whether a trial can remain consistent with clinical equipoise once the trial's own machinery begins acting on interim evidence. A weak answer will simply recite that equipoise is genuine uncertainty within the expert community; a strong answer will have to say what kind of uncertainty is required, what it is uncertainty about, and how a trial's design can preserve or erode it.

Write the question so that a full-credit answer must do all of the following:

- distinguish individual equipoise from community equipoise, and explain why the individual version was judged too fragile to do the ethical work;
- confront the apparent dilemma that response-adaptive randomisation either acts on interim evidence that is reliable enough to disturb equipoise, or acts on evidence too weak to justify tilting allocation;
- separate the decision to allocate participants from the decision to stop the trial, and assess whether that separation is principled or merely motivated reasoning;
- address the position of an individual participant assigned to the currently disfavoured arm, and consider whether the duty of personal care is better served by a higher probability of receiving the better-looking treatment or by something else;
- reach a reasoned verdict on the conditions, if any, under which response-adaptive randomisation is compatible with clinical equipoise, attending to pre-specified rules, stopping boundaries, and independent oversight.

Then provide:

(1) the examination question exactly as students would see it;
(2) a model answer of about 600–700 words that would earn full marks, written in the register of a strong postgraduate student rather than a textbook summary;
(3) a marking scheme with five must-hit points, the marks each is worth out of a total of 20, and for each point a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates get it wrong.
