Act as someone who works on the philosophy of climate economics and has to explain it to people who actually run the models. I run a workshop for PhD students in earth systems science. They can solve for optimal emissions paths, but they treat the discount rate as a plug number, usually 3%, and when you ask why they say "opportunity cost" or "people prefer now" and don't see the difference. I have one 90-minute session, and I want a teaching plan whose entire job is to seperate pure time preference from every other reason a future cost might count for less. Not a lecture on the economics (they already have that), but a session that gets them to see the leftover question: after you've stripped out risk, opportunity cost, growth, and uncertainty about who will exist, does a later benefit still count for less just because its later? And that this leftover is a normative stance you can't read off a market interest rate.

Start by brainstorming the dependency structure of the concepts, then lay out the arc. What idea has to be in place first (I'd guess the split between descriptive impatience and normative discounting, but you tell me), the one carrying example and what it has to hold fixed so it doesn't smuggle in risk or growth, the objections in the order they'll come up — 'the future might not happen', 'they'll be richer than us', 'if you don't discount the integral blows up', 'but people do discount, we measure it' — and where in the sequence to head each one off, and a final check that tells me they've got the distinction rather than just gotten quiet. Also say what I should deliberately leave out for this audience and where to say that out loud.
