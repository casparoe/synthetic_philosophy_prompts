For months our alignment discussion group has been stuck on one question: whose values should a consequential AI actually serve? One side says the system should maximize aggregate well-being across everyone, accepting that one person's large loss can be outweighed by many small gains, because any defensible decision procedure for a system with this much power has to be some kind of social welfare function. The other side says that is exactly what a legitimate AI must not do: it would force individuals to accept a single ranking of what makes lives go well, overriding each person's own conception of the good, so the system should instead be a neutral referee that protects each person's ability to pursue their own ends and only intervenes on harms everyone can already accept. Both sides agree that the AI should serve human values, that values conflict, and that the facts about human preferences are not the sticking point. The genuinely frozen question is whether aggregating across persons is a permissible way to resolve that conflict or a kind of quiet evaluative imperialism. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about facts, and not merely about first principles, but about what kind of problem conflicting values pose: one side sees a social-choice problem, the other a legitimacy problem. Diagnose this impasse: is my hypothesis right, and what would it imply about whether the disagreement is verbal or substantive, and about what would count as progress? Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
