One of the classic disputes in twentieth-century neuroscience is the so-called soup-versus-sparks controversy over synaptic transmission. On one side, physiologists and pharmacologists, most prominently Henry Dale and Otto Loewi, argued that signaling across the junction between a nerve and its target cell is chemical: arrival of the nerve impulse releases a substance, such as acetylcholine, that diffuses across the narrow gap and alters the target. Their evidence included Loewi's frog-heart preparation, in which fluid collected from a stimulated donor heart slowed a second, unstimulated heart, and the close parallel between the effects of acetylcholine and the effects of specific nerve stimulation, including their common susceptibility to blocking and potentiating drugs. On the other side, electrophysiologists such as John Eccles argued that transmission was electrical: they measured synaptic delays so short that they seemed to rule out a chemical sequence of release, diffusion, and receptor binding, and they proposed instead that the action current spread directly from the presynaptic terminal to the postsynaptic cell. The controversy persisted for decades and shifted toward the chemical account only after intracellular microelectrode recording allowed the synaptic event to be characterized against the quantitative predictions of the chemical story, after which Eccles himself changed sides.

I want to test a philosophical theory of explanation against this episode. The theory is the entities-and-activities account of mechanistic explanation associated with Peter Machamer, Lindley Darden, and Carl Craver. On this view, a phenomenon is explained by specifying the mechanism that produces it: the relevant entities, the activities those entities perform, and the spatial, temporal, and causal organization connecting them, so that each stage gives rise to the next in a chain of productive continuity. Laws are not required, and explanations may be offered as mechanism sketches that leave gaps and are later filled into fuller schemas.

Analyze the soup-versus-sparks controversy through this lens, but do not force the fit. In particular:

(a) Reconstruct, for each camp, the mechanism it defended, in terms of its entities, activities, organization, and the gaps in productive continuity it left open. Say to what extent the two sides genuinely disagreed about the mechanism itself, as opposed to disagreeing about which gaps in productive continuity were acceptable while the empirical record was incomplete.

(b) Identify what the entities-and-activities account explains well about this episode and what it leaves out. Consider how the competing sides held rival mechanism sketches, how new recording technology changed what counted as a plausible sequence of activities, and what norms governed when a gap could be left open versus when it had to be filled before a claim was taken seriously.

(c) Then run the lesson in the other direction. Does the episode show a mechanism sketch doing genuine explanatory work before its productive continuity was filled in, or does it suggest that the label "mechanism" was largely affixed after the fact, once the missing links had already been identified? What does that show about the strengths and limits of the philosophical account?

Finally, throughout the analysis, separate what is settled about this history and about the entities-and-activities account from what remains contested among historians of neuroscience and philosophers of explanation, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
