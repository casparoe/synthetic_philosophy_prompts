My research supervisor studies the boundary between productive self-examination and rumination, and over the past year she has recorded a long set of verdicts about which practices are beneficial and which shade into harmful self-focus. The practices she has classified as beneficial include: a five-minute evening journal entry answering two fixed questions, one about what worked that day and one about what to change tomorrow; narrating a recent argument in the third person during a walk while describing both parties' likely feelings; completing a structured thought record that separates an automatic thought, the evidence for and against it, and a more balanced alternative; and reviewing a week of mood ratings in order to adjust sleep, exercise, and social plans. The practices she has classified as harmful include: nightly replaying of a tense conversation for two weeks to determine what a colleague really meant; rereading all texts from a new friend in order to decide whether that person is genuinely fond of you; trying to reconstruct the precise hidden origin of a low mood by probing deeper whenever an explanation feels incomplete; and ruminating without a time limit on whether a past failure reveals a deep and stable character flaw.

Next week she is considering a new case: someone who spends exactly ten minutes each morning free-writing about why they feel anxious, with a standing instruction that every explanation must be traced back to its earliest childhood source, and who never rereads the pages or acts on anything they have written. Predict whether she will classify this morning free-writing as beneficial or harmful. More importantly, work out the competing rules that fit her existing verdicts, say what each rule predicts about the new case, identify which rule is most plausibly hers and why, and specify one further case that would cleanly settle which rule she is running.
