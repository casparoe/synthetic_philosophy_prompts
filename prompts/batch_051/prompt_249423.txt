In a philosophy of language course I am helping to teach, a student has been defending a deflationary view of generics. His line is that a generic such as "ducks lay eggs" is simply a compressed statistical generalisation, roughly equivalent to "most ducks lay eggs" or "in the normal course of things, ducks lay eggs", and that generics therefore require no special semantics: they are just statistical claims with the quantifier left vague. He uses this to argue that generics are not philosophically interesting and that the whole topic is a fuss about loose talk. Another student has objected that this cannot be right, since "mosquitoes carry malaria" is an acceptable generic even though only a very small minority of mosquitoes ever transmit the parasite, and since "most ducks lay eggs" is straightforwardly false once one counts male ducks and ducklings. The disagreement has now landed on my desk, and I want to referee it properly rather than by intuition.

I would like a careful audit of the analogy between generics and statistical generalisations. Please do the following: (i) state the correspondence the deflationary view needs, item by item, between what a statistical claim says and what a generic is supposed to say (a table of correspondences is fine); (ii) identify where the mapping breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that are genuinely damaging to the deflationary conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic; (iii) say what the statistical picture quietly imports that the target lacks — for instance, assumptions about prevalence thresholds, about counting over individuals rather than kinds, sexes, or life stages, and about how exceptions are tolerated; and (iv) give a verdict on whether the deflationary conclusion survives once these disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was being carried by the analogy the whole time. If you think a different analogy would do better, say what it is and what it captures. Please write your answer in British English.
