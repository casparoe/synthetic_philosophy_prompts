I've been reading Hegel's Philosophy of Right and keep getting stuck on one passage, in the remark to section 324. He writes: "War is the state of affairs which deals in earnest with the vanity of temporal goods and concerns - a vanity at other times a common theme of edifying sermonising. This is what makes it the moment in which the ideality of the particular attains its right and is actualised." And a bit earlier in the same remark he says war "is not to be regarded as an absolute evil."

The argument, as far as I can make it out, is that war is doing something in the life of a state that nothing else does: it makes people actually confront the fact that property and biological life are not their highest loyalty, and that a political community which no longer has any occasion to demand that confrontation decays into something merely private and comfort-seeking. I find this genuinely compelling as a diagnosis. People do seem to discover, under real danger, that they value something more than their own survival, and a society that never asks anything hard of its members does go shapeless and self-absorbed in a specific way. So I'm attracted to the theory's structure — it explains sacrifice, civic cohesion, and why certain values have to be acted out rather than merely believed.

But I also cant get past the intuition that this is exactly what a war apology looks like. Saying that war "deals in earnest with the vanity of temporal goods" sounds like a philosophical way of beautifying mass death, and a view which refuses to call war an absolute evil is not just mistaken but backwards — it turns the worst thing into a required ingredient of ethical health. I don't want to be told that Hegel is "really" right or that he's a warmonger and we can all relax. I want to know which of the two should yield.

Specifically: (1) What would it take for my intuition to be the thing that goes — for me to accept that war, or at least its standing possibility, has an ethical moment that can't be replaced by sport or risk or civic ritual — and why would that be the right concession? (2) What would it take for Hegel's claim to be amended or abandoned at this point instead, and what amendment would preserve his diagnosis of stagnation while refusing his talk of war's "higher significance"? (3) Or is there a reading of "ethical moment" and "not an absolute evil" on which the intuition and the theory turn out compatible — for instance, war reveals what people value without itself being valuable? Give me the strongest version of each of those three moves, and then tell me which you'd make and why.
