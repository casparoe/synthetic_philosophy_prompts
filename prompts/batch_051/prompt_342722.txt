I am giving a paper next month at a theology-and-science seminar, and I want to be ready for the questions rather than merely survive them. The thesis I am defending is that the standard evolutionary picture — deep time, animal predation, disease, and extinction long before any human being existed — is compatible with the doctrine of a good creation, provided one treats the Fall as an event in the human relation to God rather than a change in the biological order. My argument, in brief, is this: the goodness ascribed to creation is a teleological and covenantal judgment about the whole created order and its direction, not a claim about the subjective welfare of each creature at each moment; animal mortality and predation are structural features of finite creatures, not moral evil; and the death that Scripture links to sin is best read as covenant death, the rupture of the human relationship with God, rather than the first appearance of biological death anywhere in nature. I spent last evening arguing about this with a friend, and he kept insisting that my position makes God either indifferent to creaturely suffering that preceded any sin or powerless to create a world without it, which he said is precisely what the doctrine of a good creation was supposed to exclude. That conversation convinced me that I know my own argument but not the room I will be facing.

The audience is precise and not uniformly hostile. Two systematic theologians in the seminar hold what they call a strong doctrine of original goodness: they think the created order was originally free of predation and death, and that the Fall had physical and cosmic consequences rather than merely relational ones, so they will hear my paper as a retreat on sin. One philosopher of biology will press on whether I am smuggling moral categories into biological regularities, and will be irritated by any theological move that looks like special pleading. The chair is a biblical scholar who is open to nonliteral readings but will push hard on whether my exegetical moves can actually bear the weight I place on them. Give me a Q&A prep sheet for this audience: the objections I will most likely receive, ordered by how damaging they are, phrased in each person's vocabulary, each with a short reply and a note on where that reply is softest, what I should concede up front, and the one question I should be hoping nobody asks.
