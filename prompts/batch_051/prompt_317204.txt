So in my philosophy of math class we've been going over two ways to build the natural numbers out of sets, and after class two classmates got into it while I sat there not knowing who's side I was on. (1) One said that once you use the von Neumann ordinals, where each number is the set of all smaller numbers, 2 is obviously an element of 4, because 4 = {0,1,2,3}. (2) The other replied that in the Zermelo construction, where each number is the singleton of the previous one, 4 = {3}, and 2 is not an element of 4. So whether 2 ∈ 4 comes out true depends entirely on which coding you picked, and the question has no answer until you choose. (3) The first said the choice isn't arbitrary: the von Neumann ordinals are the standard reduction, membership lines up with the order relation, and Zermelo's version is just a curiosity, so 2 ∈ 4 is true full stop. (4) The second said standard isn't correct. Both setups satisfy the axioms for arithmetic, so if both work equally well as models of the natural numbers, there is no fact of the matter about which sets the numbers really are, and 2 ∈ 4 is only true or false relative to a model — like asking whether the number 3 is to the left of 4. (5) The first said that comparison is unfair: being left of something isn't part of the structure of arithmetic, but membership is, and anyway the second's view makes ordinary claims like 1+1=2 relative too. (6) The second said 1+1=2 holds in all the candidate models while 2 ∈ 4 only holds in some, so the distinction does exactly the work. Could you adjudicate this move by move — rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence or two of justification, then say who is more right overall and what both of them are missing?
