I am taking a history of economic thought course, and we have just spent three weeks on the socialist calculation debate. My lingering frustration is that the debate seems to have been abandoned rather than settled, and I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement itself rather than another summary of the positions.

As I understand the positions: Mises argued that rational economic calculation is impossible in a socialist economy because, without private ownership of the means of production, there is no genuine market for capital goods, hence no money prices through which the opportunity costs of alternative employments can be compared. Hayek restated the objection in epistemic terms: the knowledge required for rational allocation is dispersed among individuals, tied to particular circumstances of time and place, constantly changing, and often not in a form that could be gathered and transmitted to a central authority. Lange replied that a Central Planning Board could set prices by trial and error, raising them where shortages appeared and lowering them where surpluses appeared, while instructing managers of state-owned enterprises to minimize average cost and set price equal to marginal cost; this, he argued, would reproduce the allocative results of a competitive market and could even improve on them where externalities are involved. Hayek answered that this model assumes what it needs to show: it supposes the board already possesses, or can costlessly discover, the cost and demand data that a competitive process would itself have to bring to light.

I can describe this back-and-forth, but I cannot yet say why it stalls exactly there. The two sides appear to share a great deal: a static equilibrium framework, the premise that allocation requires information, and an implicit agreement that the question is whether a board could in principle mimic a market. Yet neither moves. My working hypothesis is that there are really two different arguments being run under one heading: an early claim about a logical or conceptual impossibility, which Lange's trial-and-error model may have successfully answered, and a later claim about discovery and incentives under uncertainty, which his static model never engages. If that is right, part of the apparent stalemate is an artifact of treating these as a single continuous debate.

What I want is your diagnosis, not a verdict for one side. Is this a substantive disagreement about the feasibility of centralized allocation under uncertainty, a verbal disagreement about what calculation means (formal computation versus market discovery), a difference about which idealizations are admissible when modeling a planning board, or a difference about burden of proof (must the critic of planning specify the procedure that fails, or must the advocate specify the procedure that works)? Or is something else doing the work? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: an empirical case, a formal result, a conceptual distinction, or a reformulation of the question? Please be specific about the structure of the disagreement rather than the history of it.
