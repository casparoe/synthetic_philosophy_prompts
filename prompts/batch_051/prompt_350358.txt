I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been reading Charles Darwin's The Descent of Man on my own. Chapter IV contains a passage that looks to me like a testable account of guilt and conscience:

"If, for instance, to take an extreme case, men were reared under precisely the same conditions as hive-bees, there can hardly be a doubt that our unmarried females would, like the worker-bees, think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers, and mothers would strive to kill their fertile daughters; and no one would think of interfering. Nevertheless, the bee, or any other social animal, would gain in our supposed case, as it appears to me, some feeling of right or wrong, or a conscience. For each individual would have an inward sense of possessing certain stronger or more enduring instincts, and others less strong or enduring; so that there would often be a struggle as to which impulse should be followed; and satisfaction, dissatisfaction, or even misery would be felt, as past impressions were compared during their incessant passage through the mind. In this case an inward monitor would tell the animal that it would have been better to have followed the one impulse rather than the other."

My amateur paraphrase of the account is this: conscience is the dissatisfaction that arises when a more enduring social instinct loses out to a stronger but shorter-lived impulse, and because the underlying instincts can differ from creature to creature, the resulting conscience can approve of very different things. I tried to test that reading against concrete cases, and here are my verdicts:

1. Snapping at a coworker because I'm tired and hungry, then feeling bad afterward even though she has already moved on — this is exactly the kind of case the passage seems designed to capture.
2. Breaking a promise to visit a friend because a better last-minute invitation came along — I feel guilty, and it fits the same pattern.
3. Feeling guilty for years about an accident I could not have avoided — the guilt is real, but there was no competing impulse, so the mechanism doesn't seem to get a grip.
4. Lying to a telemarketer to end the call — I feel a quick pang and then nothing. I can't tell whether that's because the winning impulse was more persistent or because there was no social instinct toward the telemarketer to begin with.
5. Reporting a friend's serious misconduct at work — I still think I did the right thing and would do it again, but I feel a lingering remorse; the losing instinct, loyalty, seems at least as enduring as the one that won.

Is there a single principle—Darwin's, or a repaired version of it—that captures most or all of these verdicts? Please state it precisely, say which case forces each clause, and tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. And what would that principle predict for a further case: a person who feels no guilt at all about cheating someone outside their own tight social group, while feeling full guilt about lying to a member of it?
