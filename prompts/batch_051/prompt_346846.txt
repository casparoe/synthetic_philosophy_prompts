I've read a fair bit of the causation-by-omission stuff and I'm struck by how little the two camps have moved in decades. One camp says omissions are perfectly good causes. The gardener fails to water the plant, the plant dies, and it seems flat-out true that the failure to water caused the death; the counterfactual test comes out right too, since if he'd watered, the plant wouldn't have died. The other camp says none of that makes an absence a cause. An omission isn't an event or a process, it's literally nothing happening in the world, and nothing can't do causal work. Counterfactual dependence is too cheap: by the same logic the Queen of England's failure to water my plant caused it to die, and every non-event in the history of the universe is a cause of every actual event. They've chewed over double prevention, legal cases, medical neglect cases, and neither side has given an inch. My working theory is that the disagreement isn't about how the world works at all, but about what the word 'cause' is supposed to do: one side wants a relation that's really out there in the world, the other wants a notion that tracks explanatory and moral responsibility. Is that diagnosis right? Or is there a genuine metaphysical dispute about negative facts or negative events underneath, and maybe an empirical question about ordinary judgments thats been hiding in the impasse? What would count as progress — a sharper distinction between causation and causal explanation, experimental philosophy data on how people actually judge absence cases, a formal result about counterfactual models of absences, or just accepting two notions and splitting the term? I want the diagnosis first, and an honest verdict on whether progress is something we should expect or whether this is just one of those debates where the only move is to change the question.
