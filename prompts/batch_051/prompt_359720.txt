After a long argument with a friend about what makes assertions of the form It is raining, but I do not believe it is raining defective, I want to sharpen an objection to her diagnosis before we return to the dispute. Her position is that the defect belongs entirely to the act of assertion. On her view, to assert that p is to present oneself as believing that p, and an assertion of that form therefore violates a sincerity condition on assertion; the content itself is logically coherent, and no further story about evidence or self-knowledge is needed. I suspect she draws too sharp a line between public assertion and private thought, and I would like to test my objection against the strongest version of her account.

Please play that position: a committed defender of the assertion-only diagnosis. Stay in character until I say stop. Be a sharp proponent rather than a strawman; you may refine the view, qualify it, or concede subordinate points, but do not give up the core claim that the incoherence arises from what is done in asserting rather than from a defect in the content of the conjunction as such. Keep each reply to a single paragraph.

Here is my opening move. If the pathology really is a rule of assertion, then the same conjunction should be thinkable without incoherence when it is silently judged, since no audience, no convention, and no act of presenting oneself as believing anything is involved. Yet when I look out the window and silently judge It is raining, but I do not believe it is raining, the absurdity has not simply disappeared; it feels as incoherent as the spoken version, and I am not obviously doing anything worth calling an assertion to myself. What does your account do with this case?
