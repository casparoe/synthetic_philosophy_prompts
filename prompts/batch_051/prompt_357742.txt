I'm a novelist, so treat this as a design problem about a character rather than a diagnostic one. I keep coming back to William James's "What is an Emotion?" (1884), especially this bit:

"Without the bodily states following on the perception, the latter would be purely cognitive in form, pale, colourless, destitute of emotional warmth. We might then see the bear, and judge it best to run, receive the insult and deem it right to strike, but we could not actually feel afraid or angry."

I want to write a character whose body, by stipulation, can't do the bodily part of emotion: no tears, no sinking stomach, no muscle tension, no chills, no urge to flee or strike—none of the stuff James says the emotion is the feeling of. But she still reports being terrified, furious, griefstruck, and I want the reader to take those reports as true, not as confabulation.

Could James's theory make room for a character like this by saying the relevant bodily changes are happening as brain-level rehearsals of the responses even though nothing reaches the periphery, like the brain preparing to cry without actually crying? And if he says something like that, has he just snuck back the "purely cognitive" state he said was destitute of emotional warmth, or is simulated bodily feedback enough to keep his view distinct from the common-sense one he rejected?

Answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no intro or conclusion. I'm more interested in how far the theory can stretch than in whether the syndrome is medically plausible.
