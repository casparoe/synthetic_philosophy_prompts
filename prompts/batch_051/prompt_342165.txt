I need a working definition of the 'man of his time' defence — more precisely, a criterion for deciding when a moral criticism of a past figure is anachronistic (and therefore unfair, or at least less secure) and when it is a fair application of a standard the person themselves could have recognized. The definition should say what context is supposed to do — whether it excuses the wrongness of the act, the agent's blameworthiness for it, or only something like our standing to blame — and it should sort the following six cases.

1. A Roman landowner in the first century BCE who holds people as property in a world where no sustained critique of slavery as such reaches him. I take this to be a clear success for the defence.
2. A mid-twentieth-century official in a liberal democracy who administers racial classification laws after repeatedly hearing the arguments that the laws are unjust. I take this to be a clear failure for the defence.
3. Thomas Jefferson, who writes against slavery in strong terms and affirms natural human rights, yet continues to enslave people throughout his adult life, while abolitionist arguments circulate around him. I am unsure whether his context mitigates his blame, and if so how much.
4. Christopher Columbus, whose enslavement, mutilation, and harsh treatment of Indigenous Caribbean people are condemned by some Spanish contemporaries with direct knowledge of what he was doing. The defence feels weak here, but I cannot say exactly what feature of the case defeats it.
5. Winston Churchill, whose stated views on racial hierarchy are broadly typical of his class, while his government's response to the 1943 Bengal famine draws contemporaneous criticism from British officials in India, even as the scale of the disaster is slow to be absorbed in London. I want to say the defence mitigates the first but not the second, but I am not sure that distinction is principled.
6. An early nineteenth-century abolitionist who devotes himself to ending chattel slavery while holding racial views that are plainly demeaning by our lights. It seems the defence might work for one part of the life and not the other, but I lack a criterion that explains how that can be.

Propose the definition, run it through all six, and say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer. Then revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. I especially want to know whether a single criterion can handle case 5's two halves without ad hoc tinkering.
