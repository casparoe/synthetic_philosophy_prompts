I'm on the independent trust-and-safety oversight board of a video-sharing platform, and we're stuck on a case that I'd thought was the sort of thing we'd decide in an hour. The platform is incorporated in a country with strong free-expression protections, and it hosts a documentary that's sharply critical of a former cabinet minister in a second country. That second country has a broad criminal-defamation statute and a "false news" law, and its regulator has ordered us not just to block the documentary for users inside that country but to remove it everywhere the platform operates. Its argument is that the platform does business there, so its speech laws reach everything the platform serves, wherever the user happens to be. Our own global principles say removals should be as narrow as possible and that we assess content by the law of our home country plus our own human-rights standards, but they simply don't address what to do when a foreign regulator demands worldwide removal.

Half the board thinks the answer is obvious: geo-block in the requesting country and leave it up everywhere else. The other half thinks that's incoherent: either the content is unlawful and it should come down everywhere, or it's lawful and we should refuse the order outright, because you can't split the difference on a speech violation. I had a long argument about this with a friend last night, and he kept saying that a platform that wants to operate ethically in a country has to accept that country's law as the price of entry, and that geo-blocking is a cop-out that lets us take a country's users' data and money while refusing the legal order that goes with them. I couldn't really answer that, and I also couldn't except his conclusion. Underneath it, I keep thinking the real question isn't this one documentary but whose law governs internet speech at all.

Could you draft the decision memo I'll circulate before our meeting next week? It should recommend a specific course of action — remove worldwide, geo-block only, or refuse and defend the content — with a time frame, and it should engage the strongest argument for whichever option we're rejecting, not just the easy version of it. I'd rather read it as continuous prose: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just the way you'd write a long email to the board.
