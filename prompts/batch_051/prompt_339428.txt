I'm teaching an undergraduate seminar on language and ethics, and in a few weeks we will be watching Denis Villeneuve's Arrival, the 2016 film adapted from the Ted Chiang story Story of Your Life. I want to use the film to complicate the material the course has been covering on untranslatability and the translator's responsibility, but I don't want the discussion to collapse into a shallow language-shapes-thought session. The film is usually summarized as a Sapir–Whorf illustration: the heptapods' written language encodes a non-linear experience of time, and by learning it Louise Banks begins to experience past, present, and future simultaneously. I suspect the film is actually running together two claims we have been at pains to keep distinct: first, that translation can fail because some meanings cannot be carried across languages; second, that language acquisition can succeed more deeply than translation because the learner is transformed by the new language. The first is a claim about the limits of translation, the second a claim about its replacement by something else, and the film seems to want both at once.

I would like a reading of the film through the philosophy of translation. Does Arrival treat untranslatability as a cognitive-linguistic obstacle that enough effort can overcome, or as an ethical relation to something foreign that should not simply be made familiar? Does Louise's transformation—her coming to think in the heptapod mode—refute the untranslatability thesis, or does it change the subject, since she stops translating and starts inhabiting the language? I would also like the reading to keep two distinctions in view: the difference between untranslatability and incommensurability, and the difference between a weak claim (language shapes habitual attention, memory, and salience) and a strong claim (one cannot think or perceive what one's language cannot encode). Then take up the ethics directly. Louise works as a translator under intense military and political pressure, and her decision to render the heptapods' key term as an offering of a tool rather than a threat is a high-stakes act of non-neutral mediation. Does the film have a view about whether the translator owes fidelity primarily to the source, to the audience, to accuracy, or to the consequences of what is disclosed, and when?

I plan to discuss specific scenes with the class: the first encounter in the ship, the logogram sessions, the argument with the military over the retranslation, the phone call with the general, and the closing scenes in which Louise acts on her knowledge of the future. Say concretely what each scene dramatizes about translation, where the film illuminates the philosophical problems, and where it overreaches—in particular, where it slides from a defensible point about the difficulty of translation into a fantasy of perfect understanding that translation itself could not deliver.
