I would like you to act as a professional epistemologist with a good command of Wittgenstein's On Certainty and the hinge-epistemology literature that grew out of it. I have been working on a distinction that I keep reaching for, and I want an honest assessment of it rather than reassurance. Please think it through carefully, and before you give your verdict, begin by reconstructing the distinction in its strongest and most charitable form so that I can see whether you have understood what I am after.

I have been calling my two kinds of hinges "scaffolding hinges" and "anchor hinges." By a hinge I mean roughly what hinge epistemologists mean: a proposition that is not held on the basis of evidence and is not in practice exposed to doubt, because it is what our practices of asking questions and giving reasons rest on. But I suspect this single label covers two quite different phenomena. A scaffolding hinge is a proposition one must treat as fixed in order for a particular enquiry to get going at all; if it were given up, the relevant questions would stop making sense as questions rather than simply receiving different answers. The existence of an external world, the reality of the past, and the existence of other minds seem to be like this: losing one of them does not leave you with a false belief in the relevant domain but takes away the domain itself. An anchor hinge, by contrast, is a proposition whose surrender would not make any enquiry unintelligible, but whose loss would be so costly to one's practices, relationships, and sense of oneself that one does not seriously treat it as open. The belief that one's spouse is faithful, that God exists, that one's political community is fundamentally decent, or that one had a reasonably happy childhood would be examples: a person could in principle go on reasoning perfectly well about the subject after losing any of these, but they could not go on as the same person.

The claim I want tested is this: the genuinely interesting cases that hinge epistemology is asked to handle are almost always anchors being mistaken for scaffolds. The stock examples in the literature are scaffolding hinges, but those are exactly the hinges nobody is ever seriously tempted to doubt, so they do not really put the theory under pressure. The live cases, where people actually experience the impossibility of doubt, are anchors: matters of faith, trust, identity, and ideological commitment. What keeps those in place is not that enquiry would become impossible without them, but that one would cease to be the person one is. If that is right, then hinge epistemology is, despite itself, not primarily an account of the limits of rational enquiry but an account of the price of changing one's mind; the felt "must" is practical and self-constituting rather than epistemic. I also suspect this explains why hinge epistemology has such trouble with deep disagreement: it analyses the parties as though each side had a different piece of scaffolding, when in fact each side has an anchor it cannot let go of, and the two situations are not symmetrical.

What I want from you is a candid assessment. Is this a distinction with a real difference, or do the two kinds of case turn out to be points on a continuum that collapses under pressure? Does something like it already exist in the hinge-epistemology literature under another name, and if so what distinguishes my version from the version already there? What is the hardest case for the distinction, the one where a hinge seems to be both scaffold and anchor or neither? And does treating anchors as hinges genuinely help with the problem of deep disagreement, or does it merely redescribe that problem in therapeutic language?
