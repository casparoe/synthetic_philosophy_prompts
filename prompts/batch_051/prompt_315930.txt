I hold the following view about the direction of time. The only fundamental temporal asymmetry in our universe is thermodynamic: entropy increases toward what we call the future, starting from the special low-entropy early state known as the past hypothesis. The other arrows — the psychological arrow (we remember the past but not the future), the causal arrow (causes precede effects, and interventions affect only the future), and the record arrow (things leave traces only of what has already happened) — are not independent asymmetries; they reduce to, or are macroscopic consequences of, the entropy gradient plus the past hypothesis.

Your task is not to defend or attack this view, but to map what it risks. Tell me what would count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Include: observations or thought experiments in which one of these arrows decouples from the entropy gradient (for instance, a universe whose entropy decreases toward the future; a time-symmetric universe with low entropy in the middle and maxima at both ends; an apparent demon or intervention that unwrites records); arguments that one of the arrows cannot be derived from a gradient because it is presupposed in the very idea of a record, an intervention, or a memory; and consequences the view seems committed to that may be unacceptable. For each, say whether a committed defender of the view would themselves concede that it counts against them or would dismiss it as a redescription of the data. Distinguish what would collapse the reduction, what would force a qualification, and what would leave the core untouched. Finally, assess whether the view has been insulated from counterevidence by the flexible explanatory role of the past hypothesis, and whether that insulation, if present, is a problem for a hypothesis of this kind.
