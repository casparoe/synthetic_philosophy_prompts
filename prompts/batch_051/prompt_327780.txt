Two housemates of mine got into it last night over a paragraph in Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, Book V, chapter 5 (translated by H. Rackham). The paragraph reads:

"Money then serves as a measure which makes things commensurable and so reduces them to equality. If there were no exchange there would be no association, and there can be no exchange without equality, and no equality without commensurability. Though therefore it is impossible for things so different to become commensurable in the strict sense, our demand furnishes a sufficiently accurate common measure for practical purposes."

Dana said this settles it: Aristotle is a commensurabilist. Houses and shoes are wildly different, yet he says money "makes things commensurable," so any two goods go on one scale, with demand fixing the ratio. Marcus said Dana was ignoring the sentence where Aristotle says it is "impossible for things so different to become commensurable in the strict sense." The common measure is just a rough, conventional stand-in, so Aristotle is really an incomparabilist about the underlying values. Dana shot back that "commensurable in the strict sense" is doing no work: if the practical measure is accurate enough and demand settles the trade, the strict-sense gap never shows up in anything we actually do. Marcus said it shows up precisely because the measure is demand — that's the chapter's other point, that money exists "not by nature but by custom" and "can be altered and rendered useless at will," so nothing in the goods themselves fixes how many shoes equal a house.
