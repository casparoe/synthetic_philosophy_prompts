You are a philosopher of mind with a solid working knowledge of finite-state systems and the metaphysics of conscious experience. Take your time, begin by brainstorming several ways the two sides of the clash below could be made precise, and resist resolving the tension too quickly.

I am attracted to the following line of thought, and I cannot find the flaw in it. A human mind is realized by a finite physical system: finitely many components, each capable of only finitely many relevantly distinct states. The number of relevantly distinct total states of the system is therefore finite — astronomical, but finite. If conscious experience supervenes on the total state of the system, then the number of possible types of experience available to a human being is also finite. Call this the finite-mind thesis. From it there follows an exhaustibility claim: given a sufficiently long life, or sufficiently many lives with varied histories, the space of experience-types would eventually be exhausted. Past that point, every further experience would be a token of a type already tokened, and no genuinely new type of experience would remain.

Against this stands an intuition I find equally firm: experience is open-ended. For any finite collection of experiences I can actually call to mind, I can imagine one more — a new combination of sights, sounds, and thoughts, a new emotional texture, a new temporal sequence. It does not feel as if I am drawing from a finite bag; it feels as if the repertoire is indefinitely extendable. In particular, the attempt to enumerate the experiences and then ask whether there could be one more seems to produce a novel experience on the spot, which suggests the collection can never be closed.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want a negotiated treatment. Give the strongest versions of, first, the case that the open-endedness intuition should be debunked and dropped — for example, by explaining it as a byproduct of the way imagination composes old elements into new-looking wholes, as a type–token confusion, or as a failure to keep track of finite discriminative resolution. Then give the strongest version of the case that the finite-mind argument should be amended or abandoned — for example, by attacking its supervenience premise, its assumption that experience-types form a crisp set, or its assumption that the relevant notion of possibility is physical possibility. Then consider whether the two can survive together on some reading: distinguish different senses of novelty, or different notions of what it is for experience to be exhausted, and state which version of each claim remains true. Finally, say what would justify each move — what evidence or argument should make me give up the intuition, give up the theory, or conclude that the apparent conflict was an artifact of imprecise concepts. Aim for a verdict that tells me exactly which parts of each side I can keep and which I cannot.
