In the course of covering algorithmic risk assessment for courts and clinics, I have accumulated a set of reactions to single-case probability claims, and they do not seem to hang together. A cardiologist tells a 62-year-old patient with elevated LDL that his ten-year risk of a heart attack is 14 percent, using a model built on age, sex, cholesterol, blood pressure, and smoking status. That strikes me as a perfectly good answer even though it ignores diet, exercise, and family history, all of which would move the number. But if the same cardiologist knew the patient carried a rare variant that roughly doubles cardiac risk independent of the model's variables and still reported 14 percent, that would be an error about the patient rather than a simplification. An actuary who places a specific driver in a broad, statistically robust class to fix next year's accident probability seems to me to be doing something legitimate; indeed, I would rather the actuary use a broad stable class than the most specific available class, which might rest on a handful of similar drivers and be noise. A fire investigator who says the probability that this wiring caused the fire is 90 percent also seems fine to me, even though I could not say exactly what comparison class underwrites the claim. A weather service's 30 percent chance of rain for the city tomorrow is a legitimate statement, but I do not think it is a fact about any particular address. A judge confronted with a recidivism tool that assigns this defendant a 62 percent probability of re-arrest within two years draws a different reaction from me: I want to call that number ill-posed even when the underlying comparison class is large. And a coin that has already been tossed and covered: I am happy to say the probability of heads is one-half as a report of my information, but I do not believe there is any mind-independent chance attaching to that particular toss, which has already happened.

My working principle has been that a single-case probability is legitimate just when there is a reference class relative to which the individual can be treated as a random draw, the class is broad enough to support a reliable frequency, and the appropriate class is the most specific one satisfying that constraint; where no such class exists, no single-case probability is to be had. But that principle fails my own cases in several ways. It would ratify the judge's recidivism number, which I wanted to reject. It does not explain why the cardiologist may ignore diet and yet must not ignore the known genetic variant. It is silent about the already-tossed coin, where no reference class is in view and yet one-half seems exactly the right thing to say. And it assumes the most specific stable class is unique when the same driver or patient can be partitioned in many cross-cutting ways, each yielding a different number.

Please extract from these verdicts the best principle you can, or a small set of candidate principles. State each candidate precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which of my cases, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would force me to abandon, and then apply it to the cases I keep going back and forth on: a flood insurer who says this particular house has a 1 percent annual flood risk, and a venture capitalist who says this particular startup has a 10 percent chance of becoming a unicorn.
