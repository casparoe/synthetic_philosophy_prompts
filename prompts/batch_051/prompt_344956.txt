For a story I'm working on, I've been digging through five years of grant decisions from a foundation that calls itself hardline consequentialist. Their track record: they funded antimalarial bednets (short, well-studied causal chain); funded a basic-income pilot even though the long-run effects are hazy, because the near-term poverty relief was large and the speculative long run seemed about as likely good as bad; and funded replacing diesel school buses with electric ones because of the local air-quality payoff, even with some uncertainty about battery supply chains. But they turned down a youth voter-turnout drive because even if it registers more voters, the downstream consequences run through elections and legislation and are impossible to trace, and they turned down a college fossil-fuel divestment push because the path from endowment politics to emissions was to long to evaluate.

Next month they take up a proposal to put HEPA filters in classrooms near a major highway and pair it with a curriculum where kids monitor air quality and present findings to the city council. The filter part has decent short-term evidence; the curriculum is basically designed to create a local political constituency.

What should I predict they'll do if they're being consistent with themselves? I'm most interested in the underlying rule or rules that fit their track record, which past decision is the closest analog, and where the candidate rules split on this case. If their record underdetermines it, tell me what principle they'd be locking in by funding it or by turning it down, and give me the follow-up case you'd want to see before betting.
