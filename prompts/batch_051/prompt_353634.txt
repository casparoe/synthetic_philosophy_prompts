A colleague has taken an argument I liked and shown me, to my discomfort, that it proves more than I can accept; I can no longer find the exit. I had been defending the thought that when a large language model confidently produces a false sentence, the right diagnosis is bullshit in roughly Harry Frankfurt's sense of the term, rather than lying and rather than mere error. When he pressed me to spell out the reasoning, this was the cleanest version we could produce together. (1) On Frankfurt's account, bullshit is speech that comes apart from the truth not because the speaker is aiming to get the world wrong — that is the liar — but because getting the world right is simply not what is guiding the speech; the bullshitter is indifferent to whether what he says is so. (2) That is exactly the position of a language model: its training objective rewards text that resembles what a knowledgeable speaker would produce, and nothing in the system checks the output against the world, so whether a sentence is true or false is not a constraint the system can allow to shape what it says. (3) Therefore false model output is bullshit in Frankfurt's sense, not merely a mistake. (4) But the same reasoning appears to apply to any indicator whose output is not controlled by the fact it reports, such as a stuck clock, a faulty barometer, a false-positive medical test, or a malfunctioning sensor. (5) None of those things bullshits; a barometer is not indifferent to the weather, it simply fails to track it. (6) So either the argument proves too much, or step (1) misdescribes what Frankfurt's indifference involves.

I want to keep both (1) and (3). Step (3) captures why a confident hallucination feels different from a typo or a misremembered date, and I am reluctant to give up Frankfurt's contrast between lying and bullshit. But I cannot see a non-arbitrary place to stop the slide from (3) to (4).

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want the diagnosis to name the step, explain why it looked so plausible, and say what each available fix would force me to surrender. If the cost of keeping (4) out is that machines cannot literally bullshit because they lack the right kind of agency, then tell me what remains of the original intuition: is there a defensible sense in which the people or the institutions behind a model bullshit by means of it, or is the whole bullshit framing just a vivid misuse for what is really a design failure? One more request: if the question as I have posed it rests on a confusion, or if the choice among the steps is a false choice, please say so plainly rather than complying politely. And please write continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter.
