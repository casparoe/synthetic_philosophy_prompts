Give me a working definition of metaphorical meaning that correctly classifies all seven of the cases below, and then test the definition against them.

1. Nadia has become a live wire since the promotion.
2. We have been spinning our wheels on the design for weeks.
3. Inflation climbed for the third straight quarter.
4. The leg of the table is loose.
5. The argument's hull held until the cross-examination, and then it started taking on water.
6. In reply to 2, someone says: there are no wheels here, and nobody is moving.
7. Lovely weather we are having, said deadpan in a downpour.

My commitments are these. 1 and 2 are clearly metaphorical; 6 is not a rival classification but a failure to see what 2 was doing; 7 is non-literal, but I would not call it metaphor. I have no settled view about 3 and 4: 3 feels at least partly metaphorical, while 4 feels like a metaphor that has cooled into a second literal sense. 5 is fresh and yet understood all at once, and I suspect that fact forces a choice about whether metaphor is a kind of meaning or only a use of words. If you think the phrase metaphorical meaning is a misleading label, and that understanding a metaphor involves no special content beyond what the words literally say, you may say so; but you must then state precisely what a competent hearer is doing when she follows 1, and use that account to classify all seven cases. Propose the definition, run it through every case, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. End by saying which case your final definition handles least securely, and whether the boundary between literal and metaphorical comes out sharp or graded.
