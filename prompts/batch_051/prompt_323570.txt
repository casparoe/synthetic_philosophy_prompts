In my theory of normativity seminar we've spent several sessions on the wrong kind of reasons problem, and I've realized I can't state the problem precisely because I don't have a firm grip on the central term. Could you help me build a working definition? The term is a normative reason for an attitude: a reason to believe something, to be afraid of something, to be grateful to someone, to want something, or to admire someone.

Here are six cases, with my untutored reactions:

(a) A trustworthy forecast says it will rain. That is a reason to think it will rain.
(b) A coin landing heads this morning is no reason at all to think it will rain.
(c) A growling dog is blocking my way. That is a reason to be afraid of the dog.
(d) A colleague went well out of her way to help me. That is a reason to be grateful to her.
(e) A rich eccentric offers me a fortune if I want a saucer of mud for its own sake. Is that a reason to want the mud? I cannot tell.
(f) A tyrant will spare my family only if I admire him. Is that a reason to admire the tyrant? I cannot tell.

I notice that my confident verdicts treat considerations about the attitude's object—the rain, the dog, the colleague's help—differently from considerations about the advantages of being in the attitude, as in (e) and (f). But I don't know whether the right response is to posit two senses of 'reason,' to build one definition that treats (e) and (f) as reasons of a different kind, or something else.

Please give me the definition you would defend. If you split the notion into two, give both definitions and say which cases fall under each; if you keep one notion, say how it classifies (e) and (f) and whether that classification is the right one. Begin with the definition in your first sentence, then go through (a)–(f) in order, and end by stating which of your verdicts is least secure and why. Keep the response under 800 words.
