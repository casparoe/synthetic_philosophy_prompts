I'm taking a philosophy of language elective, and we've spent a few sessions on presupposition and on how speakers coordinate on background information without stating it. The framework we've been working with, which I gather comes mainly from David Lewis, treats conversation as a kind of game with an evolving scoreboard: at any moment there is a record of what is being presupposed, which standards of precision or comparison are in force, which objects are salient, and so on. Utterances are treated as moves whose legitimacy depends on the current score, and they update the score for the next move, much as a move in chess is legal only relative to the board it is made on and then changes that board. The model is used especially to explain presupposition: an utterance like "Sam has stopped smoking" presupposes that Sam previously smoked, and if that proposition is not already on the scoreboard, hearers tend to quietly add it—what is called accommodation—as though the score were being adjusted retroactively to make the move acceptable.

I am trying to determine how far this analogy actually carries. I can see what it gets right: it captures the way an utterance is both constrained by the existing conversational record and contributes to a new one, and it explains how participants coordinate without stopping to renegotiate every assumption. But games have fixed rules, a score that is public and determinate, discrete legal moves, and success conditions, while conversation seems to lack all four: participants can change the rules mid-exchange, the relevant background is often vague or disputed, and speakers are not generally trying to score points. I would like an audit of the analogy, not just a list of similarities and differences. In particular: what corresponds to what; which disanalogies matter specifically for the explanatory job the analogy is being asked to do with respect to presupposition and accommodation; and whether the scoreboard should ultimately be understood as a genuine model of conversation or as a vivid but potentially misleading notation for facts about interpretation. If there is a better comparison, I would want to know that too.
