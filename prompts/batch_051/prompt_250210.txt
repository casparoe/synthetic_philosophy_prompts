You are an expert in social choice theory and mechanism design who regularly coaches speakers for adversarial theory seminars. Think carefully before drafting, and start by brainstorming the objections this particular audience is most likely to raise.

I am giving a seminar next week to a joint theory group of microeconomic theorists and theoretical computer scientists. Most of them work on voting rules, auctions, or algorithmic game theory, and they all know the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem and its proof. Several are attached to strategy-proof mechanisms on restricted domains—median-voter schemes for single-peaked preferences, VCG mechanisms for quasi-linear settings—and the computer scientists in the room have worked on hardness of manipulation.

My thesis is this. Gibbard–Satterthwaite shows that with three or more alternatives, any strategy-proof social choice function whose domain is unrestricted and whose range is onto is dictatorial. The field's standard response is to retreat: restrict the domain, move to cardinal or quasi-linear settings, or relax determinism. I argue this response treats strategy-proofness as a fixed ideal to be preserved by changing the problem, and that the ideal itself is the mistake. I defend the stronger claim that mechanism design should abandon strategy-proofness as a hard requirement for ordinary ordinal voting rules, and instead aim for practical non-manipulability: rules that are manipulable in principle, but where successful manipulation is costly, computationally hard, or empirically unreliable for actual voters. I also argue that the standard escape routes either beg the question, change the subject, or shift the vulnerability rather than eliminating it.

I have the impossibility result on my side; the controversial part is what designers should do with it. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet for this audience. I want the objections in roughly the order they will actually come, phrased the way this group would phrase them; an indication of which objections have real force and which are deflections; what I should concede up front and what I should hold; a short reply to each objection suitable for a two-minute answer; a note on where each reply is weakest; and the question I should be hoping nobody asks.
