I teach a unit on early Chinese ethics comparing Mozi’s impartial care, jian ai, with the Confucian view that care is rightly graded by relationship, beginning with the family. In the Universal Love chapters of the Mozi, Mozi argues that the harms of the world arise from partiality: he asks readers to consider whether someone who regarded another family as they regard their own would steal from it, attack it, or leave it to suffer. A stock criticism is that this simply assumes impartiality is the only alternative. Construct a thought experiment in which a person committed to Confucian graded love—the view that one rightly owes more care to parents, children, and siblings than to strangers—is forced by the structure of the case to feel the pull of Mozi’s diagnosis. Constraints: no one in the scenario begins by acting wrongly according to the graded-love agent; the agent is not a villain; and the resulting moral trouble must come from the principle of graded love itself as it distributes across many agents, not from a single failure of empathy or an exceptional self-interested person. The case should be one paragraph. After the scenario, walk through what the graded-love principle directs each agent to do, where the harm comes from, and why Mozi would say the case confirms that partiality is the root of disorder. Then flag which feature of the case is doing the work, what a Confucian defender would reply, and whether the case depends on treating graded love as something stronger than ordinary family partiality.
