A consortium of journals in systems biology, neuroscience, and machine learning is considering a policy intended to discipline claims that two systems "share the same mechanism." Under the proposal, any paper claiming that a mechanism identified in one system is also present in another—a mouse circuit and a human circuit, a model organism and a cell line, an artificial network and a brain region—must deposit a public mechanism-identity file. For pre-registered comparisons, the file is written before data collection; for everything else, it is posted at submission and flagged as post hoc. The file must list the features the authors count as essential to the mechanism and the features they count as mere implementation detail; it must specify at least one observation that would count as evidence the mechanism is absent even though the phenomenon it was invoked to explain is present; and it must state how much parameter variation, rewiring, or damage the mechanism is supposed to tolerate before being judged a different mechanism. The file is published as a citable appendix, and an annual audit tracks whether later papers treated disagreements over mechanism identity as resolvable by appeal to the original file. Treat this as a piece of institutional design rather than a philosophical position: what would it incentivize among authors, reviewers, competing labs, and fields that differ over how finely mechanisms should be individuated? How would it be gamed—for instance, through identity criteria broad enough to survive anything or narrow enough to fit only one system? What second-order effects follow for exploratory work, negative results, and the way the word "mechanism" gets used in grants and abstracts? Which failure modes could be fixed by amendment, and which are intrinsic to asking scientists to state criteria of sameness in advance?
