I am a novelist, and the question I bring you is a working one rather than an academic one. I am planning a novel about an artist whose work is admired long before his private conduct becomes publicly known, and I keep returning to the familiar defence: that the work and the artist can be separated, that the work is to be judged for what it is rather than for who made it. In its philosophical form — one version of the position usually called autonomism — the claim is that a work's aesthetic value is constituted by properties internal to the work, such as form, construction, and imaginative power, and that facts about the artist's moral character belong to a different inquiry that need not alter the aesthetic verdict.

I am less interested in the neighbouring debate about whether a work with immoral content can be great, and more interested in whether this defence of separation survives the way art is now actually encountered. The classical formulation seems to me to presuppose a private, contemplative viewer standing before an object whose provenance and consequences can be bracketed. That presupposition is now mostly false. When I stream a film or an album, my engagement is at once an aesthetic act and a transaction: money is routed, however indirectly, to the artist or the rights-holder; my choice is recorded, aggregated, and legible to others as a small public signal of taste or tolerance; and the artist's biography often reaches me before the work does, so there is nothing to bracket because the moral facts are part of the encounter from the start.

Does the autonomist argument survive this change, or does it need to be rebuilt on different ground? Identify the premise the new conditions bear on most directly — the claim that aesthetic value is internal to the work, or the picture of appreciation as a private, non-transactional encounter — and say whether a weaker but still usable version of the separation can be defended, and what exactly it would entitle a reader or viewer to do. I ask one further thing: tell me what would change your mind. Which argument or consideration would lead you to the opposite verdict, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail? Please answer in British English.
