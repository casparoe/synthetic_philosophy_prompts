I'd call myself a Scanlon-style contractualist: an act is wrong just in case it would be ruled out by any principle for how we generally treat each other that no one could reasonably reject. On top of that core, I hold all of the following:

1. The only reasons that matter when we're testing a principle are reasons someone has as an individual. There's no independent moral force in the sum of welfare across people; aggregating benefits across lives does no justificatory work on its own.

2. If I come across a rockslide and can save one stranger or five strangers, at no cost to myself, I should save the five. That doesn't feel like a concession to utilitarian aggregation; it just feels obviously right.

3. A surgeon shouldn't kill one healthy patient to distribute their organs to five people who will otherwise die. Full stop.

4. The best explanation of the difference between the rockslide and the transplant case is that the transplant uses the one as a mere means, while the rockslide doesn't use the person who gets left behind as a means at all.

5. Numbers only count as a tiebreaker: five equally strong complaints beat one equally strong complaint, but you cannot add up many weaker complaints so that they outweigh one stronger complaint.

6. But I also think that if I have to choose between saving one person from death and saving five people from permanent paralysis, I should save the five from paralysis. It's less obvious than the rockslide, but I'd still do it.

I cant tell whether this is a stable package or whether I'm quietly relying on something that one of the earlier commitments rules out. Go through and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look that way. Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix would be. Don't just tell me each item is independently plausible; I need to know where the load-bearing conflicts are.
