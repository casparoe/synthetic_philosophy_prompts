I've been having a slow-burning disagreement with a colleague of mine, a committed welfarist, about whether a life can go better simply in virtue of an achievement that benefits no one. The case we keep returning to is this. A gifted mountaineer completes, entirely alone and without any record, a genuinely first-rate new route of extreme difficulty on an unclimbed peak. The climb is agonizing, gives her no pleasure, and changes nothing in her reputation, relationships, livelihood, health, or future abilities; afterward the route is destroyed by rockfall before anyone else could attempt or even learn of it. By stipulation, the only difference between her actual life and an otherwise identical life that omits the climb is that in the actual life she performed this difficult and excellent act and knows she did, while the knowledge itself carries no felt satisfaction. The proposition I want assessed is not that the world has gained one more valuable achievement, but that the actual life is better for her than the counterpart life — her life is more worth living from her own point of view in virtue of the private achievement, even though no one benefits in any familiar welfare-relevant sense. My credence in that proposition is about 0.55; hers is about 0.15.

I want your own number, but the number is secondary. Decompose the question into the sub-claims that actually carry the weight: for instance, whether the goodness of a life for the person who lives it is exhausted by positive experience, desire-satisfaction, and other welfare goods that can be specified independently of the achievement; whether difficult, skilled success can have final value in a life rather than only instrumental value; whether such value requires a beneficiary or an audience or can be fully realized in a wholly private act; whether the climber's suffering and the absence of satisfaction cancel or outweigh whatever value attaches to the exercise of developed capacities for its own sake; and whether merely being the person who did the thing can make a life better even when it feels like nothing. Assign a rough probability and a one-line justification to each sub-claim, show how they combine into your overall credence, and identify which sub-claim my colleague and I most likely diverge on. Finally, name the single consideration that would most move your number, and say what kind of argument or evidence, however speculative, would speak to it.
