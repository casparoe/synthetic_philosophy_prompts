so i have this rule and i want to know if its defensible. whenever someone offers me an adaptationist story about a trait -- we evolved to love sugar because it was scarce on the savanna, jealousy evolved to guard paternity, depression is an adaptation for rumination, that kind of thing -- i apply two filters. first a symmetry check: if the opposite trait would have made just as smooth a story (had we evolved to dislike sugar, someone would be telling a toxin-avoidance story), then i treat the explanation as carrying no information and stop. second, if it somehow survives that, i still discount it heavily unless the person can point to evidence that the trait is heritable, that it's actually tied to fitness in the relevant environment, and that they've seriously considered drift or a byproduct or an exaptation rather than just asserting adaptation by default. in practice almost nothing clears this, especially not pop-science stuff, so i end up writing off most adaptationist explanations. my friend says i've basically turned the Gould and Lewontin spandrels critique into a personal bias: i hold adaptationist hypotheses to an impossible standard while offering no alternative, and a story being hard to test doesn't make it false, and adaptationist guesses are still how you generate hypotheses. is this policy defensible? if it isn't, is there a version that keeps the good part -- not being a sucker for just-so stories -- without becoming a blanket refusal of adaptationist reasoning? and when you answer, can you seperate what's actually settled in this debate from what's genuinely contested among people who know it, and not present a contested reading as the consensus.
