I've been going in circles on this, so I'm outsourcing it. I'm writing a handout contrasting two views of joint action, and I want a clean case where they come apart, but every version I build winds up being one both views read the same way.

The two views:
(1) The Searlean we-mode view. Genuine joint action requires a primitive we-intention on each agent's part—an intention whose content is something like us doing J together, not just me doing my bit for J—and this we-intention is not reducible to an ordinary individual intention plus beliefs about what the other person will do.
(2) The Bratman-style reductive view. A shared intention just is a web of individual intentions: each person intends the joint activity, each intends to do their own part partly because the other intends theirs, their subplans mesh, and all of this is common knowledge.

Build me a two-person, perfectly ordinary scenario (neighbors, coworkers, bandmates, whatever) where the Searlean view says there's a genuine collective intention but the Bratman-style view says there isnt a shared intention. Constraints: no coercion, no deception, no games or competitions, both people know the other's relevant intentions and subplans, and dont just have them announce some special phrase—the split has to come from the structure of the intentions themselves, not from missing information. It also shouldn't just be two people independently doing the same thing and slapping the word "we" on it after the fact; the Searlean verdict has to be earned by the attitude they actually have.

After the scenario, do the check. Say exactly which Bratman condition fails and why, and why a Searlean would still count the we-intention. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the real work, and what happens if you remove it—does the case collapse back into one both views count? Spell out the rebuttal.
