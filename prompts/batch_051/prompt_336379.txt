An introductory book on set theory that I am reading explains Russell's paradox through a story widely used in popular expositions. In a certain village there is a barber, and the rule governing his work is that he shaves all and only those villagers who do not shave themselves. Ask whether the barber shaves himself, and either answer contradicts the rule; the book's immediate conclusion is that there is no such barber. It then draws the set-theoretic parallel: likewise, there is no set whose members are exactly the sets that are not members of themselves, and this is why naive set theory, with its unrestricted comprehension principle, has to be given up. I want an audit of how far this analogy actually carries.

Give the correspondence table: what in the set-theoretic version plays the role of the village, the villagers, the barber, the shaving relation, and the rule that binds the barber? Then identify the places where the analogy breaks down, separating the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that do not. In particular, address the following. The barber story has a cheap resolution available: deny that any such barber exists while leaving the village and its standing rules otherwise untouched. Does the corresponding move work in naive set theory, where the comprehension principle itself asserts that for any predicate there is a set of exactly the things satisfying it? What precisely does each case refute? Does the story quietly import a pre-given totality of all objects, a village of all sets, and does anything turn on that? Finally, say whether the intended conclusion — that unrestricted comprehension must be restricted or otherwise regimented — survives the disanalogies, or whether the analogy was carrying the argumentative weight all along. If you judge the analogy defective, propose a better one that preserves the force of the paradox without the misleading features.
