Something weird goes on in public policy. The same government will refuse to fund a preventive drug because it costs more than, say, $50,000 per QALY, but will also use a value of a statistical life in road safety of several million dollars per death prevented. If a prevented death is worth maybe 20 or 30 QALYs, these numbers are way out of line; and that's before you get to the separate fact that we'll also spend absurd amounts on a named individual in the news that we'd never spend on the anonymous people those programs are supposed to save. Is there any principled reason for treating roughly the same death as worth three different amounts depending on whether it shows up as a QALY, a VSL, or a person with a face and a name? Or are these just different tools answering different questions, and the real mistake is expecting them to agree? Give me the strongest defense you can of keeping the asymmetry, test it against a case where the named patient has a poor prognosis and the statistical beneficiaries are children, and then say wich way of valuing life you'd actually trust with a budget.
