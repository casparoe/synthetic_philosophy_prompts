Consider the combination of practices at a development organization. Its European offices run campaigns urging people to adopt plant-based diets, on the ground that animal products are unnecessary and ethically indefensible for anyone with access to fortified plant foods. The same organization runs a project in a food-insecure part of northern Kenya that gives each participating household two dairy goats, and it defends the project by citing child stunting and the absence of reliable fortified alternatives there. The organization maintains that the two halves of its work are coherent: in Kenya the goats are a necessity, while in Europe the campaign targets people who have alternatives.

What view of the ethics of using animal products does this combination of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, then distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits. Does it presuppose that the permissibility of using an animal depends on the alternatives available to the user—and if so, how finely must those alternatives be described? Does it presuppose that animal interests are weighed against basic human needs only below some threshold, or that the very moral status of the act changes with context? Could a strict animal-rights view, on which it is wrong to use sentient beings as resources, still endorse the goat project as a lesser evil or a nonideal compromise, or does the project quietly rule that view out? Are the two halves of the organization's work consistent: could the reasoning that makes the goats permissible be turned back against its European campaign, or the reverse? Finally, say where your reconstruction is most likely to overreach—which features of the practice could be explained by institutional inertia, donor incentives, or mere inconsistency rather than by any ethical theory—and what argument or consideration would lead you to a substantially different verdict.
