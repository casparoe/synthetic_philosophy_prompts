I'm a high school student in a dual-enrollment course on scientific reasoning, and my study group has volunteered to write a practice exam for our unit on verifiability and progress. We've been comparing fields that seem to accumulate reliable knowledge relatively quickly—such as parts of engineering and the observational sciences—with fields that have been around just as long but do not visibly converge, such as astrology or large areas of macroeconomics. The standard explanation is that the faster fields traffic in falsifiable claims while the slower ones do not, but our instructor has kept pushing us past that label and toward the mechanism: what exactly does clear, rapid feedback do to a practitioner's beliefs, and why do some data-rich, quantitative fields still fail to correct themselves?

Please write one exam essay question on this topic, suitable for a conceptually demanding introductory college-level unit, to be completed in about 50 minutes and worth roughly 25 marks. The question should be impossible to answer well by reciting the falsifiability slogan or the claim that science is self-correcting; it should force students to engage with what has to be true of feedback for it to produce progress—for instance, whether a failed prediction advances a field only when the failure can be attributed to a specific claim rather than to a whole network of assumptions, and whether the crucial variable is having feedback that resists being explained away after the fact. Then provide a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain and the specific mistakes that should cost marks—for example, conflating verifiability with actually having been verified, treating all feedback as equally informative, or reciting the Popperian account without describing the error-correction mechanism that does the actual work.
