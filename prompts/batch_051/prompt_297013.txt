Two people in a seminar I attended got into an argument about a gamble, and I would like an outside adjudication. The gamble was this: a fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads. If the first heads comes on the nth flip, the player receives (−1)^(n−1) · (2^n / n) dollars — a gain when n is odd, a loss when n is even. Since the coin is fair, the first heads comes on flip n with probability 2^(−n). Here is the exchange as best I remember it.

Sofia: "The expected value is the sum over n of 2^(−n) times the payoff, which gives Σ(−1)^(n−1)/n. That is the alternating harmonic series, and it converges to the natural log of 2 — about 69 cents. So a risk-neutral player should pay any fee below ln 2 and refuse any fee above it."

Rahul: "That series converges only conditionally. The terms in absolute value are the harmonic series, which diverges, so the value you get by summing depends on the order in which you list the outcomes. A rearrangement of the same terms can converge to any number you like, or diverge. That means the gamble has no expectation as such, and the question of a fair fee is ill-posed."

Sofia: "But the order is not arbitrary. The mechanism produces the flips in time, and n is the flip on which heads first appears. Summing in the order the mechanism generates is the natural way; reordering the terms would be evaluating a different gamble, not this one."

Rahul: "The expected value of a gamble is supposed to be a property of its probability distribution. Enumerate the same outcomes two different ways and you should get the same expectation; otherwise the expectation is not a property of the gamble at all, and decisions built on it are arbitrary. The temporal order is part of your presentation, but the distribution itself does not privilege any enumeration."

Sofia: "Still, any actual agent can only reason with finite truncations. If I cap the game at N flips and pay nothing if heads has not appeared, the expectation is the Nth partial sum, and those partial sums converge to ln 2. So ln 2 is the value that any bounded horizon points to. You are turning a technical pathology into a practical problem that no real decision-maker has."

Rahul: "It is not merely technical. Your cap is a modification of the gamble, not the gamble. The original game has unbounded gains and unbounded losses, and the positive contributions and the negative contributions are each infinite in expectation; your cap zeroes both out. Other ways of cutting off the tail — stopping after a certain number of wins, or letting the stopping point depend on the running total — need not approach ln 2. The limit you get is imposed by the particular cut-off you chose."

Sofia: "Then maybe decision theory should not insist on a well-defined expectation. In practice I would pay half a dollar, reason with some bounded horizon, and not worry about rearrangements. If expected-value theory says there is no answer, that is a mark against expected-value theory."

Rahul: "But half a dollar is an arbitrary choice. A different rearrangement would justify a different fee, or none. The rule you are following is not generated by the gamble; you have simply picked a regularization. A decision theory that cannot distinguish paying 50 cents from paying 50 dollars for the same gamble is failing at its central job."

Please walk through their exchange claim by claim and adjudicate it. For each specific assertion, say who is right, who is wrong, and where either of them overstates or conflates distinct issues. Then explain what a fully correct treatment of this gamble says: about its expectation in the standard sense, about the long-run average payoff under repeated play, and about whether there is any uniquely rational fee for a risk-neutral agent. Finish by saying what, if anything, both of them are missing.
