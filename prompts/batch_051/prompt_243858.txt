I'm a high school student, and in our philosophy club we've been arguing about whether Bayesian confirmation theory can make sense of how scientists actually use evidence from the past. I wrote out the following argument, and I keep going back and forth about whether it is valid. It is meant to show that old evidence—evidence already known before a theory was thought of—can never confirm a theory that entails it.

(1) Evidence E confirms theory T only if P(T | E) > P(T).
(2) When E is old evidence, it was already known before T was formulated, so for the scientist doing the evaluating, P(E) = 1.
(3) If T entails E, then P(E | T) = 1.
(4) By Bayes' theorem, P(T | E) = P(T) × P(E | T) / P(E). With P(E | T) = 1 and P(E) = 1, this gives P(T | E) = P(T).
(5) Therefore old evidence that a theory entails cannot raise the probability of the theory.
(6) But the precession of Mercury's perihelion was known long before the equations of general relativity were used to derive it, and it is usually counted as strong confirmation of the theory.
(7) So the Bayesian account of confirmation either conflicts with a genuine case of apparent confirmation or is forced to say that old evidence does not actually confirm.

I can't tell whether the argument is deductively valid and the problem is one of the premises, or whether there is an invalid inference hidden in how P(E) and P(T | E) are being used. Please tell me (a) whether the argument is valid as written; (b) which step is the decisive failure, if any; and (c) what the strongest reading of that step would be and how the argument would need to be revised. At the very end, write \answer{...} where the contents are either the number of the step that should be rejected or reformulated, or 'valid' if the argument should stand.
