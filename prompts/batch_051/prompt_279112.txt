As a journalist who covers forecasting, statistics, and long-term risk, I have spent enough time around the doomsday argument to notice something peculiar about its reception: the debate feels both settled and permanently frozen. The core reasoning is not really in dispute. You condition on your own position in the sequence of all humans who will ever live. Something like a hundred billion humans have lived so far, and that observation is far more probable on a hypothesis on which the species ends after a few hundred billion than on one on which it continues to produce trillions. If you adopt a self-sampling assumption and treat yourself as a random draw from the reference class of all humans, that likelihood shift pushes posterior probability toward comparatively near-term extinction. Critics answer that no principled rule fixes the reference class from which one is supposed to be drawn, that the conclusion changes if the class is expanded to all future intelligent agents or narrowed to some biologically defined group, and that a rival self-indication assumption, on which one's own existence is itself evidence favoring hypotheses with many observers, cancels or reverses the effect. Proponents answer that declining to update at all is declining to condition on an observation one actually possesses. Everyone agrees on the algebra, and each side can recite the other's next move before it arrives. Yet neither side has moved for decades.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about mathematics, and not primarily a disagreement about empirical extinction rates, but a standoff over bedrock methodological questions: whether and how probabilistic reasoning applies to indexical facts such as one's own existence and place in a sequence, and which self-locating principles are admissible before any evidence is considered. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, rather than a restatement of the arguments. Is it a verbal dispute about what probability means in this setting? Is it a clash of starting intuitions about self-locating evidence, where each side's foundational move is exactly what the other denies? Is it a sign that the question is ill-posed until a reference class or a decision problem is fixed? And under your diagnosis, what would genuinely count as progress, whether a sharper distinction, a formal result, or a concrete choice scenario that separates the principles, or is there reason to think no progress should be expected here?
