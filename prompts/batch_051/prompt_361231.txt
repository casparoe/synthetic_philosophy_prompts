I am a novelist, and a philosopher friend recently restated the familiar slogan that the mind stands to the brain as software stands to hardware, then extended it toward my own trade. She argued that a story stands to its medium as a mental state stands to its neural substrate: plot, characters, and themes survive translation across print, audiobook, film, and stage, so the story itself must be a functional kind, doing the real explanatory work while the medium merely executes it. From the fact that a single story is realized across many media, she drew the conclusion that mental states are equally multiply realizable, and that functionalism about the mind should be as uncontroversial as the existence of adaptations.

I cannot shake the suspicion that the analogy is doing a great deal of illicit work, but I lack the theoretical vocabulary to make the objection precise. My instinct as a novelist is that the medium is not an inert platform but part of the work's identity; I want to know whether that instinct is a genuine counterexample to her functionalism or merely a point about poor adaptation. Could you audit how far the analogy actually holds? Please give me the explicit correspondence table — what maps onto hardware, software, execution, porting, emulation, hardware-specific bugs, and the like — and then locate the disanalogies. I especially want to know which disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion about mental states and which are merely superficial, whether a story's survival across media really licenses multiple realizability in the philosophy of mind, and whether the functionalist conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the analogy collapses under scrutiny, tell me what a better analogy would be, or whether the conclusion would need independent support.
