Ok here's one that's been bugging me. A friend has an argument that seems to prove you should pay literally any finite amount of money for a ticket that, with overwhelming probability, pays nothing. That can't be right, but I can't find the exact step where it breaks. Here it is:

(1) For any gamble, a rational person should be willing to pay any price lower than the gamble's expected monetary value, at least if they only care about money.
(2) The ordinary St. Petersburg game works like this: flip a fair coin until it comes up heads; if the first heads is on flip n, the payout is 2^n dollars. Its expected value is 1 + 1 + 1 + ..., which diverges.
(3) So, from (1), you should be willing to pay any finite price—ten thousand dollars, ten billion, whatever—to play the ordinary St. Petersburg game.
(4) Now make the doomsday variant: same as the ordinary game, except your ticket also has to win a separate one-in-a-billion lottery drawn before the coin is flipped; if the lottery loses, you get zero. For each n, the chance of getting 2^n is now (1/1,000,000,000)(1/2^n), so the expected value is p + p + p + ... for p = 1/1,000,000,000, which still diverges. By (1), you should be wililing to pay any finite price for this too.
(5) But paying your life savings for a ticket that, with probability 0.999999999, pays zero, is plainly insane.
(6) So something in (1)–(4) must be wrong.

I'd rather keep some version of (1) than throw out expected-value reasoning altogether, but I want to know what I'm paying. Which exact step should go, and what's the cheapest fix that keeps (1) as a rule of thumb for ordinary, finite gambles but blocks the doomsday variant? Is the right move to bound the payoffs, to make the utility of money concave or even bounded, to restrict (1) to gambles with finite expected value, or something else? And what does each fix leave unexplained?
