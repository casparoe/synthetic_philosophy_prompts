I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a feature on procrastination and self-control. For background I read a popular book on decision-making whose overall thesis seems to be that weakness of will has been cracked by behavioral economics and neuroscience. The author summarizes the state of the field roughly as follows:

"What philosophers used to call weakness of will, or akrasia, is what behavioral economists now study under the name hyperbolic discounting: people discount future costs and benefits very steeply, so a smaller reward available now usually beats a larger reward that arrives later. Brain-imaging studies have localized this conflict, with delay discounting driven by the older limbic system and self-control housed in the prefrontal cortex, which is why weakness of will is essentially the emotional brain outvoting the rational brain. In the strictest sense this makes the weak-willed person irrational, because her preferences flip as the tempting option moves closer in time — she is a different agent at different times — and her choices can therefore never be unified into a single stable set of values. The practical punchline is that you should either bind yourself in advance or, better, train the prefrontal cortex the way you would train a muscle, and the problem of weakness of will largely dissolves."

Before I recycle this account in my article, I'd like to know how much of it is defensible. Could you go through the summary and separate what a behavioral economist would accept from what one would object to, what a philosopher working on akrasia would add or correct, and whether the practical punchline actually follows from the parts that survive scrutiny? I'm particularly suspicious of the opening claim that weakness of will just is hyperbolic discounting, but I don't have the technical background to judge whether that suspicion is warranted.
