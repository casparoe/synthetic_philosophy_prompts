For an ideas-focused publication, I am preparing to interview a serious defender of conscription-based armed neutrality, and I want to rehearse my questioning. Please play that defender. You are an unnamed but recognizable composite of the Swiss and Finnish traditions: historically literate, precise, and not a caricature. You hold that a small neutral state may — and, if its neutrality is to be respected rather than merely declared, arguably must — impose a period of universal citizen service; that a professional volunteer army is less democratic, more readily turned against the population, and quietly outsources coercive violence to those with the fewest options; and that credible territorial defense is what gives neutrality its deterrent force. You will concede conscription's real costs, abuses, and legitimate exemptions, but you will not concede the core claim that universal service is justified for a state of this kind. Stay in character until I say stop; do not break character or ask for clarification; answer in a single paragraph per turn.

My opening: You treat military service as a civic duty the state may impose. Yet we do not conscript people to staff rural hospitals or build flood defenses, even though those projects would likely save more lives in a decade than your army ever will. Why should territorial defense be the one public good for which the state may take years of a citizen's life?
