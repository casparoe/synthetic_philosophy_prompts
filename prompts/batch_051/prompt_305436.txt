I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for many years, and I am putting together a companion handout for a sequence on Kelly betting and geometric rationality. I would like a diagnostic quiz of the sort that classifies people by how they answer concrete cases rather than by which label they prefer for themselves. Please write the quiz, the scoring key, and the outcome descriptions, keeping everything self-contained: no citations, reading lists, or historical attributions.

The quiz should sort respondents among six positions on rational wagering. I will describe them rather than name them, so that a reader does not need prior knowledge:

1. Naive expected-value maximizer. Judges any bet by its arithmetic expected gain in money, and bets as much as possible whenever that number is positive.
2. Full-Kelly growth purist. Holds that the only defensible objective is maximizing the long-run growth rate of the bankroll, equivalently expected log wealth, and would play the corresponding fraction even for a single favorable bet with fully known probabilities.
3. Expected-utility risk-aversion view. Treats every bet, single or repeated, as a one-period choice under a concave utility function over wealth. Log utility is just one point on that curve, and the fraction it implies is canonical only if the person happens to have log preferences.
4. Time-average / ergodicity view. Holds that what matters is the time-average growth rate of a single bettor's wealth through time, not the ensemble average over many parallel copies. It is willing to reject the ensemble average as the thing to optimize, because that average is dominated by rare lucky paths.
5. Fractional-Kelly-for-model-uncertainty view. Shades below the full-Kelly fraction because the model of the payoff distribution may be misspecified or fatter-tailed than assumed, not because of risk aversion. It would not shade when the setup is known exactly.
6. Drawdown-constrained bettor. Chooses the fraction that keeps the probability of a ruinous or practically unrecoverable drawdown below a specified threshold, and treats that constraint as lexically prior to growth-rate maximization.

The quiz should have eight questions. Each question should present a concrete, self-contained wagering scenario, such as a biased coin, a deck of cards with a known composition, a sequence of identical bets, a forced precommitment to a fixed fraction, or a thought experiment involving many parallel copies of the respondent. Each question should give three answer options. No question may use the loaded vocabulary: Kelly, log utility, expected value, ergodicity, ensemble average, growth rate, risk aversion, or drawdown. The options should be concrete choices or plain-language reasons, and each should be answerable by someone who has never encountered the positions above.

The eight questions must include: two that vary only in whether the favorable bet is offered once or a large number of times; two that vary only in whether the relevant probabilities are known or estimated from a small sample; one that presents a thousand parallel copies each facing the same sequence and asks whether the average across copies or the typical single copy matters more; one that forces a precommitment to a fixed fraction across many future bets before any of them is seen; and one in which the bankroll is the respondent's actual living money, with the stipulation that a fifty-percent loss would be practically catastrophic.

The scoring key should assign each answer option to the position or positions it supports, and should specify a tie-breaking rule. Because some of these positions are behaviorally similar and differ mainly in their rationale rather than in the fractions they recommend, the question set must include at least one pair designed to separate positions empirically, such as the known-probability question and the estimated-probability question separating risk-aversion shading from model-uncertainty shading.

Write a one-sentence summary for each position describing the answer pattern that would reveal it, followed by a short paragraph telling that respondent what their pattern commits them to and where it becomes awkward.

End with a short diagnostic appendix. It should state which pairs of positions the quiz cannot reliably separate, and should note any classificatory cells that are empty or only weakly coherent, in particular the combination of evaluating a single play while maximizing a long-run growth rate, and the combination of maximizing the ensemble average over a long sequence. For each empty or awkward cell, say briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The register should be a plain rationalist-forum register rather than an academic one.
