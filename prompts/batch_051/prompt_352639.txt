I grew up with a folktale that my family tells in five or six different forms, and I have always been struck by two things at once: everyone agrees it is the same story, and everyone is quite sure that some tellings get it wrong — even though nobody has ever written the story down. Lately I tried to make those two convictions precise alongside a third one I also hold, that no version of the story is the true original standing behind the rest. The result is an argument whose every step felt forced while I wrote it, and whose conclusion I still cannot accept:

(1) A story has no existence apart from its tellings; there is no true original behind them. (2) So if two tellings are versions of one story, that cannot be because they each match something which is not itself a telling. (3) It must be because they match each other. (4) But no two actual tellings match exactly: even the most careful retelling changes phrasing, emphasis, detail, or the order of episodes. (5) Therefore, strictly speaking, no two tellings are versions of the same story; each telling is its own story, and talk of "variants" and "versions" is loose talk that would not survive being made exact. (6) If that is right, then no retelling can be wrong — a version in which the hero never leaves the village and the monster is never fought would be not a corruption but simply another story — and the question of which version is canonical dissolves, because there is nothing for a version to be canonical with respect to. (7) But that is absurd: some retellings plainly do get the story wrong, and my family does not treat just any invented change as acceptable, despite never having had a written text.

I would most like to keep (1), (2), and (7). Each of (3) through (6) seemed forced when I wrote it down, but I cannot name which step is the one to reject, or what I would have to put in its place — in particular, what it would mean for two tellings to be the same story if there is no original for them to match and yet not every change counts as fine.
