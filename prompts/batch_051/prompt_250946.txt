I am a novelist, now writing a book in which a character, after reading popular accounts of relativity, becomes convinced that the future is already written. In preparing the book I have been working through the physics and its philosophy, and I keep coming back to a parallel I cannot decide about; I would like the equivalent of a careful critical reading of the analogy, from someone who knows the topic better than I do.

The physical idea, as I understand it, is this. Special relativity gives us no frame-independent way to separate the universe into a single advancing now. Different inertial observers slice the four-dimensional manifold of events differently, and an argument associated with Rietdijk and Putnam tries to turn this into the conclusion that no fundamental distinction between past, present, and future can be drawn: all events in spacetime are equally real, and the flow of time is an artifact of the observer's perspective rather than a feature of the world. The result is the picture usually called the block universe.

The novelist's situation looks to me structurally identical. I hold a finished manuscript in which every scene is written; the last chapter is no less fully there than the first. The sense that the scene currently under the reader's eye is now is an artifact of the reader's linear traversal of the text, not a property of the text itself. The character experiences a moving edge of becoming; the author, looking at the whole thing at once, sees a fixed block of scenes in which nothing comes into being. The reader's present-tense experience stands to the completed book roughly as the observer's now stands to the block universe.

I can already see one place where the analogy may strain. A novel has a canonical order: the printed sequence of pages is a privileged ordering, and the story's causal and psychological connections run along it, whereas the physical argument works precisely because spacetime has no canonical slicing into moments and no preferred global now. So I want to know whether that difference is fatal, or whether the right mapping is narrower—the reader's traversal mapped to the observer's now, the completed text mapped to the manifold—so that the analogy survives the disanalogy.

Please assess the parallel rather than merely elaborate it. State the best version of the mapping, element by element. Say what would have to be true of both sides for any result to transfer from the physics to how we think about narrative time, or from narrative craft back to the metaphysics. Name at least one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false: a claim about stories, or about spacetime, that someone who took the analogy at face value would be led to make and that an expert in the relevant field would reject.

Throughout, keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on the physics and its philosophy. I have read enough to know that the move from the relativity of simultaneity to the block universe is not simply the consensus of physicists or philosophers of physics: presentists, growing-block theorists, and others resist the inference, and the Rietdijk–Putnam argument depends on assumptions about what it is for an event to be real. Do not present the eternalist conclusion as established. If the way I have stated the physical idea is itself a contentious construal, say so, and flag whether the parallel would survive under the rival construals.
