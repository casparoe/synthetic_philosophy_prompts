A friend and I spent the greater part of last evening arguing over a single question: when a piece of music is rightly described as sad, is the sadness a property of the sound itself, a fact about a composer or performer, or something the listener contributes? Neither of us could say which claims were doing the work in our intuitions. I should like you to turn the disagreement into a diagnostic instrument: a sorting quiz, of the kind a philosophy-of-music blog might run, under a title such as "Which Theory of Musical Sadness Do You Actually Hold?" It should sort readers by what they say about concrete cases, not by which label they like.

The quiz should contain eight questions. Each question should present a short, inventively described musical scenario and offer two or three answer options that force a choice about where the sadness is located. Please use, or adapt, cases along the following lines, and add any further cases that cleanly separate the views:

- a minor-key tune generated by an algorithm, with no composer, performer, or title, played through a laptop speaker;
- a recording of a person's voice breaking while she describes something wholly trivial, alongside a slowed-down recording of a baby's cry;
- the same descending phrase used at a wedding in one tradition and as a lament in another;
- a performer who plays a slow, minor-key movement with a blank expression and afterwards says she felt nothing at all while playing;
- a listener going through a bereavement who finds an upbeat major-key tune unbearably sad, while a second listener finds the same tune merely cheerful;
- a listener told, falsely, that a piece was composed by someone in deep grief, and then told that it was generated by a computer;
- a listener with congenital amusia who cannot identify the piece as sad but reports it as heavy or dragging.

The scoring key should be built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: begin from the combinations of answers the eight questions allow, then place the positions in that space. It should map answer patterns onto at least these positions, stated in plain terms: the view that sadness is a real sonic property literally perceived in melodic contour and tempo; the view that we hear the music as the expression of an imagined persona; the view that we recognise a resemblance between the music and human expressive behaviour; the view that sadness is a learned appraisal, shaped by culture and personal history, not a feature of the sounds; the view that the emotion word tracks only a loose family of acoustic features, so there is no fact of the matter about whether the piece really is sad; and the view that what we call sadness is primarily a felt bodily response the music induces or affords. If any combination of answers has no plausible occupant, say so, and indicate whether the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. Flag any pair of these positions that the questions cannot reliably separate, and add a sentence on what sort of case would separate them.

No question should simply ask which of the above views the reader endorses, or whether the sadness is "in" the music. Phrase every question so that it can be answered without knowing any of the labels. Keep the tone light but precise, write throughout in British English, and fit the whole instrument — questions, answer options, and scoring key — within 1,200 words.
