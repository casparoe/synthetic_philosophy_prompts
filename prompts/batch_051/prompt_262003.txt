I have been reading around the recent alignment literature on making a model's reasoning legible rather than merely correct. The idea, as I have understood it, is that a model should be trained not only to reach the right answer but to produce rationales a person can actually follow, and that this is a distinct aim from either accuracy or post-hoc explanation. An essay I came across summarises the position like this:

'The central problem with contemporary systems is not accuracy but opacity. The proposal is therefore to treat understandability as a training objective in its own right. Since an explanation that lets a human see why the model answered as it did is just what it means for the model to honestly report its own reasoning, the natural implementation is to reward models for producing reasoning that human evaluators find convincing. Once a model reliably produces such reasoning, we may treat those explanations as faithful descriptions of the model's actual computation; and any residual mismatch between the stated reasoning and the internal processing can be trained away by penalising disagreement between the generated explanation and the model's own confidence scores.'

Is this a fair summary? I am inclined to think it runs together the idea that an output is understandable with the idea that the model is saying something true about how it reached that output, but I cannot make the case precisely. Please go through it roughly sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful defender of legibility-as-a-training-objective would accept, what they would wince at, and what they would flatly deny, and whether the final step follows from anything true earlier in the paragraph. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions and avoid presenting a contested view as the consensus. British English, please.
