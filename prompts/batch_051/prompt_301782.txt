I run an after-school maths and philosophy club at a sixth-form college, about 18 students aged 16–17, 75 minutes. I want an activity that makes them feel the force of Bertrand's paradox — that "chosen completely at random" can mean several different things, each giving a different probability, so the principle of indifference doesn't hand them a single answer. The thing is, they shouldn't meet the paradox as a lecture or a named result: I want them to run three different "random chord factories", get three different answers, get properly annoyed about who is cheating, and only then find out that all three factories were being fair.

Design it as a runnable workshop. 18 students in three groups of six. I've got 75 minutes and normal classroom kit: printed circles with an inscribed equilateral triangle, rulers, spinners, dice, counters, string, whiteboard. Give me an exact timeline, the three protocol cards word-for-word, roles for six people per group (including a rotating auditor so groups don't drift), a way of tallying long vs short chords, and a structured debrief.

I want the debrief to do three things: map what happened in each factory onto a precise way of making "random chord" precise — random endpoints on the rim, random point along a random radius, random midpoint in the disk — and say what each one assumes is uniformly distributed; then connect the disagreement to the principle of indifference and why equal ignorance doesn't choose between these; then ask them whether any of the three has a better claim to be the real "random chord", and what you'd have to believe about the circle or about physical symmetry to say yes.

Also cover predictable failure modes: what to do when the counter-tossing for the midpoint method isn't actully uniform, or when one group gets a noisy run of 30 and blames the method, or when someone tries to "fix" their protocol halfway through. Tell me how to handle each without giving the game away. And be honest about which part of the paradox the activity can't produce on its own — I suspect it's that the three answers are supposed to be exact (1/3, 1/2, 1/4) under idealised uniform distributions, whereas thier runs will be noisy, so I'll have to supply that bit — but tell me if there's a smarter way to stage it so the exactness shows up in the room rather than in my explanation.

Assume they've not met the paradox and don't know the names. I'd rather they collide with it themselves. Don't give me a lecture plan; give me a game with rules and a debrief.
