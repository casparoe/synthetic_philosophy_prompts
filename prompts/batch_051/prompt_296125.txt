I've been going back and forth with a friend about what actually makes something a state in the international-relations sense — the kind of thing that can sign treaties and join the UN, not Ohio or Bavaria. The usual starting point is the Montevideo criteria (a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states), but recognition keeps sticking out as a problem. Some entities satisfy all four and still get treated as not-quite-states, while other entities fail at least one and yet nobody treats them as nonexistent diplomatically.

So I want a working definition of "state" — the political/legal kind, not a mental state — that sorts out these cases: France; the Vatican; Taiwan; Kosovo; Palestine; Somaliland; a government-in-exile that a dozen or so states still recognize as the government of a country it no longer controls; and Sealand, that old anti-aircraft platform off the coast of England whose owner's family declared it an independent principality. France and Sealand feel like the easy endpoints to me, and I genuinly don't know what to do with the Vatican — it has basically no permanent population in the ordinary sense, but nobody thinks it's not a state. I have a hunch the declaratory theory (meet the criteria, you're a state) and the constitutive theory (you're a state if enough others treat you as one) are each missing something. Could you propose a definition that gets the easy cases right, run it through all of these, and tell me which verdicts you're least sure about or which cases force you to revise it?
