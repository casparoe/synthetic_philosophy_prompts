I'm enrolled in an upper-level moral psychology seminar, and we have just finished the unit on the situationist critique of virtue ethics. In the form we read it, the argument leans on a set of classic social-psychology experiments—someone failing to help after unexpectedly finding a coin in a phone booth, seminarians stepping past a person in visible distress when they are late for a talk, participants continuing to deliver shocks because an experimenter instructs them to—to conclude that there is little evidence for the stable, cross-situational character traits that Aristotelian virtue ethics seems to require. On this picture, behaviour is driven less by character than by morally arbitrary features of the immediate situation.

What I cannot stop thinking about is that this empirical foundation has since been drawn into the replication crisis in social psychology. Many of the field's celebrated demonstrations of situational power have proven fragile under direct replication, effect sizes have arrived smaller than originally reported, and the person-situation debate no longer looks so clearly settled in favour of the situation. My question is about what this does to the philosophical argument, not about the replication debate itself. Does the situationist critique survive the replication crisis? I want to know which premise the replication results actually bear on: the specific empirical claims about this or that classic study, the general claim that situations overwhelm character, or the conceptual claim that virtue ethics is committed to global traits in the first place. Is there a weakened but still serious version of the critique that a contemporary virtue ethicist would still need to answer? And if even that version collapses, should the conclusion be that virtue ethics has been empirically vindicated, or rather that the empirical question about character traits is simply open again?
