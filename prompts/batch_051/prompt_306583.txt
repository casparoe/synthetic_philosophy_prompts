I run the human-annotation side of a dataset of scanned handwritten school exams, and we keep having the same fight: which marks count as the letter 'a'. Annotators label every 'a' on a page, and when they're inconsistent the OCR model we train picks up garbage. I need a reusable decision procedure an annotator can run in under two minutes per mark, with no typography background, that says when a mark is an 'a' and when it isn't, and that's defensible when the annotation lead spot-checks and contests a call. Ground it in what's actually going on with letter categories and style—how much of being an 'a' is a role in the writing system rather than a particular shape, how style can deform features without destroying identity, and why the same blob with a stem is an 'a' in one kid's hand and an 'o' in another's. Give me a sequence of questions or tests, say where it stops with a yes or a no, and flag the points where the annotator has to make a judgment call rather than follow a rule. Then apply the procedure to three concrete cases: a textbook single-story 'a'; a double-story 'a' written fast enough that the upper bowl closes and it looks like a 'd'; and a Greek alpha a kid clearly used in place of an 'a'. Make at least one case where the procedure gives an answer the annotation team won't like, and say what the procedure can't decide.
