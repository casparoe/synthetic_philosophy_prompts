I have settled into a strongly bottom-up view of causation in layered systems, and I want to know exactly what it would take to dislodge me. My test case is the careenium. Suppose the careenium is a vast array of dominoes: the only events at the base level are dominoes toppling their neighbors, but the array is engineered so that those topplings realize a simple billiards-like game played by a few discs—the simmballs—which move, collide, and carom in accordance with the game's rules. Every simmball event is realized by a specific cascade of domino falls. On the view I have accepted, only the dominoes do any pushing. A sentence such as the simmball's collision with the cushion caused domino cascade C is at best a compression of a purely domino-level causal history and at worst a category mistake. I extend this to all macro-level patterns and symbols—program states, mental states, representations—right down to the substrate: the substrate does all the causal work, and higher-level descriptions, however real as patterns, are causally redundant.

Tell me what would count against this view. I am not asking for the standard objections alone; I want a ranked map of the view's exposure. Which concrete variations on the careenium would make it false—or make it correct—to say that a simmball literally pushed a domino? Which accounts of causation, intervention, realization, or explanation, if right, would undermine the inference from the domino level suffices for the cascade to the simmball level is causally inert? Which considerations would be fatal, which merely awkward, and which would a committed bottom-up theorist rightly dismiss as not genuinely counting against it? Where has the position been protected by qualifications rather than arguments, and is that protection principled or a way of making the view unfalsifiable? Finally, tell me whether the view as stated here risks enough to be worth holding, or whether it has been insulated into triviality.
