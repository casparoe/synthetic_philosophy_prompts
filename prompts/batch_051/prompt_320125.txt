Alright, I've been trying to get clear on my own view about interpersonal utility comparisons, and I keep failing to find the principle, so I'll lay out my verdicts and see if you can find it for me. Here are the cases as concretely as I can make them; the question at the end.

(1) My own pain now versus my own pain this morning: trivial. My migraine right now is worse than the headache I woke up with. Cross-person but same kind: if you tell me you have a migraine like mine, I have no trouble saying your migraine is worse than my mild headache, and I'd place a bet on it.

(2) Two students both had the same dental surgery. Both rate the pain afterwards a 7 out of 10. I treat those as roughly equal amounts of pain. If I had one strong painkiller to hand out in some stupid triage scenario, I'd essentially flip a coin, and I wouldn't feel I'd made a moral mistake either way.

(3) A dollar matters more to my graduate student than to a billionaire. I'm willing to say—in expectation, at least—that moving a dollar from the billionaire to the student increases total utility, and I'd endorse policies that do this at scale.

(4) But ask me whose Sunday is better between two friends, one who gets deep satisfaction from birdwatching and one who gets deep satisfaction from restoring old motorcycles, and I freeze. Both tell me it's one of the best parts of their week. I find I can't even say they're equal; it doesn't feel like the sort of thing that has an answer I can get to.

(5) Trade-offs across different kinds of badness leave me similarly stuck. I have no grip on wether one person's six months of moderate depression is worse than another person's two weeks of intense back pain. I'm not saying there's no fact of the matter; I'm saying I can't access it.

(6) I notice I also don't take self-reports at face value uniformly. If someone adapted to a chronic condition says their life is going fine, I believe they mean it, but I don't use that report the way I use an acute pain report. If anything, I want to give their situation more weight than they do themselves.

(7) Yet at the level of whole lives I make the coarse comparisons I refuse to make at the level of Sunday hobbies. I'll happily say a life of severe chronic pain is worse than a life with a mild limp, other things equal, and I don't retract that just because the two people might have different tastes.

So I'll compare pains (1-2), the marginal value of money (3), and whole lives at a coarse level (7), but I balk at comparing hobbies (4) and at trading depression against back pain (5), and I discount some self-reports but not others (6). The mismatches that bother me most are (2) against (5), and (3) against (4). What principle, or small set of principles, makes sense of most of this? State it precisely, test it on cases I haven't listed—say, comparing a dog's post-surgical pain to a person's headache, or one person's boredom to another's anxiety—and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle forces me to drop. Don't just tell me comparisons are hard; I want to know where the line falls and what justifies putting it there.
