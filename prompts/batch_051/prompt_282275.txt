I have converged on a particular view of generic sentences, and I want a map of what would count against it rather than a defense of it. The view, roughly, is this. Bare plural generics such as *ducks lay eggs* or *mosquitoes carry malaria* are not statistical generalizations about most members of the kind. They express that the predicate holds of the kind by default: that the property is part of the normal, characteristic, or expected course of things for that kind. This explains why a generic can be true at low prevalence (*mosquitoes carry malaria*, though very few mosquitoes do) and false at high prevalence (*books are paperbacks*, though most books are). It also explains why our acceptance of a generic seems sensitive to how striking, costly, or characteristic the property is, and why we treat generics as defeasible rules rather than as frequency claims: from *birds fly* and *penguins are birds* we do not reason as we would from a statistical major premise. I take this to be a semantic or quasi-semantic fact about the construction, not a pragmatic gloss imposed on a statistical core.

The position as I hold it predicts that generic judgments will not track prevalence in any simple way, and that the same sentence can become unacceptable when a property becomes newly or differently characteristic of a kind even if its prevalence is unchanged or rises.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters sorted rather than listed. In particular:

- Which linguistic data would a committed default/normality theorist themselves admit as counterevidence or at least as strain on the account, as opposed to data they would dismiss as pragmatic noise or as belonging to a different construction?
- Which findings from psychology, cross-linguistic work, formal semantics, or the study of nonmonotonic reasoning would be decisive against the view, and which would be merely awkward?
- Where does the view plainly overgenerate or undergenerate — properties a kind has by default whose generic is nonetheless false, or properties that are neither characteristic nor statistically typical whose generic is nonetheless true — and can it absorb those cases without becoming unfalsifiable?
- Where does the position risk collapsing into one of its rivals, either a statistical account dressed up with pragmatics or an essentialist account on which generics just track something like the kind's nature, so that the distinction I am drawing stops doing work?
- Finally, is the position as I have stated it well insulated by its qualifications, and is that insulation here a virtue or a sign that it has stopped risking anything?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which of them the view's defenders would themselves treat as counting against it rather than as challenges they set aside. I would personally find a result showing that generic acceptance tracks prevalence in a way the view cannot explain its shifted judgments the most concerning, but I do not want that prior conviction to organize the answer.
