I am a PhD student in developmental cognitive science, and I have spent the year trying to reconstruct the criterion behind my supervisor's innateness judgments. In lab meetings she is willing to call some capacities 'innate' and others 'not innate,' but she has never stated the principle; she only rules on cases. The record I have kept:

1. Newborns' visual preference for face-like configurations over equally complex scrambled controls: she calls this innate.
2. Learning to read an alphabetic script: she calls this not innate.
3. The early production and recognition of basic emotional expressions, illustrated by congenitally blind children who have never seen a face: she calls this innate.
4. Children's structure-dependent generalization in syntax — the fact that learners never settle on a structure-blind rule for yes-no question formation even though the input they hear is, by itself, compatible with one (the poverty-of-stimulus example she returns to most often): she calls this innate.
5. Absolute pitch: she calls this not innate, even in someone who developed it through early musical training.

I need to predict how she will classify a new case that has come up in our reading group: the ability to walk upright. It appears in almost all typically developing children on a species-typical schedule, without being explicitly taught, and it is remarkably robust across differences in childrearing; but its details are tuned by sensorimotor exploration, and one could argue that for a creature with the right limbs and feedback loops the available input is not too poor to explain the outcome.

Please deliver four things. First, at least two distinct principles that are jointly consistent with all five of her recorded verdicts. Second, the verdict each candidate principle predicts for walking upright. Third, your best guess about her actual classification, with reasons for preferring one principle over the others given this particular record. Fourth, a further case — real or invented — that would cleanly distinguish the candidate principles, and what each principle would say about it.
