I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on philosophical issues in privacy, and I want to build one week around Alfred Hitchcock's Rear Window (1954) as the centerpiece for thinking about the value of privacy. I keep going back and forth about what the film actually shows. On one reading, the courtyard is a portrait of privacy's value: each neighbor has made a small, ordinary life visible only through a window, and Jeff's camera-enabled watching turns them into specimens, which the film treats as an almost sexualized intrusion. On the other reading, the film quietly undermines any strong right to be unobserved, because Jeff's obsessive looking is what notices Mrs. Thorwald's disappearance and catches a murderer, and the ending seems to side with the voyeur. I want a reading for students who have read a little about privacy as control over access to persons and information, but not the subtler views that treat privacy as necessary for intimacy, role-appropriate access, or the constitution of the self.

I'd like that reading to say which conception of privacy the film presupposes or argues for — seclusion, control over observation and inference, intimacy, refuge for role-play, something else — and where the film complicates each. What should I do with the moral luck of the ending: does catching Thorwald vindicate Jeff's watching, or does the film mark the discovery as both a wrong and a rescue at once, and what would that imply for the view that privacy violations are wrong because of what they do to the watched person regardless of downstream benefits? Which scenes should I put in tension with each other (Miss Lonelyhearts, the newlyweds, the dead dog and the scream, Lisa breaking into Thorwald's apartment, the final shot of the courtyard), and what does each contribute to the argument rather than merely illustrating it? Finally, what does the film let us see about privacy that standard control-based arguments do not, and where should I tell students the cinematic metaphor distorts the philosophical issue? Keep the response to at most 1,000 words.
