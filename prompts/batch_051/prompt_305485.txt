I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar with a unit on causal inference and the interpretation of aggregate data; there are about eighteen students and I have one seventy-five-minute session set aside for Simpson's paradox. Design me a structured in-class game that makes them feel the paradox before it is named: the moment of betting confidently on a conclusion from summary data and then watching that conclusion reverse when the same data are disaggregated. The reversal should be something they produce themselves — they should reconstruct or recompute the aggregate from the stratified table and satisfy themselves that the arithmetic is not a mistake — so that the paradox arrives as an experience rather than an announced result. Avoid the familiar textbook data sets; invent a clean domain such as admissions, hospital outcomes, or treatment response, and choose numbers that are easy to work with but make the reversal genuinely unsettling. By the end I want them to hold two things at once: that the aggregate and the stratified analyses can both be arithmetically correct, and that arithmetic alone cannot tell them which one to trust.

Give me the rules and roles, the tables for the handouts, a point scheme that does not use real money, a timeline, and a debrief. The game should move through three phases: an initial judgment under the aggregate data with points at stake; a stratified phase in which the reversal appears and groups must redo the arithmetic themselves; and a final phase in which each group must defend, to a skeptical opposing group or to me, which answer they would now report and why. The debrief should map each moment of the game onto the formal features of the paradox — the reversal, the unequal distribution of cases across strata, the confounding variable, and the difference between a descriptive comparison and a causal question — and it should make clear that whether the aggregate or the stratified answer is correct depends on a causal story about the stratification variable. Include at least one miniature case in which aggregation is the correct analysis, so that students do not walk away thinking disaggregation always wins.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I anticipate at least the "these numbers must be wrong" reaction and the overcorrection I just described — and be candid about which feature of Simpson's paradox the game cannot deliver on its own and that I will need to supply from the front.
