A friend and I recently stayed up far too late arguing about whether the so-called miraculous gifts of the Spirit — tongues, prophecy, words of knowledge, healings — are for the church today, and I came away convinced that a great deal of the disagreement was really about labels rather than about what each of us would say in concrete cases. I am putting together a handout for an adult education class at my church, and I would like you to design a "which position do you actually hold?" decision tree for the cessationist–continuationist debate. The instrument should sort people by what they affirm or deny about the cases it presents, not by which label they volunteer for themselves, so no node should simply ask whether the respondent is a cessationist or a continuationist.

Construct the tree from the logical space first: think about the questions on which the positions genuinely come apart, then locate the actual positions within that space, and briefly mark any combination of answers that is logically open but that no well-known position occupies, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why it is empty (incoherent, biblically unattractive, or merely unexplored). The leaves should include at least hard cessationism (the extraordinary gifts were signs of the apostolic age and have ceased), soft cessationism (the revelatory and attesting gifts have ceased, but God still works extraordinary healings and interventions today), open-but-cautious continuationism (all the gifts remain available in principle, but modern prophecy is fallible and must be tested against Scripture), and charismatic continuationism (tongues and prophecy are expected, ordinary features of the Spirit-filled life, with genuine though subordinate revelation). Each branch node should be phrased as a question an adult member could answer from conviction without knowing where the branch leads.

Please work in at least these concrete cases, expressed for congregants rather than theologians: a missionary's medically documented healing after prayer that later relapses; a well-meaning member who prefaces advice with the words "the Lord told me to tell you"; a new worshipper speaking in tongues during a service; and a pastor who believes he received specific guidance about a leadership appointment in prayer. The audience is a congregation with both non-charismatic and charismatic backgrounds, and the tree should run to roughly ten to twelve nodes so it can be used in a forty-five minute session. Include a routing key so the facilitator can move people from node to leaf, a one-sentence note at each leaf on that view's most awkward commitment, and an explicit flag for any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate — I suspect soft cessationism and open-but-cautious continuationism may merge on several routes and differ mainly over whether a fallible gift of prophecy is operative today. Do not simply hand back the standard encyclopedia contrast; I want the distinctions that actually do the sorting work.
