I recently had a long argument with a friend about whether you can be justified in believing a disjunction — for instance, that either Alice or Ben broke the vase — without being justified in believing either disjunct. My friend said yes: you can rule out everyone else and thereby know it must be one of the two, while having no evidence against either in particular. I resisted, and afterward noticed a possible parallel that unsettled me. In constructive mathematics, a proof of "P or Q" is taken to be a proof of P or a proof of Q, and a proof of "there exists an x such that P(x)" is taken to supply a witness; showing that the alternatives lead to contradiction does not by itself establish the disjunction or the existence claim. On my friend's view, ordinary reasoning is structurally classical: "not both alternatives fail" is treated as sufficient for "one of them holds," and "not all x fail P" is treated as sufficient for "some x is P." Is this a deep structural parallel between constructive proof and a norm of justified belief, or is it a loose analogy? If it is deep, what would a constructivist epistemology say about testimony, statistical evidence, or a disjunction reached only by eliminating alternatives; if it is not, where exactly does the analogy fail?
