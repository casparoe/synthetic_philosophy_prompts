I find myself drawn to a broadly Marxian structural account of exploitation and alienation, and I also have an ordinary conviction that appears to cut against it. I want to know which should give way.

The structural account, as I understand it, runs like this. Exploitation is not fundamentally about individual bargains but about the background relation between those who own the means of production and those who, owning only their labor power, must sell access to it on terms they do not set. On this view a wage bargain can be exploitative even when it is voluntary by every local test and even when the worker is better off accepting it than not: she produces value beyond what she receives back as wages, the surplus is controlled by others, and the continuing need to return to the labor market reproduces the inequality that weakened her bargaining position in the first place. Alienation follows the same pattern: the work can be called estranged even if the worker reports satisfaction, because the goals, pace, and product are ultimately governed from outside. I find the account compelling because it unifies several things at once—why profit persists across improvements in wages and conditions, why reforms improve terms without removing dependence, and why a wider menu of jobs is not the same as freedom.

But I cannot shake a firm intuition on the other side. Imagine a worker with genuine alternatives who freely takes a job, is paid well enough that the exchange is Pareto-improving relative to her actual next-best option, would take the offer again, and finds the work meaningful. There seems to be no identifiable person whom the transaction itself wrongs, and no basis for third parties to condemn her choice as something done to her. If I tell her she is exploited and alienated, I seem to be substituting my theory for her agency: treating a structurally unequal background as though it made her advantage-taking local choice wrongful. If the local exchange wrongs no one, the charge of exploitation begins to look like a complaint about the unfairness of the world rather than about anything she or her employer did.

If both descriptions are accurate, I need a negotiated verdict. Give me the strongest version of each side. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes—would I need to show that voluntariness and Pareto-improvement are compatible with domination, or that the intuition is status-quo bias that treats the existing distribution as given? What would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes, or to be amended—would I need to give up the idea that individual transactions inherit wrongness from the background structure and settle for a structural critique that makes no claim about individual wrongdoing? Or is there a reading on which both survive, such as treating exploitation as a relational defect of the background structure while keeping the case-level judgment that this worker was not wronged by the exchange? Do not just pick a side; tell me what each concession costs and which combination is most defensible. End with one line of the form \answer{...} enclosing a short label for your bottom-line verdict, e.g., \answer{the intuition is debunked}, \answer{the theory is amended}, or \answer{compatible on a relational reading}.
