In a recent discussion about obedience and authority, a colleague defended what he called the division-of-labor view of deference. His argument was that obeying a legitimate authority is, in its essentials, the same kind of act as deferring to an expert: when I let a surgeon choose a course of treatment, or a navigator choose a route through a storm, I am not surrendering my judgment but rationally relying on someone who is better positioned to know what the situation calls for. By parity of reasoning, he claimed, a soldier who carries out a lawful order or a junior official who follows a superior's directive is doing nothing more troubling, and deference is therefore a virtue in both cases. On his telling, what goes wrong in the Milgram obedience experiments is not deference itself but only deference to an authority who had not genuinely earned it.

I suspect this analogy between epistemic deference and hierarchical deference is doing most of the argumentative work, and that it quietly imports features of expertise — demonstrable competence, a narrow and well-defined domain, the possibility of checking a track record, and the ease of withdrawing trust — into settings where those features are absent or structurally different. One thing in particular bothers me: the experimenter in Milgram's study presented all the outward markers of scientific competence, so the colleague's own analogy would seem to predict that the subjects were deferring rationally, which is the opposite of the intended moral. Please audit the analogy. Give me an explicit mapping between the two cases: what corresponds to the expert, the patient or client, the domain of competence, the evidence of reliability, and the act of deference itself. Then identify where the analogy breaks down, separating the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that are merely incidental. Finally, say whether the conclusion — that deference to authority is a virtue in the same way deference to expertise is, and that the Milgram subjects went wrong only by deferring to someone who had not earned their deference — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what a better analogy would be, if there is one.
