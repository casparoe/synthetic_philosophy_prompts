I have been trying to work out the consequences of Abelard's claim that sin is constituted entirely by the will's consent rather than by the outward act, and a colleague has turned it into an argument whose conclusion I can't accept. Here is the argument as he gave it, with sin treated as moral wrongdoing generally and not as a narrowly theological category.

(1) A sin consists entirely in consenting to what the agent takes to be forbidden. If a person is physically forced, there is no sin; if a person consents fully but is prevented from acting, the sin is already complete.

(2) The outward deed therefore adds nothing to the sin itself: once the consent has been formed, the deed neither increases nor completes it.

(3) So before God—the only tribunal at which inner guilt is finally settled—the guilt of a completed wrongdoing is exactly the guilt of the very same wrongdoing merely attempted or merely consented to.

(4) Yet human practice grades wrongdoing by outcomes. The embezzler caught before the funds clear is dealt with less severely than the one who succeeds; attempted murder is punished less than completed murder; a secret slanderer is treated differently from one whose victim is publicly ruined.

(5) If the outcome has no weight in the settlement of guilt, then these differences in treatment respond to something morally inert.

(6) To impose heavier burdens on someone because of something morally inert is unjust.

(7) Therefore the practice of grading penance and punishment by the outward deed is unjust, and the distinctions between attempt and completion, and between secret and public wrongdoing, should collapse wherever judgments of persons are made.

The conclusion seems to prove too much, or at least to cost too much: a discipline that ignored outcomes entirely would be unrecognizable, and I am not willing to say that completed murder and merely contemplated murder should be treated alike by human beings. Yet I am least willing to give up (1)—that outward events cannot alter the state of a person's will is what drew me to Abelard's view in the first place. My colleague says there is no stopping point: either human judgment mirrors the divine tribunal or it is arbitrary. Which step should I reject, and what exactly do I pay? If the argument is valid as written, then say which premise is least costly to abandon and what the resulting view looks like.
