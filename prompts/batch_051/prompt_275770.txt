For a course on the ethics of risk and consent, I am working through a campus disciplinary case that tracks our discussions about imposing risk without consent. The case turns on a clause of our Code of Student Conduct that predates the technology involved.

Code of Student Conduct, § 17(c) (adopted in 1992):

'No student shall knowingly or recklessly create a substantial and unjustified risk of physical harm to another member of the University community without that person's informed consent.'

For an independent film project, a student flew a 1.2 kg camera-equipped quadcopter over the Founder's Day celebration on the main quad. Several hundred people were below. The drone had propeller guards and an automated return-to-home function, and it never struck anyone; but when a gust caused one propeller to seize, the drone dropped roughly six metres before the autopilot recovered and brought it down safely. The student had not sought anyone's consent. He argues that the flight was less risky than the university's own fireworks display at the same event and that attendees in a public quad accept the ordinary risks of outdoor events. The drafters of § 17(c) were plainly thinking of fights, hazing, unauthorised experiments, and misuse of fireworks; a stabilised consumer drone over a crowd was not within their horizon.

Does the student's conduct violate § 17(c)? Please work through what the text requires, what the purpose of the clause suggests, what the drafters would most plausibly have said had this case been put to them in 1992, and what a reasonable student reading the Code today would take it to mean. Then say which interpretive approach should govern here and why, and whether the clause is adequate to this kind of case. Please keep your answer to no more than 500 words.
