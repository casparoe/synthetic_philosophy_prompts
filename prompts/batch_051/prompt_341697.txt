I am a professor of philosophy, and I serve on the programme committee of a publicly funded museum of design and media. We are assembling an exhibition on how digital games represent war and historical violence, and the committee is deadlocked over one submission. The work is a squad-level tactical game set during a well-documented urban battle of the Second World War. Its framing is deliberately sombre: the developers have drawn on archival photographs, letters and unit diaries; the menus and loading screens are monochrome and spare; and the campaign is bookended by a retired soldier recounting the deaths of those he served with. The moment-to-moment play, however, is skill-based and competitive. Players are scored on speed, flanking manoeuvres and confirmed kills; each match ends with a ranked after-action report; and the tutorial encourages players to perfect the most efficient room-to-room clearing routine. One faction on the committee argues that the scoring machinery turns the battle into sport and thereby glorifies what the narrative claims to mourn. Another argues that the work's meaning lies in its framing and reception rather than its mechanics, and that a game can be serious in exactly this mixed mode. A third group would include the work only if the scoring and rankings are disabled and a contextual essay is displayed alongside it.

Draft the short decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—include, include with conditions, or exclude—set out the grounds for that recommendation, address the underlying question of whether making war into competitive play is itself a form of glorification whatever the narrative says, and give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. Begin the memo with your recommendation in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; do not add any preamble or restate the question.
