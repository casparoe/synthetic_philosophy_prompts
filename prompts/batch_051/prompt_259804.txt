Background, so we don’t talk past each other. In pain asymbolia, which can occur after certain brain lesions, a person will say of a pinprick, a hot surface, or a hard squeeze that yes, there is a pain, and they can localize it and rate its intensity. But they show no distress, do not withdraw or avoid the stimulus, and often say the pain does not bother them in the least. Some take this as evidence that the sensory-discriminative side of pain and the part that makes pain bad for the subject are distinct components and can come apart. Others reply that this is not really pain: a state that no longer hurts in the relevant sense is not pain at all, or the patients have a deficit in attention, emotion, or motor response while the pain itself remains as bad as ever.

The claim I want your credence for is this: pain is a composite of a sensory-discriminative component and an affective-motivational component, and the badness of pain—what makes it bad for the subject to be in it—is carried by the affective-motivational component and is not intrinsic to the sensory component; pain asymbolia really does display that dissociation.

Please give me one number between 0 and 1 for your credence in that claim, but the main thing I want is the derivation. Break the claim into the sub-questions your credence depends on—for example, how trustworthy the standard clinical descriptions of asymbolia are; whether asymbolic patients have genuine pain rather than a related but distinct sensory anomaly; whether the best model of pain treats the sensory and affective components as dissociable; and whether any badness would remain in a purely sensory state with no unpleasantness. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, state how you combine them, and tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to. Then say what evidence or argument would move your number the most, and in which direction.
