I write market data infra at a small trading firm, and I keep running into people citing the no-trade theorem like it settles that markets are informationally efficient. As I understand it, the theorem says two rational traders with the same prior won't trade purely on a difference in private information: each would treat the other's willingness to trade as itself a signal and update until somebody backs out, so the trade disappears. So any actual trade should be liquidity, noise, or some non-informational motive.

But the order flow I look at is mostly ML models trained on different proprietary datasets, with different architectures and different loss functions. These models can't articulate there posteriors, nobody on the other side can reason about how they map beliefs to bids, and they don't share a prior in any meaningful sense — their inductive biases plus training data basically *are* their priors, and they're all different. "Common knowledge of rationality" doesn't even have an obvious meaning when neither side can inspect the other's model. Does the no-trade theorem survive this? I want to know whether this is just the premises failing in practice (they always did), or whether the theorem's real point — that prices aggregate information — depended on traders being legible to each other in a way these models aren't. If common priors were doing the load-bearing work and it's now obviously false, is there a repaired, weaker version that still says something worth saying about what prices can and can't do when the marginal trader is a black-box model trading against another black-box model? Don't restate the textbook result; I want to know what's actually left of the epistemic story about markets once the assumptions stop applying.
