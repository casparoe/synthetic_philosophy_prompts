I keep coming back to this passage from Machiavelli's The Prince (chapter 25):

"I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings, bearing away the soil from place to place; everything flies before it, all yield to its violence, without being able in any way to withstand it; and yet, though its nature be such, it does not follow therefore that men, when the weather becomes fair, shall not make provision, both with defences and barriers, in such a manner that, rising again, the waters may pass away by canal, and their force be neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous. So it happens with fortune, who shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her, and thither she turns her forces where she knows that barriers and defences have not been raised to constrain her."

A friend of mine insists this is just a fancy metaphor: fortune isn't a causal agent, so saying barriers and valour reduce its damage is either trivial ("being prepared helps") or unfalsifiable. But I feel like there's alot of daylight between that and a testable claim — namely, that a state's or leader's prior preparation and adaptable virtù actually reduce how badly exogenous shocks wreck them, and that the difference between the wrecked and the not-wrecked isn't just luck. I want to know whether that bet can be tested. Then tell me what even a clean positive result would leave unsettled — for instance, whether we should call the protective factor 'virtù' rather than institutions, resources, or something else.
