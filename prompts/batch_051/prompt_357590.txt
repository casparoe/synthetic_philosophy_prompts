I'm a machine-learning engineer, and I want a systematic translation of Kripkenstein's rule-following problem into my native vocabulary, plus an explicit residue report. My suspicion is that the clean part of the translation will be the underdetermination and that the normativity will be what gets dropped; I want that checked rather than assumed.

I think naturally in terms of supervised learning, training and test distributions, distribution shift, finite data underdetermining a function, inductive bias, regularization, simplicity priors, no-free-lunch theorems, label noise, and inter-annotator agreement. I have read a secondary account of Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein on rules ('Kripkenstein'), but no primary literature. Restate that debate in the vocabulary above, as faithfully as possible, and tell me where the translation is forced.

First, restate the skeptical challenge. This is the claim that any finite history of additions is consistent with indefinitely many rules that agree on that history and diverge on the next case — the standard illustration being an operation that agrees with addition on every pair I have actually computed, because those pairs have always involved numbers below 57, but that returns 5 as soon as either operand is 57 or above — and that no mental image, grasped instruction, internally rehearsed algorithm, or facts about my dispositions fix which rule I meant. Then restate the skeptical solution, in which there is no fact of the matter about meaning over and above assertability conditions: I am entitled to assert '68 plus 57 equals 125' not because a truth-condition independently picks out the correct continuation, but because my use agrees with the pattern of my linguistic community.

Second, map these moves onto ML one by one. I expect the clean mappings to include: a training set underdetermines the function; infinitely many zero-training-error hypotheses make different predictions on the next point; a held-out test set only supplies more labeled cases, each themselves compatible with further quus-like hypotheses; and inductive bias, architecture, and regularization function as decisions about which extension to prefer. I also expect distortions, and I want those named precisely. The biggest one, I suspect, is normativity: the rule-following question is about which answer I ought to give, whereas a trained network, like a disposition, merely outputs something; even its test error is defined only against a loss and a benchmark that someone stipulated. I also want your judgment on whether the community in the skeptical solution maps onto inter-annotator agreement and benchmark conventions, or whether that mapping fails because the labelers are themselves finite and can be put in the same skeptical predicament one by one.

Third, give me the residue report. List which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in ML vocabulary at all. Then answer this: when an ML engineer says the right inductive bias is the one that generalizes, or that the model has learned the target task, is that a solution to the rule-following problem, a restatement of the skeptical solution, or a way of presupposing that the disputed fact — which rule is in force on unseen cases — has already been fixed?

One standing request: 'Kripkenstein' is itself a contested reading of Wittgenstein, and neither the skeptical problem nor the skeptical solution commands universal assent among philosophers; within ML there is also live disagreement about what actually explains generalization. Separate what is settled (what Kripke demonstrably argues, what a no-free-lunch result actually shows) from what is genuinely contested among people who know the material, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
