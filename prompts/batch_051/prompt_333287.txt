You are a moral and political philosopher with a track record in designing classroom simulations, and you are careful about how rules, incentives, and information conditions shape what participants notice. I teach a second-year undergraduate seminar called Cooperation, Fairness, and Obligation—14 students, one 90-minute session per week. I want a structured activity, runnable in the next session with no advance reading, that makes students feel the philosophical problem of free riding and social parasitism from the inside rather than arriving at it through definitions. In particular, I want them to leave with two questions genuinely live: what, if anything, is wrong with taking the benefits of a cooperative scheme without contributing to it; and is there a difference in kind between the opportunistic free rider and the social parasite, the person who builds a persistent way of getting by on the contributions of others? I have run ordinary public-goods games before, and students tend to find the free rider rationally cute and mildly funny, which keeps the subsequent discussion shallow. I want a design that gets past that—either by making the temptation to withhold real and then making the group's resentment toward systematic non-contributors concrete, or by forcing students themselves to judge the difference between someone who cannot contribute and someone who strategically will not.

Please design the activity for the full 90-minute slot, with roughly 55–60 minutes of game play and the rest of the time for a debrief. The design should include concrete rules, roles, materials, a token or point payoff scheme that does not require real money, what the facilitator says at the start and when, and a minute-by-minute timeline. You may use two or three rounds or conditions—for example an anonymous condition and a visible condition, or asymmetric endowments and needs-based exemptions—so that the distinction between the free rider and the parasite emerges from what students do and see rather than from any announcement. The structure should put genuine decisions in front of them: how much to contribute, whether to conceal or advertise their strategy, whether to complain about or punish others' non-contribution, and how to distinguish an excuse from a strategy. Specify what winning means at each stage and what, if anything, students are told in advance about the debrief.

Then write the debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto the philosophical territory: the structure of public goods and the logic of collective action, fair-play obligations, exploitation, reciprocity, the free rider/parasite distinction understood in terms of a standing disposition or dependence rather than a one-off ride, and the question of what it means to say a contribution is available to someone in the first place. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—collusion, a dominant contributor skewing the incentives, the class treating the whole thing as comedy, someone refusing to play at all—and how I should handle each. Finally, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: real material dependency, durable social structures, the historical and structural dimensions of actual parasitism, and the stakes of real public goods. Say what I should supply in a brief closing remark so students do not overgeneralize. Before committing to a final design, briefly sketch two or three candidate mechanics with their tradeoffs; then give the full runnable version.
