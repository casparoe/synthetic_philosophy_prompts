I've been pushing a view I call the interruption rule about omissions. The idea: a failure to act is a genuine cause only when it interupts a process that was already running, not when it's a mere failure to start a process that never got going. The signalman who falls asleep at his post causes the crash; the neighbor who never learned to swim and so doesn't jump in to save a drowning kid doesn't cause the death — the water does. The problem is both omissions support the same counterfactual (had they acted, no crash or death), so counterfactual dependence can't be what's doing the seperating. And I keep hitting counterexamples: a lifeguard who shows up late to her shift has interrupted her commute, not her watching, yet we still treat her failure to be watching as a cause; and if someone interrupts a rescue they had no duty to perform, does that count as causing the death? I want an honest assessment: is the rule coherent, is it secretly a moral or normative distinction dressed up as a causal one, and what are the three strongest objections — is any of them fatal?
