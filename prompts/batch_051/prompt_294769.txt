I hold a deliberately deflationary position about what Gödel's incompleteness theorems show outside mathematics. I take the formal core to be this: any consistent, recursively axiomatizable formal system rich enough for elementary arithmetic has sentences it can neither prove nor refute; if such a system is sound, at least one of those sentences is a truth of arithmetic it cannot prove; and no such system can prove its own consistency. I hold that beyond this core the theorems have no clean philosophical fallout: they do not show that human mathematical reasoning outruns any possible machine, that the mind is non-computational, that there are truths accessible only to a non-mechanical faculty, or that any genuine limitation of artificial reasoners is best explained by incompleteness rather than by computability, information-theoretic, or resource constraints. I also hold the stronger predictive claim that no future finding in cognitive science or artificial intelligence will force a Gödelian explanation of a real limitation, because any such limitation will be statable without mentioning Gödel.

Tell me what would count against this position. I want to know in advance what would have to be true—which formal results, empirical findings, or philosophical arguments—to overturn each component, which of these are live possibilities rather than merely idle logical ones, and how they should be ranked from decisive to merely awkward. Include the objections that a defender of the position should themselves concede are telling, not only the objections critics would raise. Finally, give me an honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and if so, whether that is a problem for a deflationary view of this kind.
