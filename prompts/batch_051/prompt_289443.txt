Here's a familiar line of thought about everyday conventions. Queueing, tipping, letting people off the train before you board, not jaywalking on an empty street: these things look arbitrary in themselves, but they acquire moral force because they solve a coordination problem. Everyone is better off when we all use the same rule; each person's compliance is only worth it because they can expect everyone else's; so the established rule comes to have a kind of normative pull, and breaking it is wrong because you are free-riding on a scheme others are keeping going.

Now here's a development that's quietly undermined that picture in a lot of places. The queue is now cross-cut by paid fast-track passes, remote check-in apps, reservation systems, and hired line-standers, so there often isnt a single shared understanding of who is due to go next. In a theme park the person physically ahead of you may have paid to be there; in a restaurant someone may be holding a place in the queue from their flat through an app. The coordination argument seems to need a reasonably unified convention to get off the ground, and that's exactly what's dissolving.

So the question is whether the old argument survives this, and in what form. I want a committed verdict, not a survey of options. Is it dead in these settings? Does it survive basically intact? Or does it only survive as a repaired argument that says something narrower — maybe one that tracks exploiting others' specific reliance rather than the intrinsic authority of the local rule? Pick a side and defend it. If you think it can be repaired, give the strongest statement of the repaired argument in a paragraph and say precisely which premise had to be weakened or swapped out. If you think it's dead, tell me what, if anything, is still wrong with the person who buys their way past a queue — and whether that wrong has anything to do with convention at all.
