A friend and I have been arguing about whether a person in a large democracy ever has a genuine moral duty to vote for the lesser of two evils. My friend thinks the consequentialist answer is obviously no, and he has refined his view into the following argument, which he claims exposes a mistake in all talk of a "duty" to vote. I think something goes wrong, but each step sounds plausible on its own, and I cannot say precisely where the chain breaks.

(1) A vote for the better candidate can make the world better only if that vote is decisive: without it, the worse candidate would have won or the election would have been tied.
(2) In a large national election, the probability of being decisive is minuscule; whatever the exact model, the probability shrinks sharply as the number of voters grows.
(3) The expected moral value of voting for the better candidate is the probability of being decisive multiplied by the total good that comes from the better candidate's winning rather than the worse.
(4) The good that comes from the better candidate's winning is just how much better the better candidate is: for any realistic pair of candidates, this is the amount by which a typical citizen's life goes better under the better policies, a modest and bounded quantity. Call it v.
(5) So the expected moral value of an individual vote is the tiny probability times v.
(6) A tiny number times a modest, bounded quantity is negligible.
(7) An act whose expected moral value is negligible cannot be something a person is morally required to do.
(8) Therefore, in a large democracy there is no duty to vote for the lesser of two evils; voting can at most have expressive value.

Please assess the argument. Is it valid as stated? If not, identify the first step or inference that fails, name the type of mistake involved (for example, an equivocation between two quantities, a scope error, or a suppressed premise), and show exactly how the expected-value calculation would have to be corrected. If you believe the argument is valid, explain why the conclusion, though uncomfortable, follows.
