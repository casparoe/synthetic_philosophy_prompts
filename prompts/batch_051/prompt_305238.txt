In my dissertation research on Rousseau’s conception of political legitimacy, I have been trying to get clearer about the apparent antinomy between individual freedom and coercive enforcement of the general will. Please argue for the following position: Rousseau’s doctrine that citizens who refuse to obey the general will may be compelled by the whole body and are thereby made free is not primarily a justification of state coercion. It is instead a claim about how to distinguish laws that realize a citizen’s own reclaimed liberty from arbitrary decrees that merely express some particular will. In making this argument, address the strongest version of the liberal objection—namely, that any identification of freedom with obedience to a collective will dissolves the boundary between self-government and tyranny—and show why that objection presupposes a conception of freedom as mere unhindered choice that Rousseau would reject as insufficient for genuine self-rule.
