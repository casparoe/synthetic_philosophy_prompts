I've been trying to get straight what I actually think about historical causation, and my gut keeps giving different verdicts on cases that feel nearby. Here are some with my honest reactions.

- The bullet that killed Archduke Franz Ferdinand in 1914: a cause of his death, full stop.
- That same assassination as a cause of the First World War: I balk. I want to say it was the spark, not the cause, because the system was so primed that another crisis would probably have set off a general war within a few years. But then I also think it caused the particular timing and shape of the war we got.
- In the July crisis itself, I'm happy to say Russian mobilization was a cause and that German backing of Austria was also a cause, even though I suspect that if either had been absent the other might still have been enough.
- The Black Death as a cause of the English Peasants' Revolt: I'd say yes, through the labor shortage it created, even though the poll tax was the more immediate trigger. I'd also say the poll tax was a cause. But if you ask me whether each was necessary, I dont think either was strictly necessary.
- The fall of the western Roman Empire: I want to say Hunnic pressure was a cause and internal breakdown was a cause, but no one thing was the cause, and I get annoyed when people ask what the main cause was.

Is there a coherent principle that gets all of these, or close? I'm especially stuck on the contrast between the bullet (which I call a cause) and the assassination as cause of the war (which I resist calling a cause even though it's the same event), and also on how I can call both the Black Death and the poll tax causes while complaining that nothing was the main cause of Rome's fall. Please say what your principle would predict about a case I haven't listed: someone says the stock market crash of 1929 caused the Great Depression. And tell me which of my verdicts, if any, the principle can't accommodate, and whether the cause/background/trigger line I keep drawing is tracking something real or is just narrative salience.
