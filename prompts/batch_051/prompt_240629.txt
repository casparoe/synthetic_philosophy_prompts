Yesterday evening a friend and I argued at length about the St. Petersburg game, and the disagreement has stayed with me because neither of the standard resolutions seemed to do exactly the right work. My friend maintained that the paradox is essentially about the absurdity of unbounded money; I maintained that it is about expected monetary value being an incomplete guide to choice. We kept talking past one another. After the argument I devised the following variation, and I should like you to work through it carefully and then say which of us, if either, it supports.

The ordinary game is as follows. A fair coin is tossed until it first comes up heads; if the first head occurs on the nth toss, the player receives 2^n monetary units immediately. Since the probability of the first head on toss n is 2^{-n}, the expected value is the divergent sum 1 + 1 + 1 + ..., yet almost nobody would pay a large entry fee. Now alter the timing. Suppose that if the first head occurs on the nth toss, the prize of 2^n units is paid not immediately but n years later; a larger prize is therefore also a later prize.

First, suppose the player discounts future money exponentially, valuing a unit received t years hence at δ^t units now, with 0 < δ < 1. Compute the present expected value of the game and state, as a function of δ, the largest entry fee that an expected-value maximiser with linear utility for money should pay. Show explicitly how the previously divergent series is tamed.

Second, suppose instead that the player discounts future money hyperbolically, by the factor 1/(1+kt) with k > 0. Show that the present expected value is now the series ∑_{n=1}^∞ 1/(1+kn), which still diverges. Then generalise: for a discount factor of the form (1+αt)^{-γ}, determine for which γ the present expected value becomes finite.

Finally, address the conceptual question. Does this variation show that the standard appeal to the concavity of utility for money was doing more of the real work than it is usually credited with, or does it show the opposite — that the paradox can be created and dissolved merely by changing the rule for discounting future payments, independently of any fact about the utility of money? Since simple hyperbolic discounting is time-inconsistent, also say whether that fact undermines the claim that the current self really faces an infinite expected present value. And should an agent in the hyperbolically discounted case pay any finite entry fee? If not, articulate the principle that justifies refusing an act with infinite expected present value.
