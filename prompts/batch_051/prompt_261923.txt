I am a high school student and the moderator of a small after-school reading group on the philosophy of mathematics. Two members have spent the past several meetings arguing about a single sentence, "2 is an element of 4," and we have agreed to run the dispute as an adversarial collaboration: a format in which each side must state the other's position in terms the other accepts, and each side commits in advance to what would count against its own view. I have been asked to prepare the document such a collaboration would produce, and I would like you to draft it.

One member holds a view we call set-theoretic reductionism. On this view, numbers are not merely modeled by sets; each natural number literally is a particular set, and the von Neumann encoding gives the correct identities. There, zero is the empty set, a successor n+1 is the set {0,1,...,n}, and four is {0,1,2,3}. Since two is one of four's elements, this member takes the sentence to be plainly true.

The other member holds a view we call structuralism. On this view, numbers are positions in a structure, and a particular set-theoretic encoding is only a way of representing those positions, not a statement of what the numbers themselves are. This member notes that an alternative encoding is equally faithful: zero is the empty set, and every later number is the singleton of its predecessor, so that four is the singleton of the previous number and has that number as its only element; on that encoding, two is not an element of four. The structuralist concludes that set membership is not a genuine feature of the numbers themselves: the sentence is at most true relative to a chosen representation, and possibly a category mistake.

Please draft the joint statement in three sections.

First, a numbered list of claims both parties can sign. The claims should be substantive rather than vacuous. They should include what follows from each encoding, which facts about the numbers are independent of the choice of encoding, and what the dispute is not about. Exclude any formulation that smuggles one side's view in under neutral-looking wording.

Second, the residual disagreement. Set out the still-open propositions in wording each side accepts for its own position, so that neither is signing a caricature. Identify which of the remaining disagreements are merely verbal — cases where the parties use a term such as "number," "true," or "is a property of" differently — and which are genuine. Show the verbal component without dissolving the whole argument, unless you judge that nothing but a verbal difference remains.

Third, the cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that a settled answer to one of them would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case, argument, or kind of consideration that both sides would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts it will show. The aim is a document both could sign at the end of the collaboration, not a verdict on who is right.
