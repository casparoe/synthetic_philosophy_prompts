Before I read yet another paper on judgment aggregation, I want the full spec of what an adequate procedure has to deliver, because I keep running into people defending premise-based voting or conclusion-based voting or some weighted scheme by pointing at exactly one virtue and ignoring what it gives up. The setting I care about is a multi-member body — say an appeal panel or a commitee — that votes seperately on several logically connected propositions: whether there was a contract, whether it was breached, whether damages are owed, with the third true only if the first two are. Each member holds a complete and consistent view on all three, but as the discursive dilemma shows, proposition-wise majority vote can return yes on the first, yes on the second, and no on the third, which is collectively inconsistent even though no individual judge is. So: write the desiderata that a method for turning individual judgments on connected propositions into a group judgment has to meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a real requirement and not just a preference, and say whether you'd treat it as non-negotiable or as one camp's taste. Then flag any subsets that can't be jointly satisfied — if the full list is impossible, I want that stated straight, with which packages of requirements remain available and what they cost. Also tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the usual proposals. Don't give me a survey of the standard procedures scored against the list; the criteria are what I'm missing. And tell me what would change your mind about the list itself: which requirement you'd demote first if I pushed, and where the whole criteria-first approach is most likely to fail or be parochial to a legal setting.
