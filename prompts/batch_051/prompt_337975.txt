I'm putting together a handout for a philosophy of science discussion group, and I want a "sort yourself" quiz for the debate over whether physics could have a final theory and, if it could, whether we'd ever be in a position to know we had it. I'm less interested in the usual labels (realism, instrumentalism, etc.) and more in the actual fault lines: where people part ways on what counts as "final," whether a final theory needs to be checkable as final, and whether asking for a final theory of everything is even well-defined.

Could you write it as a decision tree with maybe 6-8 leaves? A few requirements. First, the internal nodes should be diagnostic questions phrased around concrete cases or claims, not around labels, so someone can answer without knowing the taxonomy. Please build these four cases into the splits somewhere: (1) an oracle that outputs correct predictions for every experiment you can describe but gives no explanation—does this count as having a final theory? (2) a successor theory that reproduces all current data but replaces all the underlying ontology—final progress, or evidence that "underlying" is doing nothing? (3) the old why-regress: a pencil is solid because of chemistry, then atoms, then quantum electrodynamics, then...—does this chain have to terminate for physics to finish, and would its terminus be a final theory or just where we got tired? (4) a final theory understood as the shortest algorithm that compresses all observed data—does that trivialize the whole idea, since any finite dataset has some short program? Second, work out the logical space first: for each branching question, say which combinations of answers are possible, and for any combo with no real position living in it, flag it and say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, just unappealing, or merely unexplored. Third, if two positions turn out to be indistinguishable by these questions, don't paper over it—flag the pair and say which further case would seperate them if I ever wanted to add a branch. As for leaves, make sure these end up somewhere in the tree: one view that a final theory is in principle possible but we could never verify that it's final; one view that the whole notion is empty becuase "physics" is either defined by current physics or by an unknown completed physics (the old Hempel-style dilemma); one view that physics converges toward a true-but-unreachable limit rather than a finishable theory; one view that the end realistically will be actual, a literally final set of laws we settle on; and at least one view that says the question is ill-posed but for a different reason than the Hempel-style one. Keep each leaf to a name (or "unnamed" if it lacks a standard name) plus two sentences: one stating the view, one on its most awkward commitment. Audience is non-physicists who are fine with thought experiments but won't know any equations; feel free to be a bit chatty.
