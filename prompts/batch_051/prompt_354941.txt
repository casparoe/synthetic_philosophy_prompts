I'm a backend engineer and I fell down a math-foundations rabbit hole after a coworker showed me how the natural numbers get built in set theory: 0 is the empty set, 1 is the set containing 0, 2 is the set containing 0 and 1, and in general each number is the set of all the previous ones. So on that account, 2 is literally an element of 4, because 4 is {0,1,2,3}. That bugged me, because it feels like confusing a representation with the thing itself. I keep coming back too the same thought, and I've been calling it the interface theory of number. The idea is that arithmetic is an interface: numbers aren't objects you can ask constituent questions about, they're positions in a structure, a bit like array indices. Asking whether 2 is an element of 4 is like asking whether the integer 2's memory address is contained somewhere in the integer 4's bytes -- true in one language's runtime, false in another, and not a well-formed question at the level of the interface. Under a Zermelo-style encoding 2 isn't an element of 4 at all, and under the von Neumann one it is, so whether 2 ∈ 4 just tracks which runtime you picked. The interface is what arithmetic and induction care about; the set-theoretic encoding is one possible implementation.

That's the theory. I know the word structuralism floats around, and I get the feeling I've reinvented it with worse terminology, but I don't know the field well enough to tell whether my version adds anything or just misses the point. What are the three strongest objections, and is any of them fatal rather than just uncomfortable? Also, what do I give up by saying numbers have no internal structure -- am I accidentally denying that 4 has a size, or that 2 is smaller than 4, or that you can talk about subsets of 4?
