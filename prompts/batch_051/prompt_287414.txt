I'm a PhD student in science and technology studies, and somehow I got volunteered onto my city's school district "ed-tech review committee." We screen vender pitches for monitoring-ish software before it goes to the school board: AI proctoring, keystroke logging on school laptops, hallway cameras with anomaly detection, attendance apps that geolocate students, that kind of thing. The committee meets once a month for two hours and gets through three or four pitches per meeting. The other members are a classroom teacher, a parent, an IT person, and an assistant principal. None of them have any background in social theory, and I'm the only one who tends to argue against these tools, usually by muttering about Foucault and then losing the vote. The district has about twelve thousand students, and once one of these tools gets piloted it tends to stick around because of contracts and integration with the student information system. We can't kill anything ourselves — the school board makes the final call — but they usually follow our recommendation, so I want our recommendation to be more than my personal discomfort.

I've read a decent amount of Foucault on disciplinary power, normalization, the examination, the role of visibility, and how power and knowledge feed each other. What I want from you is a decision procedure I can actually bring to the committee — not a theory talk, but a screening checklist the group can run on a specific pitch in a two-hour meeting. For each step, I need: the question in plain language (no Foucault jargon on the committee-facing side), what would count as a sufficient answer or as enough evidence from the vendor, and where the step stops the proposal with a no, where it passes, and where it gets referred to a human judgment call because the question is a values question rather than a fact question. Please note which steps reflect which Foucauldian distinction, but keep that in a separate "for me" part so I can defend the procedure without making the committee read theory.

Then run the procedure on two actual-sounding proposals: (1) a browser and keystroke monitor on school-issued laptops that flags "concerning language" in real time and routes it to counselors, and (2) a "positive attendance and engagement" app that logs students into an online identity each morning and produces dashboards comparing engagement metrics across demographic groups, grades, and special-ed status. I'd like one of the worked examples to come out as a no that the committee would probably otherwise approve, and one to come out as an answer I personally will not like — I want to know where the procedure bites back. And be honest at the end about what a checklist can't capture in Foucault's account of discipline: what gets flattened, what the procedure is structurally unable to see, and where using the procedure might itself become another normalizing technology.
