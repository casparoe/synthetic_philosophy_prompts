You are an expert in political philosophy and the economics of taxation who has been asked to serve as the neutral drafter for an adversarial collaboration between two camps in a longstanding debate over inheritance taxation. Think carefully before you write. Begin by brainstorming each camp's core commitments, the places where their apparent disagreement is merely verbal, and the sub-questions on which the substantive dispute actually turns. Then draft the document.

The two camps are as follows. The equal-opportunity camp holds that large, lightly taxed inheritances are a central mechanism by which arbitrary family advantage persists across generations, and that a robust inheritance tax is a necessary part of any serious commitment to equality of opportunity among children. They regard inherited wealth as closer to an unearned windfall than to deserved property, favor high effective rates above a generous exemption, and prefer to tax recipients on their lifetime inheritances rather than to tax the estate as such. The family-autonomy camp holds that people may transfer the fruits of their labor to their children, that the family rather than the state is the primary institution through which people save and care for their descendants, and that high inheritance taxes punish long-term savers, encourage costly avoidance, and endanger family farms and closely held businesses. They favor low or zero inheritance taxation, or at most a backstop rate on very large estates, and they believe the family's internal transfers across generations should be largely invisible to the state.

Produce the document these two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should read as a single jointly authored text, not as a dramatized debate or a ruling from an outside judge. It must contain:

1. A numbered "Agreed claims" section. The claims should be substantively non-vacuous and should cover shared empirical background, shared normative starting points, and any points on which the collaboration caused one side or the other to revise its view.

2. A "Disagreements" table with three columns: the question; the equal-opportunity camp's formulation of its own position; and the family-autonomy camp's formulation of its own position. Each formulation must be written so that a sophisticated member of that camp would accept it as an accurate and non-caricatured statement of their view.

3. A "Cruxes and tests" section. For each crux, meaning a sub-question such that resolving it would move at least one camp, state the crux in neutral terms, describe a concrete case, natural experiment, or piece of evidence that both camps agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts that evidence would show. Include at least five cruxes, spanning empirical, institutional, and normative issues, and apply the same evidentiary discipline to the normative cruxes as to the empirical ones.

The cruxes should address at least the following, and should include any further cruxes the two camps would themselves identify: (i) how much of observed intergenerational persistence in income and wealth is transmitted through financial inheritances themselves rather than through parenting, schooling, assortative mating, genes, and culture; (ii) how much tax avoidance, capital migration, and reduced saving a high inheritance tax would actually produce; (iii) whether the tax should be levied on the estate at death or on beneficiaries as they receive inheritances across a lifetime, and whether that design choice changes the moral analysis or only the administrative incidence; (iv) whether the relevant benchmark for judging the tax is equality of opportunity among children, the equal moral standing of families, or the freedom to dispose of what one has lawfully acquired; and (v) whether the family is best modeled as a unit of shared consumption and care whose internal transfers should be invisible to the state. For each crux, state the sort of evidence or argument both camps would count as bearing on it, and record each camp's prediction in advance.

End with a short jointly signed paragraph stating, in neutral language, what residual disagreement remains and what future findings would most likely narrow it.
