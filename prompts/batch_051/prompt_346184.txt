I have tentatively accepted a broadly Woodwardian interventionist account of causation: X causes Y just in case there is a possible surgical intervention on X—one that changes X without independently affecting Y except through X and without being correlated with other causes of Y—that would change Y or its probability distribution, with the associated generalization invariant across a range of background conditions. I am aware of the standing circularity objection, since the intervention concept is itself spelled out in causal terms, and I know the account is often pressed with cases where interventions seem impossible. What I want is an honest map of what would count against this position. Which possible cases, arguments, or formal developments should I treat as decisive rather than merely awkward, and which would interventionists themselves concede as genuine costs? In particular, should in-principle non-intervenable variables, failures of invariance under interventions, or the charge that the view is either circular or has been qualified into vacuity move me? Is the view by now so well insulated that it risks unfalsifiability—and if so, is that a damning problem for a theory of causation, or an acceptable feature?
