I keep losing an argument with my sister about forgiveness, and I've started to think the whole thing turns on an analogy she leans on without saying it outright. Her line is that forgiving somebody is basically like cancelling a debt. The person who wronged you runs up a kind of tab — they owe you an apology, repair, whatever — and forgiving means tearing up the IOU and eating the loss. A bank can write off a loan without the borrower doing a thing first, and sometimes the bank arguably should write it off, when there's no realistic chance of collecting. So she concludes that forgiveness can be unconditional (you don't have to wait for an apology or a change of heart) and can even be obligatory (like writing off hopeless debts is just the decent thing to do).

I don't want the usual analogies-are-just-analogies answer. I want you to actually run the mapping: what in forgiveness plays the role of the debt, the creditor, the debtor, the IOU, the write-off? Then tell me where it breaks. The part I care about: sort the disanalogies into ones that matter for her two conclusions and ones that don't. Does the debt-cancellation picture actually carry the claim that you can forgive unilaterally, with no apology required, or that you're sometimes obliged to forgive? Or does it quietly smuggle in the idea that a wrong is a finite, payable sum owed only to you, that forgiving is just not collecting, and that the wrongdoer's stance can count for nothing? And if the analogy collapses under that load, is there a better analogy (or a distinction like forgiving versus reconciling) that saves whatever in her view is worth saving?
