I direct a small UK charity that campaigns to reduce household waste and over-consumption. Month by month the same tension appears in our budgeting: we exist to persuade people to consume less, yet we constantly weigh spending money in ways that do not look frugal — staff travel to conferences, paid advertising, professional software, catered public events, equipment replaced before it is worn out. Our trustees reach for frugality to settle these questions, but they mean at least two different things by it, and the difference is what is really doing the work.

On one reading, the deontological one, frugality is a requirement on our own conduct. Running the organisation wastefully would be wrong in itself, even where it advanced the mission, because we would be doing the very thing we tell others to stop doing. On the second reading, the consequentialist one, frugality matters for what it brings about: less extraction, less waste, more people living within limits. On that reading the value lies in the outcome, and our own frugality is only one instrument among others; spending more can be exactly what the value requires when the extra spending changes other people's consumption by more than the saving it gives up.

Please turn this distinction into a decision procedure my staff and board could actually use. The constraints: these choices arise roughly monthly, each must be settled in a single two-hour board meeting on the basis of a one-page paper from me, and no one involved has training in philosophy or economics, so the procedure must not rest on our being able to attach reliable numbers to the expected effect of an expenditure or to the influence of our example. The board, not the staff, signs off, so the procedure should mark where the remaining question is one of values and has to be decided by the board rather than answered by the procedure. The stakes run both ways: money spent without effect is mission lost, but an organisation visibly failing to practise what it preaches loses the standing to preach at all.

Write the procedure itself: the questions in the order they must be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what to do when no sufficient answer can be given, where the procedure stops with a no, and which steps are judgements dressed up as procedure. It should handle at least these hard cases: expenditure that is effective but visibly unfrugal; expenditure that is frugal but ineffective; and unfrugal expenditure we are tempted to justify as promotion without much reason to think it promotes anything. Then say explicitly what the procedure cannot do for us, including which self-deceptions it cannot catch.

Finally, apply the procedure to three illustrative proposals: (i) sending a staff member to present our work at a national conference for local-government procurement officers; (ii) replacing serviceable but slow office computers so the team can produce more material; and (iii) putting most of the annual budget into a single paid media campaign rather than the small community workshops we have run for years. Make sure at least one of these comes out in a way a lifelong frugality advocate on the board will dislike, and do not soften that result; say plainly why the procedure returns it.
