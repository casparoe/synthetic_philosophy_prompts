I've been going round in circles over Quine's 'Two Dogmas of Empiricism', and a mate offered me this argument that the paper refutes itself. (1) Quine's thesis is that there is no principled analytic–synthetic distinction: no one has made empirical sense of a sentence being true purely in virtue of meaning. (2) That thesis is itself a sentence of English. (3) If it is correct, its correctness is a matter of what 'analytic', 'synthetic' and 'meaning' mean in English — a matter of language, not of any extra-linguistic fact. (4) But a sentence whose correctness is purely a matter of language is exactly what an analytic sentence is supposed to be. (5) So Quine's thesis is, if true, an analytic truth. (6) Therefore at least one analytic truth exists, which contradicts the thesis. My mate says the only honest conclusion is that Quine must concede the distinction. I'm suspicous, but I can't say exactly where the trick is. Is the argument valid as stated? If not, which step fails, and under which reading of 'a matter of language' does the fallacy show up?
