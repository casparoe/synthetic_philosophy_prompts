I'm preparing a pre-reading handout for a graduate seminar on corporate moral responsibility, and I need a decision tree that classifies views on the question: when a company does wrong, what is the proper object of blame? The tree will be used before the students have read the literature, so every branch must be answerable from a concrete case or a claim about that case, and no node may presuppose knowledge of the named positions.

Begin with this case at the root: a mid-sized manufacturer's board approves a bonus scheme that, as the board's own later inquiry finds, predictably rewarded managers for concealing safety defects, and several workers were injured as a result. Ask whether it is appropriate to resent the company itself — where resentment is the reactive attitude characteristic of blame — over and above resenting the managers and directors whose decisions produced the scheme. Let the yes/no answer define the top split: no, only individual human beings can be proper objects of blame; yes, the company, as something over and above its members, can be blamed.

Under the "no" branch, separate two leaves by asking whether corporate blame-talk is a misleading shorthand that should ideally be eliminated, or a legitimate derivative shorthand for the summed or distributed blame of the individuals involved.

Under the "yes" branch, split by what grounds the company's blameworthiness. Present these three candidate grounds as rival answers to the question of what makes the company itself blameworthy in the case, and let the student's answer determine where they land:

(a) the company's decision structure — its formal procedures, lines of authority, and standing policies — is by itself enough for literal corporate intentions, so the company is a literal object of blame (the position usually associated with Peter French);
(b) the company is blameworthy as an integrated agent that rationally coheres its members' contributions and makes commitments it can be held to, without any claim that a unified corporate mind exists (the view usually associated with Philip Pettit);
(c) the blameworthiness lies in the organization's culture — incentives, routines, and unspoken norms — and requires no corporate intentions at all.

Then introduce a split that cuts across the "yes" branch: ask whether blame in the full reactive-attitude sense requires the blamed party to be capable of feeling guilt or of being addressed as a moral responder. If the student says yes, they should land on a thin accountability view: the company can be held to account, sanctioned, and required to make amends, but resenting it is misplaced because it cannot feel guilt. Say explicitly which of (a)–(c) this thin view is compatible with, and whether it is best represented as its own leaf or as a modification of the others.

Include at least six leaves: an eliminativist/strong-individualist view; a derivative-shorthand view; a decision-structure realist view; an integrated group-agent realist view; a structural/cultural view; and the thin accountability view. Label each leaf with its standard name where one exists and "unnamed" otherwise, and give each leaf one sentence stating its most awkward commitment.

After the tree, add a short note covering two points. First, identify any pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate by case judgments alone because they differ only at the level of metaphysics rather than practical blame. Second, note any unoccupied combinations of your splits and briefly diagnose each one as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Assume the students are comfortable with abstract argument but have not read the corporate-agency literature, so define any term of art you use. Keep the entire tree, including the leaf annotations and the closing note, under 800 words. Finish by stating which placement in the tree you are least confident about, and what argument or case would most likely lead you to move that leaf or redraw a split.
