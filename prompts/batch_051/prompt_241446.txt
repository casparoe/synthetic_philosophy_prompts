I edit the science-and-ideas desk of a weekly magazine. Much of my job is weighing the historical analogies that experts and advocates attach to AI: the printing press for large language models, the Industrial Revolution for AI-driven automation, nuclear weapons for advanced AI. I need a decision procedure, usable in about twenty minutes under deadline, without any citation apparatus, and defensible to my assigning editor, for deciding whether a proposed analogy should frame a piece, be used with specific named caveats, or be rejected as misleading. Build it from what philosophy and the historical social sciences know about analogical inference: the distinction between structural similarity and vivid surface similarity, rates of diffusion and feedback loops, concentration of power, and whether an analogy is doing predictive, explanatory, or merely rhetorical work. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands off to editorial judgment. Then run the procedure on three analogies currently on my desk: the printing press as a guide to how large language models will reshape political and intellectual life; the nuclear-weapons moment as a guide to how states should govern advanced AI; and the Industrial Revolution as a guide to AI-driven labor displacement. Give me at least one verdict the desk won't like, and mark the steps where the procedure is a judgment call rather than a rule.
