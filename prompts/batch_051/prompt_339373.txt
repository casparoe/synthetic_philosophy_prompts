ok so i'm a phd student doing interpretability-adjacent work and i've been trying to write down what i actually think about making models legible, partly so my advisor stops asking me what my dissertation is even arguing. i ended up with these committments. each one feels right on its own, but i have this background feeling the package doesn't quite hang together, and i'd rather find out now. can you act as a hostile referee and audit the set? for each pair that's genuinely in tension, say how severe it is and what the cheapest fix would be, and rank the tensions rather than giving me a flat list. for pairs that only look like they're in tension, say why the appearance is misleading. then tell me which single revision does the most to make the whole thing consistent and what it costs. here's the list:

1. in high-stakes settings (medicine, law, lending, whatever) we shouldn't deploy a model unless the humans accountable for it can understand why it did what it did, and not just know that it's accurate on average.
2. post-hoc explanations only count as legibility if they track the actual process that produced the output; otherwise they're just PR for the model.
3. chain-of-thought is the best real window we have into a model's reasoning, and requiring models to show their work on consequential tasks is a decent default even if it's noisy.
4. but i also think that when we train models on human feedback about their reasoning—rewarding them for producing explanations that raters find convincing—we mostly select for polished rationalizations rather than faithful reports of why the model did what it did.
5. the gold standard for legibility is mechanistic: actually identifying the circuits or features that implement the computation. behavioral proxies (explanation-quality ratings, benchmark wins, whatever) only matter to the extent they connect back to real mechanisms.
6. i'm willing to accept a moderate accuracy loss for a big gain in legibility, even in the clinic or courtroom; i'd rather have a somewhat worse model i can audit than a black box i can't.
7. and i'm optimistic that we can train models to be legible from the inside out—nudging them toward more modular or monosemantic internals—without imposing a hard ceiling on capability.

i genuinely can't tell where the strain is. the chain-of-thought stuff and the mechanistic stuff feel like they should conflict somehow, but that might just be because one is verbal and the other is internal. so be specific: which pairs are real problems, which pairs dissolve, and what's the cheapest way to make me consistent?
