For a graduate seminar in the philosophy of astrobiology, write out the requirements that any measure of civilizational advancement must satisfy to count as adequate for comparing civilizations across space, time, and possible forms. I want the criteria themselves, not a survey of the existing scales. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp. Then address whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — in particular, whether a single scalar can be simultaneously faithful to energy-based rankings and to structure-based rankings, or whether any scalar must arbitrate between those axes by a weighting the underlying facts do not dictate. That is the part I most want to see. Also indicate which requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive measures. Skip the scorecard; I will run it in class.

Set the problem as follows. Kardashev's ladder ranks civilizations by the rate at which they can command energy, through the familiar rungs keyed to the energy of a home planet, a host star, and a host galaxy. John Barrow's proposed scale inverts the direction: it ranks civilizations by the smallest structures they can reliably engineer, descending from bulk materials through molecules and atoms toward nuclei and below. The two rankings can plainly diverge, and neither alone dictates how its own axis should be weighted, so I am asking for the criteria any adequate measure must meet, whatever direction it points. The criteria should contend with, among others: a civilization that has spread across its planetary system but operates near biological efficiency rather than building megastructures; a civilization that achieves molecular manufacturing but never surrounds its star with collectors; a civilization that could harness a star's output and deliberately declines to do so; a civilization that shifts its economy toward reversible, thermodynamically efficient computation and thereby lowers its energy draw; and a pair of civilizations in which one controls more energy while the other controls finer structure. The criteria should also settle, with reasons, whether a measure must track capability, knowledge, complexity, resilience, or well-being, whether consistency with the known physical limits on computation and signaling is a non-negotiable constraint, and whether advancement may be treated as reversible or as monotone across a civilization's own history.
