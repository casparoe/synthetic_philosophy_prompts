I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether an institution can disrespect you even when every individual you deal with are perfectly polite and follow the rules. We kept going in circles because we couldn't agree on what 'recognition' even requires, so I tried to pin down some criteria. Recognizing someone in the attitude sense — as opposed to merely treating them correctly in action — seems to involve at least these: (a) treating them as a source of claims that have weight, not just as an object to be managed; (b) responding to them as a particular individual with a point of view, not just as an instance of a category; (c) being disposed to treat them as an addressee, someone to whom you could in principle justify what you do and who could push back; and (d) not having your outward treatment be systematically decoupled from the underlying acknowledgment of their standing.

Now the case I want to test. A social media platform hires human moderators to review flagged posts. They work from an exhaustive decision tree: for any post, they tick boxes — does it contain a slur, does it threaten a specific person, does it discuss self-harm — and the tree tells them what to do. They're explicitly instructed not to read posts as messages from particular people, and not to consider the poster's situation or point of view, because that would supposedly introduce bias and inconsistency. The moderators are polite in any direct interaction, they never act cruelly, and the rules they apply are, lets stipulate, substantively reasonable. One part of me wants to say this is recognition at its purest: every user gets exactly the same treatment, no favorites, and the rules embody the standing each person has. Another part of me wants to say it's the opposite of recognition, because nobody is actually seen as an individual — everyone is just a feature vector attached to an account.

The question: does this system recognize the users in the attitudinal sense, or not? Go through the criteria one by one, flag the places where they underdetermine the answer, and say whether the case shows that the criteria themselves are missing something — e.g., maybe (b) is asking for something other than recognition, or maybe the whole list is too demanding.
