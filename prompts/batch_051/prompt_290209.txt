I'm an undergraduate in an upper-level philosophy of social science course, and we are currently working through David Lewis's account of convention. A long-form article I read over the weekend, written by someone who is more of a popularizer than a specialist, summarizes his position in the following compressed way:

"Lewis showed that a social convention such as driving on the right is a self-perpetuating regularity. Once enough people follow a rule, it becomes rational for everyone else to fall in line, even when a different rule would have served just as well; conventions are therefore arbitrary equilibria. They typically arise and stabilize without any agreement or central design, and they survive only because each person expects the others to conform and prefers not to be the odd one out. On this picture, a convention carries no genuine obligation — the only reason to comply is prudential. For Lewis, then, a social norm is just a convention of this kind. So when people speak of a duty to queue, to say please, or to observe the rules of courteous interaction, they are mistaking a stable pattern of mutually self-interested expectation for a real requirement."

I find that summary intuitively plausible, but I suspect it blurs several distinctions my instructor has been careful to draw, and before I raise it in class I would like to know how much of my suspicion is warranted. Could you walk me through the summary more or less claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful Lewisian would accept, which parts are defensible shortcuts, and which parts distort his actual position? I am especially unsure of four things. First, is it fair to say that a Lewisian convention carries no genuine obligation and is followed only for prudential reasons, or does that read more into Lewis than is there? Second, would Lewis accept the slide from "convention" to "social norm," or are those distinct categories in his treatment? Third, the phrase "once enough people follow a rule" sounds to me like a threshold claim, whereas my instructor described Lewis as requiring a coordination problem in which each person prefers to conform conditional on the others' conforming, together with common knowledge of those expectations and preferences — is that difference substantive or just a harmless simplification? Fourth, does the closing inference that talk of duty merely confuses mutual expectation with a real requirement follow from anything Lewis actually says, or is it the article's own addition?
