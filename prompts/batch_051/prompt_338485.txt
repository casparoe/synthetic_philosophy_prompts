I am a novelist at work on a book about a town that quarrels over what its public monuments may and may not say. In the research I have become more interested in something narrower than the familiar question of whether a community's memory or the archival record should give way when the two conflict: I want to know how an institution built to manage that conflict would actually behave. I would like you to analyze a mechanism I have invented, treating it strictly as a piece of civic machinery rather than as a cultural or moral position.

The mechanism is this. A city creates a Historical Reconciliation Board. Any resident may submit a memory claim concerning an event or person commemorated on public property. The claim is heard by a panel of twelve residents, randomly drawn from a pool of registered voters who have lived in the town for at least twenty years. If a majority of the panel finds that the claim reflects a genuine and widely held community recollection — whether or not it accords with the archival record — the city must add the remembered version to the marker in the same size type as the official account, introduced by the fixed phrase "Many in this community remember that…". A successful claim also bars the city from removing or demoting the archival version for twenty-five years, and the panel's decision, along with a transcript of the recollection offered, is published online. To fix an image, suppose the marker dates a mill fire to 1902 while several old families insist the fire came in 1911, during a strike the official account omits; under the rule, both sentences would stand side by side indefinitely.

I want a disciplined walkthrough of how this would play out: what it incentivizes among archivists, elected officials, descendant and heritage organizations, and ordinary residents; how it would be gamed, and by whom; what the plaques and the underlying records would look like after a generation; and whether the failure modes can be repaired without abandoning the scheme. Where would it break first, and is that first failure procedural, epistemic, or political?
