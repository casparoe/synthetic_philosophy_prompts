I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've run into an argument I can't debug. I'd like a precise diagnosis.

It started from a mundane observation about code: on an infinite input domain, no finite test suite uniquely determines a function. For any finite set of passing input-output pairs, there are infinitely many functions that pass every test in the suite but diverge on some untested input. That much is uncontroversial. But when I applied the same thought to what a person means by a symbol, I got a conclusion that seems plainly false, and I can't find the bad step.

Take the symbol '+'. I mean addition by it. Now consider the following argument:

(1) If I mean addition by '+', then there must be some fact about me in virtue of which I mean addition rather than some deviant function.

(2) The only candidates for such a fact are: (a) my past applications of '+' and the examples I was taught, (b) a mental formula, image, or stored algorithm, or (c) my dispositions to answer.

(3) Past applications and taught examples are finite, and any finite set of examples is compatible with infinitely many functions that agree with all of them but diverge elsewhere. For instance, pick a bound N larger than every number I have ever actually used in a computation. The function that matches addition whenever both inputs are below N, and otherwise returns 5, agrees with every computation I have actually performed, but it is not addition.

(4) A mental formula, image, or stored algorithm is just finite syntax, and finite syntax determines its own application only relative to an interpretation. But an interpretation is itself a rule or symbol, so it raises the same problem one level up rather than bottoming it out.

(5) A disposition cannot do the job either. My actual dispositions are finite, noisy, and error-prone: I make mistakes, I tire, and I have no stable disposition for inputs too large for me to process. An idealized disposition can only be specified as the disposition to answer correctly under ideal conditions, which smuggles in the very notion of correctness we were trying to explain.

(6) So no fact about me determines that I mean addition rather than the deviant function.

(7) Therefore there is no fact of the matter about what I mean by '+'. Ascriptions of meaning do not report facts; they are moves in a social practice of holding one another to norms.

I find (7) absurd. Surely there is a fact that I mean addition and not the deviant function. But I cannot locate the exact place where the argument goes wrong. I have a vague suspicion about step (4) or step (5), but I can't formulate the objection without feeling that I'm helping myself to the conclusion.

Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and what kind of failure it is (an equivocation, a scope shift, a suppressed premise, an illicit move from each to all, or something else). If it is valid, say which premise is the strongest candidate for rejection and give the strongest objection to it.
