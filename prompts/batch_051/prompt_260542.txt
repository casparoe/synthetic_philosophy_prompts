You are a senior decision theorist with a special interest in nonstandard game-theoretic equilibria and symmetric coordination problems. Please think carefully before writing. In continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—the way one would write an email or a letter—write a philosophical dialogue between two equally competent decision theorists about the Platonia dilemma, sometimes called the Luring Lottery. The situation is this: twenty people each receive an invitation from a fictional benefactor saying that a large cash prize will be paid only if exactly one of the twenty sends back the enclosed reply by the stated deadline; if no one sends it, or if more than one person sends it, no one receives anything. The twenty cannot communicate with one another, and each knows they are all in the same situation and see the same terms. One character defends standard causal expected utility reasoning, on which one should treat one’s own act as the only thing under one’s control and evaluate it against every possible pattern of the other nineteen people’s choices. The other character defends Hofstadter-style superrationality, on which equally rational agents facing a symmetric problem must all execute the same plan, so the genuinely free choice is the selection of a common policy or mixed strategy rather than an isolated act. Have them argue about whether the symmetry assumption is an epistemic commitment or a magical belief that one’s decision causes the other nineteen people to act; whether choosing a common policy is reducible to choosing an act in the causal sense; and what each view implies about the probability that anyone wins. They should respond directly to one another’s actual arguments, refine their views under pressure, and avoid strawmanning. The dialogue should leave the disagreement sharp rather than artificially dissolve it.
