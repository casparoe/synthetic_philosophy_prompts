A developed democracy is considering creating a Long-Horizon Review Directorate. The proposal's rationale is longtermist: ordinary legislative and regulatory time horizons systematically underweight the far future, and a standing counterweight is needed before short-sighted policies foreclose civilization's long-term potential. The Directorate has one principal power: it may issue a continuity order suspending any statute, regulation, local ordinance, or administrative license for up to twelve years. It may do so only when its Modeling Division certifies, on its own findings, that the target law raises the probability of an existential catastrophe within the next century by at least one percentage point relative to a baseline in which the law is not enacted. Each order must state the modeled mechanism in general terms, but the underlying models, data, and sensitivity analyses are classified and shielded from discovery. Orders take effect immediately and may be overturned only by a two-thirds majority in both legislative chambers. The Directorate has seven members serving single, fourteen-year terms; the executive appoints them from a shortlist prepared by the scientific academies, there is no legislative confirmation, and the risk determination is designated a nonjusticiable scientific judgment, so courts may not review the merits. Its budget is set as a fixed fraction of GDP and is not subject to annual appropriation. The Directorate also has a standby power to detain a person for up to ninety days without the ordinary criminal-procedure safeguards if it certifies that the person sits on the critical causal path of an imminent existential event; this decision is reviewable only by an internal panel.

Walk through how this institution would actually behave once it exists. What does the existence of this override do to the legislature when it drafts ordinary laws, to regulators who know their decisions can be suspended, and to firms deciding whether to invest in technologies that might trip the model? Where is it gamed first—by industry, by the Directorate's own modelers, by the executive selecting members, or by litigants trying to trigger or block orders? What second-order effects should be expected: regulatory uncertainty, capture of the risk models, expertise monopolies, preemption of democratic deliberation, or others? Which failure modes are internal to the design rather than accidental, and can any be fixed without removing the feature that is supposed to make the Directorate effective? If you think the institution would settle into a stable political role, say what that role is; if not, say what breaks first.
