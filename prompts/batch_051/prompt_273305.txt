I am genuinely torn between a view of legal proof that strikes me as almost forced, and an intuition I cannot shake.

The view is straightforwardly probabilistic. A legal system should minimize the expected harm of its errors, and because convicting the innocent is far worse than acquitting the guilty, the rational rule is to convict or find liable when the probability of wrongdoing on the total evidence, whatever its source, clears the relevant threshold — more likely than not for civil liability, a high probability for criminal guilt. Proof beyond a reasonable doubt would then just be that high threshold, set by the relative costs of false convictions and false acquittals. This view is simple, unified, and treats all evidence alike except as a means of raising or lowering probability.

But my intuition refuses to accept naked statistical evidence, even when the numbers sail far above the threshold. Suppose 90 percent of the buses on a route belong to the Blue Bus Company, a bus injures someone and leaves, and the only evidence against the company is the base rate. Or suppose 95 percent of the spectators at a rodeo jumped the fence without paying, and one known spectator is sued on those numbers alone. I would not want the plaintiff to win in either case. The resistance does not disappear when the base rate is pushed to 99 percent, and it carries over to criminal cases: I would convict on an eyewitness identification I believed made guilt 99 percent likely, but not on a 99 percent statistical profile. The problem, then, is not the level of probability. It is that evidence about a class seems never to say anything about this particular defendant.

Present both sides at full strength. On one side: the intuition is tracking something real that the probabilistic view leaves out — a distinction between a probability that is about the case and a probability that is merely about a reference class, a requirement that an accusation fit the accused in particular, or the idea that proof demands something like knowledge rather than mere high credence. On the other side: the intuition is an artefact of loss aversion when a specific person stands before us, and the contrast with individualized evidence cannot survive scrutiny once we see that eyewitness memory and DNA matching are themselves probabilistic and reference-class dependent. Tell me what each side would have to give up, whether the probabilistic rule can be amended to respect the intuition without becoming ad hoc, and whether I can keep the probabilistic rule for my own beliefs while denying that the law should simply apply it.
