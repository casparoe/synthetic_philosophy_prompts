You are a professional philosopher who works on population ethics and the nonidentity problem. Please think through the following slowly, and before you commit to a prediction, explicitly brainstorm the candidate principles that could fit the evidence I give you.

Last night I had a long argument with a friend about the nonidentity problem, and I would like an expert reconstruction of the principle she is actually running, plus a prediction about a new case we did not discuss. She is not a philosopher by training, but she has thought hard about these cases and is confident in her verdicts. Here they are.

1. Depletion. A government can either conserve its resources, in which case a certain large number of future people will exist and have a high quality of life, or deplete them, in which case the same number of future people will exist — but different individuals, because the two policies lead to different personal lives — and will have a somewhat lower but still genuinely worth-living quality of life. She says choosing depletion would be wrong.

2. Early parenthood. A young person can either have a child now or wait several years until she is in a much better position to raise one. If she has the child now, this particular child will have a difficult childhood and a lower but still worth-living quality of life; if she waits, a different child will exist and have a much better life. She says having the child now would be wrong.

3. Extra child. A couple in an already flourishing society considers having an extra child. The child would have a life worth living, and the child's expected well-being would be exactly equal to the society's current average, so no existing person's welfare changes and the average welfare stays the same. She says having the child is permissible but not required: there is no obligation to create the extra well-off life, though it would not be wrong to do it.

When I pressed her, she accepted that in cases 1 and 2 the particular future people she is protecting could not have existed under the alternative choice, so it is hard to say that any specific person is made worse off by the choice she condemns. She did not think this forced her to change her verdicts.

Now imagine a foundation that can fund exactly one of two projects. Project A would bring about a population of 10,000 people with an average lifetime well-being of 80. Project B would bring about a distinct population of 20,000 people — with no overlap in membership — and an average lifetime well-being of 70. Every individual in either project would have a life clearly worth living, no currently existing people are affected, and the two projects cost the same. What should I expect her to say about whether the foundation is required to choose A, required to choose B, or free to choose either? Which candidate principle or principles best capture her three earlier verdicts, which of those principles still fit the foundation case, and where do the surviving principles diverge? Give me your best prediction, your reason for preferring the principle behind it, and, if her earlier verdicts underdetermine the matter, tell me which further case would cleanly settle which principle she is actually running.
