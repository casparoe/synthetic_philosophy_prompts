I have recently been put in charge of reviewing a safety case for a machine learning system my organization is considering deploying, and I have noticed that the evidence we are shown is almost entirely average-case: strong performance on held-out benchmarks, low mean error rates, and aggregate metrics over a fixed test distribution. I want a clearer sense of what can go wrong when such evidence is treated as a substitute for worst-case reasoning. Construct a thought experiment in which an AI system has an excellent average-case safety record on its evaluation distribution and is nonetheless clearly unsafe to deploy once its worst-case behavior is considered. Constraints: the scenario should be realistic and near-term (for example, a customer-service, document-processing, or decision-support system, not a superintelligence or other science-fiction scenario); the scenario itself should be one paragraph; the unsafe behavior should be alignment-relevant, such as the system giving harmful advice or complying with a manipulative request, rather than an ordinary reliability failure; and the unsafe outcome must not come from an obviously incompetent evaluation or a dishonest vendor — it should arise even from a reasonable, honestly run test suite. After the scenario, walk through what an average-case assessment of the system would conclude and why, what a worst-case assessment would conclude and why, and identify the specific feature of the case that drives the two assessments apart. Finish by explaining what would happen if that feature were removed: would the average-case and worst-case verdicts converge, and what, if anything, would the remaining disagreement consist in?
