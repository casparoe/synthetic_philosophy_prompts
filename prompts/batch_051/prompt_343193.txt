I have settled, though not irrevocably, on a textualist account of statutory interpretation. On the view I find myself holding, the meaning of a statute is fixed by the ordinary public meaning its words would have carried for a competent speaker of the language at the time of enactment, read against the whole text and the public legal context; it is not fixed by what legislators privately intended, hoped, or would have said, nor by what a later court can recover from the legislative record. I reject the purposivist alternative not because I think legislatures lack purposes, but because the "purpose" of a multimember body usually looks to me like a construction rather than a discovery, and because textualism seems better able to keep statutes public and stable, so that citizens can rely on the text they can read and judges do not become unelected co-authors. Canons of construction are acceptable to me only insofar as they track how competent speakers actually use language.

What I want from you is not another defense of that position, but a map of its exposure. What would count against it? I want to know which actual or hypothetical cases, which facts about language, and which arguments should be decisive; which should be merely awkward; and which a committed textualist would themselves concede as counting against the view. I especially want the places where the view may have quietly insulated itself: when there is ambiguity, vagueness, drift in usage, or a surprising application, does textualism fall back on canons, background assumptions, or a "reasonable reader" who supplies what purpose or policy would have supplied, and if it does, is that a concession that should be owned, or a refinement that does no damage? If my statement of the view is too indeterminate to be tested, say so plainly and test the strongest defensible sharpening instead of the weakest.

One further request about how you answer. If any part of this is badly posed, or the view as I have described it cannot honestly be tested, tell me that directly and do the nearest well-formed thing rather than complying politely. And when you reach a verdict, say whether the sharpened view is genuinely risking anything — whether there are real or possible findings and cases that would rationally move a textualist — or whether it has been made compatible with every outcome. I would rather be told that the view is unbeatable and that this is a problem than be handed a handsome defense of it. For my own part, the things that would move me most are cases in which the ordinary meaning is determinate and yields something no one can defend, and arguments that the standard textualist escapes from those cases are purposivism under another name. Please don't give me a bibliography or citations; work from arguments and from cases you can characterize in your own words.
