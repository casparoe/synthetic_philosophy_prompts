After a long argument with a friend, I realized I have been applying an epistemic shortcut whenever brain–computer interfaces and similar sensing systems are involved, and I want it examined. The argument concerned a study I work on, in which a BCI lets a user move a cursor by decoding motor intention from scalp signals. Occasionally the decoder attributes an intention that the user sincerely denies: the decoder says the user was about to command a leftward movement, while the user insists deliberation was still open and no decision had been made. My rule is to believe the user and to discount the decoder's conflicting classification nearly to zero, whatever its accuracy on trials where the two agree. I treat the device as evidence about neural preparation, not as rival evidence about what the person intended.

The rule has spread beyond the study. I apply it whenever a device attributes a mental state the person could in principle report—a mood, a lapse of attention, a memory, a preference—and the person reports it differently. I make exceptions only where the state is not consciously accessible, such as a classifier flagging prodromal seizure activity, or where the person cannot report at all.

My friend says this is not a principle but a rationalization. A self-report, he objects, is just one noisy, reconstructed, delayed channel among others; a decoder can have better temporal resolution and failures that are more measurable, and the rational policy is to weigh each channel by its accuracy for the state in question. Assigning self-reports near-infinite weight and decoders near-zero the moment they conflict is, on his view, Cartesian prejudice about privileged access.

I concede that self-report is fallible. But I have countervailing reasons I cannot easily formalize. The classifier was trained largely on the user's own earlier reports of intention, so the apparently independent channel may inherit the reporter's biases while looking objective, and I cannot audit the feature pipeline myself. More forcefully, I suspect cognitive liberty requires keeping the authority to settle what someone intended, felt, or remembered with the person wherever the person can speak; letting a device overrule a sincere self-report would be a form of mental-privacy violation even when the device predicts well.

So I am asking whether this shortcut is defensible once stated, or whether I am rationalizing a romantic overvaluation of the first-person voice. Assess the policy as a policy: where it is reliable, where it fails, and what a restated version would have to say to keep its protections without its blindness. In particular, when should a conflicting decoder output shift my belief about what the person thought, rather than merely my belief about the person's neural activity, and how should that depend on the use to which the attribution will be put—assistive technology, clinical monitoring, legal evidence, or workplace evaluation?
