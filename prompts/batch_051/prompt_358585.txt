In my epistemic game theory class we did the blue-eyed islanders puzzle and I'm still stuck on what exactly the visitor's announcement does. The setup: 100 islanders, all blue-eyed, perfect logicians, with no reflective surfaces and a taboo against discussing eye color. Rule is, if you ever work out your own eye color you have to leave at midnight. Everyone sees 99 blue-eyed people, so everyone already knows that at least one person on the island has blue eyes. Then a visitor arrives and says out loud, in front of everyone, "there is at least one blue-eyed person here," and that somehow starts a countdown that makes all of them leave 100 days later. What did the announcement add if every single person already knew the content beforehand? I know the standard answer is supposed to be about "everyone knows that everyone knows that..." but I want it spelled out at the level of the knowledge states: what exactly changes in the group when the same sentence is said publicly, versus if the visitor had taken each person aside and told them the same thing privately one by one? Is there a name for the higher-order thing that gets created in the public case, something distinct from everyone merely happening to know the same fact, and where exactly is the boundary between those two? I dont just want the label, I want to know what the thing actually is.
