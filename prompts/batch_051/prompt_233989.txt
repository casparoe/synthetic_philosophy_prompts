I'm a philosophy of science PhD student, and I've been going back and forth over a paragraph I read in a popular intro to Bayesian epistemology (I won't name it, its one of those rationality-for-smart-people books). It summarizes van Fraassen's Reflection principle like this:

"Van Fraassen's Reflection principle says you should treat your future self as an expert: roughly, your current degree of belief in any proposition should equal the degree of belief you expect yourself to have later on. He proved that if you think now that you'll change your mind in the future, you're incoherent, because a bookie could sell you a set of bets now and then a further set once your future opinion forms, and together they'd guarantee you a loss no matter what. So Reflection rules out changing your mind at all — it says a rational agent should never update on evidence — and since that's obviously crazy, the principle must be too strong."

I've got a feeling something is off: the 'never update' conclusion doesn't sound like something van Fraassen would sign off on, and I thought Reflection was supposed to be compatible with ordinary conditionalization. But I can't figure out exacly which parts are fine, which are a bit mangled, and whether the "obviously crazy" conclusion actually follows from anything accurate in the summary. Could you go through it piece by piece and tell me what a formal epistemologist would accept, what they'd wince at, and whether the last step is fair?
