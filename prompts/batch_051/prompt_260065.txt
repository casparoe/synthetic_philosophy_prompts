Yesterday evening I spent hours arguing with a friend about the conditions under which we accept sentences of the form "Fs are G" — bare plural subjects, with no quantifier like all, most, or some. She had firm verdicts on a long list of them, and I could not find the rule behind them. She accepted as true "Mosquitoes carry malaria," although only a small fraction of mosquitoes ever carry it, and "Sharks attack swimmers," although such attacks are extraordinarily rare. She also accepted "Tigers have stripes" and "Dogs bark" as true. But she rejected as false "Ducks are female" — about half of all ducks are female — as well as "Peacocks are male" and "Dogs are brown," even though there are plenty of brown dogs. A purely statistical rule, true just in case most Fs are G, seemed to fit the rejections and the tiger and dog cases, but it foundered on the malaria and shark cases. A rule of true just in case the property is dangerous or newsworthy fit malaria and sharks, but not stripes and barking, and not the rejections. She said both proposed rules were wrong, and the argument ended before I could test anything further.

I would now like to know, as a matter of prediction, what she would say about three sentences we never got to: "Ducks lay eggs," "Lions have manes," and "Cats give birth to live young." I am less interested in the bare answers than in the rule she seems to be running. Please state the two or three most plausible rules that fit her earlier verdicts; say what each predicts for the three new sentences; give your best guess about her verdicts and the reason for preferring one rule; identify which of the three new sentences does the most work in separating the rules; and name a further sentence — not one already used — that would settle the matter. If her earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine the rule, be explicit about what assumption would have to be made for each candidate rule to win.
