Write a dialogue between two game theorists who disagree about how much inferential weight to place on iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies when mixed strategies are permitted, applied to the following game. Payoffs are written as (Row, Column).

        L          R
T    (2, 0)     (0, 1)
M    (0, 0)     (2, 1)
B    (0.75, 1)  (0.75, 0)

Both characters accept the formal facts. B is strictly dominated by the mixed strategy 1/2 T + 1/2 M, which yields an expected payoff of 1 against L and 1 against R, strictly more than B's 0.75 in either column; no pure strategy strictly dominates B. Once B is deleted, L is strictly dominated by R, since L yields 0 and R yields 1 against both surviving row strategies. In the full game, however, L is not strictly dominated, since it yields 1 for Player 2 against B while R yields 0. Once L is deleted, T is strictly dominated by M, since T yields 0 and M yields 2 against the only surviving column, R. Iterated elimination therefore leaves (M, R) as the unique survivor.

Where the two characters differ is whether this sequence licenses the prediction that rational players will end up at (M, R). The first character, an eliminationist, holds that the elimination is innocuous bookkeeping: B is never a best response to any probabilistic belief over L and R, so deleting it costs nothing, and the later deletions follow from common knowledge of rationality under expected-utility maximization, making (M, R) the correct benchmark prediction. The second character, a skeptic, holds that while the first deletion is unproblematic for an individually rational player, each further round compounds a strong and often false assumption — not merely that the players are rational, but that each is confident the other is rational, confident of that confidence, and so on. The skeptic should spell out a concrete belief under which a rational Player 2 would keep L in play, and should argue that the procedure thereby turns a weak rationality constraint into an equilibrium-strength hypothesis while presenting it as mere pruning. Neither character should be a strawman: the skeptic may grant that the elimination is valid under common knowledge of rationality, and the eliminationist may grant that the iterated procedure does require higher-order beliefs. The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth, with each responding to the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them.
