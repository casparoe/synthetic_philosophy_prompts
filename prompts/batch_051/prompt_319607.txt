I am a philosophy professor, and I have been building an argument out of Section X, "Of Miracles," in Hume's *Philosophical Essays Concerning Human Understanding* (1748; later retitled *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*). Everything in the chain looks sound to me, but the conclusion appears to be too much for any Humean to accept, and I want a precise diagnosis.

Here is the passage I am working from:

> It being a general Maxim, that no Objects have any discoverable Connexion together, and that all the Inferences we can draw from one to another are founded merely on our Experience of their constant and regular Conjunction; 'tis evident we ought not to make an Exception to this Maxim in Favour of human Testimony, whose Connexion with any Events seems, in itself, as little necessary as any other.

The argument I have reconstructed goes like this:

(1) All assurance derived from human testimony depends on observed regular conjunctions between testimony and the facts testified to; there is no discoverable necessary connection tying reports to truth.  
(2) Where we have a uniform experience against some event E, that experience amounts to a proof against E.  
(3) Human testimony in favor of E can at most make E probable, because testimony is known from experience to be sometimes false.  
(4) A weaker evidence can never rationally overturn a stronger one: probability cannot defeat proof.  
(5) Therefore, when our past experience is uniform against E, no human testimony can rationally establish that E occurred.  
(6) Now take a genuinely novel event: a black swan before any European had seen one, the first successful heavier-than-air flight, or an experimental result that conflicts with a previously exceptionless regularity. Each such event is, at the time it is first reported, contrary to a uniformity that the available past experience supported.  
(7) So by (1)–(5), no human testimony could rationally establish any genuinely novel event.  
(8) But that cannot be right: science and ordinary life rationally accept well-attested reports of events that were previously contrary to uniform experience.

I do not want to give up (1)–(4), since they look like the heart of an empiricist account of testimony. But the argument seems to force me to accept that the first black-swan report could never be rationally believed, which is absurd. Where does this argument go wrong, and what does rejecting the relevant premise or step cost? Please answer in American English.
