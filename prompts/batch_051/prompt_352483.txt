First, curiosity is a desire to know something for its own sake, not because it's useful or pleasant to imagine. Second, if a mature person has a desire for X for its own sake, that's at least some evidence that X is worth having for its own sake, unless the desire is obviously pathological or corrupting. Third, ordinary curiosity, like wanting to know how a medieval guild worked or why bats roost upside down, is not pathological or corrupting in that way. Fourth, so the objects of ordinary curiosity are sometimes worth knowing for their own sake. Fifth, if some things are worth knowing for their own sake, then the settled disposition to pursue such knowledge for its own sake is a virtue. Sixth, therefore curiosity is a virtue and knowledge can be valuable even when it serves no further purpose.

I suspect the dodgy bit is the jump from 'a desire for X is evidence X is worthwhile' to 'X actually is worthwhile', or maybe the move from 'sometimes worth knowing' to 'the disposition is a virtue'.
