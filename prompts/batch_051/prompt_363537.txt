I'm a retired organic chemist and I've been going through the Federalist Papers, mostly because I realized I'd never actualy read them straight through. In No. 63 (Hamilton or Madison, depending which edition you trust) I hit a passage that's been nagging me:

"there are particular moments in public affairs when the people, stimulated by some irregular passion, or some illicit advantage, or misled by the artful misrepresentations of interested men, may call for measures which they themselves will afterwards be the most ready to lament and condemn."

The argument is that the Senate exists partly as a kind of Ulysses contract: the people bind themselves in advance against their own temporary errors. But what if you don't want to count on there actually being a "temperate and respectable body of citizens" around to do the checking? Say a country instead puts this in its constitution: any law passed while a state of emergency is in force expires after 120 days unless, after the emergency has been lifted and a new legislature elected, that new legislature repasses the exact same text, without amendment, in ordinary session. Walk me through how this rule actually behaves. What does it do to when and how governments declare emergencies in the first place, what does it push into ordinary legislation instead, and where does it get gamed first? Can you patch those games without losing the cooling-off effect entirely, or does the mechanism just turn into a dead letter?
