Having spent my career in the natural sciences and come to philosophy only recently, I am reading Nietzsche's *On the Use and Abuse of History for Life* (translated by Adrian Collins) and find myself treating one of its central claims as though it were a hypothesis about organisms and populations rather than merely a piece of rhetoric. The passage that has fixed my attention is this:

"We do need history, but quite differently from the jaded idlers in the garden of knowledge, however grandly they may look down on our rude and unpicturesque requirements. In other words, we need it for life and action, not as a convenient way to avoid life and action, or to excuse a selfish life and a cowardly or base action. We would serve history only so far as it serves life; but to value its study beyond a certain point mutilates and degrades life: and this is a fact that certain marked symptoms of our time make it as necessary as it may be painful to bring to the test of experience."

The position I am inclined to accept is roughly this: the value of historical acquaintance is instrumental to the capacity of an individual or a culture to act and to flourish, and there is a point beyond which further historical study becomes positively harmful—it 'mutilates and degrades life', as he puts it. On that view the three modes of history Nietzsche later distinguishes—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—are healthy only insofar as they serve this vital function, and each can become a pathology when it detaches from that function.

Now I want to be honest with myself about what would count against this position. In the laboratory, a hypothesis is taken seriously only if one can say in advance what observations would be inconsistent with it. What would be the analogous defeaters here? My question turns on the passage: is the claim that there is a point beyond which historical study mutilates and degrades life the sort of claim that could be genuinely tested, and what would be the strongest test against it? Please distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be awkward, say what a defender of the position would themselves concede counts against it, and give an honest assessment of whether the position, as stated, has been qualified into unfalsifiability—and, if it has, whether that is a defect in a claim of this kind. I am not asking for a reading list or for citations; I want the structure of the position's exposure. Please answer in British English.
