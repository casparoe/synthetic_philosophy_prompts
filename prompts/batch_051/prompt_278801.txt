I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas desk, working on a column about the puzzle of material constitution — the old chestnut about a statue and the lump of bronze it's made from, which seem to be two different things even though they occupy exactly the same space. A philosopher I interviewed for the piece emailed me this argument afterward, and now I'm not sure wether I'm seeing a real problem or getting set up. I'd really rather not publish something a first-year grad student could take apart.

Here's the argument as he gave it. (1) On Monday there is a lump of bronze. On Tuesday a sculptor molds it into a statue of a horse. At noon Tuesday, the statue and the lump of bronze are made of the exact same atoms in the exact same arrangement and fill the same region of space. (2) But they differ: the lump existed on Monday, before the sculpture was made, while the statue did not; and if you crush the thing into a ball right now, the lump of bronze survives but the statue is destroyed. (3) If two things are identical, they cannot differ in any respect — whatever is true of one is true of the other (the indiscernibility of identicals, a.k.a. Leibniz's Law). (4) So the statue is not identical to the lump of bronze. (5) But then there are two distinct material objects exactly where common sense says there is one. And there seems to be no principled place to stop: the lump is also distinct from the "portion of bronze," which is distinct from the "collection of copper and tin atoms," and so on, so every sculpture turns out to sit atop an enormous stack of overlapping objects, perhaps infinitely many. (6) The same reasoning applies to you and your body: your body, the biological organism, existed as an embryo before you, the person, did, and it could survive in a permanently vegetative state after you, the person, are gone; so by the same steps you are not your body, and there is more than one being in your chair. (7) That conclusion is absurd enough that something in (1)–(6) has to give.

Act as a professional philosopher working in metaphysics. Start by brainstorming all the different places this argument could be pushed back on, then tell me which premise or step you'd actually reject — or, if you think the argument is sound and the real move is to bite the bullet and accept coincident objects, defend that: why shouldn't the many-objects conclusion count as a reductio? I have a soft spot for ordinary objects. I'd like to keep statues, lumps, and bodies, and I'd rather not multiply objects beyond necessity, but I want to know what those commitments would cost.
