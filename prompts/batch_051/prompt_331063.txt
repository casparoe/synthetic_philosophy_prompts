At my school's economics club, the student president has a record of verdicts about whether a trade between two rational people should be accepted. She has said that a coin-flip bet should be refused when one person is privately told the coin's bias and the other is not. She has said a buyer should refuse to buy a used car when the seller has privately inspected it and the buyer has no way to inspect it. She has said a baker and a farmer should trade bread for wheat when each merely wants what the other has and neither is hiding information. She has said two farmers facing publicly known opposite weather patterns should agree in advance to pool their harvests. And she has said two rival sports fans with opposite hunches about a game, but no private information, can rationally bet against each other.

The next case the club will vote on is this: two traders begin with exactly the same public information about an asset, and then each privately receives a different signal about the asset's value. The two signals are equally accurate, and both know this, but neither learns the other's signal. Judging only by their own signal, each would expect the trade to be profitable, and each knows the other is willing to trade. Should she say, for consistency with her earlier verdicts, that the trade should be accepted or refused?

Identify the plausible rules that fit all of her earlier verdicts, explain which candidate rules disagree about the new case and what each predicts, give your best prediction of her verdict along with the reason for it, and propose one further case that would most cleanly distinguish the rules you are torn between.
