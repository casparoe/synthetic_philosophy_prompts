Okay here's a thing I keep chewing on. In "The Right to Privacy" (Harvard Law Review, 1890), Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis argue that the common law needs to recognize solitude as a protected interest because modern life makes people vulnerable to exposure. Here's the passage I keep coming back to:

"The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury."

The injury they care about is publicity: gossip printed in newspapers, portraits circulated, private life shown to an audience. The harm lands as shame or distress at being exposed.

Now swap in a different threat. A state never publishes anything about you. It buys or compels streams of behavioral traces — locations, purchases, app usage, maybe a social credit score — and runs models over them to infer things it uses to treat you: loans, security screening, school placement, whatever. Nothing is printed; sometimes no human ever sees the results. You aren't shamed, and you often never learn what was collected or what got inferred.

Does their argument survive that? If yes, identify exactly which premise still does the work and what now counts as the "mental pain and distress" (or whether that premise has to be quietly dropped). If no, say what part of the argument dies and whether a repaired version works that doesn't lean on publicity at all — e.g., grounded in control over your data rather than protection from an audience. Dont just say "privacy is control"; show how you get there from their text or exactly where it breaks.

Lead with a one-sentence bottom line (survives / dies / half-survives), then at most 250 words. The quote doesn't count toward the cap.
