You are a professional philosopher specializing in applied ethics and the philosophy of medicine. I am a novelist writing a near-future story in which a cheap, repeatable treatment slows human aging severalfold, and I want the moral debate in the book to be sharper than the usual objections about pride and tampering with the natural order. Here is the asymmetry I want you to examine. In current practice, we treat it as obviously right, even admirable, to spend enormous sums rescuing a particular ninety-year-old from a stroke, cancer, or heart failure, one disease at a time; yet many of the same people regard the project of slowing or reversing aging itself as vain, unnatural, or selfish, even though it would prevent the very conditions we already fight case by case. Is there a principled difference between treating age-related disease piecemeal and treating aging itself, or is the asymmetry only an unexamined bias toward accepting death, of the kind some call deathism? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the case where the interventions are the same mechanism: a single drug given to an eighty-year-old that clears arterial plaque and, as a side effect, adds decades of healthy life. Think carefully, start by brainstorming the strongest justifications for the asymmetry before you evaluate them, and then give me your considered view. Write your answer as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write an email or a letter.
