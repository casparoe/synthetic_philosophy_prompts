I'm on the product policy committee of a company shipping a general-purpose AI assistant that's about to be used across a bunch of different countries. We have to sign off on a policy question before the next release, and we keep going in circles. The assistant increasingly gets asked for guidance on contested practical questions — whether to divorce, whether to vote for a particular measure, whether to quit a job to care for an ailing parent, whether a treatment conflicts with the user's faith. Users have incompatible values and life plans, but we ship one model to everyone. On the committee there are three camps. (1) The assistant should help each user pursue whatever they say they value, tailoring answers to the user's own commitments, within safety limits. (2) The assistant should answer in the way that does the most expected good across all users — an aggregate view — even if it offends some groups. (3) The assistant should refuse to take sides among visions of the good life, laying out the live options fairly and not endorsing one; call it the liberal-neutrality camp. A lurking issue is that the model was fine-tuned with human feedback from contractors who mostly share one cultural background, so option (3) may in practice just be option (2) with that background's answers wearing a "I'm not advising you" disclaimer. Please draft the short written opinion I'd circulate before Thursday's meeting. Recommend one policy that engineering can actually implement, engage the strongest objection to it seriously, and say clearly where the position you reject isn't so much wrong as answering a different question than the one we can operationalize. Keep it under about a page; the engineers need a crisp default, not a seminar. Then end the memo with a final fixed line: \answer{...} containing a short label for the policy (something like "tailor-to-user", "aggregate", or "neutral").
