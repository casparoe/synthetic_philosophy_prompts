You are a professional philosopher working on the epistemology of legal proof and the nature of statistical evidence. Please think carefully, begin by setting out two or three candidate principles that might explain the pattern of verdicts below, then test each against the cases, and finish by stating which principle, if any, best captures the pattern.

I have been collecting my own reactions to cases involving what is often called naked statistical evidence, and I want to know whether a single defensible principle underlies them.

(1) The blue bus. A pedestrian is struck by a bus that leaves the scene. The only evidence is that the Blue Bus Company runs 80 per cent of the buses on that route and the Red Bus Company 20 per cent; there is no witness, paint trace, or other evidence linking either company to the crash. I would find Blue Bus liable on the balance of probabilities.

(2) The gatecrasher. A rodeo arena seats exactly 1,000 people. The promoter's records show that 300 paid at the gate and the other 700 climbed in without paying. A person is picked at random from the crowd, and the only evidence against him is that 70 per cent of those present did not pay. I would not order him to pay for admission.

(3) The prison yard. One hundred prisoners are in a yard when a guard is beaten by ninety-nine of them acting together. One prisoner demonstrably took no part, but the recording is too grainy to show which one. Each prisoner is tried separately, and the only evidence against each is that 99 per cent of those in the yard joined the attack. I would not convict any individual beyond reasonable doubt.

(4) The contaminated tablets. A patient is made seriously ill by a contaminated batch of a generic medicine. It is established that 90 per cent of tablets of that medicine sold in her region during the relevant month came from manufacturer A and 10 per cent from manufacturer B, and the two products are indistinguishable. The patient sues A. I would find A liable.

These verdicts trouble me. Cases (1) and (4) treat a high base rate as enough for a finding against a company in a civil case, while case (2) refuses to treat a fairly high base rate as enough against an individual, and case (3) refuses to treat an even higher base rate as enough in a criminal case. I do not want the difference to be simply the size of the percentage, nor simply that the defendant is a company in some cases and a person in others, but I cannot yet say what better principle explains the pattern.

Is there one defensible principle – whether about the distinction between statistical and individualised evidence, the choice of reference class, the kind of causal connection a proof should establish, or something else – that delivers all four verdicts? If so, state it precisely and apply it to a fifth case that I have not listed. If not, tell me which of my verdicts the principle would force me to overturn, and why that is the cheapest revision.
