You are an expert in drug policy and the economic theory of crime. I want a rigorous, candid assessment of a concept I have been developing; please reconstruct it in its strongest form and identify the nearest existing ideas before turning to objections.

I call it the enforcement elasticity of harm. Drug policy debates usually compare legal regimes — prohibition, decriminalization, legalization — as if each regime had a fixed effect. I think the more informative variable is enforcement intensity: the expected legal penalty and probability of detection imposed on suppliers and consumers of a substance, expressed as a cost per unit of the activity. Now consider an index of total drug-related harm that includes direct health damage from use, but also the harms that enforcement itself generates or shapes: black-market violence, corruption, adulteration risk, unpredictable potency, unsafe administration, and the collateral costs of arrest and incarceration. The enforcement elasticity of harm is the percentage change in that total-harm index associated with a one percent increase in enforcement intensity.

My central claim is that total harm is U-shaped in enforcement intensity. At low intensity, enforcement reduces harm by raising price and suppressing use. Beyond some optimum, additional enforcement mostly makes markets more violent and less observable, pushes use toward riskier forms, and adds enforcement-generated harms faster than it averts use-generated harms. On this picture, the question whether a drug should be prohibited is the wrong question; the right question is where current enforcement intensity lies relative to the optimum and what the sign and magnitude of the elasticity are there. I read Portugal's decriminalization of personal possession as a reduction of intensity in a range where the elasticity was strongly positive, which is why many outcomes improved without much change in use. I read cannabis legalization as a shift from supplier-side enforcement toward regulatory controls, whose promise is that they can hold total harm down at a lower enforcement-generated cost.

Is this a real analytic tool, or have I merely relabeled something the economics of crime or the drug policy literature already has? Is the U-shape well grounded, or is it a just-so story? What does the concept silently assume about substitutability, time horizons, and the commensurability of the harms being aggregated? I would like the three strongest objections and an honest verdict on whether any of them is fatal rather than merely qualifying.
