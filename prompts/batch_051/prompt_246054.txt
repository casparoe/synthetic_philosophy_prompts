I work on the news desk of a popular-science magazine, and the most persistent genre in our submissions is the astonishing-coincidence story: two strangers on a flight discover they were born in the same maternity ward on the same day; someone dreams of a rail accident hours before one happens; a lottery winner collects a second jackpot. We have no settled policy for these pieces, and I have come to suspect this is a design problem rather than a fact-checking problem: we publish them when they feel surprising, but much of what they describe is exactly what the birthday paradox should lead one to expect once a large population and a large number of opportunities are taken into account. I am therefore considering an editorial rule I call the coincidence box. Any piece that leads with a striking coincidence, or that uses a personal anecdote as evidence for a wider claim, would have to carry, near the top, three items: a plain statement of the event; our best estimate of the population and of the number of comparable opportunities over the relevant period; and the number of similar events that chance alone would lead one to expect, using the most generous baseline the article's own framing can justify. If no such baseline can be produced, the piece would run with a standing label stating that the event has no assignable surprise value, and the writer would not be permitted to lean on the anecdote when arguing for a conclusion.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a piece of editorial virtue. You are an expert on base-rate reasoning, the epistemology of anecdote, and the behavioural economics of newsrooms and public relations. Start by brainstorming the ways a canny publicist, a deadline-pressed editor, or an enterprising reader would game the rule: choosing a sympathetic denominator, manufacturing anecdotes that pass the box, laundering the box into a badge of rigour, and anything else that occurs to you. Then analyse the second-order effects: what the rule does to the mix of stories we commission, to reader trust in the box itself, to the market position of an outlet that imposes it while competitors do not, and to the kind of anecdote that ends up on the front page. Give me a definite, committed verdict rather than a menu of possibilities: where does the scheme break first, are the failure modes fatal or fixable, and what would be the earliest sign, six months in, that it is working rather than merely generating compliance theatre?
