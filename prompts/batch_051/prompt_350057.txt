I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I think best in terms of specs, requirements, and edge cases. I keep encountering two flatly opposed claims about grit, fortitude, and resilience. One side treats them as genuine virtues, roughly of a piece with courage and self-control: capacities to persist through difficulty, absorb setbacks, and keep commitments when giving up would be easier. The other side treats them as ideology: ways of praising people for enduring conditions they should not have to endure, laundering structural failure as private character, and extracting effort from people who are already being failed by their institutions. I suspect the two sides are not merely disagreeing about values but are running different implicit theories of what these traits are, and I want the full specification before I commit to either.

Write me the desiderata for an adequate theory of whether, and when, grit, fortitude, and resilience are virtues, ideology, both, or neither. By "adequate" I mean good enough to adjudicate, for a concrete person in a concrete situation, whether praising or cultivating one of these traits is pointing at a genuine virtue or is the kind of move the critics call ideological. For each requirement, give me: (a) a precise statement of it; (b) the case or consideration that forces it; and (c) whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. A table for the requirements list is acceptable.

Please organize the deliverable around the following.

1. Classification cases. The theory should return defensible verdicts on at least: a founder who persists for years with a failing company despite mounting evidence, losing their savings; an employee in a job with an abusive manager who tells herself she is being resilient; a neighborhood that rebuilds after a flood when the flood defenses were known to be underfunded; an athlete who plays through concussion symptoms; a political prisoner who refuses to betray colleagues under torture; a terminal patient performing relentless positivity; a soldier who fights bravely in a war they privately believe is unjust; a person who quits a high-status job to protect their health and is told they lack resilience; and a person with an anxiety disorder who manages to complete ordinary daily tasks. The theory need not know the answer to each case in advance, but it should tell me exactly which further facts decide it. If any pair of cases must come out the same way, say so; if any pair pulls apart, say why.

2. Distinctions it must not collapse. These should include at least: the goal pursued (worthwhile, neutral, worthless, harmful) versus the trait as a standing disposition; the source of the hardship (natural, self-created, interpersonal wrong, structural injustice, legitimate but hard requirement); the cost of persisting and who bears it; the first-person project of cultivating the trait versus third-person praise of or demand for it; describing or measuring the trait versus endorsing it as a virtue; and grit as a stable individual-difference construct versus fortitude as a moral virtue exercised with judgment. Tell me which of these an adequate theory may treat as irrelevant and which it cannot.

3. Empirical constraints. The theory should stay consistent with things that are robustly known without my having to check named studies: burnout is real and chronic over-persistence damages people; sunk-cost persistence is common and often irrational; whether persisting helps or harms depends heavily on context and goal; and people can sincerely report resilience while also rationalizing. If any requirement quietly assumes grit is uniformly adaptive, mark it and say what it would take to salvage it.

4. Tensions. State explicitly whether any pair of requirements cannot be jointly satisfied. I can already see one candidate tension: the demand that grit be a single, situation-general disposition (so we can call someone gritty simpliciter) versus the demand that the trait count as a virtue only when aimed at worthwhile ends with adequate judgment. If satisfying one requires modifying or abandoning the other, I want that flagged; if such tensions are a result in themselves, show the result.

5. Exclusionary weight. End by identifying the single requirement that does the most work in excluding the popular flat positions — the always-a-virtue view, the always-ideology view, and the standard depends-on-the-end compromise — and say briefly how each fails against it. A one- or two-line scorecard of the main families of views is fine, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than the scoreboard.

Finish with a final line of the exact form \answer{...} containing a short label, a few words at most, for that single most exclusionary requirement.
