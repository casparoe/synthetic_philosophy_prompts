I keep noticing a structural parallel between Quinn's self-torturer and the Great Filter line of reasoning about the Fermi paradox, and I would like help deciding whether it is deep or superficial. In the self-torturer, an agent accepts a very long chain of adjacent trades, each of which adds a barely noticeable increment of pain in exchange for something worth having; every neighboring pair of settings favors the higher setting, yet the agent would refuse to jump from the first setting to the last if the whole change were offered at once. In the Great Filter, a long chain of transitions from non-life to a detectable technological civilization—none of which looks impossible taken alone—yields a silent galaxy once their probabilities are multiplied. Both cases set a per-step verdict (each adjacent trade is acceptable; each transition is reasonably likely) against a whole-sequence verdict (the completed sequence is unacceptable; the completed sequence is astronomically improbable). Is the self-torturer's intransitivity formally the same kind of local-to-global failure as the Great Filter's probability product, and do the usual diagnoses of the self-torturer (for example, that the agent should evaluate the whole sequence at once, or that the adjacent steps are not suitably independent) carry over to the Great Filter? Please give the precise mapping between the two structures, say what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer from one to the other, and identify at least one thing this parallel would predict that turns out false.
