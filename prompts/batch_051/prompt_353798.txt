Two members of my evening philosophy discussion group got into an argument over Bertrand Russell's essay *On the Notion of Cause*, which one of them had us read. They disagreed about whether the concept of causation can be eliminated without loss. I'm going to recount their exchange as faithfully as I can, without putting words in either of their mouths.

The first, Alan, said Russell was right in spirit: the law of causality is a hangover from a prescientific way of thinking, and a mature science like physics does not need an irreducible relation of cause and effect; what we call a cause is really just stable succession plus our habit of ignoring background conditions. Miriam answered that stable succession cannot distinguish a genuine cause from a coincidence or from a common cause, so Alan's proposed replacement leaves out the very thing causal talk is for. Alan replied that the natural fix—counterfactual dependence—is context-sensitive and depends on which background facts we hold fixed, so it is at least as philosophically suspect as cause itself. Miriam said that is true of many higher-level scientific concepts, and we rightly keep them when they are predictively useful and support interventions, not because they name a primitive. Alan then said intervention is itself a causal notion, so her reduction runs in a circle. Miriam responded that an intervention can be specified without using the word cause—as an exogenous variable, something that cuts into the system from outside the model and allows us to test whether changing one thing changes another. Alan came back that to know a variable is exogenous you already need to know what causes what, so the circularity is just hidden inside the model. They ended there.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or something in between, and briefly justify each rating. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing about Russell's actual position or about the options for thinking about causation.
