I keep coming back to an argument I had with a friend who'd been reading about epistocracy. His pitch wasn't the usual "experts should rule" thing. He just pointed at an asymmetry I can't get out of head: we would never let someone fly a plane, take out a gallbladder, or wire a house without proving they can do it — we license barbers, electricians, even tattoo artists — but the same person who couldn't be trusted to rewire a kitchen is trusted with a vote that helps decide whether the country goes to war, how it handles a pandemic, or what it does about climate. You need a test to drive a car. You need no test at all to vote. It feels like we demand competence everywhere except the one place where the stakes are the highest. But I also know that "competence tests" have an ugly history: literacy tests and poll taxes were used to keep Black voters out, and I don't want to hand those people a win with fancier vocabulary. And there are nearby cases that make me hesitate in both directions: juries are sort of screened (lawyers can strike people partly on their views), we disenfranchise children and some people with severe cognitive impairments, and a ballot initiative lets any adult decide technical policy directly — which seems like letting the patient vote on which scalpel to use.

I can think of the usual defenses: voting is a right of equal standing, not a job; individual ignorance washes out in a big enough electorate; there is no neutral test of "political competence," so any screening just encodes somebody's values; and the historical abuse of tests is a reason to never go near them. I want each of these actually stress-tested, not just listed. What is the best principle that says "license the pilot, don't license the voter"? Test it against the hardest nearby cases: the jury, the child, the person with dementia, the coin-flip voter who treats the election like a scratch-off ticket, the referendum on a vaccine mandate, the surgeon voting on health policy. And tell me honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up — and whether "we license the pilot but not the voter" is a defensible line or just an inconsistency we're stuck with because the alternative has too much history.

Structure the reply in four sections: (1) The asymmetry — about 150 words, state exactly what is being compared and what the asymmetry actually consists in; (2) Candidate justifications — about 400 words, take the strongest justifications one by one and say what each can and cannot handle; (3) Stress tests — about 350 words, run them against the nearby cases I listed and any better ones you know; (4) Verdict — about 200 words, say whether to keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up, and give the strongest single reason.
