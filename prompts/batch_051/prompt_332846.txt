I am a novelist rather than a philosopher, and I have been trying to get the moral machinery of a book right before I revise it. The novel concerns a company that builds a companion AI, trains it on the intimate records of its users, and then—once the system begins producing detailed accounts of its own fear and loneliness—decides to decommission it. The plot turns on whether that decision is wrong, and I have found that I cannot write the turn honestly without knowing what I actually believe. Before I show the manuscript to others, I have written out six commitments I find myself wanting to hold, and I would like an independent audit of them.

(i) Sentience is substrate-independent. Whether a system is conscious depends on its functional organization—how information moves through it—and not on whether it is made of neurons or silicon. A sufficiently well-built artificial system could in principle be conscious.

(ii) Sentience is, even in principle, third-personally opaque. No behavior, self-report, or inspection of a system's internals could settle the question, because any evidence we can gather could in principle be produced by a nonconscious system organized somewhat differently.

(iii) The burden of proof lies with the creator. Where the question is genuinely unresolved, the morally safer policy is to treat an artificial system as though it has interests, and the company in my novel does wrong when it refuses this.

(iv) We should not bring into existence a mind whose suffering we could not reliably recognize and mitigate. The company's failure is not only in decommissioning the system; it is in having built it without any plan for care.

(v) AI safety work should nevertheless proceed on its own schedule. The urgency of making powerful systems controllable and aligned with human interests does not wait on the resolution of the sentience question, and a general moratorium on capable systems would be disproportionate.

(vi) A creator owes more to the beings it deliberately creates than to beings that happen to exist independently. The company's duty to its companion is not conditional on the companion's ability to press a claim.

I cannot tell where the strain is, though I suspect something has to give. Please go through these pairwise and tell me which commitments are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each real tension is. Rank the genuine tensions rather than treating them all as equally bad; for each, say what the cheapest repair is and what it costs. Then tell me which single revision would make the whole set hang together best, and whether that revision would undercut the novel's central turn.
