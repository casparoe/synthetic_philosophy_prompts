In my philosophy of biology course this term, the unit on adaptation closed with a discussion of Gould and Lewontin's paper on spandrels. The instructor gave us what he called the standard reading, and I want to know whether it is actually fair to the authors. On his account, Gould and Lewontin showed that adaptationism as a research programme is false, or at least deeply discredited. He said their central claim was that most of the traits we see in organisms are non-adaptive byproducts — spandrels — of how bodies are built, and that the main scientific failing of adaptationists is that their stories are unfalsifiable just-so stories. From this he drew the moral that, after Gould and Lewontin, the burden of proof lies with anyone who wants to claim a trait is an adaptation: one should treat structural or constraint-based explanations as the default, and demand that selectionist explanations rule out those alternatives before being taken seriously. This sounds to me like it converts a healthy methodological caution into a much stronger, almost anti-adaptationist thesis, but I am new to this literature and not sure where the boundary is.

Could you go through his summary claim by claim and say what Gould and Lewontin would themselves accept, what they would reject, and whether the concluding moral actually follows from what the paper argues? I am especially interested in whether the paper is best understood as a corrective to how biologists ought to reason — a pluralist demand that alternative hypotheses be tested — or as a substantive claim about how rarely natural selection explains the features of organisms.
