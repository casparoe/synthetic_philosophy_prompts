An analogy has become load-bearing in my research group's discussions of inverse reinforcement learning, and I would like it audited. A labmate defends the claim that inferring a person's preferences from behavior is a largely solved problem, on the grounds that inverse reinforcement learning is just revealed preference theory with trajectories in place of consumption bundles: the reward function corresponds to the utility function, the observed policy corresponds to the demand function, and the optimality assumption corresponds to the weak axiom of revealed preference. His conclusion is that the underdetermination of reward functions is an old difficulty that economists already know how to handle, so current worries about preference inference are overblown.

Could you work through the analogy rather than only gesture at it? Give an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the data, the object being inferred, the rationality or consistency assumptions, the uniqueness results, and the testable content in each setting. Then identify the disanalogies that matter, separating structural disanalogies from cosmetic ones. One point I especially want pinned down: in inverse reinforcement learning, with dynamics and discounting held fixed, many distinct reward functions can render the same observed policy optimal. Does revealed preference theory contain a genuine analogue of that non-uniqueness, or does the analogy import a uniqueness guarantee that the choice-theoretic side never actually had? Finally, tell me whether the labmate's conclusion (that behavior is a transparent enough window on preferences, and that underdetermination worries are overblown) survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there is a better analogy for what inverse reinforcement learning does.
