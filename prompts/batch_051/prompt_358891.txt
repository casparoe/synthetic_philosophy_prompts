An old friend has gotten into Epicurean-flavored arguments lately and can't stop bringing up Lucretius at dinner. His pet move is the symmetry argument: the stretch of time after I die is just like the stretch of time before I was born — both are just long periods of nonexistence where I'm not around. Since no one is upset that they didn't exist before they were born, he says, it's inconsistent to be upset about not existing after you die. He uses it to argue that death isn't a harm to the person who dies, and that grief and "he was taken too soon" talk are kind of confused. I can't shake the feeling the analogy is doing more work than he admits. Before I was born, there wasn't yet a me with a life underway; after I die there's a whole biography that stops mid-stream, with projects, relationships, and a person who existed. But when I try to say exactly what that changes, I get tangled up — especially around whether a harm has to be something you experience.

So: audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table — what maps to what between prenatal and posthumous nonexistence — and then the places the mapping breaks. Seperate the disanalogies that actually matter for his conclusion (that death isn't a harm and our grief is misplaced) from the ones that are just superficial. Then tell me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the symmetry was carrying the whole argument. If there's a better analogy for what death is like, I'd want that too.
