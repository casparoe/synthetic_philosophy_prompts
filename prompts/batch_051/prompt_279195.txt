A friend walked me through Pascal's mugging a while ago and I've been turning it over since; I wound up with an argument that seems to prove too much, and I can't find the bad step. It goes:

(1) A choice is rational only if it maximizes expected utility.
(2) Expected utility is probability times utility, summed over outcomes.
(3) Utility is unbounded above: for any finite value n, there's a possible state of affairs better than n (a long enough history of flourishing lives, say).
(4) Now take any ordinary action A with finite expected utility v, and any probability p > 0. By (3), there's an outcome with utility above v/p, and a gamble that yields that outcome with probability p. That gamble has expected utility above v.
(5) So for every ordinary action, there's a tiny-probability, enormous-payoff gamble with higher expected utility.
(6) By iterating, the theory says you should hand over whatever the mugger asks: if he says "give me $100 and I'll use it to bring about 10^25 units of value with probability one in 10^18," the theory says fine—for any plan you name, there's a bigger payoff and a smaller probability that make the exchange have higher expected utility than keeping the money.
(7) But a theory that tells you to empty you wallet for a stranger with a story like that has refuted itself, hasn't it?

So either one of (1)–(3) is false, or the move from (5) to (6) is invalid. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most want to keep (1) and (2) and throw out (3), but capping utility seems to mean picking a number, and I can't see a principled place for the cap. I also keep half-convincing myself that the trouble is in (4), sliding from "there's a possible outcome" to "there's a gamble I can actually take." What are the main ways out—bounded utility, discounting or ignoring tiny probabilities, giving up expected-value maximization, something else—and what do they each give up?
