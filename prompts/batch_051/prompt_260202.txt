The question:

Some philosophers of math think the real numbers were discovered, and others think they were defined (or constructed). What could decide that dispute? Discuss with special attention to the standard constructions of the reals (Dedekind cuts, Cauchy sequences) and to the role of completeness. Where do you stand, and why?

The student's answer:

"I come down on the discovered side. The key fact is that Dedekind cuts and Cauchy sequences, even though they start from quite different materials, give you the same object up to isomorphism: any two complete ordered fields are isomorphic. That tells us the particular construction doesn't matter, so what we're really talking about is the real line itself, a unique object we discover rather than invent. Cantor's diagonal argument shows the reals are uncountable, and it also shows there's no cardinality strictly between the naturals and the reals; that settles the continuum as something determinate, independent of how we notate or define it. The heart of the reals is completeness: the reals are the only field in which every bounded set has a least upper bound, and that property is what makes them the continuum. Since no definition can create the fact that every bounded set of rationals has a least upper bound (or fails to), the reals must be out there waiting for us."
