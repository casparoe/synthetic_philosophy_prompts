Having recently retired from an experimental chemistry laboratory and begun reading Franklin's autobiography closely, I find myself drawn back to his "bold and arduous project" of moral perfection. His method, as he describes it, was to draw up thirteen virtues, each with a short precept, and to keep a little book in which he marked a black spot every evening for each fault committed against each virtue. He concentrated on one virtue per week, tried to keep the earlier lines clean as he added new ones, completed the course in thirteen weeks, and repeated it four times a year. He seems to have treated the whole thing as a sort of self-experiment, and he reports his results with some irony.

I have been sketching a modernized version of this project for a small group of retired scientists and engineers, and I would like you to analyze it as a mechanism: what it would actually incentivize, where it would be gamed, where it would fail, and whether the failure modes are fixable without destroying its Franklinian character. I am less interested here in whether moral perfection is the right goal than in how this particular instrument would behave.

Here is the proposed protocol.

First, the original thirteen virtues and precepts are kept, with one modification: after the first complete course, a participant may replace the virtue of chastity with one personal vice of their own choosing, supplied in writing.

Second, each participant keeps a paper ledger with one page per virtue, seven columns per page, and thirteen lines per week. Each evening, for every virtue, the participant must either leave the square blank or mark a spot. A blank week on any line is treated as one spot on every day of that week, so that neglect cannot be disguised as success.

Third, for every spot, the participant must write on the facing page a short causal note: the time, situation, and cue that preceded the fault. The note need not be lengthy, but it must identify something specific.

Fourth, at the end of each week, before moving on to the next virtue, the participant writes a single intervention aimed at their most common failure that week: an environmental change, an implementation intention, or an explicit rule. They must record in the following two weeks whether the intervention succeeded.

Fifth, at the end of the thirteen-week course, a participant may choose to continue only with those virtues whose lines had any spots in the final four weeks. A review committee of three alumni examines the ledger and may require the course to be repeated if it finds missing causal notes, a suspicious pattern of blanks, or signs of systematic under-reporting.

Sixth, participants meet monthly in small groups and display only their aggregated spot totals across virtues. No one may name a particular virtue as a confession or boast.

Please treat this as a piece of machinery. What would it incentivize participants to actually do, as opposed to what it asks them to do? How would a rational but imperfect participant game the ledger without improving? Which virtues are most vulnerable to corrupt self-report, which to scrupulosity, and which to gaming? What effects does the monthly group display have, and does it create a market for strategic under-reporting? How does the system differ from what Franklin himself ran, given that his version had no review committee, no interventions, and no audience? And finally: if you were asked to fix its worst failure mode while keeping the paper ledger and the spot-marking ritual, what would you change first?
