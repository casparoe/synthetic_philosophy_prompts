I spent thirty years doing behavioral ecology, and now that I’m retired Ive been reading Quine, and a passage early in chapter two of Word and Object keeps nagging at me. He describes a field linguist faced with a completely unfamiliar language: when a rabbit darts by, the informants say something like gavagai, and the linguist can verify that their assent to that word tracks rabbit sightings fairly well. Then Quine says this still does not decide whether the term picks out rabbits, undetached rabbit parts, temporal rabbit-stages, or the universal rabbithood. A friend who studied philosophy tells me Quine showed that no possible behavioral evidence could ever settle the matter—not just the data the original linguist happened to collect, but any data at all. I’m not so sure; it sounds to me like he may have only shown that a lazy field protocol can’t settle it.

So here is what I want. Suppose the claim is: for a radical translator, all possible behavioral evidence remains compatible with incompatible translations of a term like gavagai. Design the strongest test you can of that claim. Be concrete: what observations, manipulations, or experiments could bear on it? For example, could one use cut-up rabbits, rabbit tracks, animals behind screens, informants raised with different exposures, or training of novel terms? For each test, say exactly what result would push you toward or away from the claim. Then step back and say what the strongest test would still leave untouched—is there a residue of the claim that no experiment could ever reach? Im especially interested in where the line is between underdetermination by finite data and genuine indeterminacy. Finally, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and dont dress up a contested reading as the consensus.
