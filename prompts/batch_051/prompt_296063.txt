Over the past year I have been trying to decide whether to leave a research job I care about for a better-paying position in another city, and something about the way my deliberation keeps stalling has begun to seem theoretically important. The difficulty rarely feels like comparing the two options directly. It feels like trying to fix the relative weights of intellectual interest, salary, proximity to my parents, and long-term flexibility, where every plausible weighting immediately calls to mind another equally defensible weighting. Whenever I try to justify a particular weighting, I find myself needing to weigh considerations about that weighting, and so on; I stop only because I grow tired or because a deadline intervenes, never because the question has actually been settled.

I have started to suspect that this is not just my own weakness. My thesis is that a hard choice is hard because it is higher-order: the alternatives are not incommensurable in themselves, but the choice among schemes for making them commensurable is underdetermined by the values one already has. I call this the regress of comparative standards. I notice the same shape in smaller cases, such as deciding how to spend a sabbatical, or how much weight to give an old friendship when it collides with a professional opportunity. On this view, deliberation about a hard choice does not fail because the values resist comparison; rather, choosing a standard of comparison is itself a hard choice of the same shape, and the process has no fixed point. A hard choice, I want to say, is ultimately a choice about what is to count as having chosen well, and that meta-choice reproduces the original difficulty instead of resolving it.

I would like an honest assessment of this idea. Is it a coherent account of hard choices, or have I merely redescribed ordinary uncertainty with extra machinery? Does it already exist in the literature under a better name, and if so, how does that view handle the apparent regress? Most importantly, where does my account break down: are there clear cases of hard choices that do not have this higher-order structure, or natural stopping points I have missed?
