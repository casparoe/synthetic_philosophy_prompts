So a friend has been trying to get me into a syndicate investing in AI moonshots, and his pitch is basically Pascal's mugging with better slides: tiny probability, astronomical payoff. It's made me try to write down what I actually think about expected value reasoning, and before I show the list to anyone I'd like a consistency audit. Here's where I've landed:

(a) For ordinary, well-behaved gambles I'm an expected utility maximiser. In casinos, insurance, and the like, I think you simply take the higher expected utility, and I'd defend that without hedging.

(b) The St Petersburg game doesn't bother me much. I accept that its expected value in money is infinite, and I think the fact I'd only pay about twenty quid for a single play, rather than any finite amount, is fully explained by the diminishing marginal utility of money. I don't think it refutes expected utility theory.

(c) The Pasadena game is another matter. Flip a fair coin until it lands tails; if the first tails comes on the nth flip you get (-1)^(n-1) times 2^n/n, in pounds: so +2, -2, +8/3, -4, +32/5, and so on. In the natural order the expected value is 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which is ln 2, but the series is only conditionally convergent, so you can reorder the terms to get any real number or to make it diverge. I think the upshot is that the game has no expected value at all, and that "ln 2" is an artefact of an arbitrary ordering. I'd be reluctant to pay anything for it.

(d) On Pascal's mugging itself, I think the right response is to refuse to hand over my wallet, and I'm content to say the reason is that the mugger's probabilities are made up rather than estimated. I don't claim to have a crisp rule for how small a made-up probability of a made-up utility is to small.

(e) More generally, I hold that a decision rule shouldn't be sensitive to how a gamble is presented — reordering states, relabelling them — so long as the probabilities and payoffs are the same.
