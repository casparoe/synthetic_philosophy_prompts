The debate in philosophy of psychiatry over whether delusions are beliefs has, to my eye, been stuck for decades. Doxasticists emphasize that delusions are sincerely asserted, often defended with apparent reasoning, and sometimes acted on; anti-doxasticists emphasize that they are often compartmentalized, unrevisable in the face of direct counterevidence, and at times merely avowed without guiding action, which makes them look more like imaginings or verbal endorsements than beliefs. The two-factor theory seems oddly orthogonal to this dispute: it explains the content and the maintenance of a delusion, but one can accept that explanatory story and still disagree about whether the resulting state is a belief. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are weighting different features of the folk-psychological concept of belief—sincere judgment and inferential commitment on one side, integration into action and evidence-sensitivity on the other—so the disagreement is partly conceptual rather than empirical. Is that diagnosis right? If so, what kind of conceptual disagreement is it, and what would count as progress: a principled decomposition of "belief" into several distinct notions, an empirical study of how delusions actually guide reasoning and behavior, or something else? If my hypothesis is wrong, what is really keeping the impasse in place?
