For some time I have been working with a distinction between sponsored and unsponsored assertion, and I want it checked before I continue relying on it.

By a sponsored assertion I mean an assertion with an agent behind it who is answerable for its truth: someone who can be asked to defend it, who is expected to have arrived at it through some minimally competent relation to how things are, and who stands to lose credibility if it fails. A person who says "the train leaves at six" sponsors that sentence. On Frankfurt's picture, the liar and the bullshitter are both sponsors with different defects: the liar sponsors a sentence he takes to be false while intending to mislead, and the bullshitter sponsors sentences while being indifferent to their truth. Both failures are failures of a truth-answerable agent.

By an unsponsored assertion I mean sentence-shaped output that carries the surface demands of assertion — it has the grammar, the confident register, and the implicature of testimony, and it invites the reader to take it as information — but that has no truth-answerable backer within the system that produced it. The system is not indifferent to truth the way a bullshitter is, since indifference is a defect of an agent that could have cared. It does not regard its output as false, as a liar does. It is not in the business of either caring or not caring about truth. I want to say that unsponsored assertion is a fourth posture, not a blend of lying, bullshit, and error.

My central claim is that most of the dispute about whether LLM hallucination is lying, bullshit, or error is mis-posed. Hallucination is best classified as unsponsored assertion. It belongs to none of the three familiar categories because each of them requires a sponsor standing in some defective relation to the truth, whereas the model lacks the sponsor rather than the virtue. The evaluative consequences therefore land entirely on the human beings and institutions who choose to present the model's output as if it were sponsored: the publisher who lets a generated statistic into an article, the company that places the model in front of customers without a warning, the reader who converts unowned sentences into private belief. On this view a model cannot lie, but a person can lie with one; a model cannot bullshit, but an institution can bullshit through one.

A case I think the distinction handles: a newsroom publishes an AI-generated figure without checking it. Ordinary reaction oscillates between saying the model hallucinated, confabulated, or made things up. My distinction says the model did none of those things — it issued an unsponsored assertion — while the newsroom may have committed a familiar epistemic wrong, perhaps reckless bullshit or negligent error. I also think the distinction predicts that treating hallucination as a moral failing of the model is a category confusion that quietly insulates the human sponsors from responsibility.

What I want from you is an honest assessment. Is the concept coherent, or does it smuggle a speaker back in by using the language of assertion for model output at all? Is it already current under some other name — an account of testimony without a testifier, scripts, bots, delegated speech — and if so, does my version add anything? Where does it break: is there a case that is unsponsored in my sense yet deserves first-person blame, or a case that is sponsored but behaves in every important respect like hallucination? And tell me what would change your mind: which consideration would lead you to fold unsponsored assertion back into one of the three familiar categories, or to conclude that the fourth category names something real but does no work in explaining why hallucination matters.
