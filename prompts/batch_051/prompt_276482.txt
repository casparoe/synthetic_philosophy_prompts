I am writing an essay whose running claim is that the still-live disagreement between Hartian positivism and Fullerian procedural natural law is not the familiar one about iniquitous statutes in wicked regimes, but about whether a well-run nonstate order of rules counts as law. I want a single thought experiment, built to specification, to sharpen that claim.

Construct a case in which a Hart-style positivist must classify a community as pre-legal — a set of primary rules of obligation without a legal system — while a Fuller-style natural lawyer must classify that same community as law. This is the reverse of the standard Nazi-statute direction, in which the positivist affirms legal validity and the natural lawyer denies it.

The disagreement must be driven entirely by formal features of legality, not by the justice of what the rules require. The positivist's denial should follow from Hart's account of a society governed only by primary rules, with no rule of recognition, no rule of change, and no rule of adjudication. The natural lawyer's affirmation should follow from the community's rules doing well on Fuller's desiderata of legality: generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, stability over time, and congruence between the rules as declared and the conduct of whoever applies them. Set the case in an ordinary setting — a long-running online community, a professional body, a residents' association — and write the scenario as a single paragraph. Do not stipulate the verdicts; the case must generate them from each theory's own criteria.

After the scenario, walk through both sides: which feature each theory treats as decisive, and why it delivers the reverse verdict. Then isolate the single feature doing the work, and say what happens if that feature is removed — for instance, by adding one crude secondary rule such as a written amendment procedure or an adjudication body. Does the disagreement collapse, or does it relocate? Finish by giving the strongest attack a defender of Hart's position would mount against the case, and say whether the case can be answered without rigging the scenario.
