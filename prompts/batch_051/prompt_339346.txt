I run a behavioural insights unit in a large English local authority. The unit reviews, designs and signs off on around forty interventions a year — defaults in forms, changes to the framing or ordering of options, social-norm messages, reminders and similar 'nudges' — across council tax, recycling, adult social care, public health and council-backed lending. Each proposal gets about forty-five minutes in a weekly design meeting, and the team must decide whether to proceed, proceed with modifications and senior sign-off, or reject the proposal as manipulative or unacceptably paternalistic. No one on the team has formal training in philosophy. Each decision affects at least ten thousand residents, and our written justifications are audited by an elected scrutiny committee; we therefore need to know which steps in our reasoning are mechanical and which are judgement calls.

Write a procedure the team can use. It should distil the underlying philosophical distinctions about paternalism, manipulation, autonomy, welfare and the preservation of freedom of choice into a checklist or ordered sequence of tests that a non-philosopher can run in the time we have. Say which distinctions must survive the simplification and which can be dropped, and mark every step that is really a judgement call dressed up as a procedure. Among the questions we need the procedure to handle are these. When does an intervention engage a person's own ends, and when does it substitute the authority's ends for the person's? Why are 'they can opt out' and 'we are only giving people information' each insufficient, on their own, to show that an intervention is a nudge rather than manipulation? When, if ever, does transparency render permissible an intervention that would otherwise be objectionable? And how should the procedure distinguish an intervention that works through a bias we would not endorse as a reason — such as inertia in a default — from one that works through an emotion such as shame or anxiety?

Then apply the procedure to three proposals. (1) The council pension scheme changes from opt-in joining (employees must return a form) to auto-enrolment from the first pay period, with an opt-out of roughly equal effort. (2) The recycling service sends each household a message reporting that its recycling tonnage is below the average for its street, using social comparison to raise compliance. (3) A resident at risk of problem gambling applies to the council-backed lender; before the application can be completed, a six-hour cooling-off pause is inserted, asking the applicant to reconsider. At least one application should reach a conclusion we would find inconvenient — an effective intervention rejected, or sent to elected members for sign-off. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: the residual cases in which the team will have to deliberate rather than follow steps.

Please write the answer in British English. The procedure should be self-contained: no citations, footnotes or recommendations for further reading.
