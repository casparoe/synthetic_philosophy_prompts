I'm a retired field ecologist — I spent my working life counting things and watching what a small intervention does to a system — and since I retired I've been reading philosophy, mostly ethics and decision theory, enough to know I don't know much. A former colleague of mine now does AI safety review at a company that builds personal assistant agents: the kind that can pay a bill, book a room, cancel a subscription, reorder things, reply to emails on your behalf. He asked me, as his resident philosopher, whether "avoid side effects" can be turned into a workable release standard without the company writing down a full specification of what people value. I said I'd think about it, and now I'm asking you.

What I want is a reusable decision procedure for his job, not a one-off. Here's the set-up. He reviews proposed agent behaviours once or twice a week before release, and each review gets about 45 minutes. The stakes: individual actions can spend real money up to a few hundred pounds, send messages, alter accounts, or change records; rarely life-critical, but side effects can land on third parties — a meeting moved, a shared file altered, a notification sent to the wrong person. The company won't sign off on anything that could become a local news story, and he has to write a short justification for every descision for a release board that includes non-technical people.

Please turn whatever the philosophy actually supports into a checklist he can run: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a human because the question is about values rather than facts, and which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure. It needs to be usable by someone without a philosophy background, and honest about where it will misfire.

Some distinctions I suspect matter, and I'd like the procedure to handle them rather than wave at them: the baseline problem — side effects compared to what, doing nothing or what would have happened anyway?; reversibility — some side effects can be undone cheaply, some can't; whether the impact is concentrated on one person or spread thin across many; and the tension that a perfect no-op would be extremely low-impact but also useless, so you can't just minimise impact and call it a day.

Run it through three worked examples: (1) an agent asked to cancel a subscription that, in doing so, deletes the payment history for that service; (2) an agent told "make sure I don't double-book this week" that reschedules two meetings without checking with the other attendees; (3) one of your own choosing where the procedure says they shouldn't release something the product team is sure is harmless — make it a case they'll hate. And say clearly what the procedure can't do: in particular, where "low impact" just bottoms out in needing a real account of value.

Oh, and British English please.
