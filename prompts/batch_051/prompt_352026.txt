I'm a novelist, though these days I seem to spend half my time chairing the board of a small writers' association. Our bylaws are full of holes: they don't say what happens when the treasurer resigns mid-term, whether the vice-chair can spend money between meetings when something urgent comes up, or who gets to decide when a dispute falls in a gap nobody thought to write a rule about. The board's default has been "the chair just decides, and the board ratifies later." One board member keeps reaching for Locke — John Locke, the Second Treatise of Government, the chapter "Of Prerogative," section 159 — and quotes this sentence at us: "WHERE the legislative and executive power are in distinct hands, (as they are in all moderated monarchies, and well-framed governments) there the good of the society requires, that several things should be left to the discretion of him that has the executive power: for the legislators not being able to foresee, and provide by laws, for all that may be useful to the community, the executor of the laws, having the power in his hands, has by the common law of nature a right to make use of it for the good of the society, in many cases, where the municipal law has given no direction, till the legislative can conveniently be assembled to provide for it."

I can't tell whether that passage actually licenses our default or whether it's narrower than it looks, and I've got to give the board something we can use without any of us becoming Locke scholars. So: turn what Locke is really saying there, along with whatever the broader theory says about constitutional gaps and interregna, into a reusable procedure our board can run every time the rules run out. A decision procedure or checklist in words, not a one-off answer. We meet monthly, some decisions can't wait a month, none of us are lawyers, and the stakes are usually small but occasionally involve real money or expelling a member. Work the procedure on two or three test cases — including at least one where it gives us an answer we won't like — and flag the points where no procedure can eliminate judgment, and what the procedure can't do. And please: no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables; just continuous prose, like a letter to the board.
