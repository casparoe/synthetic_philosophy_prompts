An argument I reconstructed from Montaigne's essay about the Tupinambá of Brazil looks like an argument for cultural relativism, and it now seems to prove too much. I cannot find the step to reject.

(1) Montaigne reports that the Tupinambá kill and eat captured enemies, but only after entertaining the prisoner well and as the completion of an ethic of vengeance and courage; he argues that this is no crueler, and probably less cruel, than what Europeans do when they torture and burn people alive in religious conflict.
(2) If that is right, then our readiness to call the Tupinambá barbarous while overlooking our own comparable cruelties cannot be tracking any cross-cultural standard of cruelty; it can only be tracking custom.
(3) But if such judgments track only custom, then no practice can be judged better or worse than another except relative to a custom.
(4) The comparison in (1) is itself just such a judgment across customs: it ranks the European practice against the Tupinambá one.
(5) By (3), that ranking is itself only the expression of one custom, not a correction of another.
(6) So Montaigne's critique of ethnocentrism, if (1)–(3) hold, loses the very critical force it claims: it can replace one custom with another but cannot rationally convict a custom of error.

Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep the critical force of (1) and (2) rather than accept (6), but I cannot see how to stop the slide without abandoning the comparison the whole essay depends on.
