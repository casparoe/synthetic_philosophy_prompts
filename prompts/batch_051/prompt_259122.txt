I've been thinking on my own about two competing ways to rank how advanced a civilization is. The familiar one is the Kardashev scale, which ranks civilizations by the total power they can command: all the energy of a planet, then a star, then a galaxy. The less familiar one, which I understand John Barrow proposed, runs in the opposite direction: it ranks civilizations by the smallest scale at which they can deliberately shape matter, moving from engineering at roughly our own size down to engineering atoms, nuclei, or the structure of space and time itself. I'd like a probabilistic verdict rather than an "it depends." Here is the proposition I want assessed: if we had to collapse a civilization's advancement into a single number, the smallest scale at which it can routinely manipulate matter is a better index of its actual capability than its total energy consumption. What is your honest credence in that proposition, as a number between 0 and 1? Then decompose the number. In particular, how much of your estimate rides on what "advancement" is even supposed to track (control and understanding versus sheer throughput), how much on the concern that energy use is confounded by population size and waste while small-scale engineering cannot be faked by merely adding more people, and how much on the possibility that information processing, not energy or scale per se, is the variable doing the real work? My own instinct is around 0.65, but I want your number and your breakdown, and I also want to know which single sub-question would move your credence the most and what sort of evidence would change it.
