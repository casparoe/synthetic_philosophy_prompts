I am writing a short essay for a seminar on philosophy of law and the family, and I want to work through Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) in a specific way. Instead of asking which side had the better legal argument, I want to treat the decision as an episode in which two rival accounts of what marriage is for collided. The first I will call the Institutional Theory: marriage is an objective social form whose point includes, and is centrally ordered toward, permanent comprehensive union and the bearing and rearing of children, so the state's interest lies in recognizing and stabilizing that form as a public good rather than merely ratifying whatever arrangements adults prefer. The second I will call the Autonomy Theory: marriage is a legal status that secures equal dignity and a bundle of material and social benefits for a chosen, committed, caring adult relationship, with procreation treated as neither necessary nor central. My working assumption is that the majority opinion mostly speaks the language of the Autonomy Theory while the dissents lean, unevenly, on the Institutional Theory, though neither side announces itself in these terms.

As background you may take for granted that the Court held that same-sex couples have a fundamental right to marry under the Due Process Clause and that denying them marriage violates equal protection, and that the dissents objected on grounds of democratic self-government, the absence of a textual basis for the right, and the consequences for religious liberty and for the traditional understanding of the family. With that in mind, please write a careful case analysis that runs in both directions. From the theories to the case: what each theory says the real stakes were, which claims in the majority and dissenting positions instantiate which theory, and where the mapping is loose or fails. From the case to the theories: what each theory would commit someone to beyond the same-sex marriage issue—for example, what the Institutional Theory implies for marriages that are, or are intended to be, non-procreative, and what the Autonomy Theory implies for state recognition of other adult caregiving relationships—and how the Court's silence on those implications reveals the limits of the two theories. Finally, identify the axes of the case that are not reducible to the disagreement over what marriage is for, such as the proper judicial role, federalism, the weight of constitutional text, religious liberty, and the expressive significance of exclusion, and then give an honest verdict on whether the Court settled the philosophical dispute or only chose a level of abstraction at which it could decide the case.

- axes_not_reducible: an array of objects, each with the fields axis (a short string naming one contested axis of the case) and explanation (one sentence on why that axis is not reducible to the marriage-purpose debate).
