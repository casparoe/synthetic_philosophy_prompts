Not sure if I'm framing this right, but I could use a hand. I run the safety-review process at a smallish medical-device maker. Every quarter or so we decide whether to ship a change or kill it, and I convene a panel of three to seven engineers to vote thumbs up/thumbs down. I've read just enough to know the Condorcet jury theorem says, roughly, that when each voter is better than chance and they vote independently, a majority is more trustworthy than any single member and it gets stronger as the panel grows. Heres the problem: my people have all read the same risk memo and sat in the same meeting, so the independence part is basically shot, and I dont know what that does to the number. I need a reusable procedure — a kind of pre-vote checklist — that tells me, before I rely on a panel decision, whether to treat the majority as genuinely more reliable than the panel's best individual, treat the vote as advice only, or kick the call up to me or the head of QA. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with "dont aggregate this", and where it flags a judgment call rather than pretending to be math. Then run the procedure on two cases. Case one: a six-person panel, all of whom took the same two-day training and read the same summary, votes 5-1 to ship a battery change. Case two: a five-person panel in which each member independently inspected a different subsystem and formed their own conclusion votes 4-1 to block a firmware change. One of these should come out the way I won't like. And tell me straight what the theorem won't do for me.
