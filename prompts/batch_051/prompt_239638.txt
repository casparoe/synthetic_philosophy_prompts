I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy, and for the past year I have been puzzled by a pair of commitments I keep meeting but cannot name. In discussion, a colleague will defend the view that if a firefighter faces a collapsing building and can carry out either one stranger or five strangers, the mere fact that there are five on one side does not by itself settle what the firefighter should do; each of the five, she says, has a claim no stronger than the one, and treating the five together as a single unit that outweighs the one involves a kind of arithmetic error about persons. Yet the same colleague, once the topic turns to ventilators, vaccination schedules, or road-safety spending, sits entirely comfortably with summing expected deaths and years of life across a population and letting the larger figure settle the choice. She does not appear to feel any tension between these two stances, or, if she does, the principle that separates them has never been made explicit.

I am trying to work out whether this combination of views has a name, either together or in its two parts. I take it the first component is a thesis about whether numbers of people matter in their own right in rescue cases, while the second is fairly ordinary public-health reasoning; but is there an established label for the first thesis, and is there an established label for holding the first in face-to-face rescue cases while accepting head-counting in institutional ones? More than a name, I want the boundaries: is the first thesis meant to cover every choice in which adding more people to the larger group does not change the loss borne by any individual, or only choices about saving lives, or only one-off decisions rather than standing policies? And if the rescue/policy distinction is doing the work, what is the most defensible way to draw it without making the position merely ad hoc?
