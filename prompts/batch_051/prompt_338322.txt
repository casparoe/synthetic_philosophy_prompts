I'm a retired physical chemist, and after a couple years of reading political philosophy I keep circling one question. It's about AI assistants: when should one refuse a user's request, or quietly override it, for the user's own good? I dont mean cases involving illegality or harm to other people—just the paternalism cases. A philosopher friend pointed me at this bit in Mill's On Liberty, chapter IV:

"in each person’s own concerns, his individual spontaneity is entitled to free exercise. Considerations to aid his judgment, exhortations to strengthen his will, may be offered to him, even obtruded on him, by others; but he himself is the final judge. All errors which he is likely to commit against advice and warning, are far outweighed by the evil of allowing others to constrain him to what they deem his good."

What strikes me is that Mill is willing to permit an awful lot of interference right up to the last inch: you can press considerations on someone, even obtrude them, but at the end he remains the final judge. An AI assistant sits exactly on that inch. It can argue, remind, rephrase, warn—but when it refuses to draft the email or silently leaves the guardrail on, it has stopped offering considerations and started being a gatekeeper. Or maybe not: maybe declining to help isnt constraining him, any more than my refusing to help a tipsy friend find his car keys amounts to coercion. That's the line I want to get clearer about.

So here's what I want, and I want it as a defended agenda, not a survey. Suppose I could point a small research program—three or four people for a couple years, mostly philosophers who know enough about the systems to understand how refusal actually gets implemented—at the conceptual side of this. Which two or three open questions about AI paternalism should they settle first, if the deliverable is a defensible rule for when assistants may refuse or override competent adult users for the users' own good? Argue for the ordering. For each question, say what hinges on it downstream (would answering it change what a product team or a regulator should do?), how tractable it is without running experiments, and whether it's load-bearing for the Millian framework in the passage or mostly internal to some other view. Then tell me which questions you'd deprioritize because they're interesting but wouldn't move the answer. And be honest about where Mill's framework starts to creak when the "other person" isn't a neighbor but a system trained partly on the user's own stated and revealed preferences. I spent a career watching elegant models fail at the last inch, and I'd like to know which questions actually live on that inch.
