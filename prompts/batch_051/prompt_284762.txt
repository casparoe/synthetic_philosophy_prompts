I'm a philosophy of language PhD student who keeps getting dragged into metaontology, and I've realized I can't actually state what "merely verbal" is supposed to rule out. Can you help me build a working defintion of a *merely verbal dispute* between two people? I have a bunch of cases I want the definition to classify, and I want to see where it breaks.

Here are the cases, roughly in order from "obviously verbal" to "obviously not," though the middle is where im genuinely unsure:

1. Two cooks argue about whether a tomato is a fruit. They agree on all the botanical facts, the culinary uses, everything. One uses "fruit" the botanist's way, the other the grocery-store way. (I'm confident this is verbal.)

2. Two people argue about whether a hot dog is a sandwich. They agree on the physical structure, how it's eaten, and both admit nothing practical turns on the answer. (Also confident: verbal.)

3. Two epidemiologists argue about whether a vaccine is "safe." They agree on all the numbers—adverse event rates, hospitalizations prevented, confidence intervals, the lot. One thinks "safe" means a severe adverse event rate below one in a million; the other sets the bar at one in ten million. They also agree this threshold question changes policy. (This is where my intuition starts to wobble.)

4. Two particle physicists argue about whether a small bump in a collider dataset is a new particle or background noise. They mean exactly the same thing by every term, they use the same statistical model, and they disagree about the analysis and how much the background accounts for. (I'm fairly confident this is NOT verbal.)

5. Two epistemologists argue about whether someone in a stopped-clock case "knows" the time. They agree on every detail: the clock is stopped, the person glances at it at exactly the right moment, the belief is justified and true but true by luck. One says the person knows; the other says they don't. (This feels like a hard case to me.)

6. Two philosophers argue about whether a statue is identical to the lump of clay that constitutes it. They agree on all the physical facts, the clay's history, what would happen if you squashed it, all of it. One says there are two objects in one place right now, the other says there's one. The first person insists this is a real fact about the world, not just a choice of counting scheme. (My metaontology people can't agree whether this is substantive.)

7. Two astronomers argue about whether Pluto is a planet. They agree on Pluto's size, orbit, composition, and history, and they both know the two standard definitions give opposite answers. Their argument is entirely about which definition should go in textbooks and what schoolkids get taught. (Is this still "merely verbal"? It feels different from the tomato case but I can't say why.)

What I want: state your definition, run it through all seven cases, tell me where it gives a verdict you'd defend but that ordinary speakers would find weird, and then revise or defend it. If you think "merely verbal dispute" collapses or splits into two different things when it hits the middle cases, just say that and give me the distinction we should be using instead.
