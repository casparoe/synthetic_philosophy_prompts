Just got back from a three-hour argument with a friend about whether AI alignment is solvable "in principle." The whole time we kept using the phrase, and every time one of us made a point the other would say "sure, but that's not what I mean by in principle." I wound up writing down the seperate senses we actually cycled through. Between the two of us I think we covered most of the standard ones, but now I'm wondering whether there's one we missed. Here's the list:

1. Existence: there is a policy (some function from situations to actions) that would keep an AI aligned even if no finite agent could ever find it or write it down.
2. Computability: there's a Turing machine that implements the aligned policy; time and memory are ignored entirely.
3. Physical realizability: the algorithm could in principle be run in the actual universe given enough matter, energy, and time.
4. Learnability in the limit: with unlimited data and updates you'd eventually latch onto an aligned policy and never leave it, though you might never know you've arrived.
5. Verifiability: if someone handed you a claimed solution, you could in principle check that it works, even if you can't find one yourself.
6. Solvability by an ideal reasoner: a perfect Bayesian / Laplace's demon type agent could solve it, so it's solvable "in principle" even though no real agent can.
7. Well-posedness: there's a determinate fact of the matter about what counts as aligned, so "in principle" just means the question has a definite answer independent of our limitations.

Give me one more sense of "in principle" that is genuinely distinct from these seven — not a restatement, not a special case, and not a conjunction of two of them — and say whether adding it makes the claim "alignment is solvable in principle" easier or harder to defend than it looks from the list alone. Also, if you think two of my seven actually collapse into each other, tell me where and why.
