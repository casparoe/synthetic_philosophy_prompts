I am writing an essay for a high school bioethics course on the view sometimes called the hedonistic imperative: the proposal that humanity should use genetic engineering and other biotechnology to phase out the biological capacity for suffering, replacing it with gradients of well-being across all sentient life. I want to work through a definition of suffering that this project would be targeting, and I have a borderline case I cannot settle.

Here are the criteria I am considering for suffering in the relevant sense. (1) Negative valence: the state feels bad to the person in it. (2) Aversive motivation: the person is moved to escape, stop, or avoid the state while it is occurring. (3) Reflective rejection: on calm reflection, the person would prefer not to be in the state for its own sake. (4) No compensatory role: the state does not enable or deepen some distinct good that the person reflectively endorses.

Now consider this case. A person watches a tragedy that they know in advance will make them sad. The sadness is genuine and unpleasant, and during the film they feel a pull to look away or stop, which they override. They chose the experience, would choose it again, and regard the sadness as part of what makes the work valuable; they would not take a drug that removed the sadness while leaving the rest of the experience intact.

Does this aesthetic sadness qualify as suffering under the four criteria? Please go through each criterion honestly, and where the criteria underdetermine the answer, say so. Then address the deeper question: if the hedonistic imperative aims to abolish all suffering, does this case show that its operative definition of suffering needs to be narrowed to something like suffering that is not reflectively endorsed or embedded in a valued whole, or does it instead show that the hedonistic imperative is committed to eliminating even valuable sadness, and that this is a cost its defenders should accept?
