My comparative psych professor spends half the semester invoking Morgan's canon and the other half complaining nobody applies it right. Her one-line version: don't put a fancier mind into an animal than the behavior actually forces on you. Her verdicts so far:

- a squirrel that caches thousands of seeds and digs them up months later: spatial memory, not mental time travel.
- an octopus that gets steadily quicker at a puzzle box over trials: operant learning, not causal understanding.
- a chimp that grooms the loser of a fight afterwards: tension regulation, not empathic consolation.
- a raven that solves a novel multi-step tool problem on first exposure, no training on the steps: fine, that's means-end planning.
- a border collie that fetches a novel toy from a pile after hearing a new name once: exclusion by novelty, not word learning.

Next week's case is a cleaner wrasse in a mirror-mark test. A brown mark is put on its throat, where it can't see it without the mirror. With the mirror visible it swims over, looks, then scrapes its throat against a rock. It doesn't do this with a transparent mark, and it doesn't do it when the mirror is covered.

What should we predict she'll say? Does she grant the wrasse self-recognition or some self-directed mirror use, or does she reach for a lower process? What rule is she actually running — a last-resort canon reading, a controlled-test criterion, a neural-complexity cutoff, or something else? Which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog? And if the record underdetermines it, what further case would settle which rule she's using?
