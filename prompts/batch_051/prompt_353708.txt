I am currently taking a course on trust, communication, and moral accountability, and we have been debating whether people have a duty to be legible to others — whether we owe it to one another to make our intentions, reasons, and commitments interpretable rather than opaque. One side argues that opacity is a quiet failure of respect: it leaves other people unable to rely on us, to coordinate with us, or to blame and praise us accurately. The other side replies that the demand for legibility can be coercive, forcing people into available social scripts and making the less powerful party do the work of presenting themselves in terms the more powerful party will accept. A distinction we keep circling is the difference between being intelligible within a shared practice and being answerable on demand, and I want to see whether a literary case can help separate those two ideas.

I want to bring Herman Melville's story Bartleby, the Scrivener to bear on this. The plot turns on Bartleby's famous repeated refusals of his employer's requests, offered politely but without any real explanation, and on the lawyer-narrator's increasingly desperate attempts to understand, accommodate, and finally rescue him. Read the story through the lens of the duty-to-be-legible debate: does the story present Bartleby's opacity as a wrong done to the narrator, or the narrator's insistence on a legible account as a wrong done to Bartleby, and where does it refuse either simple verdict?
