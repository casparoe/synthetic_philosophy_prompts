I am working through Bernard Mandeville's *The Grumbling Hive: or, Knaves Turn'd Honest* (1705), the verse fable he later folded, together with prose remarks, into *The Fable of the Bees: or, Private Vices, Publick Benefits*. I keep returning to one stanza, which follows his catalogue of the professions and of the callings he describes as knavish, and I find it harder than the rest of the poem. Here is the passage:

    Whilst others follow'd Mysteries,
    To which few Folks bind Prentices;
    That want no Stock, but that of Brass,
    And may set up without a Cross;
    As Sharpers, Parasites, Pimps, Players,
    Pick-Pockets, Coiners, Quacks, Sooth-Sayers,
    And all those, that, in Enmity
    With down-right Working, cunningly
    Convert to their own Use the Labour
    Of their good-natur'd heedless Neighbour:
    These were called Knaves; but, bar the Name,
    The grave Industrious were the Same.
    All Trades and Places knew some Cheat,
    No Calling was without Deceit.

Could you give me a close and patient reading of this passage, written in British English? My difficulty is not the vocabulary: I take "Mysteries" to mean trades or crafts, and "may set up without a Cross" to mean that one may enter such a calling without capital. What I cannot settle is the structure of the claim. When Mandeville says that, "bar the Name," the "grave Industrious were the Same" as the sharpers, pimps and pick-pockets, is he making a point about hypocrisy — respectable acquisitive callings differ from cheating only in the label they are given — or a stronger point about how an acquisitive commercial order actually works, namely that its prosperity runs on the same self-interested cunning, merely regularised and renamed? Does the closing couplet, "All Trades and Places knew some Cheat, / No Calling was without Deceit," dissolve the distinction between greed as a private vice and greed as a public engine, and if so, in which direction does the dissolution cut? Is Mandeville telling us that acquisitiveness is a vice whose useful effects must be faced without flattery; that the very classification of greed as vice and as public benefit cannot survive this analysis; or that the passage is satirical in a way I have not yet correctly identified? If it is satire, please say whom or what it targets, and show how the wording — especially "cunningly" and "bar the Name" — carries the satirical work.

Anchor your answer in the exact wording of the passage, and please do not smooth away the irony; I would rather the strain in his position remain visible than be explained off.
