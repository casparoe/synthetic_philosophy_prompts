I've been trying to work out, just for my own satisfaction, when a person counts as the cause of a bad outcome because of what they didn't do. I've collected my gut verdicts on a handful of cases, and I can feel a rule behind them, but whenever I try to state it, it cracks somewhere. I'd like you to help me find the rule, or tell me what has to be given up.

Here are my cases and verdicts.

1. I promised a neighbor I would water her tomato plants while she was away. I forgot, there was a heat wave, and the plants died. I would say I caused the plants' death.

2. A dog was tied up outside a shop in the heat with no water. I walked past, saw how distressed it was, and kept going; the dog died later that day. I don't think I caused its death, though I think I did something wrong by not stopping.

3. A lifeguard on duty was looking at his phone while a swimmer struggled in the water directly in front of his chair; she drowned. I would say the lifeguard caused the drowning.

4. On another day, an ordinary beachgoer who is a strong swimmer, and the only other person on the beach, watched a swimmer go under in the same way and did nothing — didn't shout, didn't throw the rescue ring beside him. I don't think the beachgoer caused the death.

5. I noticed that a board on my front step was rotting and never repaired it; a delivery driver later put his foot through it and broke his ankle. I would say I caused the injury.

6. A woman who had spent years as a paramedic was eating in a restaurant when a man at the next table began to choke. She knew exactly how to clear his airway, and no one else in the room did, but she stayed seated, and the man died. I find myself wanting to say she caused his death, even though she was off duty and had no relationship to him. This verdict sits badly with case 4, and I can't say why the two feel different.

My working hunch had been that a failure to act is a cause only when it is a failure to do something the person had a specific responsibility to do — through a promise, a job, or ownership. But case 6 seems to break that, unless having a rare skill that no one else present has is itself a kind of responsibility, which seems to stretch the idea too far.

Please give me the following: a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts. For each candidate, say precisely what rule it states, which case pushes toward each clause, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then tell me what each candidate would say about a case I'm still stuck on: a stranger at a playground sees a toddler about to run into a busy road, could easily pull the child back, and does nothing; the child is hit. Finally, offer one further case of your own, chosen to distinguish the candidates from each other, and say how each would rule on it.
