I have been trying to work out what, if anything, licenses the use of works whose owners are gone — orphan works, abandoned software, and the gray zone between them — and my judgments across cases keep resisting any principle I can state cleanly. I would like you to extract the best principle, or the best small set of principles, that captures my verdicts, and tell me honestly where I have to revise something.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

1. A portrait studio closed in 1981; its negatives were never sold or assigned, and no successor can be identified. A county historical society scans some of the 1960s portraits and puts them online. I think this is plainly fine.

2. A novelist died a decade ago. Her unfinished, unpublished draft sits in a library archive; her daughter, who controls the literary estate, refuses to allow a scholar to produce an edited edition. I think the scholar should not publish the draft over the daughter's objection, even though in practice the work is as unreachable as any orphan.

3. A software company still exists and still owns a 1994 program that it no longer sells, supports, updates, or has enforced in years. A user writes a compatibility patch so the program runs on modern computers and distributes the patch for free; the company says nothing. I think the patch is fine, but I would not defend someone reselling the full program.

4. A small game studio went bankrupt in 1998 and its assets were never transferred to any currently existing entity. After two decades of dormancy, a fan group remakes the game commercially. I think this is permissible — or at worst the kind of act about which no one with standing remains to complain.

5. A label that published field recordings in the 1970s has dissolved, and the original rights-holder is untraceable. A filmmaker uses forty seconds of one recording in a profitable documentary. I think the use was fine at the time; if a rights-holder surfaced later, I would want them paid something for continued use going forward, but I would not want retroactive damages for the years when there was, by all appearances, no one to ask.

6. A programmer releases a free utility and then dies. For twelve years a loose community keeps it maintained, fixed, and available. A company takes the current, community-maintained code, improves it, and sells a product without crediting or sharing revenue with the maintainers. I think the company has done something wrong, but the wrong is against the maintainers, not against the long-dead programmer.

The only general idea I have managed to articulate is something like: a claim weakens through neglect and is refreshed by the care that keeps a work alive; absent owners forfeit by default, and those whose labor keeps a work available acquire something like use-rights. I know this sounds like a mix of abandonment and adverse possession applied to ideas and files rather than land, but neither legal analogy has ever felt exact to me. The idea fits cases 1, 3, 4, and 6, but it seems to give the scholar in case 2 a standing I do not want him to have, and I worry it proves too much — that anyone who merely mirrors an old file would count as having earned a say.

Please give me the following. First, the best candidate principle, or small set of principles, with the case that motivates each clause, stated precisely enough to apply to new cases. Second, the principle's verdict on a case I have not listed: a legally defunct publisher's out-of-print translations are scanned without permission to build a public online archive, and fifteen years later a distant institutional successor to the publisher emerges and asks to be paid for the archive's continued operation — what does the principle say? Third, an honest statement of which of my six verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without special pleading, and whether I should revise that verdict or accept a principled exception. If the right conclusion is that no one principle can do all of this, say so and tell me what the most economical package of principles looks like.
