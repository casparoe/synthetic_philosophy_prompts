I'm working through the paradox of deontology for a dissertation chapter, and I want to check whether there is a response to it that does work none of the standard ones do. The paradox, as I understand it, is this. A deontologist who accepts an agent-centered constraint against killing an innocent person seems to hold that a world with fewer such killings is better in the respect that the constraint tracks, all else equal. But the same deontologist typically denies that she may kill one innocent person even to prevent five other innocent people from being killed. She therefore forbids an act that would leave the world with fewer violations of the very constraint she endorses, and the challenge is to explain why this does not amount to a practical inconsistency or an unprincipled attachment to clean hands.

The responses I have already cataloged are these five.

(1) The agency response: the constraint governs what the agent does, not what happens. That five other people will be killed by someone else is a bad state of affairs, but it is not a violation by the agent; the deontologist's duty is to avoid murdering, not to minimize the total number of murders.

(2) The threshold response: deontological constraints need not be absolute. The prohibition can hold up to a threshold of catastrophe and give way beyond it, so the one-versus-five case only looks paradoxical if one assumes the constraint is exceptionless.

(3) The intention/foresight response, drawing on the doctrine of double effect: the constraint forbids intending a death as an end or as a means. Killing the one person to save five would intend her death as a means, whereas refusing would merely foresee the five deaths. The two acts differ in kind, so the deontologist is not weighing one intentional killing against five side-effect deaths on a single scale.

(4) The inviolability or separateness-of-persons response, associated with Nozick's idea of side constraints: persons are inviolable and not to be sacrificed for the good of others. Using one person as a resource to prevent five deaths treats that person as a means in a way the constraint rules out, so the paradox assumes an interpersonal aggregation the deontologist rightly rejects.

(5) The contractualist response: a principle permitting one to kill an innocent person to prevent five killings is one that the person killed could reasonably reject, while a principle forbidding the killing could be justified to each person. The appearance of paradox arises only if one evaluates the rule from an aggregate, outcome-focused standpoint rather than from the standpoint of each affected person.

I suspect this list is close to exhaustive, and in particular I am not asking you to retrieve a famous response I forgot if all the famous ones are already here. I want a further response — familiar or not — that is importantly different in structure from these five. For clarity, I will not count a restatement of the idea that the action belongs to the agent, or a special case of a threshold view, or a combination of items (1) and (4), or a variant of the claim that numbers do not aggregate. What I want is a response that does different work: it should block, dissolve, or reframe the paradox by a different route.

Then assess the response the way you would in a seminar. Give the strongest objection to it, identify where your assessment is most likely to fail, and say which argument or consideration would lead you to reverse your assessment of it.
