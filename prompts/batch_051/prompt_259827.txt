The other night in our decision-theory reading group, someone kept shutting down a friend of mine with the line that "people with intransitive preferences are just money pumps." My friend had been arguing that it's no failure of rationality to prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A if the options never actually come together in a single choice situation — like you're ranking possible lives or career paths, and the trade-offs just happen to cycle on you. The money-pump guy rolled his eyes and repeated his slogan anyway, as if that settled the matter. I'm not actually asking you to settle whether intransitive preferences are rational. What I want is for you to unpack that slogan the way it actually gets used. What are the different precise claims "intransitive preferences make you a money pump" could be expressing? For instance, it could be a descriptive prediction that some trader can exploit you, a normative claim that exploitability by a foreseeable series of trades is enough to make a preference structure irrational, a claim about what a sequence of pairwise choices reveals, a claim about diachronic incoherence as opposed to synchronic incoherence, and probably a few others I'm not seeing. For each precisification, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or just false, and more importantly, which reading (if any) would actually license using the slogan to dismiss my friend's point that the options never actually come together. Lay it out as a menu; I want to bring something concrete to next week's session instead of just letting the slogan do all the work.
