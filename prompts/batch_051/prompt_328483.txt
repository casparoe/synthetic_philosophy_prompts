I keep noticing a pattern in arguments about aging and life extension for which I have never found a precise word, and I am trying to work out whether one exists or whether the thing needs to be coined. The pattern is easiest to see in individual conversations. A person will treat as obviously good a whole range of medical interventions that restore someone to the species-typical state for their age — a new heart valve for a seventy-year-old, insulin, a hip replacement, a flu shot, a skin graft — and will take it for granted that it would be irrational to decline these things in the name of letting nature take its course. Yet when the subject turns to interventions aimed at slowing or delaying the aging process itself, the same person will say that extending the human lifespan is unnatural, that it is selfish, that death is what gives life meaning, or that we should not play God. What puzzles me is not the disagreement but the location of it: the moral line falls between restoring the current age-typical state and improving upon that state, and almost no one can state a principle that justifies putting the line there. Pressed on the difference between a vaccine and a rejuvenative therapy that keeps an older person's immune system functioning as it did decades earlier, they will fall back on words like normal, natural, or human, but the words are doing the work that an argument should do.

The same selective acceptance of death shows up in print and in policy debates. A writer who argues that a longer life would rob us of urgency and meaning will, elsewhere, urge older readers to pursue aggressive treatment for a condition that would cut a decade off an already-long life; a bioethicist worried about hubris will sign a statement against radical life extension but raise no parallel objection to a bypass. Death is treated as something to be fought off at every margin except the one margin where fighting it would change the species-wide trajectory. This makes me suspect the underlying thing is less a view about death than a rule about baselines: the current human condition is converted into a moral baseline, and the burden of justification is felt only by those who would move above it.

I know this is somewhere in the neighborhood of status quo bias and sour grapes, but neither seems to capture it. Status quo bias is usually an unreflective preference for things remaining as they are; here the status quo is actively moralized, and the people involved are not merely comfortable with the current lifespan but feel that departing from it upward is a defect in the one who wants to. Sour grapes, in its modern use, is about adopting a preference because the good is unattainable; these people are not exactly telling themselves that a longer life is not worth having — they are telling others that wanting one is wrong. Is there an established concept for this specific configuration, and if so, how exactly does it relate to those two neighboring ideas? If there is none, I would want a term and a definition precise enough to mark its edges: does it cover someone who declines to have their body cryopreserved after death, someone who refuses a hypothetical mind-upload, or a person who accepts all present-day medicine and yet declines a genuinely effective anti-aging therapy — one that adds healthy years faster than the years pass — on the grounds that species-wide extension would be bad for humanity? A bare name would not settle the question for me; I am interested in what the phenomenon is and where its boundaries fall.
