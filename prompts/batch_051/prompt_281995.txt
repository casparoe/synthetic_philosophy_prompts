A distinction I have been relying on in my dissertation is beginning to carry more weight than I originally intended, and I would like it examined before I build a chapter around it. I have been calling the two sides propositional ineffability and cultivational ineffability.

By propositional ineffability I mean the claim that there is some content, a fact or a truth, that language is in principle incapable of representing, so that the correct attitude toward it is frustrated assertion: one knows there is something to say, but no sentence can say it. By cultivational ineffability I mean something weaker and, I suspect, more defensible: that the relevant knowing in many mystical contexts is not a content awaiting its failed description but a capacity or transformation whose acquisition is constitutively non-descriptive. What is unsayable, on this second reading, is not an object of knowledge that resists predication but the knower's own transition from one condition to another. A teacher can bring about that transition without stating what it consists in, much as someone can be taught to balance on a bicycle without being told the physics of their equilibrium.

My central claim is that a surprising number of debates about whether the ineffable can be known dissolve once one stops reading every occurrence of unsayable in the propositional register. When certain mystical texts or practices say that the highest thing cannot be spoken, or that silence is the best instruction, they are often, on my reading, making the cultivation claim: not there is a fact F that language could not encode, but there is a transformation T that cannot be caused by, and is not best reported as, descriptive speech. The speech or silence is instead a technique for instilling T. If that is right, then the question can what cannot be said be known? splits. Under propositional ineffability, the object of knowledge seems radically elusive, since one cannot specify what it is one knows. Under cultivational ineffability, the knowledge can be perfectly real while being non-reportable, in roughly the way knowing how to bring about a state is real even when one cannot articulate the mechanism.

I would like an honest assessment of whether this distinction is coherent, whether it already exists in the literature under another name, what it gets right, and exactly where it breaks or stops being distinctive. I am especially worried about three points. First, the distinction may collapse: to claim that a practice instills a specific transformation T, one must single out T by description at least enough to identify what is and is not the intended outcome, and that looks like propositional content leaking back in. Second, cultivational ineffability may not deserve the word ineffability at all; if it is just tacit know-how, there is already a well-developed literature on that, and my label may be relabeling someone else's result. Third, it may under-describe what some mystics actually claim, since at least some seem to assert a non-discursive cognition with genuine object-directedness, as if the ineffable were something encountered rather than a state acquired. I would rather keep the distinction and give up the label if necessary, but I want to know what I am paying if I take either side.
