I'm a novelist, not a mathematician, and I'm working on a book where a retired factory foreman keeps trying to prove that probability is bunk. He's obsessed with Bertrand's paradox, and I want him to give a version of the argument that's real and not a strawman, so the reader can't dismiss him too easily. This is the argument I have him give.

(1) Imagine a factory that makes solid cubes, and all you know is that the side length is somewhere between 0 and 1 cm. The principle of indifference says that if there's no reason to prefer one possibility over another, treat them as equally likely. So any two equal-length intervals of side length should get equal probability; side length is uniform on [0,1].
(2) Face area is side length squared. So the face area is less than 1/4 cm² exactly when the side length is less than 1/2 cm, which has probability 1/2.
(3) But you could have started with face area, which also ranges from 0 to 1. Applied to area, the principle of indifference says equal-size intervals of area should be equally likely, so area is uniform on [0,1] as well.
(4) If area is uniform, the probability that face area is less than 1/4 is 1/4, not 1/2.
(5) It's the same factory, the same cube, the same event. A single event can't have two different probabilities.
(6) So the principle of indifference is unsound when a continuous quantity is involved, and anyone who uses it is just picking a formula out of a hat.

I want to know whether this argument goes through, and if it doesn't, where exactly it fails. Maybe the foreman is actually right, and the correct response is that he's right about the principle but wrong to conclude probability in general is bunk? My narrator is supposed to push back, but she shouldn't score a cheap point. Also, could you separate what's settled about Bertrand's paradox and the principal of indifference from what is still genuinely contested among people who know the topic? I don't want to write the foreman as if one contested view is the consensus.
