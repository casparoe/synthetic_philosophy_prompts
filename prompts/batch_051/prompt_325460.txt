Below is a passage from Isaac Newton's General Scholium, appended in 1713 to the second edition of the Principia; the wording is from Andrew Motte's 1729 English translation.

"He is Eternal and Infinite, Omnipotent and Omniscient; that is, his duration reaches from Eternity to Eternity; his presence from Infinity to Infinity; he governs all things, and knows all things that are or can be done. He is not Eternity and Infinity, but Eternal and Infinite; he is not Duration and Space, but he endures and is present. He endures forever, and is every where present; and, by existing always and every where, he constitutes Duration and Space. Since every particle of Space is always, and every indivisible moment of Duration is every where, certainly the Maker and Lord of all things cannot be never and no where."

I would like help working through what this passage is claiming and how it bears on Newton's account of space and time. In particular:

(1) What distinction is Newton drawing when he says God is not eternity and infinity but eternal and infinite — and, correspondingly, not duration and space but one who endures and is present? Why does the distinction matter for what he wants to say about God?

(2) What does it mean for God's existing always and everywhere to constitute duration and space? Is Newton making space and time attributes of God, identifying them with God, or arguing for something weaker? And what does the last sentence establish — how are we meant to get from "every particle of Space is always" and "every indivisible moment of Duration is every where" to the conclusion that the Maker and Lord of all things cannot fail to exist at every place and time?

(3) Earlier in the Principia, Newton distinguishes absolute time and space from the relative, sensible measures taken from them. How does this passage connect to that earlier distinction — does it clarify it, change it, or give it a theological foundation? Be precise about how much the passage actually establishes and how much it leaves open.
