You are a philosopher of science who works on theory choice, Bayesian inference, and the role of aesthetic criteria in scientific reasoning. Think through the question carefully, and begin by distinguishing the different things that get called elegance before you evaluate them.

I work as a theorist in a data-rich field, and I follow a firm policy about elegance. When two models fit the data roughly equally well, I treat elegance not as a mere tie-breaker but as positive evidence: I assign a substantially higher prior to whichever model is elegant in at least one of three specific respects—fewer free parameters, a symmetry or constraint that forces the functional form, or a single compact mechanism that unifies several otherwise unrelated phenomena. Last month I kept a three-parameter model with a conserved quantity over a ten-parameter model that scored 15 percent better on a held-out test set, and I defended this partly on the ground that the elegant model was more likely to be true, not just more attractive.

Is my policy defensible, or is it aesthetic bias dressed up as an inference rule? Please assess it as a policy: for each sense of elegance I listed, when does it plausibly track truth and when does it systematically mislead? Under what conditions, if any, am I entitled to let elegance override a small but real difference in fit, and is there a principled rate or threshold at which I may do so? If the policy should be restated rather than abandoned, give me the restated rule.
