I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about whether language death is really a tragedy. His view was unsentimental: languages die because their speakers find another language more useful; whatever can be said in the dying language can be translated into the one that replaces it; and preservation campaigns mostly serve linguists, nationalists, and the guilty consciences of the comfortable rather than the speakers themselves. I kept reaching for George Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four — specifically the appendix on Newspeak — to explain the part of my disagreement I couldn't quite articulate. The argument ended in a stalemate, and I want you to help me do better next time by giving me a serious critical reading of the novel through the lens of language death and the value of linguistic diversity.

What does the novel actually claim is lost when a vocabulary is deliberately shrunk — not merely words but concepts, distinctions, irony, nuance, and the ability to frame political dissent? Does Orwell dramatize the strong thesis that without a word one cannot think the thought, and how defensible is that thesis once we separate it from the more modest claim that some thoughts are harder to formulate and transmit in a reduced language? I want the reading to take the language-death debate itself seriously: distinguish the instrumental value of a language as a repository of knowledge, taxonomy, and cognitive diversity from its value as a bearer of identity and community, and tell me which of those losses the novel makes vivid and which it overstates or distorts. Then say where the book resists the conservationist reading one might want to extract from it: whether the Party's planned extinction of English is genuinely analogous to the slower, often voluntary language shift of ordinary communities, and whether treating speakers as custodians of a threatened language risks the paternalism my friend accused me of. I would like the honest critic's version — faithful to Orwell and to the philosophical debate, able to admit where the novel gets the ethics of linguistic diversity wrong, and ending with what the work adds to the argument that the essays and typologies alone cannot supply.
