Last night a friend and I had a long argument about a decision I am facing: whether to remain in a research career I find meaningful but financially precarious, or move into an unrelated industry job that is secure but, to me, mostly drudgery. My friend kept insisting that if neither option is simply better than the other, then there is no fact of the matter about how they compare, and therefore any decision between them is ultimately arbitrary. I resisted that conclusion. It seemed to me that we were running together several distinct claims: that the options cannot be measured on a common scale; that no positive comparative judgment holds between them; and that a choice cannot be rational unless it is supported by a judgment that one option is better. By the end, I suspected we were not actually using the same theory of hard choices, but were talking past each other.

I would like to turn that argument into something useful. Please write a classification instrument—a "which theory of hard choices do you actually hold?" quiz—for an undergraduate philosophy reading group. The point is to sort readers by their pattern of judgments about concrete cases, not by which label they prefer, so no question may simply ask whether they believe in incommensurability, parity, or comparability.

Please build the quiz out of the logical space rather than out of a list of received positions: first articulate the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions within that space. The instrument should use at least the following distinctions:

- Whether any comparison between two options must yield one of three verdicts—better than, worse than, or equal—or whether this trichotomy can fail;
- Whether equality and parity are one relation or two, and whether parity is best understood as a sui generis fourth comparative relation or as a rough form of equality;
- Whether a failure of the trichotomy means the options are genuinely incomparable, or merely that they are comparable by a fourth relation such as parity;
- Whether rational choice between hard alternatives requires a completed comparative ranking, or whether an agent may rationally choose in the absence of any "better than" or "equal" verdict, for example by committing to a value and thereby creating reasons;
- Whether apparent incomparability is better explained as vagueness in evaluative predicates rather than as a distinct evaluative relation.

Each quiz question should present a concrete case. Use the choice I was arguing about last night as one case, and add others of a similar kind (for example: choosing between two pain-relief charities of different kinds; choosing whether to break a confidence to prevent a small harm; choosing between attending a close friend's performance and finishing an important but not urgent piece of work). For each case, offer two or three answer options that track the distinctions above without using terms like parity, incommensurability, or trichotomy.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto positions including at least: strong comparabilism; weak comparabilism that adds parity as a fourth relation; hard incomparabilism; vagueness views; and voluntarist or commitment views. If any two of these positions cannot be separated by any question you can construct, say so explicitly rather than pretending the quiz discriminates them. Also flag any combination of answers that appears logically available but has no position occupying it, and briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Aim for eight to ten questions. Phrase everything so an undergraduate with no background in value theory can answer confidently; the instrument should sort readers by what they say about cases, not by whether they recognize the jargon.
