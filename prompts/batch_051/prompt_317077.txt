I am preparing a pair of cases for a law-and-philosophy seminar on the proof paradox, and I want them built to a precise specification. The claim the pair is meant to test is that the intuitive difference between naked statistical evidence and individualized evidence is not a difference in posterior probability, since the two kinds of evidence can support exactly the same probability, but rather a difference in what the evidence would have looked like if the defendant had not been liable.

For present purposes, take naked statistical evidence to be evidence whose probative force comes entirely from a frequency about a class to which the disputed event belongs, with no causal or diagnostic link to the token event itself. Take individualized evidence to be evidence that is downstream of the particular event or of the defendant's conduct on that occasion, such as an eyewitness report or a trace the event left.

Construct two civil cases governed by the preponderance standard. In each case there are exactly two possible sources of the plaintiff's harm, and the background evidence is perfectly symmetric between them, so the prior probability that the defendant is the source is 0.5. In the first case the single piece of evidence is a naked statistical frequency about a class of similar occasions, for example all appointments of a certain kind on a certain day, with the record of the individual occasion unavailable; choose the frequency so that the posterior probability of liability is exactly 0.8. In the second case the single piece of evidence is individualized, an eyewitness report or a trace generated by the occurrence; choose the witness's error profile or the trace's reliability so that the posterior probability of liability is also exactly 0.8. Keep both cases ordinary and realistic, involve the same stakes and the same kind of injury, and do not use the standard blue bus or gatecrasher fact patterns.

After presenting the two cases, show what each of the following two views says about them and why. The probability-only view holds that a civil verdict may rest on any evidence if the probability it confers on liability exceeds 0.5, so the source of the probability does not matter. The individualized-evidence requirement holds that a verdict may rest only on evidence linked to the particular event or to the defendant's token conduct, so naked statistical evidence fails however high the probability. Be explicit about the prior probability, the likelihoods or frequencies that take you to the posterior, and the resulting posterior probability, rather than using loose terms like likely or overwhelming.

Then identify the structural feature doing the work. Explain the difference between the two cases in terms of what the evidence would have looked like in the nearest world where the defendant was not the source, and do not simply appeal to the word individualized. As a check, modify the statistical case by adding a single trace generated by the particular event itself, described in one sentence, while keeping the posterior probability at 0.8, and say whether the individualized-evidence requirement now sides with the probability-only view; if it does not, say what further element would bring them into agreement. End with the strongest reply available to someone who holds the probability-only view: how should they diagnose the resistance to the statistical case, and what is the real difference between the two cases on their account?
