I'm a PhD student in comparative history, and for the last month I've been going back and forth between Ibn Khaldun and the cliodynamics literature, feeling like the two are talking past each other. This passage is the one that set me off, from the Muqaddimah (trans. Rosenthal, p. 123):

"Royal authority is a noble and enjoyable position. It comprises all the good things of the world, the pleasures of the body, and the joys of the soul. Therefore, there is, as a rule, great competition for it. It rarely is handed over (voluntarily), but it may be taken away. Thus, discord ensues. It leads to war and fighting, and to attempts to gain superiority."

In the surrounding sections Ibn Khaldun treats this competition as part of a cyclical process: a dynasty coheres through asabiyya (group feeling), seizes power, then gradually loses that cohesion as its members become accustomed to luxury and the ruler subcontracts coercion to outsiders. Eventually a less corrupted group from the periphery displace it. That is the part I get. What I'm less sure about is whether the argument survives the shift to modern quantitative macrohistory. Turchin's secular cycles appear to reproduce the rise-and-collapse pattern using variables like elite overproduction, state fiscal strain, and immiseration, and asabiyya doesn't enter the model. Does Ibn Khaldun's specific mechanism—especially in the quoted passage, the motive of competing for the goods of royal authority—still have any explanatory role once you have structural-demographic models that can be fitted to data and make predictions? Or is it just a vivid redescription of elite competition that cliodynamics has now absorbed and made precise? I'm less interested in whether Turchin gets the detail right than in whether Ibn Khaldun's argument survives this development in form, needs repair, or turns out to have been doing something else.

Could you answer in British English, please?
