I'm a retired physical chemist, and I've spent the last couple of years reading early modern philosophy for the first time. I'm stuck on something where my old physics and my new reading seem to collide, and I'd like you to help me work through it.

Leibniz's principle of identity of indiscernibles says, roughly, that there cannot be two distinct things that are exactly alike in every respect; if two things are indiscernible, they are one and the same. I know there are arguments about whether it's necessarily true, but set that aside. Here's my itch. Modern quantum mechanics, as I learned it, treats two electrons or two helium-4 atoms as not just hard to tell apart, but as not having separate identities in the first place. You antisymmetrize or symmetrize the wavefunction, the labels "particle 1" and "particle 2" are just bookkeeping, and the statistics (Fermi-Dirac, Bose-Einstein) only come out right if you count an exchange as not producing a new state. My old grad-school textbook put the point by saying the particles are absolutely indistinguishable, not merely hard to tell apart. A philosopher friend now tells me this is often taken as a flat-out counterexample to Leibniz: here are two — well, two of what? — things that share all their properties, differ only numerically, and yet are not one. So Leibniz was wrong.

I want to know what Leibniz himself would have said about this, rather than what the textbook voice says. I have a hunch he would not have felt refuted, but I cant quite articulate why, and I also suspect some of my hunch rests on confusing Leibniz's "indiscernible" with the physicist's "we can't tell them apart experimentally." My friend also says the really interesting question is whether Leibniz would have taken the quantum case as evidence for his anti-atomism and his doctrine of monads, or as a threat to it — he was, after all, suspicious of material atoms long before quantum mechanics.

Here's what I'd like: write me the report as if you're reconstructing Leibniz's likely response from his own commitments, not from what later commentators happened to say. Use American English. Organize it by sections, roughly like this:

1. "What Leibniz's principle actually requires" (about 250 words). Spell out, as carefully as you can, what counts as a property or a "respect" for him, whether spatiotemporal location counts, and what work the principle is doing in his system.

2. "The quantum case as a physicist would put it" (about 300 words). I want to make sure you and I have the same target in view: permutation invariance, the failure of distinguishability, and exactly which claim is supposed to refute Leibniz.

3. "What Leibniz would find congenial" (about 350 words). Where do the quantum facts line up with things he already wanted to say about space, place, and the status of material things?

4. "Where he would say the physicists have changed the subject" (about 350 words). Here I especially want you to test my hunch about the epistemic/metaphysical slip: is "indistinguishable" being used in two different senses, and would Leibniz be entitled to insist on the difference?

5. "What he would ultimately say about the counterexample" (about 300 words). Would he swallow it, reinterpret it, or deny it — and what would he have to give up or double down on to do so?

6. "Your own verdict" (about 200 words). Is Leibniz's would-be response a genuine escape, or is it early-modern special pleading? I'd like your honest assessment, not a diplomatic one.

Oh, and if I've misstated the physics or the metaphysics in the setup, please flag that early but don't let it derail the sections.
