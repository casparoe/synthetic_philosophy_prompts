I edit the annotations for a study Bible aimed at educated lay readers, and the edition is committed to historically grounded explanation of what the biblical texts meant in their own literary and cultural setting. The annotations team handles several proposed number notes per biblical book, and there is no shared method for sorting them. A contributor will want to annotate the 153 fish in John 21, the three sets of fourteen generations in Matthew's genealogy, the ages in the Genesis genealogies, or the fact that a word occurs seven times in a pericope. Some of these notes are real insight; many are pattern-hunting, and the difference matters. A note is not a harmless curiosity: it reaches every reader of the passage and shapes how it is taught, and one numerological note can damage the edition's credibility with the scholars whose trust it needs.

Write a reusable decision procedure that a line editor with a humanities background and no training in statistics can run in about twenty minutes on any proposed number note. The procedure should yield one of three verdicts — accept, reject, or escalate to the general editor — and should spell out the questions to ask in order, what kind of evidence counts as an adequate answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it must hand the question to a person because the choice is hermeneutical or theological rather than factual. Do not appeal to secondary literature; the value of the procedure must be in its internal criteria.

The distinction I want preserved is roughly this. A number is doing interpretive work when the text itself marks it or when it belongs to a convention a competent ancient reader could have applied — seven for completion, twelve for the tribes, forty for a period of testing, and the like. A number is an artifact of the search when it becomes significant only after an interpreter has chosen what to count, in which language, with which rules, and stopped when the result felt meaningful. Build that distinction into the procedure, and keep it honest about where it cannot be applied.

Then run the procedure on these three proposed notes:

1. A contributor annotates the catch in John 21: 153 is the sum of the integers from 1 to 17, 17 is 10 plus 7, and the number therefore signifies completeness.
2. A contributor annotates Matthew's genealogy: its three blocks of fourteen generations are deliberate, because fourteen is the numerical value of David's name in Hebrew, and the structure therefore makes a Davidic claim.
3. A contributor annotates a feeding narrative: five loaves plus two fish make seven, and seven signals completion.

Make sure at least one of the three receives a verdict I will not like; I am personally drawn to the one about Matthew, but I want the procedure to decide rather than my preferences. End by saying what the procedure cannot decide and how I should handle those cases.
