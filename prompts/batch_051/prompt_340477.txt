I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a piece on what classic texts actually say about the sources of laughter. A bestselling popular psychology book I am reading summarises Hobbes's theory of humour this way: Hobbes claimed that all laughter is an expression of contempt; whenever we laugh, we are celebrating our own superiority over someone else's weakness, and therefore humour is essentially hostile. The book also says that modern superiority theory inherits this view, holding that every joke—from slapstick to puns—works by making the audience feel better than the butt of the joke, and that this is why we laugh less in egalitarian societies. That last claim struck me as empirically odd, and the summary as a whole feels like it makes Hobbes sound cruder than he was. I have since looked at Leviathan, and I find that Hobbes describes laughter as arising from what he calls sudden glory, a self-congratulatory comparison with the perceived defects of others or with our own past uneasiness, though I worry I am reading him too charitably. As a journalist, I do not want to repeat a distortion. My question is: Is the popular book's summary a fair characterisation of Hobbes's view and of the superiority theory of humour that descended from it? Please separate what is accurate from what is a common simplification or overstatement, identify any places where the book conflates Hobbes's specific analysis with the broader theory, and say whether a careful defender of superiority theory would accept the book's conclusion that an egalitarian world would have less laughter. I am less interested in a verdict than in the reasoning: which parts of the summary would a historian of philosophy accept, which parts would they wince at, and does the book's empirical aside about egalitarian societies follow from anything in the accurate parts of the theory.
