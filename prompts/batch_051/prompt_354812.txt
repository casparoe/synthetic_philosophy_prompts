In my reporting I move between constitutional theory and the governance of emerging technologies, and there is an analogy I keep noticing that I cannot resolve on my own.

On one side is an old constitutional problem: whether a present generation can bind future ones. The devices are familiar — entrenched constitutional clauses, public debt carried across generations, treaties that outlive their signatories, perpetual trusts. The difficulty is familiar too: the future parties were not present at the bargain, cannot be coerced now, and may well regard today's arrangements as a dead hand. On the other side is a newer problem: the pre-AGI bargain, by which I mean agreements made now, before artificial general intelligence exists, that are meant to constrain the actors of a post-AGI era — actors likely to be far more powerful than the signatories and possibly indifferent to their preferences.

The structural resemblance that holds my attention is this. In both cases, a present party tries to govern a future party that cannot be bargained with directly because it does not yet exist, and so must be reached indirectly through the scaffolding built in advance: institutions, default options, incentives, and the initial conditions the future party will inherit. But the analogy may break down precisely there. Constitutional binding of future generations operates inside a continuous legal order among successive groups of ordinary human citizens, and it survives partly because later citizens have strong reasons to keep that settlement working. An AGI transition could be a rupture of the order itself: the future actor may be able to disregard the very institutions that make intergenerational commitment enforceable.

I would like a careful adjudication of whether this is a deep identity — two instances of one general problem, commitment against radically asymmetric future power — or a loose analogy that obscures more than it reveals. Please map the two cases onto each other precisely, say where the mapping holds and where it fails, state what would have to be true of both cases for the constitutional precedent to be a trustworthy model for pre-AGI bargains, and identify at least one thing the analogy seems to predict that would in fact be false.
