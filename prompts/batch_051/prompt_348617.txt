In many monotheistic communities, ordinary believers regularly ask God for specific outcomes: they pray that a sick relative recovers, that a child returns safely from war, that a drought ends. The practice is run in a way that suggests petitions can make a difference — people speak of prayers being answered or going unanswered, and they keep asking even for outcomes that, if God is omniscient, God already knows will or will not happen. At the same time, the theology these communities affirm says that God is immutable: God does not change, is not moved by new information, and does not revise a plan. Some practitioners append to every request a submission to God's will; others say prayer is really about changing the one who prays — and yet the same people still ask for specific results.

This looks like an inconsistency, or at least a clue about an unstated theory. I want a reconstruction of the implicit philosophy of divine action and petitionary prayer that the practice presupposes. In particular: (a) what does the practice strictly require us to think about God's relation to time, to created causes, and to our requests, and which of these commitments are merely optional glosses rather than presuppositions? (b) Is the practice coherent if God is strictly immutable, or does it tacitly assume that God would have done otherwise had we not prayed, and can that assumption be squared with immutability? (c) Which rival accounts — for example, prayer as a created cause that God has eternally willed to include, prayer as changing the petitioner rather than God, prayer as pure address with no request-like function — license the observed practice, and which are ruled out by it? (d) What would have to change in the practice, or in the theology around it, if we replaced the presupposed view with the straightforward thought that a petition can only matter if it changes God's mind or gives God new information?

If the question is badly set up — for instance, because a sprawling practice of this kind may not presuppose any single theory, or because asking what a practice presupposes is the wrong question when several rival theologies can endorse the same behavior — tell me that directly and then do the useful thing that remains.
