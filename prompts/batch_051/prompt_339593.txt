You are a philosopher of social science and decision theory who works on institutional design and the governance of transformative AI; you are comfortable with game-theoretic reasoning and with distinguishing empirical, conceptual, and normative layers of a disagreement. I want you to think through a debate that seems to me to have stalled, and tell me what kind of disagreement it actually is. Think carefully before you answer.

Here is the debate, as I have observed it among technically sophisticated people who do not appear to be talking past each other on purpose.

The question is whether, before artificial general intelligence (AGI) is built, actors such as labs, states, and funders should try to lock in bargains about how the resulting power, control, and benefits will be distributed afterward. Examples include advance commitments to share access to models or compute, treaties that would transfer control of a successful project to an international body, or equity-like arrangements that would disburse a project's windfall rather than leaving it with the developer. The two camps are these.

The bargain-lockers say that getting commitments in place now is valuable even though enforcement mechanisms are weak, because a credible-looking agreement can function as a focal point, shape the expectations of funders, employees, governments, and the future system's builders, and raise the cost of a later repudiation. They reject the idea that an agreement is either externally enforceable or worthless. They also argue that waiting until the shape of AGI is clear means waiting until whoever is ahead has no reason to bargain at all.

The bargain-skeptics say such arrangements are nearly worthless because any actor or coalition that ends up with a decisive AGI lead will face a radical power asymmetry and can repudiate the earlier agreement at trivial cost. Agreements made under one distribution of power cannot constrain actors under a radically different one, and agreements about an outcome nobody can yet specify will be too vague to constrain anything anyway. On their view, the real work must be done elsewhere: changing racing incentives, building verification capacity, or making unilateral commitments that do not depend on another party's later cooperation.

The impasse: both sides agree that AGI would concentrate enormous power, that current enforcement is weak, and that specifications are uncertain. Yet each side regards the other as making a basic mistake. The skeptics say the lockers are treating unenforceable wishes as if they were contracts; the lockers say the skeptics are confusing weak enforcement with no effect and assuming that a bargain must have an external enforcer to matter at all. The discussion cycles through the same stylized cases, such as a weak party signing a treaty with a rising hegemon, or a startup employee locking in equity before the firm becomes an unaccountable monopoly. Nobody moves.

My working hypothesis is that both sides are relying on different informal theories of what it is for a commitment to bind someone in the first place, and that most of their explicit arguments are downstream of that. I offer that only as a hypothesis, not as a conclusion.

I want a diagnosis of this impasse, not a survey of the positions.
