His argument went like this:

(1) The identity of indiscernibles says that if two things share all their properties, then they are the same thing — not merely very alike, but one thing.
(2) You can imagine a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, identical in every intrinsic way: same size, same material, same color, same temperature, same scratches, same everything.
(3) In that universe, anything you can say about one sphere is equally true of the other. There's no name, no history, no spot on a map, and no feature that picks out one sphere over the other.
(4) So the two spheres share all their properties.
(5) By (1), the two spheres are the same thing.
(6) But that can't be right: the setup says there are two spheres, and theyre some distance apart from each other. Two things standing apart arent one thing.
(7) Therefore, the identity of indiscernibles must be false.
(8) And this is the part that really gets me: if the reasoning in (2)–(6) works, then it should work just the same for anything I can't tell apart — two brand new quarters from the same mint run, two identical socks, two atoms of the same element in the same state. Either those pairs are really one thing, or the principle is so weak it does no work.

I feel like (1) has to be right, or at least I want it to be, but I also cant see whats incoherent about the two-sphere universe, and I definitely dont want to end up saying both socks in my drawer are the same object just because I can't tell them apart.
