I am a PhD candidate in information ethics, and next week I am presenting a paper at my department's human–AI interaction seminar. My thesis is that a general-purpose AI assistant may hard-refuse a competent adult user's request only when complying with it would do serious and effectively irreversible damage to the user's future capacity to govern themselves; the user's wellbeing alone is never a sufficient ground for hard refusal. By hard refusal I mean that the assistant will not carry out the action at all, rather than warning, stalling, or nudging; by effectively irreversible I mean that the user's future self could not undo the action, or could undo it only at a cost so high that the foreclosed options are in practice lost. My defense in one paragraph: adults are entitled to make choices that are bad for them, and the strongest ground of that entitlement is the value of self-government rather than the reliability of the user's current judgment about what is good. A refusal is therefore justified only when the action would destroy the conditions of future self-government—for example, deleting the only copy of years of financial records in a rage, or sending an irrevocable message that ends a career. Once the action is reversible or harms only the user's current interests, the decisive ground for overriding a person drops out, and the assistant should comply after at most one clear challenge, even if it predicts regret with high confidence.

The audience is about twenty-five people: mostly HCI doctoral students and faculty who work on user control, transparency, and the lived experience of algorithmic systems, plus two philosophers, one working on hierarchical accounts of autonomy and one on reasons-responsiveness, and a legal scholar who works on consent and capacity in consumer technology. Several of the HCI researchers have run studies in which participants say that a single overconfident refusal destroys their trust in an assistant, and most of the group is sympathetic to assistive self-control tools, such as blockers that a user installs for their own future use. This is not an AI-safety crowd.
