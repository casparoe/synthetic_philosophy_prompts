I am an undergraduate taking a course that includes the history of mathematics, and I have been reading Newton's Principia in the 1846 American edition of Andrew Motte's translation. I came across a passage in Book I, Section I, Lemma II, where Newton argues that the inscribed and circumscribed parallelograms around a curvilinear figure approach the figure itself. He writes:

"For the difference of the inscribed and circumscribed figures is the sum of the parallelograms Kl, Lm, Mn, Do, that is (from the equality of all their bases), the rectangle under one of their bases Kb and the sum of their altitudes Aa, that is, the rectangle ABla. But this rectangle, because its breadth AB is supposed diminished in infinitum, becomes less than any given space. And therefore (by Lem. I) the figures inscribed and circumscribed become ultimately equal one to the other; and much more will the intermediate curvilinear figure be ultimately equal to either. Q.E.D."

To my modern eyes, this looks remarkably like a definition of convergence: the difference between the approximating figures and the curvilinear figure can be made smaller than any given quantity, so the curvilinear figure is their common limit. That makes me puzzled about why the later epsilon-delta definition of a limit is treated as such a major conceptual achievement. If Newton already says "less than any given space," is the difference really more than the lack of a symbol for epsilon? I suspect I am projecting modern ideas back onto Newton, but I cannot locate exactly where the analogy breaks down. What, if anything, is Newton doing here that differs from a modern limit argument?
