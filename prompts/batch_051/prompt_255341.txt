I've been chewing on a position I call the "budget view" of laws of nature, and I'd like an honest assessment. I got to it by arguing with a friend who does physics, and he keeps telling me its either obvious or nonsense, which makes me think it might be neither. The idea is this: Humeans say laws are just compact summaries of the actual distribution of matters of fact, and anti-Humeans say laws are something extra that governs or produces the regularities. My view says both are looking at the wrong thing. On the budget view, a law of nature is a constraint on the total "spend" of some quantity across any possible history of the world. Take conservation of energy: it doesn't describe what happens and it doesn't shove particles around; it says that every physically possible history has to balance the books. What we call regularities are just what you get when all the possible histories have to stay under the same budget. Generalising, each law is an accounting rule over the space of possible worlds, and the actual history of the universe is just one set of entries in the ledger.

I'm aware this sounds like I've reinvented something, maybe symmetry principles or the idea that laws are constraints rather than causes. What I want to know is: is this a genuine third option between Humeanism and anti-Humeanism, or does it collapse into one of them? What does it get right, and where does it break? Give me the three strongest objections and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable. And please write the whole thing in British English.
