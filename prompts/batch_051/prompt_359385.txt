I've been reading about how moral norms actually die — the abolition of Britain's slave trade, the end of dueling in England, footbinding in China — and I keep hitting the same three stories. One camp treats these as victories of moral argument: the old norm collapsed once its justifications stopped being persuasive. The economic-interest account, associated with the Williams thesis, says British abolition mainly tracked the declining profitability of the West Indian sugar economy and the rise of competing industrial interests, with humanitarian rhetoric as window dressing. The honor account associated with Appiah says the mechanism in dueling — and, in different form, in the other cases — was a shift in what counted as honorable or shameful, not reasoned persuasion and not a cost–benefit recalculation. Each camp presents its mechanism as the explanation, but each seems to fit one case cleanly and the others only by special pleading, and the same episodes get claimed by all three sides, with the same facts read as evidence for different causes.

This isn't idle curiosity for me. I help run a small campaign trying to shift a norm inside my institution, and I wanted the historical literature to tell me which lever actually matters — argument, incentives, or status. Instead I find a stalled debate that mirrors our internal arguments: everyone in my reading group accepts roughly the same facts about these cases, yet nobody moves. My working theory is that the impasse isn't a factual dispute that more archives would settle; it's that 'argument,' 'interests,' and 'honor' are being used as answers to different questions — individual persuasion, aggregate material constraints, and the maintenance conditions of a status system — so the three accounts mostly talk past one another and only look like rivals.

Diagnose the structure of this disagreement. Is it verbal, substantive, or several questions sharing a single heading? Do the camps differ about facts, about standards of evidence, about which case counts as the central exemplar, or about what an explanation of a norm's end must deliver? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction such as proximal versus distal cause or origin versus enforcement, a comparative framework across cases, a particular kind of process-tracing, or an honest conclusion that the question is underdetermined and no convergence should be expected? I want the diagnosis and its consequences, not a verdict on who is right about slavery or dueling.
