Question: In Being and Time, Heidegger starts from the everyday use of equipment, his example being a hammer. Explain what is normally hidden about equipment while it is working smoothly, and what a breakdown (the hammer breaks, it's too heavy, the material is missing) discloses about the tool and its context. Then say, briefly, why this analysis is meant to undercut the idea that we first encounter things as neutral, merely present objects.

Student answer: While I'm hammering, the hammer is ready-to-hand. It withdraws into the task; I don't meet it as a thing with properties, and that is what Heidegger means by saying its being lies in its in-order-to. A single tool is never the real unit: the hammer is what it is only through its assignment to the nails, the wood, the bench, and the project of building, and while everything works this whole referential context stays in the background. When the hammer breaks or turns out too heavy, that context stops hiding. The unusable thing becomes conspicuous, and the obstruction or absence makes the whole network light up, so I see at once what the hammer was for and how the other equipment was already assigned to it. Heidegger calls this showing-up of the broken tool the present-at-hand: the tool now stands there as an independent physical object with properties like weight, extension, and hardness, and only because it has this underlying presence-at-hand can the referential context become visible at all. So breakdown is what first gives us things as objects of theoretical description, and it shows such objects are derivative — but derivative in the sense that they are the reality underneath our practical absorption, which is only our way of being caught up in the network. The Cartesian mistake, then, is starting with the present-at-hand as if it were primary and neutral; Heidegger's point is that absorbed practical dealings come first.
