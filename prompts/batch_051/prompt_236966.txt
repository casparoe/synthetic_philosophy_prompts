At our epistemology reading group last week, Sam and Dev got into it about Fitch's paradox, and I wrote down their exchange because the rest of us couldnt agree on who had the better of it. It went something like this.

Sam: "The upshot of Fitch is that verificationism is cooked. Take the knowability principle: every truth can be known. Now pick some unknown truth p—say, the exact number of grains in a particular sand dune. The conjunction 'p is true and no one knows p is true' is itself a truth. So by the principle, that conjunction can be known. But it can't be. If someone knew it, they'd know both conjuncts: they'd know p, sure, but they'd also know that no one knows p. And since knowledge is factive, if they knew that no one knows p, then it would be true that no one knows p—which contradicts their knowing p. So here's a truth that's unknowable, flatly contradicting the principle."

Dev: "Careful. The conclusion isn't just that some particular truth is unknowable. The proof shows that the conjunction couldn't have been true in the first place, on pain of contradiction with the principle. But that conjunction is true exactly when p is true and nobody knows it. So the proof says: there can be no true p that's unknown. That's the collapse result—every truth is known. And that's not a local counterexample to the principle; it's a reductio so strong that the verificationist has to reject the way the principle was applied, not the principle itself."

Sam: "Right—so it is a refutation. You either give up the principle or accept that there are no unknown truths. But there obviously are unknown truths. The exact number of grains in that dune probably is one right now, unless somebody happened to be sitting there counting it. So the principle goes. Verificationism only escapes by denying the obvious."

Dev: "No. The verificationist keeps the principle by restricting how it gets instantiated. The proof feeds it a proposition about knowledge—'p and no one knows p.' That's a reflective, epistemic sort of statement. The principle was never meant to cover statements about our own epistemic situation or the totalty of what's known. Restrict it to propositions that don't embed knowledge claims, and the Fitch conjunction never gets through the door. The proof never touches the principle the verificationist actually holds."

Sam: "That restriction is ad hoc and it abandons the view. The principle was 'truths are knowable,' full stop—a metaphysical claim about truth as such, not a rule about some safe fragment. Once you say only non-epistemic truths are knowable, you've already conceded that some truths, the epistemic ones, aren't. That's the concession that matters. And the proof doesn't lean on anything exotic; it uses only factivity and distribution of knowledge over conjunction. If your theory of truth can't survive those two, it wasn't a theory of truth."

Dev: "Factivity and distribution aren't the issue; the loaded step is the final one. The move from 'it's impossible that p is true and unknown' to 'every true p is known' goes through only classically. An intuitionistic verificationist can accept the first—no truth can be shown to be unknown—and refuse the second. What she's stuck with is something like 'every truth is not-not-known,' or 'you can't assert an unknown truth.' That's a lot less absurd than 'every truth is already known,' and it's consonant with verificationism, not fatal to it."

Sam: "That's a distinction without a difference for someone doing semantics. If your own system proves, for arbitrary p, that p and 'no one knows p' can't both hold, then you've written the possibility of outrunning verification out of the language. Anti-realism was supposed to explain how truth could consist in the possibility of verification even though most truths never actually get verified. After Fitch, keeping the principle means restricting it to a fragment, or retreating to intuitionistic logic, or refusing to assert obvious truths about our own ignorance. Each of those is more expensive than just admitting the principle was false."

Dev: "You keep treating the intuitionistic retreat as though it's a desperate patch. But the whole point is that a verificationist is entitled to a logic in which truth isn't settled independently of our capacity to recognize it. It's not an accident that the collapse to 'every truth is already known' needs classical logic; it's exactly the step that smuggles in the realist picture of a world full of hidden, settled unknown truths. An anti-realist who refuses that step isn't retreating—she's being consistent."

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each claim, say whether it's sound, overstated, or mistaken, and why. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and who (if either) you think is more right overal.
