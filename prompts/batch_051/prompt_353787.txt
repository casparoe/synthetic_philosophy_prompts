Before I go any further down this rabbit hole, I want the standard of evaluation itself made explicit rather than smuggled in. I keep encountering the same move in discussions of the Allais paradox: someone points out that most people prefer 1A to 1B and 2B to 2A, concludes that the independence axiom is descriptively false, and then treats that as settling the normative question, as if any adequate theory of rational choice under risk must accommodate the folk pattern rather than classify it as a mistake. Write the desiderata a theory of rational choice under risk must meet, where the theory is meant to serve as a normative standard for an ideally consistent agent choosing among lotteries, but one that can still explain why so many reflective people make the Allais choices. Give me the full list of criteria: the choice patterns it must get right (common-consequence, common-ratio, and at least one case where descriptive and normative intuitions point the same way), the distinctions it must not collapse (for example, risk and ambiguity, or the descriptive warrant of a preference and its normative force, or the evaluation of a single choice and the evaluation of a plan of choices), and the structural properties it should preserve (stochastic dominance, dynamic consistency, or whatever you take to be non-negotiable). For each requirement, say what consideration makes it a requirement. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are only the preferences of particular camps, and say explicitly whether any subset of the list is jointly difficult to satisfy. Finally, score expected utility theory, cumulative prospect theory, and one other contender of your choice against the list, briefly. For each requirement whose negotiable or non-negotiable status you assign, tell me which assignment you are least confident about, and what argument or example would make you reverse it.
