You are a philosopher of mind and action who is also comfortable thinking about experimental design. I want you to work through the following thesis slowly and carefully: the slogan that deliberation crowds out prediction.

Start by brainstorming, in your own words, the most defensible version of the thesis, and distinguish its readings explicitly. On a descriptive reading, agents in the middle of deciding simply find it hard, or refuse, to assign probabilities to their own possible actions. On a normative reading, a deliberating agent is rationally forbidden from doing so while the deliberation remains open. On a conceptual reading, there is no coherent mental state that is at once open deliberation between A and B and a settled first-person probability assignment to the proposition that one will do A. A spectator can sensibly have a credence about what the agent will do; the agent, while their deliberation is genuinely open, apparently cannot, or should not. If I find myself thinking I'll probably do A, so the idea goes, I have already begun to close off the very alternatives I was supposed to be weighing.

Now treat this as a claim that can be partly probed empirically rather than one that can only be settled from the armchair. Design the strongest test you can of the descriptive reading, while saying what such a test could do for the other two readings. Be concrete: what kind of choice should participants face? How would you separate a first-person forecasting condition from ordinary deliberation? What control conditions would you run, and exactly what would you measure? The thesis makes predictions at several levels. Under it, first-person probability reports made mid-deliberation should be slower, less confident, less stable, and less calibrated against the agent's eventual choice than the forecasts of a well-informed observer; the very act of forecasting may also change what the agent eventually does relative to a no-forecasting control. Tell me which pattern of results would count as support for the thesis, which pattern would count against it, and which confounds—introspective confabulation, experimenter demand, the fact that being asked for a forecast is itself an intervention—you would try to rule out.

Finally, be exact about what a positive result would not settle. Suppose first-person forecasts really do fare worse than third-person ones, and the request to forecast measurably disturbs deliberation. Would that establish a rational or conceptual prohibition on self-prediction during deliberation, or only a psychological discomfort with mixing two stances? Could the normative or conceptual version of the thesis survive a negative experimental result, and if so, why? I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want your own clearest construal, your best operationalization, and an honest account of the residue no experiment can reach.
