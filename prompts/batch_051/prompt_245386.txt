I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've spent the last month reporting a piece on whether a big social-media redesign caused the spike in depression-related emergency visits among teenagers in the two years after it rolled out. Every draft comes back from my editor with the same surgical strike: the word "caused" becomes "was associated with," and in the margin she's scribbled our newsroom's favorite four words, "correlation is not causation." I get the folk-wisdom version of it. But I've also been reading around Pearl's causal hierarchy and the do-calculus — the idea that association (seeing), intervention (doing), and counterfactuals (imagining) sit on different rungs of a ladder, and that with the right structural assumptions you can sometimes climb from the first rung to the second. That makes me think the slogan might be covering a whole family of different claims: that an observed association doesn't by itself tell you the direction; that an association might be entirely confounded; that correlation can vanish even when there's a clean causal link (nonlinearity, cancellation, threshold effects); or the deeper Pearl-style claim that a bare probability distribution simply doesn't contain interventional facts, full stop. Some of those are true-but-trivial, some are substantive, and some look to me like they must be false as blanket statements.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims "correlation is not causation" might be making, and for each say whether it's a platitude, a defensible substantive thesis, or just false? Then tell me which reading my editor is actually relying on when she refuses to let a causal verb into a story whose evidence is observational — and whether, under something like the do-calculus, her blanket veto is defensible or whether she's using a heuristic as if it were a theorem. I want the menu version, not one cleaned-up interpretation, because I've started to think its doing very different jobs depending on the argument it gets dropped into.
