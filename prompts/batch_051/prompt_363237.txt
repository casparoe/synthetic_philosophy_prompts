My school renovated its cafeteria this year. The lunch line was reorganized so the salad and fruit are at the front, at eye level, while the dessert window sits in a far corner with its own slow line. Nothing was banned; anyone can still buy whatever they want. The school said the change was simply better cafeteria design, and almost everyone — me included — accepted it without much thought. A few months later the school board proposed a rule limiting students to one sweetened drink per lunch. The rationale was the same: arrange things so the healthy choice is easier to make. This time students and parents were angry, and some of the same parents who had praised the redesign signed the petition against the drink rule.

I find I have both reactions, and I want to know whether they can be held together. In the first case, the school deliberately made the tempting option harder to reach and did not say that it was steering us. In the second, it made the tempting option harder to obtain, but through a public rule. Both cases replace the choices we would have made on our own with choices steered toward the school's goal for our health. The obvious defense is that the redesign keeps each option available while the rule takes one option away. But that distinction starts to look doctored under pressure: if the dessert window operated for only one hour a day, it would still be called available, but no one would call the arrangement respectful of our freedom. From the other side, if the rule capped drinks at three a day, almost no one would be affected, yet it would still be an open limit. What principle, if anything, lets us accept quiet redesigns while objecting to open limits, and where is the line? One possibility is that the real distinction is just that a rule is visible enough to resist and a rearrangement is not. If that is the real distinction, then our usual reactions seem backwards: the influence we are less likely to notice would be the one we should worry about more. Please build the strongest justification you can for treating the two cases differently, test it against the hour-a-day case and a nearly costless rule, and then say honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
