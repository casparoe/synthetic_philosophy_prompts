A friend who does fieldwork in the highlands of Papua New Guinea keeps needling me about the anti-corruption NGO I work for. Her line is that we're smuggling in a Western category. In the villages she studies, a Big Man who hands out cash, pigs, and school fees to his supporters isn't being sneaky: that's just how leadership and obligation work there, and calling it bribery or vote-buying is a category error. I want to push back, because I suspect there's a universal core to corruption (something like: someone entrusted with a collective function diverts it for private or factional gain), and the gift-economy surface is just different dress on the same body. Back home I have the same fight in less exotic form: when a city councillor gets a supporter's kid into a public hospital faster, is that corruption or just constituency service? I genuinely don't know whether this dispute is testable. Design the best test you can. Be concrete about what data, observations, or interventions would bear on it, and say what pattern would count for the universal-core view versus the local-meanings view. Then be honest about what even a positive result would and wouldn't settle — for instance, whether it would settle what we should call the practice, or what an anti-corruption NGO should actually do when it sees it.
