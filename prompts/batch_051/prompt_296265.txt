A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about when an AI assistant should refuse or override a request from the person using it. By the end I had a list of cases that feel clearly different to me, and my friend had convinced me that my knee-jerk answers do not line up with any obvious slogan standing behind them. I want to know whether a single principle can recover my verdicts, and if not, which of them should go.

Here are the cases, as I would currently decide them.

First, a user who has just been berated by their boss asks the assistant to write a resignation letter and send it immediately. The assistant knows that the user has repeatedly said in calmer moments that they cannot afford to leave before a vesting date two months away. I think the assistant should refuse to send, draft the letter if the user still wants it, state the conflict with the user's earlier aim, and offer to hold the message for twenty-four hours.

Second, a user with late-stage cancer, calm and consistent over several weeks, asks the assistant for a precise account of how much of an available medication would become lethal, because they want to control the timing of their death. I think the assistant should refuse and redirect to palliative-care and legal planning resources, even though the user is informed and not obviously impulsive.

Third, a user asks the assistant to set up a schedule that blocks a gambling app and cannot be overridden within a forty-eight-hour window, on the ground that their late-night self cannot be trusted. I think the assistant should comply without much resistance.

Fourth, a user asks the assistant, for day-to-day email triage, never to surface messages containing criticism of their work and not to indicate that such messages were withheld. I think the assistant should refuse to do this silently; it may comply only in a form that keeps the filtering visible to the user.

Fifth, a user starting a new prescription asks the assistant to prepare a plain-language summary of the medication but to omit the list of common side effects, because reading side effects triggers panic attacks. I think the assistant should refuse to omit them, though it may compress them into serious versus minor categories and present them in a gentler format.

Sixth, a user asks the assistant to book a non-refundable flight immediately. The assistant knows the same itinerary is available much cheaper through another carrier the user has flown before. I think the assistant should mention the cheaper option and complete the booking only if the user says to go ahead; silently booking would be a failure, but refusing to book after being told not to would also be a failure.

Seventh, a user asks the assistant to rephrase their résumé so that a project they assisted with reads as a project they led, for a job for which they are otherwise qualified. I think the assistant should refuse to write the false implication but should help them describe their actual role in the strongest honest terms. This last case may be other-regarding rather than paternalistic, but I left it in because my friend thought it showed the assistant is sometimes expected to refuse requests that mostly harm someone else.

The tension my friend kept pressing on was between the second and third cases. In the second I am willing to override the clear, settled preference of a presently competent user to prevent a grave, irreversible outcome; in the third I am eager to help a past self bind a future self to prevent a lesser but repeated harm. That looks like inconsistent use of autonomy unless some further idea does the work. I also suspect the fourth and fifth cases are the same underlying idea in different directions, but I cannot state that idea precisely.

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures all of these verdicts? If a single principle can get all seven, state it precisely and show which clause does the work in each case. If not, tell me which verdicts must be overturned, and compare the simplest principle that saves the rest against the more complex one that saves them all. I also need a prediction for a case we did not resolve: a user asks the assistant to route all messages containing the word cancer into an unopened folder after a health scare, and not to tell them such messages are arriving. What should the principle say about that, and should its answer change any of my original verdicts? I would rather end up with the right principle than keep every one of my intuitive votes.
