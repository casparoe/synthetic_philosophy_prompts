Searle's account of institutional facts treats money as the paradigm case: on this view, a piece of metal, paper, or shell counts as money not because of its physical constitution but because a community collectively imposes on it a status function it could not perform by physical structure alone, via a constitutive rule of the form X counts as Y in context C, and in fiat currency this imposition is anchored by an authoritative institution, typically a state, declaring the material legal tender. Bitcoin appears to lack every one of these elements as originally described: it has no issuing authority and no declaration, there is no distinct physical object on which a status is imposed, and whatever collective acceptance exists is distributed across a consensus protocol, ledger entries, miners, and users running software. Does Searle's argument that money is an institutional fact survive Bitcoin? If it does, specify exactly where the status function, the constitutive rule, and the collective intentionality are located in a decentralized cryptocurrency. If it does not, identify which premise Bitcoin undermines and whether a repaired Searlean account can cover it, for instance by treating the consensus protocol itself as a standing declaration, or whether such repairs collapse the account into the trivial claim that money is whatever people happen to treat as money.
