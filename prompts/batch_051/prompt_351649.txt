After spending my working life in the natural sciences, I have recently been making my way into political philosophy, and I have found Aquinas's discussion of tyrannicide in his treatise on kingship particularly striking, because it resists the convenient reading one often gets in textbooks. I would like you to help me think through a concrete case rather than just the abstract argument.

The case I have in mind is the attempt on Hitler's life of July 20, 1944. As I understand the salient facts: a group of German military officers and civilians, including Colonel Claus von Stauffenberg, plotted to assassinate Hitler with a bomb at his East Prussian headquarters; the bomb exploded but Hitler survived with minor injuries; the conspirators were then rounded up and executed, and the broader resistance around them was destroyed. The plotters included men who had sworn personal oaths to Hitler as soldiers, but also men in senior positions in the military and state administration.

From my reading, Aquinas's position is roughly this: he distinguishes between a ruler who is a tyrant from the start and one who was legitimately instituted and later became a tyrant through his exercise of power. He argues that it is often prudent and even obligatory for a people to tolerate a tyrannical ruler rather than rise up, because the attempt to remove him can produce worse evils, such as civil war or an even crueler successor. He denies that a private person may simply take it upon himself to kill a tyrant; if action is to be taken, it belongs to public authority or to the community in some sense, and if no human remedy is available, one must turn to God. But there is also a strand in him that permits, or at least treats as more defensible, the removal of a usurper who never had a legitimate title.

I would like you to run the July 20 plot through that framework as carefully as you can. Who are the actors in Aquinas's categories, and do any of the conspirators count as public authority or only as private persons? Was Hitler a tyrant of the first or the second kind? What would the theory say the plotters were obligated to do, and what would it say they were forbidden to do? Then tell me where the theory explains the moral reasoning the conspirators themselves used, and where it fails to register important features of the case, for example the fact that the regime's criminality was not just oppression of the community but a systematic atrocity, and that ordinary legal and political remedies were unavailable. Finally, say what this episode reveals about Aquinas's framework: does it confirm it, show its limits, or expose an anomaly it cannot handle without being stretched? Please write as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write an email or a letter.
