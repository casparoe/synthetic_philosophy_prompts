I'm a high school senior and the best man at my older brother's wedding later this summer, and I'm stuck on the toast. I'm also taking my first philosophy elective, and last week we read three short pieces on love by Harry Frankfurt, David Velleman, and Niko Kolodny. The way my teacher framed it, there are at least three rival views in the room: love as an appraisal, where you love someone because you correctly register their good qualities; love as bestowal, where the love comes first and is what makes the beloved's qualities shine for you; and love as justified by the history and relationship two people have built, rather than by either the person's properties or a bare gift of value. Every draft I write either turns into a performance review of my brother ("funny, patient, good with spreadsheets...") or collapses into "I don't know why, I just love them," and both feel false in different ways. The audience will be my grandparents, the couple's non-philosopher friends, and my philosophy teacher, so the toast has to work for people who have never heard of this debate and still be something I could defend in class afterward.

Please act as a professional philosopher of love who is also an experienced wedding speechwriter. Think carefully about the structure before you write. I'd like you to begin with a short brainstorm in which you sketch how a purely appraisal-based toast, a purely bestowal-based toast, and a purely history-based toast would each sound and where each one would fail in front of this audience. Then draft the actual toast, about 350 words, in my voice as a high school best man who is a little nervous and not smug. The philosophy should do real work in the speech rather than being decoration: it should avoid ranking the groom's qualities as if he had won a competition, avoid the lazy claim that love has no reasons at all, and instead make room for the third idea by centering two or three small concrete details that only the couple share and that would mean nothing to an outside evaluator. It should be warm and light, appropriate for a wedding, and written in American English.
