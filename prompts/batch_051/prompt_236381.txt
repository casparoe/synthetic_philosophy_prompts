So me and my mate spent the whole walk back from the pub arguing about whether my brother is still on our residents' association committee, and neither of us gave any ground. The association's standing orders were written in 1998, long before the committee did half its business in a WhatsApp group, and the relevant clause says:

A member of the committee who gives the secretary notice in writing of their resignation shall cease to hold office upon receipt by the secretary of that notice.

What happened: after a big row in the committee group chat about replacing the bike shed, my brother posted just before midnight: "fine, I've had enough, I resign from the committee." The secretary was in the chat and read it. About three minutes later my brother posted: "that was a joke, obviously not resigning, don't be thick." Nobody else replied until the next morning, when the secretary logged him as resigned and declared the seat vacant.

My mate's line is that the rule is mechanical: a WhatsApp message is writing, the secretary received it, so the resignation happened the instant it was read — and the retraction came too late. My line is that resigning is something you do with words, not something the words do to you; the first message was plainly not meant as a serious performative, and everyone in the chat could see that, so the rule never engaged. I'm less sure than I sounded though.

So work it through properly: does the clause, read literally, catch him? Does it catch him read by its purpose? What would the people who wrote it in 1998 have said if they'd been asked about a sarcastic message retracted before the recipient had even replied, and what would a reasonable committee member take the rule to mean? And is there a reading that treats him as still on the committee without turning every annoyed text into an un-retractable resignation — or wether the rule should just stay mechanical and the committee gets to take people at their words?
