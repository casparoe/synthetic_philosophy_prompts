I have a rule I've been following for years, and a friend recently told me its just an is-ought fallacy with extra steps. The rule is this: when I'm in a new place, I treat the fact that a local everyday convention is near-universal as strong, default evidence that following it is the right thing to do there—not just that it's what people do, but that I have something like an obligation to do it. I apply this to the small stuff: tipping in restaurants, how to queue, whether to jaywalk, which side of the escalator to stand on. If I'm somewhere that everyone crosses against the light when there's a gap, I cross and I treat crossing as the proper local norm; if I'm somewhere that lines are loose clumps rather than strict single-file, I don't impose a strict queue; if I'm somewhere where 20% tipping is expected even for mediocre service, I pay it and treat myself as bound to pay it.

My friend says local prevalence can't make a practice right—these conventions are usually arbitrary (a city could have settled on either side of the escalator) and sometimes actively bad, since he thinks American tipping props up low wages and queueing norms are just a way of punishing people in a hurry. I half-agree with him. But I also think locals have more information than I do about how local coordination problems actually work, that following the local rule usually keeps everyone better off, and that a visitor who barges in imposing their own norms is making a different kind of moral mistake. So my question is whether my rule is defensible as a belief-forming policy, or whether it's just social-conformity bias dressed up as principle. Even if local consensus should count for something, how heavily should it count when it conflicts with my own judgment that a convention is exploitative or pointless? Is there a version of the rule that keeps the deference without treating every local custom as moral law—and how should I tell, in advance or on the spot, which conventions actually have a claim on a visitor and which ones l don't have to follow?
