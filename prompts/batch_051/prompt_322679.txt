I've been teaching an undergraduate course in the philosophy of logic, and I keep returning to Carroll's tortoise — the dialogue in which Achilles tries to force the tortoise to accept the conclusion of a simple syllogism, and the tortoise never refuses any premise while still refusing the move from premises to conclusion. The puzzle recently became personal when I found myself in an argument in which my interlocutor cheerfully granted every step of a proof and still withheld the conclusion; I realized I had no ready account of why they were being irrational rather than merely adopting a different inferential policy. I have since collected what I am told are the standard responses to the puzzle. There is (1) the rule-versus-premise response: the tortoise treats modus ponens as a further premise when it is in fact a rule of inference, so the threatened regress never gets going. There is (2) the rule-circularity response: we can derive the validity of modus ponens by a derivation that itself uses modus ponens, and the circularity is acceptable because what is being justified is exactly what is being used. There is (3) the inferentialist response: to accept 'if P then Q' and 'P' is already, in part, to be committed to 'Q', so the tortoise's stance is semantically incoherent rather than merely obstinate. There is (4) the proof-theoretic response: the elimination rule for the conditional is justified by standing in harmony with the introduction rule that fixes the conditional's meaning. There is (5) the Wittgensteinian dissolution: the regress arises only from the confused demand that every rule be paired with an interpretation of the rule, and it disappears once we see rule-following as a practice. And there is (6) the skeptical response: the tortoise is right that deduction bottoms out in brute dispositions or ungrounded norms, and the request for a deeper justification should be abandoned. I want one more response to the tortoise that is not on this list and not a restatement, special case, or combination of (1)–(6). It need not be true, or even plausible; it needs to be structurally different. Then give me your honest assessment of whether your new response advances the debate beyond the six on the list, or whether it just repackages one of them.
