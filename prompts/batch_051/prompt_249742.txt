Two friends of mine, Dana and Marcus, got into it on a long drive, and I keep replaying the argument because I couldn't referee it. The scenario they were arguing about is this: you're handed a coin from a jar that you know contains coins with all sorts of biases—some almost always come up heads, some are fair, some almost always come up tails—but you know nothing at all about the particular coin you've got. Dana said that in that situation no single probability of heads is rationally required; the honest state is a set, maybe the whole range from 0 to 1, on the grounds that plumping for any one number (even 0.5) is just inventing a precision your evidence doesn't have. Marcus said that can't be a belief state at all, because probability is supposed to guide action. If you had to accept or decline a bet that pays $1 if it lands heads and costs 50 cents, a point probability hands you one expected value, but a set hands you a jumble of expected values, some positive and some negative, so it won't tell you what to do.

They went back and forth after that. Dana said the failure to deliver a single answer is a feature, not a bug: in near-total ignorance, refusing to pick is exactly what good judgment looks like, and imprecise probabilities can still guide action through rules like "don't choose an option when some alternative beats it under every probability in the set," which may leave the decision open, and so be it. Marcus said in real life you still have to choose, and once you do, you're acting as if some precise probability were the one, so the set view is either empty or a precise view in disguise. He also brought in Dutch books: someone who would accept certain trades one at a time but end up guaranteed to lose when they're bundled together is usually called incoherent. Dana replied that the Dutch book argument assumes you have a single fair price for each bet, and that's the very thing she denies—if your prices are intervals and you only accept trades the whole set says are at least okay, the bookie never gets his sure loss. And acting in the face of an unresolved comparison doesn't reveal a hidden exact credence, it just means practical choice sometimes runs ahead of what your evidence settles.

I've gone back and fourth on this, and they've each accused the other of mistaking healthily incomplete judgment for refusing to have a view. Could you adjudicate it move by move—say who is right about which specific point, what each is missing, and who is more right overall? While you're at it, I'd appreciate it if you'd separate what's settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested, and not present a contested position as the consensus. American English, please.
