In the preface to his History of the Latin and Teutonic Peoples, Ranke tells the reader that he has not set himself the task of judging the past or instructing the present, but only of showing what actually happened. For Ranke, this is the regulative ideal of historical objectivity: a historian who works from primary sources, suppresses his own preferences, and lets the documents speak can converge on the past as it really was. Later critics such as E. H. Carr and Hayden White hold that this ideal rests on a confusion. The historian inevitably selects, arranges, and emplots the surviving traces; the resulting narrative is a construction, not a discovery. I want to know whether anything in this dispute can be given empirical discipline. Suppose one treats Ranke's claim not as a metaphysical thesis about a ready-made past, but as a comparative claim about historical method: that, given enough shared primary sources, independent historians from different backgrounds will converge on the same account of a bounded episode—not merely on the same dates and names, but on the same causal structure. Design the best test you can of that comparative claim. Specify the kind of episode you would use, the historians or coders you would compare, the controls you would impose on source access and background commitments, and the measure of convergence you would use. Then say what a high degree of convergence would show, what it would leave untouched about White's deeper objection that even a converged narrative is still one possible emplotment among others, and whether a failure of convergence would refute Ranke's ideal or only show that the method was not yet Rankean enough.
