I’m teaching an undergraduate medieval philosophy seminar, 14 students, 75 minutes. We’ve just read Anselm’s Proslogion and Gaunilo’s reply. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the pull of Anselm’s move — that there’s a difference between a thing’s being in the understanding and its existing in reality, and that Anselm thinks you can’t just treat that difference as optional when you’re thinking about the greatest thing — and then makes them see why Gaunilo’s island parody feels like the obvious objection but might not be a perfect fit. I don’t want to lecture them into it; I’d rather the rules make them run into the problem.

Try to build it around this passage from Anselm’s Proslogion, chapter 2:

“When a painter first conceives of what he will afterwards perform, he has it in his understanding, but he does not yet understand it to be, because he has not yet performed it.”

Maybe before showing them the passage, they’re planners or painters or architects who have to rank plans for the greatest possible building, and they find themselves treating “actually gets built” as a extra point that doesn’t show up on the blueprint. Then give them Gaunilo’s island version as a parallel project and have them vote on whether it goes through. But I’m not attached to that setup — if you have a better way to have them act out the distinction, use it.

Please give me concrete rules, materials (keep it to paper, whiteboard, maybe index cards), a timeline with rough minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity to the actual moves in Proslogion 2 and Gaunilo’s reply. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, especially where students will seperate “exists in the understanding” from “imaginary” or “not real” in a way that makes Anselm look dumber than he is.

After the debrief, tell me where your own mapping is most likely to fail and what would change your mind about whether this activity actually gets at the argument rather than just coaching students to say the right thing. I’ve had activities that felt great and taught nothing.
