I run the development team at a television production company. In a typical year we make between ten and twelve biographical dramas and limited series, almost all of them about real people who are now dead. Because our subjects are dead, the direct legal exposure is modest; the constraint I want is ethical, and it has to be stronger and more articulate than our current practice. Every script arrives with a change-list: private conversations that no source records, timelines compressed by years or decades, composite characters, motives tidied into a shape an audience can follow, invented causal links between documented events, last words rewritten. We presently decide these items one by one, mostly by asking whether the living family would object and whether a critic could catch the change. That is not a principle; it is risk management dressed up as care.

What I want is a reusable decision procedure that a script editor can run on a draft before we commit to production. Ground it in whatever the best philosophical thinking actually supports about four things: whether the dead can be wronged, harmed, or only remembered; what truthfulness in fiction demands beyond factual accuracy; when an invented or altered detail about a documented life damages the public record on which later audiences rely; and when the real addressee of the obligation is the dead person, the living survivors, the viewer, or the shared record itself. The editors will not have read the philosophy. The procedure must therefore carry each distinction it needs inside its own questions, with a sentence explaining what each question is doing.

The constraints are these. A junior script editor with no philosophical training must be able to run the procedure across a change-list of roughly forty items in under an hour. Each item must leave the procedure with one of three classifications: permissible as written; permissible only if the change is signalled in the text or framing; or not permissible without a written justification that I personally sign. At each step the procedure must say whether it is testing a matter of record, a matter of interpretation, or a matter of value, and it must explicitly mark the points where no procedure can decide and a human has to. It should also say what it cannot do in principle, and it should not fall back on the law; I already have lawyers, and I am asking about what we should do when the law is silent.

Apply the procedure to these three cases, which I have altered in detail but which match the structure of what we face.

A film about a singer-songwriter who died six years ago. The draft compresses the final decade of her life into eighteen months and invents a scene in which, during a tour, she seriously considers leaving her children. No source even hints at this; the writer says the scene is needed to show the cost of the life. Her adult children are alive and have publicly objected.

A film about a long-dead physicist concerning a priority dispute over a key derivation. The draft shows him glancing at a rival's notebook before his own breakthrough, so that the audience concludes he took the idea. The historical record is genuinely unclear; no relatives are alive; and the change makes the science much easier to tell.

A drama about a long-dead military commander whose conduct in a particular battle was, on the evidence, indecisive and lucky. The draft gives him a decisive speech that turns confusion into intention. The speech has passed into school textbooks as if it were real, and our funders want it in the film because audiences expect it. I suspect the procedure will tell us this is not permissible as fact; I want to see how it reaches that conclusion, whether any framing would change the classification, and what I would need to justify personally if we kept it.

Give me the procedure first, then the three applications, then a short statement of where the procedure stops and a person must decide, and of what it cannot settle.
