Analyze the superior-orders defense raised by Nazi defendants at the Nuremberg trials through the lens of Frankfurt-style cases and the principle of alternative possibilities. Start by making the relevant positions precise: a PAP-based reading treats the defense as claiming that the defendants lacked moral responsibility because they could not have done otherwise under threat, while Frankfurt-style cases suggest that the mere absence of an alternative does not by itself defeat responsibility if the agent still acts on their own decision. Then offer a disciplined mapping of the trial episode onto the Frankfurt structure: who or what occupies the role of the would-be intervener, what counts as an agent’s own decision, and whether the historical circumstances are better described as eliminating alternatives or as shaping the will under coercion. Finally, identify where the analogy breaks down. What does this episode expose that Frankfurt-style cases leave underdescribed about coercion, duress, bureaucratic diffusion of agency, and the difference between an unavailable alternative and a legally or morally excusing condition? Does the Nuremberg case, so analyzed, weigh for or against PAP as a sufficient account of when coercion undermines responsibility?
