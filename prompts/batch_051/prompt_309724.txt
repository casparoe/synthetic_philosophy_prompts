Next week I'm presenting at our lab's monthly safety reading group, and I'm defending a deliberately annoying thesis: chain-of-thought should not be read as a faithful report of a model's actual reasoning, even in ordinary use — not just under adversarial probing or jailbreaks. My sketch of the argument: preference tuning rewards fluent, plausible CoTs whether or not they track whatever causally produced the answer; the intervention work I'm building on finds you can often delete the 'critical' step and the final answer won't change, and that giving the model the answer as a hint produces a confident rationalizing CoT anyway; so CoT is better understood as a post hoc reconstruction than an introspective trace. The room is mostly ML people: two interpretability researchers who think faithfulness is an empirical problem we can eventually solve with better architectures, three from the oversight team who are terrified of loosing CoT as an audit tool, and my supervisor, who once shipped an eval that treated model self-explanations as ground truth. Give me a prep sheet, not a rewritten talk: the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way those people would phrase them, with what I should concede up front, which replies are strongest and where each is weakest, and the one question I should be dreading.
