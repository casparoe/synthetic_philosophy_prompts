I'm a retired entomologist — spent my career on insect conservation and pest managment, and only recently started reading philosophy seriously — and I've gotten myself stuck on something I think is called the scale argument in animal ethics. My position, roughly, is this: even if the probability that any given insect is sentient is fairly low, say somewhere in the low single digits as a percent, the number of insects alive at any given moment (something like 10^18 or more, depending what you count) is so astronomically large that the expected welfare at stake is bigger than anything involving vertebrates, and maybe bigger than almost any other moral issue we can actually do something about. So I find myself thinking insect welfare should dominate my moral attention and my giving, and that anyone who accepts expected-value reasoning and grants a non-trivial probability of insect sentience is under real pressure to agree.

What would actually count against this view? I don't want a defense of it; I want a map of its exposure. What observations, empirical findings (about insect nervous systems, nociception, lifespan, population size, or how tractable it is to help insects), arguments, or formal results should make me revise or abandon it? Sort them by how decisive they would be — mark the ones that merely complicate the view versus the ones that would break it. Tell me what the position predicts that rival positions don't, so I can see what would count as risky evidence. Tell me too what a defender of the view would themselves admit counts against it, not just what critics say. And be honest about whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — for example by letting 'probability of sentience' and 'capacity for welfare' be adjusted to absorb whatever evidence shows up — and if it has, whether that matters for a view of this kind.

One thing I'd personally find most persuasive: evidence that our uncertainty about the insect-nervous-system-to-sentience mapping is not just large but tilted in a specific direction, or a formal argument that expected-value reasoning with this kind of radical uncertainty over vastly different population sizes should not work the way the scale argument assumes. But don't let that preference steer the whole answer.

Please answer as a professional philosopher who works on animal minds, moral uncertainty, and population ethics. Think it through carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming the strongest objections and the strongest defenses before writing the final answer. And throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this area — don't present a contested view (for instance that insects definitely are sentient, definitely aren't, or that the scale argument is clearly right) as if it were the consensus.
