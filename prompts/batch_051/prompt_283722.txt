I've been torn for a while between a principle I find theoretically compelling and an intuition it seems to flatly contradict. The principle is luck egalitarianism: roughly, that inequalities are unjust only when they arise from brute luck — things no one chose, like being born with a disability or into a poor family — and not when they arise from option luck, the risks people knowingly took. I'm drawn to it because its clean, it unifies a lot of my judgments, and it seems to take people seriously as agents: if you chose the gamble, you own the outcome. It even gives a tidy decision procedure: ask whether the disadvantage traces to a choice or to an unchosen circumstance.

But here's the thing I cant get past. If my friend starts day-trading his savings, ignores everyone's warnings, and ends up destitute, I still think he should have a bed, food, and medical care — not because it's prudent, or to keep him from being a burden, but because I think he's owed decent treatment as a person. Same for the motorcyclist who rode without a helmet and now needs brain surgery. My intuition isn't that their outcomes should be made equal with everyone else's; it's that there's a floor below which no one should fall, no matter how badly they chose. And I don't half-own this intuition — I hold it firmly, even though I also feel the pull of the principle, which would say their plight is at least partly not an injustice because they chose the risk. I can't make myself believe they should be turned away.

Could it be amended rather than abandoned, say with a basic minimum, an insurance metaphor, or a requirement of equal dignity?
