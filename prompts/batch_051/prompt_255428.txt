I'm grading a homework assignment for a philosophy of language class. The question I set was:

"Linguists and philosophers of language often contrast universal generalizations like 'All ducks lay eggs' with generic ones like 'Ducks lay eggs.' Explain why 'Ducks lay eggs' and 'Mosquitoes carry malaria' are both generally judged true even though most ducks (males, ducklings) never lay eggs and most mosquitoes never carry malaria. Then consider this proposal: a generic 'Ks are F' is true just in case most Ks in the contextually relevant subclass are F — say, mature female ducks for the first example, and mosquitoes that have fed on an infected person for the second. Does this proposal give a satisfying account of the two examples? Why or why not?"

Here's one student's answer (typos theirs):

"A generic like 'Ducks lay eggs' is true because it describes a characteristic feature of the kind, not a statistical claim about every member. Generics tolerate exceptions: 'Dogs have four legs' is true even though some dogs lose a leg, and 'Birds fly' is true even though ostriches and penguins can't. So a semantic theory based on 'all' or on a raw majority over the whole kind fails — most ducks don't lay eggs, and most mosquitoes don't carry malaria. The majority view can be repaired, though, if we choose the domain carefully. For 'Ducks lay eggs' the relevant domain is adult female ducks during their breeding years, and most of those do lay. For 'Mosquitoes carry malaria' the relevant domain is female Anopheles mosquitoes that have fed on an infected person, and most of those carry the parasite and can pass it on. So the restricted-majority account handles both examples. The only leftover problem is to explain which domain restrictions are allowed, but thats a pragmatic issue about context, not an objection to the semantics. Overall I'd say the proposal is basically right, and the original 'majority view' just needed better domain selection."
