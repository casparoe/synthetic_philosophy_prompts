In our seminar this term on methods in political philosophy, we have been examining reflective equilibrium as a procedure: one begins with considered judgments about particular cases and with candidate general principles, then works back and forth, revising both judgments and principles until the overall set reaches a stable, mutually supporting fit. A classmate has argued that this procedure is not merely a way of making one’s views coherent, but is truth-conducive—that it makes our resulting moral or political conclusions more likely to be correct. I am unsure how one could even bring evidence to bear on such a claim, since there is no independent moral criterion against which to check the outputs of reflective equilibrium.

I want to know how one would test the claim that reflective equilibrium improves the epistemic quality of a person’s conclusions, rather than merely increasing the internal coherence of their beliefs or their subjective confidence in the result. Could we, for example, compare groups of people who use reflective equilibrium with groups who reason from fixed principles or defer to authority, and then look for convergence across independent inquirers, stability under exposure to new cases, or later considered judgments after time has passed? What would a positive result in such a test actually show, and what would a negative result rule out? More importantly, what residue would remain untested even under the strongest feasible design? I suspect that any such test would only ever measure coherence or satisfaction, not truth-conduciveness, but I want to know whether that suspicion is too quick. Please set out the strongest test you can, be explicit about how it would be operationalized, and then say exactly what it would and would not settle.
