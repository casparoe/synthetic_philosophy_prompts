I've been trying to get clear, in concrete terms, on what Frege's quantifier notation actually buys over the old subject-predicate logic in which 'all' and 'some' merely modify a subject term. The following argument looked to me like the kind of ordinary all/some reasoning the old logic was supposed to handle, yet its conclusion is obviously false. I want the failure located exactly, not just gestured at.

(1) Let n be an arbitrary natural number.
(2) n is smaller than some natural number — in particular, n + 1.
(3) Since n was arbitrary, every natural number is smaller than some natural number.
(4) In (3), 'every natural number' is the subject and 'is smaller than some natural number' is the predicate; since the predicate is a single component of the sentence, the phrase 'some natural number' must stand for a single number.
(5) Therefore there is a single natural number — the one standing in the predicate — such that every natural number is smaller than it.

That yields the false claim that there is a greatest natural number, so at least one step is wrong. But each step felt compulsory as I wrote it. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what is the correct version of that step? I can already symbolize the whole thing as the difference between ∀x∃y and ∃y∀x; what I want is the exact point of failure in the above steps, stated in plain terms. Say how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the point you are least sure about.
