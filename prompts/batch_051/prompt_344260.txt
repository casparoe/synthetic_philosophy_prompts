I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so bear with me. Our team runs post-incident reviews for production incidents, and right now the process feels very Cartesian: after an outage, a designated person collects evidence, interviews people, and writes a single canonical "what happened" doc that then gets shared around as the truth, like management is waiting for the one show to be delivered to their screen. I recently fell down a rabbit hole reading about Dennett's multiple drafts model of consciousness — the idea that there's no little theater in the head with an audience watching; instead there are lots of parallel drafts of content being revised all over the place, and asking what you experienced doesn't discover the one true show, it just takes a snapshot of whichever drafts are currently winning, and the question partly creates the answer. It made me wonder what our incident reviews would be if we actually ran them that way.

Here's the system I want you to analyze. We replace the single postmortem with a shared drafting space. After any incident, any engineer can start a draft narrative, and anyone can edit, fork, or merge anyone else's drafts for thirty days; full edit history is kept and nothing is ever deleted; there's no final version and no single owner. The default view orders drafts by most-recently-edited, so a draft naturally sinks unless somebody keeps touching it. At any moment, a manager or an auditor can press snapshot, which freezes the current set of drafts and their lineage into a read-only record for outside use — but internally the drafts keep evolving after the snapshot. The official story of the incident is whatever that first snapshot happens to catch.

Walk me through how this would actually behave. Not weather it's a good idea in the abstract — I want the mechanics. What does it incentivize in the engineers writing and editing? Where does it get gamed first (I can already picture someone planting a self-exonerating draft early and keeping it freshly edited so it's the first thing every snapshot catches)? What second-order effects does it have on trust, blame, and organizational memory — does the company end up with a worse memory, or just a more honest one? And can any of the failure modes be fixed without turning it back into the one-author, one-truth system I started from?

For the format, give me these sections in order, each roughly a short paragraph: The setup; What it incentivizes; Where it gets gamed first; Second-order effects; Can it be fixed; Bottom line.
