Got in an argument about Good's theorem over the weekend, and I can't find the bad step even though the conclusion feels ridiculous. Here's the argument:

(1) Good's theorem says that for an expected-utility maximizer, any cost-free information that arrives before a decision has nonnegative expected value. The standard proof: after seeing the information you can always do whatever you'd have done without it, so worst case it's a wash; and if the information makes a different act look better by your updated lights, taking that act only helps.

(2) So a rational person should never turn down cost-free information, and should be willing to pay for information whenever there's a chance it'll improve their decision.

(3) Now Ada, a novelist, is deciding whether to keep working on her manuscript. A stranger offers, at no cost in money, time, or effort, to tell her for certain whether it will ever be published. Ada declines. She says that if the answer were no, it would crush her and she'd quit; and she thinks quitting would leave her worse off than writing on in ignorance. So she'd rather not know.

(4) My friend says Good's theorem makes Ada irrational. If she's worried the news will crush her, fine—put the crushed feeling into her utility function; the theorem doesn't care what the utilities are. The signal is still cost-free, so it can't have negative expected value.

(5) So by (2), Ada should take the forecast, and she should even pay for it if it has any chance of changing whether she writes.

(6) But that seems crazy: refusing to be told a painful truth about your own life's project looks like a paradigm case of a reasonable—maybe the reasonable—choice. So which step goes wrong, and what do you commit to by rejecting it? I'd like to keep Good's theorem and still say Ada is rational; tell me what that costs.
