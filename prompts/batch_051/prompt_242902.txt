Been chewing on a claim sometimes phrased as deliberation crowding out prediction. The thought is that there's something off, maybe even incoherent, about assigning a probability to your own choice while you're still making it. If I say mid-decision "I'm 70% that I'll take the job," that doesn't present itself as a sober forecast; it feels like I've already half-decided, or like I'm reporting what a well-informed stranger would guess about me. So: is that a testable claim, or just a way of talking?

Design the best experiment or observational study you can to test the claim that genuine deliberation is in tension with assigning probabilities to your own choices. Give me specifics: what choice situation do subjects face, what incentives do they get, and what do we elicit from them at which moments (a probability before deliberating, one during, a final decision, maybe a post-hoc report of how much they were really deliberating)? I especially want you to seperate three things that could otherwise get confused: accuracy of the self-assigned probabilities, quality of the eventual decision, and whether subjects report the probability as something they owned or as a prediction about someone else. Also include a control where people predict someone else's choice in an otherwise identical situation, so we can tell apart "this choice is hard to forecast" from "there's something special about forecasting yourself."

Then say what pattern of results would count as the claim being true and what pattern would count as false. Finally, be honest about the residue: does any behavioral data let us distinguish "deliberation makes self-prediction impossible" from "deliberation makes our self-predictions miscalibrated because we're trying to do two things at once"? Can an experiment ever bear on the strong reading—that the predictive stance and the deliberative stance are incompatible in principle—or are we stuck with the weaker psychological versions? And if we get a clean positive result, does it show that people can't assign probabilities to their own choices, or only that asking them to do so changes the choice?
