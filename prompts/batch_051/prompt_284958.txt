Last month two of my colleagues at the logistics firm where I work spent most of a planning meeting arguing — in terms I only learned later, a clean collision between the inside view and the outside view — about how we should set the deadline and budget for a rewrite of our order-fulfillment system, and I have been turning the argument over ever since because each of them kept escaping the other’s traps without either clearly winning. The engineering lead has produced a granular plan: 147 tasks, each with an owner and a dependency map, leading to a go-live date eleven weeks out. Everyone in the room also knew the industry record for internal platform migrations of this scope: on average those projects run about twice as long as initially planned, and only about a third land within 25 percent of the original date. Our team, however, has shipped its last three projects on time.

Jonas defended the granular plan. “The industry base rate is a blunt instrument,” he said. “It bundles in companies that changed scope midstream, teams that lost their one indispensable developer, systems that turned out to be built on fragile legacy code. We know this system; we studied the dependencies. Our plan is detailed precisely because the details matter. If I double the timeline just because the industry average doubles, I’m throwing away information we actually have. Granularity is not decoration; it is how you surface the couplings that cause slips, and we surfaced them.” Maya pushed back. “Everyone who is about to run late has a granular plan. That is why the base rate exists: detailed simulations keep failing in the same systematic way. A detailed plan simulates the project going the way you currently understand it. It cannot contain the event you have not imagined — the API that does not do what the documentation says, the reorg that takes away your database specialist, the compliance rule that changes in month two. The outside view does not say our specific knowledge is worthless. It says our knowledge should adjust a distribution, not replace it with a story.”

Jonas would not concede. “Adjust how? Which bucket do I use? Projects our size? All firms? The last five migrations at this company? The choice of reference class does more work than the base rate does, and I can make the answer come out almost anywhere by choosing differently. And for the new tax-calculation module there is no comparable project in our history at all. At that point the outside view is just a guess dressed up as rigor.” Maya answered, “That is why you fix the reference class in advance, write down the adjustments before you know what answer you want, and use the class of projects that looked novel at the time for the novel parts. Novel-looking projects still, on average, ran late. And I am not saying kill the project. I am saying do not let the inside plan masquerade as the median outcome.”

Then Jonas made what I thought was his strongest move. “A plan is not only a forecast. The team that is told the realistic date is twice as far out will staff differently, protect less aggressively, and work will expand into the space allowed for it. The inside plan may be optimistic, but optimism is part of the production process. Plan for the pessimistic base rate and you make the base rate worse.” Maya would not grant it. “Keep the inside plan as an internal pacing target. But the date you give the board and the budget you sign should be the outside-view date. If you genuinely believe eleven weeks, you will understaff and under-procure, and then the miss becomes a self-fulfillment of its own.” At that point Jonas played the card everyone expected: “Three on-time launches in a row. The relevant outside view for this project is this team, not the whole industry. Three for three is not nothing.” Maya replied, “Three for three is exactly the sort of small sample that lets a team become the next data point in the industry distribution. I will credit it as a modest adjustment, but you do not get to overturn a whole base rate on three trials and a hunch.” Jonas answered, “Then the outside view has become a collection of ad hoc adjustments: add a little for team history, subtract a little for the tax module. Where is the rigor you were selling?”

I stayed quiet during all of this and honestly could not referee it. Please adjudicate the disagreement as it actually unfolded: identify which specific claims each of Maya and Jonas gets right, which claims each overreaches on, and what both of them are missing. Then give an overall verdict on who is more right, and why. Please resist ending in a draw; take a stand even if the verdict is mixed.
