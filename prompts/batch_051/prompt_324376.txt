I am taking a philosophy of artificial intelligence course, and we have just worked through Turing's 1950 paper introducing the imitation game. I keep returning to the passage on extrasensory perception. As I understand it, Turing considers what happens if the interrogator, the human contestant, or the machine has telepathic powers. The test is supposed to rest entirely on typed exchanges, but if telepathy works, the interrogator might be able to detect the human directly, or a human contestant might be able to pick up the interrogator's thoughts, and the game would no longer be testing what it is meant to test. To preserve the setup, Turing seems to want the competitors isolated from one another in a way that blocks not just ordinary sensory contact but telepathic signaling as well. He also writes as though the statistical evidence for telepathy is too strong to dismiss. I cannot decide whether he genuinely believed in extrasensory perception, or whether this is only a dialectical concession, trying to show that the test can be made rigorous even on an outlandish hypothesis. If he did believe it, does that undermine the rest of the paper, and should we not then take him less seriously as a thinker about intelligence? If he did not, why would he grant so much to the idea and bother redesigning the setup around it? What is the right way to read this section, and does the answer matter for how we should understand the imitation game?
