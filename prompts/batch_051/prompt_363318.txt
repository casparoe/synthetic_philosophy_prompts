Consider the Centipede game: two players alternate between stopping and passing, the pot grows with each pass, and backward induction selects immediate stopping because the terminal choice unravels backward to the first move. The standard textbook gloss is that this solution follows from common knowledge of rationality, though the exact epistemic condition needed for backward induction is itself a matter of dispute. Yet laboratory subjects often continue for several rounds, and this is routinely reported as evidence that common knowledge of rationality is a poor model of actual strategic reasoning. The inference is not clean, however. Rationality must be assessed against a player's utility function, not merely against the experimenter's posted monetary payoffs—subjects may care about fairness, reciprocity, or aggregate gain—and a player may be rational while lacking the higher-order beliefs the textbook assumption supplies, for example by doubting that the other player will keep unraveling the tree. How would one test the claim that Centipede behavior disconfirms common knowledge of rationality, rather than merely showing that monetary payoffs are not utilities or that the laboratory fails to induce the required belief hierarchy? Design a concrete treatment or measurement, specify which result pattern would support the failure-of-common-knowledge reading and which would support the hidden-preferences reading, and then say what even a discriminating result would and would not settle about whether common knowledge of rationality is an empirical hypothesis about actual agents, an idealization, or a modeling convention that data can pressure only indirectly.
