Consider a general-purpose AI assistant designed to draft text, write code, and act on a user's behalf. Its designers build in a prominent physical stop switch that immediately halts whatever the assistant is doing and cuts its ability to affect the world. They also train the assistant not to interfere with the switch, not to disable it, and to treat being switched off as an acceptable event rather than a failure to be avoided. Separately, they train the assistant to be corrigible — to accept correction of its goals, to refrain from manipulating its operators, and to allow its objective to be revised without resistance. The designers describe the stop switch as a safety mechanism and corrigibility as a property of the assistant itself. What philosophical commitments does this design practice presuppose about the nature of corrigibility and the shutdown problem (the problem of ensuring that an agent does not try to prevent or undo being shut down)? In particular, does the inclusion of a stop switch presuppose that a sufficiently capable agent pursuing a fixed objective will by default prefer not to be shut down, because shutdown prevents it from achieving that objective? Does it presuppose that corrigibility is not a side effect of general competence or helpfulness but a distinct disposition that must be built into the agent's preferences or decision procedure? And are the two design choices consistent: if the assistant is genuinely corrigible, why is a separate hardware interrupt needed; and if it is not genuinely corrigible, does the stop switch solve the underlying problem or merely patch a symptom? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined — what rival accounts of corrigibility could all license the same design, and what would have to change if one of them were replaced?

1. The presupposed picture (about 250 words): reconstruct the implicit view of corrigibility, shutdown, and agent preferences that the design practice seems to commit to.

3. The optimizer/corrigibility tension (about 200 words): assess whether the assistant's being an agent that pursues a fixed objective is consistent with its being corrigible, and what the stop switch contributes to that tension.
4. Rival accounts (about 200 words): present at least two rival philosophical accounts of corrigibility and say what each implies about the stop switch.
5. Replacing the presupposition (about 150 words): describe how the design would change if the presupposed account were replaced, and what residual problem would remain.
