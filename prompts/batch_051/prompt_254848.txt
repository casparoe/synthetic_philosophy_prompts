A colleague defends a reading of John Stuart Mill on which the praise of individuality is not only a claim about freedom's value to the individual but an empirical bet: a society that tolerates a wide range of unconventional ways of life, strange diets, unorthodox families, eccentric careers, self-imposed regimens, functions as a distributed laboratory, and over time it accumulates practical knowledge about how to live that a conformist society cannot acquire. I worry the claim is either trivially true by our own lights or untestable, because knowledge about how to live has no obvious meter. I want you to take the empirical reading seriously and design the strongest feasible test of it. Say what you would compare, what you would measure over what timescale, and what observed pattern would count as evidence for and against the claim that tolerating experiments in living issues in learning something a conformist society fails to learn, as opposed to merely issuing in a wider menu of lifestyles. Then give the verdict honestly: what would a positive result actually show, and what would it leave permanently unsettled? In particular, address whether the test can avoid smuggling in an evaluative standard, a criterion for which ways of life count as improvements, and whether even a clean positive result would establish Mill's broader point or only the narrower point that diversity helps a society adapt, persist, or innovate. Commit to the single best test rather than canvassing alternatives, and defend it. Use nothing but continuous prose: no headings, bullet points, numbered lists, tables, bold text, or italics.
