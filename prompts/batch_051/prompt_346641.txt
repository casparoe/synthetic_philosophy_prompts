In my philosophy of explanation course I give students the following four criteria for distinguishing a reason from a mere cause. For something, R, to be a genuine reason why a state of affairs, S, obtains, I say that: (1) R must answer the question why S obtains in a way that makes S intelligible, rather than merely citing the antecedent event that happened to produce S; (2) R must be capable of being cited as a consideration in favor of S — something that can be offered, at least in retrospect, as what speaks for S's being as it is; (3) the relation between R and S must support counterfactuals of the right sort — had R not held, S would, other things equal, have been less likely or absent; and (4) R must in principle be available to some subject: a reason is the kind of thing that could be had, entertained, or acted on by someone, even if no one in fact does so.

Now consider the following phenomenon. Biologists regularly speak of traits as existing because of what they do: the heart exists because it pumps blood; the stick insect's camouflage exists because it causes predators to overlook it; the bird's wing shape exists because it is aerodynamically efficient. The explanatory pattern is real and powerful: a variant of the heart that failed to circulate blood would have been eliminated, and the persistence of the trait across generations depends on the effect it produces. Yet natural selection requires no mind that represents, weighs, or otherwise has the rationale. In the phrase I want you to examine, the rationale floats free of any reasoner. The pumping of blood explains the heart's presence, and does so through a non-accidental, counterfactual-supporting mechanism, without the reason's ever belonging to a subject's perspective.

My question is this: on the four criteria above, is the relation between the heart's pumping blood and the presence of hearts a relation of reason, or only of cause? Work through the criteria one by one, and say explicitly where they underdetermine a verdict. Then take up the deeper question they force. Does this case show that criterion (4) should be abandoned, because it is a residual prejudice that prevents our account from recognizing a free-floating rationale as a perfectly good reason? Or does criterion (4) mark exactly the distinction needed here: that the heart's pumping blood is a cause that we may treat as if it were a reason, but not a reason in the full sense? Argue for one of these positions, and say what your verdict implies about whether the teleological language of biology should be taken literally or as an indispensable metaphor.
