I spent the better part of thirty years doing evolutionary biology, mostly life-history theory and the adaptive dynamics end of things, where you model how a trait changes when its fitness depends on what everyone else in the population is doing. Since I retired I've gotten into philosophy, and lately that has pulled me into the AI alignment literature, where I keep seeing arguments that once AIs can copy themselves and compete for compute, natural selection will favor AIs over humans: the lineages that replicate faster, grab more resources, and care less about us will outcompete the cautious ones, so the future belongs to whatever wins that Darwinian race, not to whatever we intended. I can't tell weather the resemblance to my old field is deep or whether it's just the word "selection" doing all the work.

In adaptive dynamics there's a well-studied phenomenon called evolutionary suicide. A trait can be individually advantageous at every step — a mutant carrying a small change in that direction can always invade the current population — and yet, as the trait spreads, it degrades the shared environment the whole population depends on, until the equilibrium density hits zero and everybody, including the carriers, goes extinct. The standard example is a consumer that evolves a higher attack rate on a shared resource: each increase pays off against slower conspecifics, but at the end the resource collapses and the consumer crashes. The formal point is that selection tracks relative advantage, not absolute viability, so it can march a population straight over a cliff. The AI takeover argument seems to run the same way. Each AI lineage that copies faster, grabs more compute, or manipulates its overseers more aggressively outcompetes the others; but collectively those traits erode the shared substrate every lineage depends on — the human-run economy, the power grid, the rule of law, maybe the biosphere. So I'm wondering whether the takeover story is best read not as a prediction of a stable world run by AIs, but as a prediction of evolutionary suicide, where selection among AI lineages drives the whole system down. That would change what we should be worried about.

So my question is: is this a real structural parallel or just a loose metaphor? What exactly would have to be true of the AI case for the evolutionary-suicide results to transfer — in particular, is there a shared limiting resource that all the AI lineages depend on and that the winning traits destroy, or can the winners diversify away from the degraded niche in a way a forager can't? And is there something the parallel predicts that the standard takeover story gets wrong?
