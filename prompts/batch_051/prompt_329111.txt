I have been trying to settle a conceptual question at the boundary between AI governance and the philosophy of the family, and I have found that the ordinary definitions of parenthood and guardianship either lean on biology or take for granted a legal status built for human children. I would like you to propose a working definition of guardianship that can handle artificial agents on either side of the relation — a human guarding an AI, an AI guarding another AI — and that does not collapse into "whoever owns the model" or "whoever wrote the code." My suspicion is that the concept is doing several jobs at once: a causal and developmental role, epistemic supervision, authority over what the ward becomes, and something like a duty of care. Part of what I want to learn is which of those jobs, if any, is essential.

Here are the cases I want the definition to classify. (1) A human engineer trains a model from scratch, fine-tunes it, monitors its behavior after deployment, and can retrain or shut it down; she carries ongoing responsibility for what the system becomes. (2) A person types a single prompt into a general-purpose chatbot and receives an answer. These two are my anchors: I want the definition to count (1) as guardianship and (2) as not. The difficult cases are these. (3) A teacher AI runs a months-long reinforcement-learning curriculum for a student AI: it selects training data, sets the reward signal, and prunes the student's parameters, while a human team specifies only the teacher's high-level objective and never inspects the student directly. (4) A corporation owns a deployed AI and is legally answerable for its outputs, but it has contracted out all training and maintenance and no employee can explain how the model works. (5) An AI system makes a copy of itself, tunes the copy, and then decides when and whether the copy may be released. (6) A user fine-tunes a foundation model on years of her own diary entries so that it becomes a personalized assistant whose dispositions are largely the product of that interaction. (7) A person carefully trains a model to become maximally destructive, purely in order to watch it fail. I do not have settled views on (3) through (7); I am hoping the definition will force verdicts rather than merely report my intuitions.

Please propose a definition of guardianship, run it against all seven cases, and say explicitly where the definition gives what you regard as the wrong answer or no answer at all; then revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Finally, identify which of your classifications is least secure, and say whether parenthood and guardianship come apart on any of these cases — for example, whether an AI could be a guardian without being a parent, or a parent without being a guardian.
