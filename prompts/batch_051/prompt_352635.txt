Leibniz's best-of-all-possible-worlds theodicy basically argues: God is perfect in knowledge and goodness, so he actualizes the best of all possible worlds; therefore all the evil in the actual world is the least there could be, since a better world would have been actualized instead. The argument quietly assumes there is such a thing as the best world, i.e., that possible worlds are bounded above under the relation better-than. Now suppose that's false: for any world, you can make it slightly better by adding one more flourishing life with no compensating cost, so there's no maximum, like the positive integers. Does the theodicy just die, or can it survive in a weakened form—say God need only actualize a world above some threshold of goodness, or a world from which any improvement would cost something? Which premise is doing the real work, and is the weakened version still recognizably Leibniz's position, or a different argument altogether?
