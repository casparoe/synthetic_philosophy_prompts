I have no formal background in philosophy; I work as a software engineer, and my framework for thinking about this comes from machine learning. Over the past year I have been reading about experimental philosophy and about the evidence that people from different cultural backgrounds give systematically different answers to canonical philosophical cases — Gettier-style cases, trolley-style cases, puzzles about reference, and so on. I have been developing a way of thinking about that evidence, and I want an honest assessment of it, because I cannot tell whether I have found something real or have just dressed up a familiar complaint in technical vocabulary.

I call the idea intuition overfitting. In machine learning, overfitting happens when a model is trained on a sample that is much narrower than the population it will later be evaluated on: the model fits quirks of the training data so closely that its training error is low, but its error on new data is high. I think something structurally similar is true of a great deal of philosophical theorizing. The training data are the intuitive judgments that philosophy treats as evidence: the judgment that the Gettier case is not knowledge, that the bystander may not be pushed onto the tracks, that a proper name refers to the causal source of the name rather than to whoever satisfies the associated description, and so on. The training distribution from which those cases and judgments were drawn, however, is very narrow: for the most part, educated people in modern Western, industrial, democratic societies, and in practice often the subset of those people who become philosophers. The field then builds theories that fit those intuitions closely, and it treats goodness of fit to those intuitions as evidence that a theory is correct.

Here is the central claim. When experimental philosophers run those same cases on people from other populations, they are doing something analogous to evaluating the model on a held-out test set drawn from a different distribution. The resulting drop in agreement should not be treated as a minor anomaly or as evidence that those other populations are confused. It should be treated the way an engineer treats a large train–test gap: as a sign that the original model was overfit to the quirks of its training distribution, and that the features it learned do not generalize. On this view, many of what philosophers call the deliverances of intuition are not evidence about the target phenomena at all; they are measurements of one population's cognitive defaults, and theories built to match them are overfit to those defaults.

I am not claiming every intuition is like this. My guess is that some intuitions are robust across populations and some are local. The distinction I have been using is between robust intuitions, which survive distribution shift and are plausibly tracking something real, and local intuitions, which are artifacts of the sample and carry little evidential weight. The practical proposal is a kind of regularization: other things equal, a philosophical theory should be penalized for relying on local intuitions, and the field should prefer theories that predict and accommodate cross-cultural variation rather than treating one narrow sample as the default.

What I want to know is whether this is a real idea or just a metaphor with technical words attached. Specifically: is the overfitting analogy doing any actual work, or does it just restate the familiar point that intuitions vary and should be handled cautiously? Has something like this already been worked out under a different name in experimental philosophy or nearby fields, and if so, what does the existing version say that mine misses? What does the account get right, and where does it break when pressed — for instance, is there a principled way to separate robust from local intuitions without already assuming which intuitions are the good ones? And what is the strongest objection from someone who defends using intuitions as evidence, and does that objection damage the account or only force me to modify it?
