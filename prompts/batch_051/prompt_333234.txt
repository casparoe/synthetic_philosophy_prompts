Me and my sister have been going back and forth about her job. She works remotely for a design agency in a country that passed a 35-hour-week law back in the early 2000s, partly after some high-profile burnout deaths that got compared to karoshi. The law has a section that says: "An employer may not require or permit an employee to work more than thirty-five hours in any seven-day period. Working time means any period during which the employee is required to remain at the workplace, to be on call, or otherwise to hold themselves in readiness for work at the employer's direction."

Here's the part that started the argument. Her employer went fully remote a few years ago and announced there was no fixed hours anymore; they say "we don't clock-watch, just be available." But the team chat is active from about 7:30 in the morning till 10 at night, and in practice you get a message from a project manager if you don't reply within ten or fifteen minutes. My sister logs maybe 33 or 34 hours of actual concentrating-on-work a week, but she checks the chat all evening, while cooking, on weekends, during movies; she says she hasn't felt off-the-clock once since 2021. Her boss says she's under the cap and should just learn to ignore the phone. My sister says being required to stay half-available is the whole point of the law.

So I'm trying to figure out how that section applies. If you had to argue it from the plain wording, from the purpose of the law, from what the people who drafted it in 2001 would have said if you'd shown them Slack and a smartphone, and from what a reasonable worker reading it today would understand, what does each approach give? Which one should win here, and why? I don't care that much about the legal answer for her specifically—I want to know what the law was ever actually protecting, and whether our idea of "working time" is now just a list of obsolete places and shifts.
