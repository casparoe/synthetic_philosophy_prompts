I sit on the Climate Resilience Subcommittee of a coastal city's capital planning board. We must recommend this quarter whether to raise the design standard for stormwater and coastal flood infrastructure from a 100-year event to a 500-year event, at substantial cost to ratepayers. Our staff ran an ensemble of eleven downscaled regional climate models; eight project rainfall and sea-level combinations that would overtop current designs within thirty years, one supports the status quo, and two produce projections our engineers regard as physically implausible but cannot exclude with confidence. Several of the models share code ancestry, so treating their outputs as independent votes would inflate confidence in the majority, and one model reports uncertainty ranges much narrower than all the others. Two members of the Subcommittee have staked out positions: one argues the city should be robust across all plausible futures and adopt the higher standard now; the other argues public money should not be spent on tail outcomes that may be artifacts of a few questionable models. Draft a policy memo for the Subcommittee with a clear recommendation—raise the standard now, retain the current standard, or adopt an adaptive trigger that raises the standard only if specified observations cross thresholds—along with the criteria by which you weighed the ensemble, and an honest statement of the strongest consideration a dissenting member could raise against your recommendation.
