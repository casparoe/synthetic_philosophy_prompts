Here's his argument, numbered exactly as he gives it:

(1) Either the butler killed the victim or the gardener killed the victim.
(2) In general, when "P or Q" is true, the matching conditional "if not-P, then Q" is true. That's just disjunctive syllogism wearing a conditional's clothes.
(3) So: if the butler didn't kill the victim, the gardener killed the victim.
(4) The conditional at (3) is the very same claim as the counterfactual "if the butler hadn't killed the victim, the gardener would have killed the victim." Changing "didn't" to "hadn't" and adding "would have" is just tense and mood; it doesn't change what proposition is being claimed about the butler and the gardener.
(5) Therefore: if the butler hadn't killed the victim, the gardener would have killed the victim.
