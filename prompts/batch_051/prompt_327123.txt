Okay, here's a reading I've wanted to put on a syllabus for a while but never quite wrote down. Take Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day and read it through Aristotle on temperance and self-control. Aristotle distinguishes the temperate person, whose appetites are already in line with practical wisdom so he doesn't have to fight them, from the continent person, who has to wrestle the bad appetites and wins. Stevens feels like a third, maybe fourth case: he mostly won't even admit the appetites are there, and he rebrands the whole thing as dignity and profesional duty. So here's what I want. Is Stevens best understood as temperate, continent, self-deceived, or just repressed — and does that scheme even have a slot for someone whose self-control is real but hitched to a bad end, namely his loyalty to Lord Darlington and the appeasement crowd? I want a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophy seriously, that points to actual scenes (the pantry scene with the book, the moment Miss Kenton tells him she's leaving and he simply maintains the facade, the final meeting at the pier), and that says where the novel admires Stevens, where it quietly shows the cost, and what it adds to the virtue theory debate that the theory misses. Don't reduce the book to an illustration; I'd rather you say what Ishiguro shows about self-control that the taxonomy doesn't.
