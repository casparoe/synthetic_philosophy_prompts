I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on moral psychology, and I've been spending time with Joseph Butler's Fifteen Sermons (1726). In the early sermons, especially the one on self-love and benevolence, he draws this distinction between self-love as a general desire for one's own happiness and particular passions like hunger, pity, or curiosity that aim at their objects directly. The bit that's bugging me is where he argues that particular passions can't be reduced to self-love because they can conflict with it, so self-love can't be the whole story about motivation. I dont have the exact wording in front of me, but that's the gist of the passage I'm thinking of.

What I want to ask: when people today defend psychological egoism, is Butler's distinction actually aimed at them? Compare his target with the standard forms of psychological egoism on the table now—say, the claim that all actions aim at the agent's own pleasure, the claim that all motives are ultimately self-regarding, and the claim that all actions are motivated by the satisfaction of desires that are the agent's own. Which of these survive his point, which are untouched by it, and which just collapse into something boring and unfalsifiable? I'm trying to figure out where the genuine disagreement is after Butler's distinction is granted.
