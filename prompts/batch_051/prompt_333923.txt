I'm in a philosophy of math class this term (long story, my roomate is a math PhD student and we got in a huge fight about whether numbers are "real"), and my professor keeps using the same analogy to settle the identity problem. She says natural numbers are less like individual objects and more like positions in a structure, like offices in an organization: the office of treasurer exists even if nobody happens to be filling it this month, and it makes no sense to ask whether the treasurer's office is a member of the board of directors — that depends on the bylaws, not on anything about the office itself. Ditto, she says, for numbers. The question "is 2 an element of 4?" comes out true on one set-theoretic reduction and false on another, so asking it about the numbers themselves is a category mistake; the number 2 is the position in the structure, not any particular set that happens to occupy the position.

I get that she's pushing a structuralist line, and I can feel myself half-convinced, but I worry the office analogy is doing more work than its licensed to do. Walk me through it: what corresponds to what (the office, the bylaws, the person in the role, the org chart, the organization itself), where the mapping starts to break, what the office picture quietly imports that the mathematical case might not have, and whether the conclusion — that "is 2 an element of 4?" is an ill-posed question — actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If offices are the wrong source analogy, is there a better one?
