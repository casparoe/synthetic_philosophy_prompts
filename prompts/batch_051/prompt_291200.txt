I've been down a rabbit hole reading about the dispute between counterpart theorists and defenders of transworld identity, and an old difficulty for transworld identity, once I worked it through, left me with an argument I cannot find my way out of. It seems to show that no ordinary object has any essential material origin. Since I am reluctant to accept that, I want to know which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.

Let T and T' be two actual tables of the same design, made from two wholly distinct portions of matter, H and H'. Take a finite sequence of portions H0, ..., Hn such that H0 = H, Hn = H', and each H_{i+1} differs from H_{i} by exactly one molecule.

(1) T could have been originally made from H0, since it actually was.
(2) For each i, if T could have been originally made from H_{i}, then T could have been originally made from H_{i+1}. A one-molecule difference in the original matter is too small to determine which table it is.
(3) Therefore, by repeated application of (2), T could have been originally made from H_{n}, that is, from H'.
(4) If origin essentialism is true—if a table's origin is at least partly constitutive of which table it is—then T could not have been originally made from H', a portion of matter wholly distinct from the portion from which T actually originated.
(5) Therefore origin essentialism is false.

This conclusion seems too strong. It would mean that which table a table is floats entirely free of the matter from which it originated, and that any table could have had any other table's material origin. I would rather keep origin essentialism than accept that. So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Explain why the rejected step looked fine, and say what the main options—strict origin essentialism, a vague boundary, a counterpart-theoretic treatment, or whichever you think best—have to pay to give it up. If you think the trouble is in the move from (2) to (3), please state the modal principle that blocks that move precisely. Please write your answer in British English.
