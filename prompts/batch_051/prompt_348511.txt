For the past several months, my philosophy of science reading group has been going in circles—almost literally—over the circularity objection to Woodward's interventionist account of causation, and I want to understand what kind of disagreement we are actually having. Both sides accept the same bare-bones description of the view: X causes Y just in case there is a possible intervention on X that would change Y (or change its probability distribution), with causal relationships understood as ones that remain invariant under such interventions; and an intervention on X is itself characterized as a special kind of causal process—one that changes X independently of other causes of X and that affects Y only through X. The objectors in the room say this circularity is obvious and fatal: the notion to be analyzed reappears inside the definition, because an intervention is specified in causal terms, and saying that the intervention must be "independent" of confounds or must "break" the influence of other causes merely hides the causal content one level down rather than removing it. The defenders reply that Woodward never promised a reductive analysis in non-causal terms; the account is deliberately non-reductive, and circularity is vicious only if the aim was elimination of causal vocabulary. When objectors add that the conditions distinguishing a genuine intervention—no confounders, no direct path around X—can be verified only once the causal structure is already known, the defenders answer that the account is not offered as a discovery procedure for causal structure, so this epistemic demand misses the point. Nobody in the room disputes a single causal claim about any of the toy systems we have examined, yet the two camps have repeated these moves for months without any visible change of position.

My own hunch is that the impasse is not about which causal claims come out true, but about what an account of causation is supposed to accomplish: whether a non-reductive account can be genuinely illuminating rather than mere bookkeeping; whether a semantic or metaphysical account of causation is obliged to double as a method for learning causes; and where the burden of proof lies when a theory openly employs the very notion it sets out to characterize. But I can also see a deflationary reading of the situation, on which "analysis" and "circularity" mean different things to the two camps and part of the argument is merely verbal. Could you diagnose what this disagreement actually is—substantive or verbal, a difference in aims or standards, a hidden empirical or formal question, a confusion about what a theory of causation must deliver, or some mix of these? And given that diagnosis, what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a formal result, a better statement of the circularity worry, a change of question, or a reasoned case that the debate is ill-posed? Please focus on the structure of the impasse rather than simply taking a side. If I have misdescribed Woodward's position or the circularity objection, or if my framing is itself confused, tell me so plainly rather than working within it.
