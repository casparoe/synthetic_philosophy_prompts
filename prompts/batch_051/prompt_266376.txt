In Chapter V of the Second Treatise of Government, Locke writes:

> Though the earth, and all inferior creatures, be common to all men, yet every man has a property in his own person: this no body has any right to but himself. The labour of his body, and the work of his hands, we may say, are properly his. Whatsoever then he removes out of the state that nature hath provided, and left it in, he hath mixed his labour with, and joined to it something that is his own, and thereby makes it his property. It being by him removed from the common state nature hath placed it in, it hath by this labour something annexed to it, that excludes the common right of other men: for this labour being the unquestionable property of the labourer, no man but he can have a right to what that is once joined to, at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others.

I am trying to understand exactly what work the final proviso—"at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others"—is doing in Locke's own argument at this point, not what later writers have made of it. In particular:

1. Is the proviso a separate condition an act of appropriation must satisfy, or is it better read as a gloss on what it takes for labour to exclude the common right of others without injury?
2. If the proviso fails at the moment someone mixes his labour with something, has he acquired no property at all in the thing, or only a property that is incomplete, revocable, or otherwise encumbered?
3. How should "enough and as good" be assessed in cases where the thing appropriated is not acorns or apples but land, and where an individual's appropriation may change the total stock of usable goods rather than simply removing one item from it?

I am especially interested in whether the acorn-gathering example that immediately follows this passage depends on the proviso for its force, or whether the example is meant to show that small acts of gathering are self-evidently unproblematic even before the proviso is brought to bear. Please give the most defensible reading of the passage itself, distinguishing what the text says from what a later defender or critic of Locke might need it to say.
