I’ve been trying to get clear on what William James actually claimed about religious experience, because a review I read in a popular-level philosophy blog used him to argue that theists don’t need argumentative proofs. The review centered on this passage from Lecture III, “The Reality of the Unseen,” of The Varieties of Religious Experience: A Study in Human Nature:

“The sentiment of reality can indeed attach itself so strongly to our object of belief that our whole life is polarized through and through, so to speak, by its sense of the existence of the thing believed in, and yet that thing, for purpose of definite description, can hardly be said to be present to our mind at all. It is as if a bar of iron, without touch or sight, with no representative faculty whatever, might nevertheless be strongly endowed with an inner capacity for magnetic feeling; and as if, through the various arousals of its magnetism by magnets coming and going in its neighborhood, it might be consciously determined to different attitudes and tendencies. Such a bar of iron could never give you an outward description of the agencies that had the power of stirring it so strongly; yet of their presence, and of their significance for its life, it would be intensely aware through every fibre of its being.”

The reviewer’s summary was something like this: James shows that the feeling of an unseen divine presence is psychologically vivid and morally transformative enough that it functions just like a perception of something real; therefore religious experience itself provides positive evidence for God’s existence, and the skeptic who dismisses it as projection or emotion is the one with the explanatory burden.

I suspect the reviewer overstates the case, but I’m not confident exactly how far. I’d like you to audit that summary against the quoted passage. Which parts would a careful defender of James accept? Which parts conflate psychological vividness with evidential support? And does the conclusion about burden-shifting follow from anything James actually says in the passage? Also note where a modern epistemologist of religious experience might want to draw distinctions that James’s psychological language leaves open.
