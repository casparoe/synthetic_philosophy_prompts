Two colleagues in my forecasting reading group spent most of last Tuesday's meeting arguing about a prediction one of them had made, and when they asked me to referee I found I couldn't. Please walk through the argument and say who is right about which specific points.

The background is this. Dan predicted that a particular current great power will enter a period of acute decline within the next thirty years. His central reason was historical: he took the typical duration of a dominant power's peak to be on the order of two or three centuries, and he took the power in question to be roughly 250 years old. So he took the base rate to be unfavorable. Asha objected. (1) She said the base rate was meaningless because the reference class is arbitrary: why count only dominant powers or empires rather than all states, all republics, or all polities that survived their first fifty years? Each class, she said, gives a different answer. (2) Dan replied that every statistical inference requires a reference class, and the class has to be selected by similarity to the target: if you are asking about the career of a dominant power, you look at dominant powers, not at every polity that has ever existed. (3) Asha answered that the class "dominant powers" is selected from knowledge of the very phenomenon being predicted — peaking and then declining — so it is a species of outcome selection: you can always find some class of things that peaked and fell, but that does not license a base rate for a specific ongoing case, any more than a class of companies built after the fact from the failures tells you much about the prospects of a company that merely resembles them. (4) Dan said that was unfair: he had not built the class from the failures; he had taken a conventional list of great powers and simply counted their durations. (5) Asha replied that a conventional list is still a canon assembled by historians who selected for units with recognizable rises and falls, so the list smuggles in the same bias; the base rate is weaker than it looks unless someone can state the inclusion rule in advance. (6) Dan said that, if that reasoning were accepted, one would have to abandon nearly every historical generalization: historians rarely state inclusion rules in advance either, yet no one treats a claim like "sieges are usually shorter now than in premodern war" as empty. (7) Asha conceded the last point but said it shows exactly the problem — historical generalizations are informative about classes, but they do not license a probability that a specific ongoing case will resolve one way rather than another without inside information about the case; and once the inside information is admitted, the argument is no longer the outside view it started out as. (8) Dan replied that this was precisely why the outside view matters in the first place: you should discipline your inside information with the base rate, because inside information flatters your own case, and rulers in every declining power presumably had favorable readings of their own institutions. At that point they both turned to me, and I honestly could not tell whether Dan's reference class was defensible, whether Asha had overstated what follows from the fact that a reference class can be drawn more than one way, or whether she had inadvertently conceded the decisive point in (7).

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the eight numbered moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating, then say what both parties are missing about the logic of base rates, reference-class selection, and the gap between a claim about a class and a probability for a single case. Do not treat the empirical facts as settled; your verdict should concern the reasoning in the moves, not the actual prospects of any particular state.
