I'm taking a course this term on the epistemology of AI interpretability, and I've built an argument for a paper that I now find suspicious because its conclusion seems absurd. We've been discussing the fact that an interpretation of a model—an account of what a feature, a circuit, or a layer is doing—is itself a claim produced by some interpreter: a human, a team, or another model. That observation led me to the following argument:

(1) A model is interpretable only if there is some interpretation of it that we are justified in trusting. (2) An interpretation is the output of an interpreter, and we are justified in trusting the interpretation only if we are justified in trusting that interpreter's competence on this task. (3) To be justified in trusting the interpreter's competence, we need either an account of the interpreter itself or else a trust in the interpreter that we adopt without any such account. (4) The second option gives up exactly what interpretability was supposed to provide: a reason to trust the target model that does not itself rest on uninterpreted, black-box reliance. (5) So we must take the first option, interpreting the interpreter; but the same demand then applies to the interpreter of the interpreter, and we are on a regress. (6) The regress cannot be completed, so no model is interpretable.

That conclusion cannot be right, since interpretability work seems to produce claims that genuinely guide decisions about whether to deploy or trust a model. But I cannot locate the weak step. I am tempted to reject (3) and say an interpreter's reliability can be established by cross-checks, causal interventions, and predictive success without interpreting the interpreter itself; yet I then worry I have simply hidden the interpreter inside those cross-checks, since they too must be designed and read by someone. Or perhaps the flaw is that the argument proves too much, applying equally to human testimony—we trust people without interpreting their brains, and we do not take that to make testimony impossible. Which step should I reject, why did that step look compulsory, and what would rejecting it cost for the rest of interpretability practice?
