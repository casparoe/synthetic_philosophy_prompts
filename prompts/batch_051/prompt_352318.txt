A few months ago a friend and I argued about whether two intelligent systems that can never interact could still be said to cooperate. I ended up with a view I now call "cooperation without contact," and I would like an honest assessment of it. The setup is this. Two agents—say, two civilizations that can never exchange even a photon, or two copies of an artificial mind sealed off from one another—each must decide whether to spend some of its own resources to benefit the other. Each is better off if both spend than if neither spends, but each is worse off spending alone. There is no channel between them, no reputation at stake, no enforcement, and no future point at which either side could verify what the other did. Yet each spends purely because it predicts the other is spending, based on shared reasoning or an accurate model of how the other thinks. My claim is that this is still a real kind of trade: acting on a sufficiently good model of another mind is not fundamentally different from acting with the other mind itself.

I find the idea compelling, but I also worry it has no principled stopping point. It seems to imply that I could "trade" with a partner that never existed at all, provided my model of that partner is detailed enough. And if no one ever checks, I cannot tell whether this is genuine cooperation or just two private decisions that happen to mirror each other. So my questions: is the idea coherent? Does it already exist under some name I have not encountered? Where exactly does it stop being trade and start being two agents independently doing what they would have done anyway? I would like the three strongest objections, and a sense of whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
