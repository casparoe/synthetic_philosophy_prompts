I'm putting together a module on risks that hide in plain sight, and I need a worked example that doesn't lean on science-fictional machinery. I'm using "antimeme" in a broad sense: a piece of information whose content makes it less likely to be noticed, remembered, or passed on — not because its being suppressed, but because of something about the information itself. Please construct a thought experiment around a single antimeme that is genuinely harmful in aggregate and nearly impossible for its victims to coordinate against. Constraints: no magic, no supernatural agencies, no memory-wiping drugs or technology beyond what a modern office has; the antimeme must be boring and bureaucratic, not scary or uncanny; it must not work merely by being long, encrypted, or buried in fine print; the affected community must be literate, have smartphones and functioning institutions, and keep written records; and the harm has to come from people failing to act on the information, not from a villain exploiting the gaps. Set it somewhere gray and institutional — a water utility, a hospital billing office, an insurer's claims department, whatever — and keep the scenario to one paragraph.

After the scenario, walk me through the machinery: (a) state the exact content of the antimeme and why it drops out of working memory and out of conversation, (b) explain how it nonetheless survives in the population long enough to do the damage, and (c) identify the single feature that is doing the antimemetic work — then show how the case stops being antimemetic if you change just that feature (say, by making the information alarming or attaching a legal penalty). Finish by saying how a skeptic who thinks antimemes are just boring ideas that don't spread would attack the case, and whether the attack lands. The point is to force a clean distinction between "boring and ignored" and "antimemetic," so make it a knife-edge case rather than a trivial one.
