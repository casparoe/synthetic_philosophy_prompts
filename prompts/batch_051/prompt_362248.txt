I retired a few years ago from a career in experimental biology and have been slowly working through decision theory, and the Ellsberg paradox keeps nagging at me because it should be a trick but apparently its not. I can restate the problem: an urn has 90 balls, 30 red and 60 that are black or yellow in an unknown mix. Most people, when choosing between a bet that pays on red and a bet that pays on black, take the red bet; but when choosing between a bet that pays on red or yellow and a bet that pays on black or yellow, they take the black-or-yellow bet. I can even see the formal inconsistency: under a single probability for each color the first preference looks like p(red) > p(black), the second looks like p(black or yellow) > p(red or yellow), which subtracts down to p(black) > p(red). So a single distribution can't do it. But I can't get my hands around the conceptual shape of why.

So my ask is this. Give me an intuitive explanation of exactly how these choices violate Savage's sure-thing principle, and where the principle does its work when someone insists on a single subjective probability. In particular, what is it about ambiguity — uncertainty about the probabilities themselves rather than about which ball comes out — that makes the sure-thing principle the wrong place to dig in, for someone like Ellsberg? I gather the umbrella term is ambiguity aversion, the preference for known probabilities over unknown ones even when the unknown isn't obviously worse. And is the conceptual cost of relaxing the sure-thing principle bigger or smaller than the cost of giving up the idea that uncertainty can always be captured by point-valued probabilities? I want to understand why two reasonable people can look at the same choices and one sees incoherent behavior while the other sees a framework that is too cramped.
