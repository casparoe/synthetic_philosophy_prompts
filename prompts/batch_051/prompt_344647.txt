I have recently been moved from a technical research role into the governance and risk team of a large AI lab, and I now own a quarterly race-dynamics assessment that goes to our chief operating officer. In each assessment I have to judge whether the lab is being pulled into a harmful development race and whether any specific move would defuse it; the memo therefore determines whether we delay a deployment, make or retract a public commitment, or open a negotiation with a rival. I have at most one day to run the assessment, I cannot see inside rival organizations, and my director, who signs the memo, will not accept a list of vague heuristics — though she also understands that a number I cannot defend is worse than an explicitly marked judgment call.

A colleague from our international security team argues that Fearon's rationalist explanations for war are the right framework. His version is this: a development race, like war, is costly and ex post inefficient, so the interesting question is why rational actors would end up in one rather than in a negotiated arrangement both would prefer; the candidate answers are private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and issue indivisibility. I know the labels, but I do not yet have a way to turn them into something I can operate with when a memo is due.

Please translate that framework into a reusable decision procedure: a checklist, or a flowchart in words, that I could run in under a day against any concrete competitive situation in AI development. It should preserve what the theory actually says rather than a watered-down version, but it must remain usable by someone who is not a political scientist and later by the rest of my team. For each of the three mechanisms, tell me what observable signatures I should look for in an AI context, in what order to run the checks, and what to do when the relevant information is unavailable. Mark clearly which steps are genuine checks and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then apply the procedure to three cases: one, two labs in a public frontier-model race that have signed a paper-only agreement to share safety results before deployment; two, a government deciding whether to impose export controls on advanced chips; and three, my own lab, which currently holds a clear lead and has publicly committed to staged, safety-first deployment — the case where I would strongly prefer the procedure to say there is no race risk, and it must tell me the less comfortable truth if the structure says otherwise. Finish by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do and where it is most likely to misfire. Structure the response in this order: a section called The translation, restating the three mechanisms in terms of AI development in roughly 300 words; a section called The procedure, giving the step-by-step checklist with order, stopping rules, and judgment points marked, in roughly 800 words; a section called Three worked applications, running the procedure on the three cases above including the one I will not like, in roughly 700 words; and a section called Limits and failure modes, saying what the procedure cannot do, in roughly 300 words.
