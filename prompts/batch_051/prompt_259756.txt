Question: Some philosophers and scientists have proposed that brain size may be morally relevant, in the sense that a being with a larger brain, or with a brain of a particular structure, makes stronger moral claims on us. Evaluate this proposal. In particular, distinguish between treating brain size as intrinsically morally significant and treating it as merely evidence for some other morally significant property, and then take a clear position on whether brain size should influence moral deliberation.

Student answer: Brain size could not be what ultimately makes a being matter morally. If it were, then sperm whales and elephants, whose brains are much larger than ours, would have stronger moral claims than adult humans, and within our own species a person with an unusually large brain would be owed more concern than a person with an average one. Both implications are widely rejected, which strongly suggests that brain size is not intrinsically significant. The traits that actually matter are psychological ones, namely the capacity to suffer, to feel pleasure, and, for especially strong moral status, self-awareness or a sense of oneself over time. Brain size is at best a rough and noisy correlate of these traits across species; corvids, for example, have brains far smaller than many mammals yet display surprisingly flexible problem-solving. Since brain size is only a correlate and not a moral criterion itself, it is morally irrelevant, and any practice that relies on brain measurements to determine how much concern a nonhuman animal or a developing human fetus is owed is making an error of the same general kind as judging someone by skin color. Consistency therefore requires that brain size be given no weight at all in moral deliberation.
