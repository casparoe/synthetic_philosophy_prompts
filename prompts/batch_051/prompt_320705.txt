A reading group I'm in has now spent three evenings on the sunk cost "fallacy" and we've got absolutley nowhere. The purist side says a cost you can't recover should never count: if a degree, relationship, project, or gig is worth continuing only because of what you've already put in, that's an error, and the cure is to ignore the past and compare only expected future costs and benefits. The other side says this is too quick: sticking with something after a big investment can protect your reputation, stop you updating against yourself as a quitter, keep regret at bay, or treat your past effort as something it would be wrong to just write off. By now every reply is predictable: purists say all of those are really forward-looking costs in disguise, so no genuinely sunk cost is doing any work; defenders say the purists have just defined 'rational' so narrowly that only their conclusion could follow and are ignoring what it's actually like to honour a past commitment. The frustrating bit is that both sides seem to appeal to different examples as "obviously" decisive, and nobody moves.

My working theory is that this isn't one debate but three stuck together: a descriptive one about whether people who honour sunk costs actually end up better off; a normative one about whether 'rational' is meant to govern a single choice or a whole life; and maybe just a verbal one about what counts as 'the reason' for continuing. But I'm not confident, and I've been wrong about this kind of thing before. What I want is a diagnosis of the stall itself, not a verdict on sunk costs.
