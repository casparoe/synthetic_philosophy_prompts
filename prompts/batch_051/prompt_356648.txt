I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on global priorities, and I've been mulling over a funding mechanism that a friend at a mid-size foundation described. They're thinking about replacing their program officers' discretion with an automated formula built on the standard importance–tractability–neglectedness framework, but with a prediction-market twist for tractability. I want someone to really walk through how this thing would behave, because I suspect the incentives get weird in ways the foundation hasn't noticed.

Here's the proposal. The foundation gives out about $120M a year, mostly in global development and pandemic preparedness. Any organization can apply, under whatever cause label it chooses—no fixed taxonomy. Each application gets three scores, all normalized to 0–1. Importance is set every two years by the board for each cause label, based on an anonymous survey of fifteen domain experts, and the board reports the median. Neglectedness is computed as 1 minus that cause's share of total philanthropic spending from public filings over the previous three years. Tractability is the odd part: at the application stage, the applicant has to pre-register one 12-month outcome indicator. There's also a short qualitative due-diligence review. Then the foundation runs an internal prediction market in which program staff and paid external reviewers trade contracts that pay out if the milestone is met; the current price is the tractability score. Applicants and their organizations can't trade, and neither can the staff or reviewers who did the due-diligence review for that application. The priority score is importance × tractability × neglectedness, and the foundation funds everything above whatever cutoff exactly exhausts the annual budget.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral argument about whether cause prioritization is good. Walk through what it does to who applies, which milestones they pick, and how they carve up cause labels to look neglected. Then get into the tractability market: who profits from pumping or suppressing prices, what a shallow subsidized market does to the score, whether private information leaks in through staff who can't trade directly but talk to people who can, and whether the price ends up tracking tractability or just measurability. Then talk about the foundation itself: what gets funded, what important but hard-to-measure work gets squeezed out, and where the whole thing gets gamed first. Say which failure modes are patchable and which are baked in.

Finally, could you separate what's actually settled here from what's genuinely contested among people who study prioritization and prediction markets — e.g., don't present it as established fact that an internal market will aggregate information well in a thin subsidized setting, or that neglectedness can be read off public filings without massive relabeling effects. And please write it in American English.
