I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I want help thinking through something from my own professional life, partly because I'd like to write a short explainer about the general pattern if I can pin it down.

I'm on a listserv of about forty science writers. Every so often someone posts a draft — an op-ed, a book pitch, a preprint explainer — and asks for feedback. The norm is that detailed, line-by-line comments are welcome. Here's the recurring problem: if one or two people send detailed edits, the author gets a great set of notes. If twenty or thirty people do, the thread turns toxic — the author gets buried under contradictory suggestions, people start arguing about each other's edits, and a couple of members have actually quit the list over it. Everyone knows this; we say it out loud after every blow-up.

But when the next draft goes out, my brain does the same thing. I think: "If I don't send edits, maybe the author gets no feedback at all. And if I do send them, the chance I'm the one who tips the thread into chaos is tiny." I've heard at least three other members describe almost the exact same thought. So a bunch of us send edits, the thread explodes, and we all promise it won't happen again. Then it does.

We even tried a sign-up for who would do a first pass, but since the author always says "any and all feedback is great," the sign-up doesn't stick.

So I have two questions. Practically: next time someone asks for feedback, should I send detailed edits or hold back? And structurally: is there a deeper principle that explains why individually sensible choices keep adding up to something nobody wanted? I suspect the same pattern shows up outside listservs too — citizen science projects, open-source maintainership, leak-reporting channels, that kind of thing.

You're a philosopher who works on collective action and group epistemology. Think carefully. Start by brainstorming two or three different ways of characterizing the structure of what's happening, then commit to the one you find most illuminating and use it to give me a real recommendation, not generic advice.
