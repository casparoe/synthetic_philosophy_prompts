I've been going round in circles with my flatmate, who does Romance historical linguistics, about the hoary old question of when exactly Latin stops being Latin and starts being Italian. The standard observation is that each generation of speakers can understand its parents and its children fine, but the ends of the chain are mutually unintelligable; there's never a felt moment of rupture, just a sorites chain stretched over centuries. I want to twist this, because I suspect the usual philosophical machinery was built for the ship of Theseus or the heap of sand, where you have a single persisting object whose bits get swapped or added one at a time. A language isn't like that: the "bits" are the speakers, and a whole generation of them gets replaced wholesale every few decades, while the language persists only as a pattern over the population.

So here is the variant I'd like you to work through. Imagine a closed village over sixty generations. In each generation, the children acquire their parents' speech with a fixed, measurable drift: on a standard battery of a thousand diagnostic sentences, exactly three change in acceptability relative to the parents' judgements. Adjacent generations always communicate without strain, but the endpoints are classical Latin and modern Italian, mutually unintelligible. Now add one extra ingredient: the village keeps one written copy of the original Latin grammar, and once every five generations a committee reads it aloud and tries, for a year, to speak in accordance with it, before everyone slips back into the vernacular. Does that frozen written standard create a sharp boundary anywhere? What should the supervaluationist, the epistemicist, and the degree-of-truth theorist each say about the sentence "these villagers still speak Latin", taken at various points along the chain? And is there any principled difference between this case and the original ship of Theseus, given that here every "plank" is discarded completely rather than replaced one at a time?
