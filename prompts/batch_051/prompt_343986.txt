I am a novelist. My current book follows a professional coffee taster, and the research for the character has pulled me into the philosophy of taste. There is a thought experiment I keep returning to: two experienced tasters agree that a coffee they have known for years no longer tastes as it once did, yet they disagree about where the change lies. One says the coffee itself tastes exactly as it always did and only his standards of liking have shifted; the other says his standards have not moved and the taste itself has altered. From this I have assembled a view I call the Ledger View of Qualia. Its claim is that there are no intrinsic qualities of experience over and above a subject's running record of discriminations, acceptances, and refusals. A statement such as 'it tastes the same to me, but I no longer like it' is not a report of a private datum; it is a decision about how to keep that record, a choice about which strand of one's own comparative history to treat as authoritative. Qualia, on this view, are the shadows thrown by the bookkeeping, not the items being entered. The view seems to dissolve the standoff between the two tasters, since both readings can be rendered consistent with the single ledger and no further fact is left for them to dispute. I would like a candid assessment. Name the three strongest objections to the Ledger View, and say whether any of them kills the view rather than merely troubling it. I would also like to know whether I have reinvented a position that already exists in the literature, and if so, where my version stops being distinctive.
