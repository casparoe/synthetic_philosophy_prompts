I recently moved for a research posting to a lower-middle-income country and now do my own food shopping on a local salary; for the first time, the debate about veganism where alternatives are scarce has become a weekly practical problem rather than an abstract one. I am writing a paper on how individualist arguments from animal ethics travel badly outside affluent consumer contexts, and I need a single tightly designed case to organize the argument around. Please construct it to the following specifications.

I need an ordinary, realistic scenario in which three positions come apart in a particular way. An act-consequentialist, who evaluates the purchase by its expected effects on welfare, should conclude that a particular low-income person's purchase of animal foods is not wrong. A structural-injustice theorist of food, who holds that in a broken food system the decisive moral failures belong to the actors that made adequate plant-based food unavailable or unaffordable, should also conclude that the buyer does not act wrongly — but for reasons that are not consequentialist. A rights-based abolitionist vegan, who holds that using animals as food is wrong in a way that does not disappear when the buyer is poor, should conclude that this same purchase is wrong.

Set the case in a peri-urban area of a lower-middle-income country, with plausible income and food-price levels; internal consistency matters more than exact data. The buyer should be a primary caregiver with real nutritional obligations — not someone who could replace the animal food with beans at no extra cost and no nutritional loss. The consequentialist's permissive verdict must be earned, not stipulated: it should arise from the small marginal effect of one purchase on an already-stocked supply chain, the higher cost per unit of usable protein and absorbable micronutrients of the available plant foods, and the opportunity cost of moving scarce money away from other pressing needs. The structural-injustice theorist's verdict must be generated from her own commitments: she should locate the wrongness upstream, with the public and corporate decisions that produced the scarcity, and should deny that the constrained buyer is the appropriate object of blame. Make sure she does not collapse into the consequentialist position in sociological language; she should reject the idea that the right question is whether this purchase maximizes expected value at all. The buyer should not be ignorant of how her food is produced, should not be so desperate that agency is defeated, and should not be mistaken about her options.

Write the case itself in no more than one paragraph of scene-setting. Then verify the construction against each specification, step by step: for each theory, state the verdict on the buyer's action — right, wrong, permissible, excused, or not the appropriate object of moral assessment — and on whether anyone else in the case acts wrongly; then give the reasoning that yields those verdicts. Identify the specific feature of the case doing the most work, and say which verdicts would shift, and how, if that feature were removed. End with the strongest attack a defender of the abolitionist view could make against the case as constructed, and specify what single change to the case would most strengthen her side.
