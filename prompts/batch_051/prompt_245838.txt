Consider the following argument that everything that actually exists, exists necessarily.

(1) Everything is such that something is identical to it — in symbols, ∀x∃y(y=x).
(2) (1) is a truth of logic, so it is necessary: necessarily, everything is such that something is identical to it.
(3) If necessarily everything is such that something is identical to it, then everything is such that, necessarily, something is identical to it.
(4) Therefore, everything is such that, necessarily, something is identical to it — that is, nothing that actually exists could have failed to exist.

I cannot accept (4): surely I might never have existed. Yet (1) and (2) look like trivialities, and (4) follows from (2) and (3), so my suspicion falls on (3). I can see that (3) moves the necessity from outside the quantifier to inside it, but I cannot say whether that move is legitimate or what would license it. Is the argument valid? Which step is the first to fail, and on what reading of (3) would it be true?
