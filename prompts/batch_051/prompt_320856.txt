Lately I have been returning to an argument with a friend of mine who studies theoretical biology. He maintains that an ant colony can be said to have a mind in something better than a merely metaphorical sense. His case runs as follows. A single ant is a reflex-driven machine with a small behavioral repertoire, yet a colony handles foraging, nest-site selection, division of labor, and defensive coordination in ways that look adaptive, flexible, and directed at goals; colonies even show something resembling learning when their preferences change after bad experiences. He then presses the analogy: the individual ant is to the colony roughly what a single neuron is to a brain; chemical signals and antennal contacts play the role of synaptic transmission; pheromone gradients and interaction rates are the distributed activation patterns; and the colony's collective decision-making is its cognitive processing. On the strength of this correspondence, he concludes that the colony is not merely like a mind by courtesy of a figure of speech. It is a mind in good standing, and if we hesitate to call it conscious, that hesitation, he thinks, betrays an arbitrary prejudice in favor of skull-bound, fast-signaling, nervous-system minds rather than any principled boundary between minds and non-minds.

I would like an audit of this analogy rather than a verdict based on my unexamined intuition. Please make the correspondence explicit—what maps onto what, and how closely—and then identify the places where the mapping strains or collapses. In particular, which features of neurons and nervous systems does the analogy quietly import that a colony may not possess, and which of those disanalogies genuinely matter for the conclusion that the colony is a mind, as opposed to being incidental differences like propagation speed or chemical medium? After that, say whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there is a better analogy that preserves the genuine insights of the ant-brain comparison while marking its limits. I am also struck by the possibility that the analogy slides between two different claims: that a colony is a mind in the sense of high-level, integrated, goal-directed information processing, and that a colony has the unified, subjective point of view we usually intend when we say that something is conscious. Does the ant-brain analogy carry both steps, or only one of them, and does the answer change the force of my friend's conclusion?
