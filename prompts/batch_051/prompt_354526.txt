A friend who works at a medium-sized engineering firm has been telling me about a review process they're piloting, which he calls a sunk cost amnesty. Every quarter, each project's budget is reset to zero, and the written case for keeping a project alive may not mention anything already spent, any hours already logged, or how close the team is to finishing. The idea is that the old numbers are exactly what drags good people into escalation of commitment, so the deliberation should be forced to look only at future costs and future returns. I find this weirdly appealing, but I also suspect it would just push the bias somewhere else.

Could you treat it as a mechanism rather than as an abstract principle? I'd like to know what it would actually incentivise among the project leads (who still know the old numbers even if they can't write them down), the senior managers who judge the quarterly cases, and the people deciding what to propose in the first place. How would a clever team game it, for instance by smuggling completion status into future cost estimates or by dressing up sunk spending as something with salvage value? And are the predictable second-order effects on risk-taking, on documentation, or on which projects get launched worse than the escalation problem the rule is meant to cure? I'd also like a view on whether you could patch the obvious failures without quietly reintroducing the original bias.
