I've been trying to think clearly about the ethics of eating animal products in places where plant-based alternatives are scarce, costly, or culturally complicated — the version of the debate that gets skipped when people assume a middle-class life in a wealthy country with supermarkets full of alternatives.

I'm drafting a long-form essay for general readers, and I want it organized around the two or three unresolved conceptual questions that would most change the overall conclusions if they were settled one way rather than another — not around whatever nutritional or environmental studies happen to be in circulation, since I can't adjudicate those myself.

So I need a prioritization: among the underlying disputes in this area, which are most upstream, and which are overrated? Rank the candidates below, or replace some with better ones I'm missing, and defend the ordering with explicit criteria — for example, how many other questions depend on the answer, how much practical behavior would actually change, and how tractable the question is for a non-specialist writer.

Candidate disputes I keep running into:

- Is the core problem a matter of individual consumer obligation, or is it really about institutional and structural failures such as subsidies, food environments, and supply chains? If the structures are missing, how much should fall on individuals?
- The usual fallback is that we should avoid animal products wherever doing so is genuinely feasible. Does that feasibility include social, cultural, and psychological costs, or only physical and financial ones? Does the line shift depending on what others around you are doing?
- What baseline of nutritional adequacy should be assumed? Is achieving adequacy itself part of what is being negotiated in low-resource settings, or is it a fixed constraint that limits responsibility?
- What is the right unit of evaluation: a single meal, a household's weekly budget, a full year, a dietary tradition, or the whole supply chain? Does the verdict change when you change units?
- Does your position within the food system — consumer, producer, seller, intermediary — change what you are obligated to do, and does the consumer-choice framing distort the debate?
- If one person's abstention will not change production and will be offset by other consumers, is abstaining still required, merely symbolic, or beside the point?
- Which harm is doing the real moral work: the suffering of individual animals, environmental damage, the loss of pastoral livelihoods, or the social role of shared animal-source foods? How much of the disagreement is just people weighting these harms differently?

The deliverable is a defended agenda: tell me which two or three of these questions I should build the essay around, which I should mention only in passing, and which I should drop — and justify the cuts and the ranking.
