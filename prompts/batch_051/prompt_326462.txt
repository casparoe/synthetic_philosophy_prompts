I've been going back and forth on the blackmail puzzle, and I want help negotiating between a principle I'm attracted to and an intuition I can't shake. The puzzle: revealing a true, embarrassing secret about someone is ordinarily legal (the information is true and you're allowed to tell it), and asking a person for money is legal. But combining them into "pay me or I'll tell everyone" is blackmail — a crime, and something most people think is genuinely wrong. I'm drawn to a clean composition principle: if two acts are each permissible on their own, doing one conditional on the other, or threatening one unless paid, should also be permissible. The principle handles a lot of ordinary cases: I may refuse to sell you my car, and I may ask for money, so it should be fine to say "pay me my asking price or I won't sell." But then the principle forces me to say the blackmailer does nothing wrong, and I can't get myself there.

A friend who does contract law made it worst by pointing out that you can legally be paid for silence before any threat is made — signing an NDA, settling quietly — while the exact same silence, offered after "pay me or I'll talk," is criminal. He also noted that a lawyer can write a letter saying "pay up or I'll sue," and we mostly think that's fine. So the difference isn't just "threat plus demand for money." The intution I'm left with is that the blackmailer is exploiting the victim's vulnerability in a way those other conditional threats don't, but I can't articulate what that something is.

Please present both sides at their strongest. Tell me what would have to be true for the composition principle (or my tidy picture of permissibility) to be the thing that goes; what would have to be true for the intuition that blackmail is wrong to be debunked — say, as a reaction of disgust at people profiting from other people's secrets rather than a reliable moral signal; and whether there's a reading of "permissible" or "wrong" on which both survive. I want a negotiated verdict, not a victor. And at the end, say how confident you are in the verdict and single out the single point you're least sure of.
