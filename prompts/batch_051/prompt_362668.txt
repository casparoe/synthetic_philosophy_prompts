I've been a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and formal epistemology stuff, so I know the problem of logical omniscience is old news, but it still strikes me as a debate that never converges. The standard possible-worlds semantics for knowledge and belief gives you closure: if an agent knows P and P entails Q, the agent knows Q, including every logical truth, since those hold in every world. Nobody thinks humans are like that. Yet the proposed fixes—awareness models where agents can only know what they explicitly represent, impossible worlds where logical consequences don't have to hold, algorithmic knowledge models that make reasoning a step-by-step procedure—have been around for years, and none of them has become the standard. The two camps just talk past each other.

My working theory is that it's really a normative-vs-descriptive tangle. One camp treats epistemic logic as a theory of ideal reasoning, so closure is a feature, not a bug, because the formalism never claimed to describe humans. The other camp treats it as a predictive model of actual cognition, so closure is an artifact that leaves the model with no contact to what real bounded agents do. People slide between what an ideal agent would believe and what a human reasoner will infer from these premises without noticing. But I could be wrong; maybe underneath there's a genuine formal disagreement about the semantics of implicit commitment, or about whether content can be assigned to sub-propositional states at all.

I want a diagnosis rather than a survey of the models. What kind of disagreement is this, actually—verbal, normative vs descriptive, a hidden formal question, or just a hard technical problem? And given the diagnosis, what would count as real progress: a theorem, a distinction, a reframing, or should I expect it to stay stalled? Please first spend some time steelmanning each camp as an expert in epistemic logic and formal epistemology, then give your honest verdict.
