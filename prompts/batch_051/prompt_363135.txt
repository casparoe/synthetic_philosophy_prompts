You are a professional philosopher specializing in the theory of practical reasons and the internalism/externalism debate about reasons; I want only your own reasoning, so do not cite texts, list readings, or produce a bibliography. Think carefully, and before you commit to a prediction, begin by informally brainstorming the candidate rules that could explain the record I describe, including at least one rule that is not the most obvious one.

In my seminar on practical reasons I have been tracking a colleague R.'s verdicts on a sequence of internal-reason cases, and I want to predict how she will rule in a new case. I also want an account of the rule she seems to be running. Here is her record, reported as neutrally as I can.

Case A: A has no desire to attend his sister's reunion. He feels no affection for her, no sense of family obligation, and no concern with appearing dutiful. He has attended such a reunion before and found it empty, and there is no motivational state of his from which reflection, correction of factual errors, or imaginative engagement would generate a desire to go. R says: he has no reason to attend.

Case B: B strongly wants to avoid future pain, but through an easily corrected factual error he believes the prescribed treatment is inert. If the error were corrected, he would plainly want to take the treatment. R says: he has a reason to take it.

Case C: C has no present desire to visit his mother in the hospital, but he loves her and identifies with being a devoted son. If he were prompted to imagine her condition, he would want to visit; he simply has not done this imagining yet. R says: he has a reason to visit.

Case D: D has promised to return a colleague's call, but he has no present desire to do so, no concern for the colleague, no desire to avoid guilt, and no attitude of his from which such a desire could be generated by sound deliberation. Nor is there any reason to think that actually making the call would awaken a latent concern. R says: he has no reason to return the call.

Now the new case. E has never played Go and has no present desire to learn it. He has no curiosity about games, no social motive that bears on them, and no broader desire for achievement, novelty, or a richer life from which a motive to play could be reached by sound deliberation. It is nevertheless true that if E were made to sit through a short series of introductory games, he would acquire a stable and absorbing love of Go, and this would become a lasting source of satisfaction for him. The only route to that outcome is the initial series of games, which he currently has no present desire and no soundly derived motive to undertake.

How will R rule on whether E now has a reason to try Go? Structure your response in the following four sections:

1. Candidate rules (about 250 words): state the rules that survive her record, giving the strongest version of each.
2. Predictions for E (about 200 words): say what each candidate rule predicts about E and why.
3. Best guess (about 150 words): say which rule R is most likely running, and justify the preference.
4. Decisive further case (about 150 words): describe one additional case that would most cleanly discriminate between the surviving rules, and state which verdict goes with which rule.
