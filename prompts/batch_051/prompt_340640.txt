In the course of researching a piece on appeals to nature, I keep coming back to a puzzle in John Stuart Mill's The Subjection of Women, and I want it thought through honestly. Mill is rightly read as the great critic of nature-based defenses of women's subordination. Near the start of the book he insists that no one can know the nature of the two sexes, because we have only ever observed them inside an institution of male domination; until that institution is altered, he argues, claims about what women are by nature cannot serve as evidence. But in the very same passage he does not remain agnostic. He calls what is currently taken to be feminine nature an artificial thing, produced by forced repression in some directions and unnatural stimulation in others, and his later argument appears to assume that under equal and non-coercive conditions a more genuine nature would emerge and could be studied. Are these two strands in contradiction? Lay out the strongest candidate reconciliations (different senses of nature, a descriptive-versus-normative distinction, a counterfactual account of natural development, a shift of target rather than of standard, or anything else promising), say what each reading costs in textual fidelity and philosophical coherence, and then give a verdict on which reconciliation is most honest and which is special pleading. After the prose, put the readings in a table with these columns: Proposed reconciliation; How it reconciles Mill's skeptical and naturalistic strands; Main cost or strain; Verdict.
