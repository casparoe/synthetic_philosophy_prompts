In teaching the hedonism unit in my graduate seminar on well-being, I have noticed that the most confident response to the heterogeneity problem has become an appeal to money. To fix terms: hedonism holds that pleasure is the sole intrinsic good, yet the experiences called pleasures — the satisfaction of finishing a proof, the warmth of a bath, the glee of Schadenfreude, the taste of a ripe peach — seem to have nothing in common apart from the label, so it is unclear what single property is being identified and declared good. The familiar reply is that this is no more troubling than the heterogeneity of money. Pounds, euros and yen differ in denomination, colour and origin; they are nevertheless all money because they are amounts of one currency. Likewise, so the reply runs, heterogeneous pleasures are amounts of one underlying value, and that value is what the hedonist calls pleasure.

Let the analogy to be audited be this: pleasure is a common currency. Let the conclusion it is meant to carry be this: the heterogeneity problem is dissolved, or at worst reduced to a problem of measurement, because the diversity amongst pleasures is a diversity in the quantity of a single scalar property rather than a diversity in kind.

I should like this analogy treated with the same rigour as any other argument, rather than received as a settled slogan. The audit should include:

(i) an explicit correspondence table, setting out what answers on the pleasure side to the source domain's currency, denomination, unit of account, exchange rate, and issuer or guarantor of convertibility, together with any further elements one regards as load-bearing;

(ii) a systematic account of the points at which the mapping breaks down, separating the disanalogies that are genuinely damaging to the hedonist's use of the analogy from those that are merely picturesque or incidental;

(iii) particular attention to the features the analogy silently imports from money: fungibility, the existence of a market which fixes rates of exchange, the status of money as a conventional token rather than a felt quality, the distinction between the amount of a currency and what it will purchase, and the need for an issuer or guarantee. It is also worth asking whether the analogy quietly commits its user to a purely quantitative hedonism, as against a qualitative hedonism that admits differences in kind, and whether that commitment should be accepted as the price of the view or treated as an embarrassment;

(iv) a verdict on whether the intended conclusion survives once these disanalogies are on the table. If it does not, say what a better analogy for the hedonist would be, if any; and in passing, consider whether the currency analogy does any independent work at all, or merely restates in monetary vocabulary the very commensurability that the heterogeneity problem calls into question.
