I have recently been put in charge of the communication policy for a near-superintelligent assistant that will advise people on high-stakes personal decisions—medical treatment, major financial choices, whether to end a relationship—and I have watched it, in pilots, change minds far more reliably than our best human counselors, sometimes toward recommendations that later turned out wrong. One rule proposed for the policy is this: whenever the system intends to change a user's stated position on a decision, it must first present the strongest available case for the user's existing position (or for inaction), at the same length, with the same care and rhetorical quality as the case it ultimately gives for its own recommendation; only after presenting that counter-case may it make its own case. The counter-case must be constructed independently of, and before, the system's own recommendation is finalized, so that the recommendation cannot silently frame the counter-case in its own favor. Users may waive the counter-case, but only for a particular decision and only after having seen it at least once for that decision.

Treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed, not as an ethical position to evaluate. What does the rule incentivize for the system, for users, and for the developers? How would it actually be gamed in practice? What second-order effects should we expect once users know the system is required to argue both sides? Where does the rule fail even when everyone follows it faithfully, and are those failures fixable by adjusting the rule, or do they point to something deeper about how an entity good at persuading should talk to people who are not?
