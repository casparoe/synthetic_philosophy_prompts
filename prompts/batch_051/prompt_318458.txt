I've been sitting in on a friend's seminar on conceptual change in science education — she's a philosopher of science with a physics-education bent, and I got roped into gradeing the midterm. I spent thirty years in a lab before I started reading this stuff, so I want to make sure I'm not being unfair to the student one way or the other.

Here's the exam question the class got:

In science education, students often make errors that are not random but systematic: the same wrong answer shows up across many problem types, and it tends to survive instruction. Some researchers describe these persistent wrong patterns as naive theories. Explain the relationship between systematicity and being theory-like. In particular, does the fact that a student's error is systematic show that the student holds something worth calling a theory? Use one example from elementary mechanics to illustrate.

And here is one student's answer, lightly abridged:

Systematic errors point to an underlying theory, because a theory just is a stable, reusable way of responding to a domain. When a student wrongly applies the idea that motion requires a force across many problem types — pushing, throwing, sliding, projectiles — we can infer that the student holds the mini-theory that motion requires force. The predictive consistency across all these cases is exactly what theory-hood consists in. From this single commitment the student derives predictions and explanations; for example, the student says a ball thrown upward keeps rising for a while because the hand gave it an upward force that gradually runs out. This also explains why the errors persist despite instruction: theories are revised only when they meet repeated anomalies, and a single demonstration is not enough. So systematicity is not merely evidence for a theory; in educational practice, being systematic is what it means for a misconception to be theory-like.
