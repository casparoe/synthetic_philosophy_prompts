Consider the following gamble. A fair coin is tossed until it comes up heads. If the first heads occurs on the nth toss, you receive £(−1)^{n−1} · 2^n / n, so the first few possible payoffs are +£2, −£2, +£8/3, −£4, +£32/5, and so on, where negative values are losses.

Now consider this argument.

(1) The expected value of a gamble is, by definition, the probability-weighted sum of its possible payoffs.
(2) The probability that the first heads occurs on the nth toss is 2^{−n}; the contribution of the nth possible payoff is therefore 2^{−n} × (−1)^{n−1} · 2^n / n = (−1)^{n−1} / n. So the expected value, summed in the natural order n = 1, 2, 3, ..., is the alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., which converges, to ln 2 in this ordering.
(3) The series in (2) is only conditionally convergent, not absolutely convergent. For any conditionally convergent real series, the rearrangement theorem says its terms can be reordered so as to sum to any chosen real number, or to diverge to +∞ or −∞.
(4) A quantity that can be changed to any value, or made infinite, merely by reordering the terms used to compute it cannot be a well-defined property of the gamble itself, unless some one ordering is privileged and the others are illegitimate.
(5) Therefore the gamble has no well-defined expected value. The convergence in (2) is an artefact of the arbitrary natural ordering, and expected-value reasoning cannot tell us what a fair price for playing the gamble is, or whether any finite price is worth paying.

Is this argument valid? If not, identify the exact step that fails and state the corrected version of that step. If the argument is valid, say precisely what it shows about the use of expected value in decision theory. In either case, take a stand on whether step (4) is a disguised stipulation and whether the natural ordering n = 1, 2, 3, ... is genuinely privileged. Write your answer in British English.
