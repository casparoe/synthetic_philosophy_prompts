I've spend the last year in an epistemology reading group that keeps circling the peer disagreement debate, and I'd rather have a diagnosis than a verdict. The positions are familiar: conciliationists say that when I find out an epistemic peer — someone equally smart, equally well-informed, looking at the same evidence — disagrees with me, I'm rationally required to move substantially toward them, often all the way to suspending judgment, because their disagreement is itself higher-order evidence that at least one of us screwed up the reasoning and I can't non-question-beggingly insist it was them and not me. The steadfast side says that's too quick: my belief was formed by actually weighing the first-order evidence, and unless I'm given a specific reason to think my reasoning went wrong — a reason that doesn't just appeal to the bare fact of disagreement — I'm entitled to hold on to it. The same examples come up every week: dividing the restaurant bill, doing mental long division, two equally good doctors reading the same scan. The two camps report opposite intuitions as if showing the case again will settle it, and nobody moves.

My own working theory is that the impasse isn't, at bottom, about disagreement at all. It looks to me like the conciliationists are answering a question about what belief an ideal agent should end up with once all the evidence is in — including evidence about how she and her peer processed that evidence — which is basically expected-accuracy reasoning, while the steadfast people are answering a question about what the first-order evidence itself supports, treating rationality as a kind of fidelity to your own reasons rather than error-minimization over the space of possible mistakes. If that's right, the two camps have partitioned the question differently, and the real fight is over whether there's an independence constraint on how higher-order evidence may bear on first-order reasoning — or maybe it's a disguised fight about uniqueness, whether a body of evidence permits at most one rational doxastic attitude. But I'm genuinely unsure. So diagnose the structure of this disagreement from what each side actually says: how much of it is verbal, how much is bedrock clashing intuitions about cases, and is there a hidden formal question about accuracy versus truth doing the work? And once you've diagnosed it, tell me what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a theorem, a genuinely new kind of case — or whether we should expect none, and why.
