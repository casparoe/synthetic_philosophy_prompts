One of my old tutors had a funny way of handling conflicts between principles and case intuitions, and I wrote down four of her rulings because they kept surprising me.

First, when a clean principle handled every case except one odd one, she kept the principle and said the stray case was probably being driven by how it was framed. She wouldn't add an extra clause for it.

Second, when the group's case judgements went intransitive — A better than B, B better than C, but C better than A — she wouldn't let us treat it as a counterexample to transitivity. She said each pairwise judgement was probably tracking a different feature, so they weren't really three verdicts about one relation.

Third, she once threw out a rival principle that fit all the cases, because it had four clauses and each looked ad hoc — bolted on just to mop up a counterexample that disappeared when the case was redescribed. She called it curve-fitting and said it would have fit almost any pattern of intuitions.

Fourth, she did revise a principle once, when a counterexample stayed stable across framings and survived discussion, and the amendment made sense independently rather than just for that one case. She said that's when a case earns the right to count as evidence.

Anyway, she's now looking at a new dispute. There's a simple principle that fails exactly one case. The case is stable across framings and holds up under scrutiny, so by her own lights it should count as evidence. But the only fix is a single clause with no independent motivation — exactly the sort of bolt-on she dismissed before. What do you predict she'll do: keep the principle and accept the recalcitrant case, add the clause, or suspend judgement? Which methodological rule is she actually running, which other rules fit her record just as well, and which further case would best separate them?
