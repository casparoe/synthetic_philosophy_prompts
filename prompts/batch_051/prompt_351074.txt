In my philosophy of law class we've been doing the paradox of blackmail, and I'm stuck on an asymmetry the readings never quite get at. It's legal to spill someone's embarrassing secret, and it's legal to ask them for money, but the moment you make one conditional on the other — pay me or I spill it — it's a crime: two permissions become a wrong just by being packaged as a threat. Yet the state packages threats in the same shape every day when a prosecutor says, plead guilty to this lesser charge or I'll take you to trial on the rest and ask for the maximum. That is also threatening to make someone worse off unless they hand over something you want. So what I'm after is the strongest principle that says the prosecutor's threat is fine and the blackmailer's isn't, followed by an honest verdict on whether it actually works. If you're tempted to say the difference is that the prosecutor's threat serves the public while the blackmailer's serves private gain, test that against someone who says, pay me or I'll report the crime I saw you commit: reporting crime is exactly what we usually tell people to do, but the law still treats the demand as blackmail. What really separates the state from the blackmailer — and after you've lined up the hard cases, should we keep the asymmetry, or is plea bargaining basically official extortion while blackmail law is just punishing private extortion?
