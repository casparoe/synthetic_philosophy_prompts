I have been working through Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus, and the sentence I keep testing myself against is this one: "Whatever causes no annoyance when it is present, causes only a groundless pain in the expectation." I read that as the load-bearing premise for the claim that death is nothing to us. On my understanding, the Epicurean position is that an event can count as bad for a person only if it is present to that person as an evil—only if there is awareness, or at least annoyance, at the time. Since death is not present while I exist, and I am not present when death is, the badness of death dissolves into a fear of something that will never be experienced as evil. For the purposes of this exchange, treat me as committed to the view that death is not bad for the one who dies.

My question is: what would count against it? I want a map of the view's exposure. Which observations, imagined cases, conceptual arguments, and results in nearby areas—prudential value, the temporality of harm, grief, suicide, premature death, posthumous harms, asymmetries between past and future—should move someone who holds the Epicurean line? Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and do not restrict yourself to the standard deprivation-account objection. In particular, I would like to know which defeaters a sophisticated Epicurean would themselves concede as counting against the view, as opposed to dismissing as already accommodated or as irrelevant to the thesis. And I would like an honest assessment of whether the view can only be saved by qualifying it into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a problem for a claim of this kind. I would personally find most persuasive a defeater that shows the quoted sentence's criterion is doing more work than it can bear, or that it misdescribes how a life can go badly for someone.
