Here are the criteria I was taught for English prepositions: an uninflected word that normally precedes a noun phrase, combines with that noun phrase to form a single constituent, and marks a grammatical or semantic relation—spatial, temporal, causal, or more abstract—between that phrase and another element in the sentence. Standard instances are clear: *in the room*, *from the roof*, *after the storm*.

Now consider *ago* in *ten years ago*. It is uninflected, it demands a preceding measure noun phrase and is unacceptable without one (*We left ago), and it seems to form a constituent that can be fronted as a unit (*Ten years ago, we met*) and can serve as a short answer (*When did it happen? Ten years ago*). But it follows its complement rather than preceding it; *ago ten years* is impossible. It also resists stranding: *How long ago did it happen?* is fine, but *How long did it happen ago?* is not, whereas many prepositions allow their complement to be moved without them (*Which room did she sleep in?*). It cannot be modified in the way many prepositions can (*right in*, *just after*, but not *right ago*, *just ago*), and it is restricted to temporal measure phrases rather than ordinary referring noun phrases (*ago the war*).

Does *ago* qualify as a preposition? Or is it a postposition? Or is it better left as a traditional adverb with a mandatory measure phrase? Work through the criteria one by one, say where they underdetermine the verdict, and then say whether this case shows that the “pre-position” word-order criterion is a real defining feature of the category or a parochial accident of English grammar.
