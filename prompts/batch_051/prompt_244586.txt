Alright, here's one I've been stuck on since I was reporting a piece about genetic discrimination and polygenic risk scores. I realized I couldn't explain to myself—let alone my editor—what actually makes a trait the kind of thing that ought to be a protected characteristic. People argue about whether weight, height, neurodivergence, or a criminal record should be protected, but they rarely give a principle; they just wave at immutability and a history of oppression. But religion is protected even though it's chosen, and left-handedness is about as immutable as traits get and nobody's marching to protect lefties. So those two gestures can't be doing the real work.

Give me a working definition of a suspect ground of discrimination—the principle that tells us which traits deserve protection and which don't. Then run it on this list and sort each into protected, not protected, or genuinely close: race, sex, religion, sexual orientation, age, disability, a twenty-year-old felony conviction, body weight, height, left-handedness, IQ, political affiliation, caste, and having a polygenic risk profile for schizophrenia. I don't want the definition to just ratify whatever the law currently happens to protect; I want the thing a court or legislature ought to be using.

Then tell me which two or three cases embarrass the definition most, and whether you'd rather patch the definition or defend the surprising verdict. And if you think there are really two seperate principles operating—one about not subordinating groups, the other about not stereotyping individuals—say which cases split along that line. Last thing: should concealability or visibility matter at all, or is that a red herring?
