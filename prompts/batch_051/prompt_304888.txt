I teach an undergrad philosophy of mind seminar — fourteen students, one two-hour block, a room with a whiteboard and chairs we can shove aside — and next week we're on Dennett's four kinds of creatures: Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian. In past years I've lectured the distinctions and students leave able to parrot them (natural selection; operant conditioning; letting hypotheses die in one's stead; thinking with words and other mind-tools) without, I think, feeling why these rungs form a ladder of increasing power rather than just four parallel strategies, or why each step is such an enormous saving in the number of real trials, failures, and deaths a lineage has to eat before it hits on something useful. I want them to discover that hierarchy themselves through how much better or worse they perform under different rules, not through me saying it.

Please design a runnable two-hour game in which the students themselves are creatures facing the same shifting environment, but different groups are forced to adapt through different rungs. What I'm after is something like this: the Darwinian group is stuck with only selection across compressed generations and no learning within a lifetime, and they end up slightly humbled; the Skinnerian group can beat the Darwinians but only by churning through a lot of public mistakes; the Popperian group can test candidates privately before acting, and everyone can watch the cost savings; and the Gregorians are handed some lightweight shareable notation or code that ends up letting them do things the others can't even frame as possible. I want participants to notice the performance gaps themselves before anyone names the four kinds.

Give me the full session plan as continuous prose, like an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. It should say how to divide or rotate fourteen students, how to compress selection so the Darwinian condition genuinely works without boring them into mutiny, the scenario and exact rules for each condition, cheap materials, and a scoring scheme with real small stakes but no money. Then give a debrief script that maps specific moments from the game onto each rung and onto the underlying idea that each level offloads the trial-and-error of the level below it. Be explicit about what predictably goes wrong — I'm guessing the Darwinians will quietly start learning like Skinnerians, and the Gregorians might treat their notation as a prestige object rather than a tool — and tell me how to handle those. Also be upfront about which feature of the idea this exercise can't reproduce and what I'll have to supply from the front to make up for it.
