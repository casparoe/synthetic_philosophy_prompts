A colleague has defended the following argument about solar geoengineering, and I have not been able to locate the step where it goes wrong.

(1) The most influential objection to developing solar geoengineering — technologies that would reflect a fraction of incoming sunlight, such as stratospheric aerosol injection, in order to hold temperatures down while emissions fall — is that simply having the option would weaken the will to cut emissions: governments and firms would treat the fix as a backstop, emit more now, and the technology would partly underwrite the damage it is meant to manage. Call this the moral-hazard objection.

(2) If the fact that a technology's availability can weaken precautionary behavior is by itself a decisive reason not to develop it, then the same reasoning condemns seatbelts, which can produce risk-compensating driving; flood insurance, which can subsidize building on floodplains; and vaccines, which can reduce caution about infection.

(3) We do not treat those effects as decisive reasons to forgo the technologies; we manage the behavioral response and keep the technology.

(4) So the moral-hazard objection, taken at face value, proves too much: either it must be abandoned, or it must be restated in terms that no longer support the conclusion originally drawn from it.

(5) The obvious restatement appeals to termination shock. Solar geoengineering holds temperatures down only while it continues to be deployed; if it were interrupted — by war, political collapse, or technical failure — the warming that had been masked would return within a decade or two, far faster than many ecosystems and agricultural systems could track.

(6) But every continuously operating intervention carries a termination hazard: a patient dies if dialysis stops; an aircraft on autopilot can be lost if the system disconnects at altitude. In those cases we do not reject the technology; we impose reliability, redundancy, and supervision.

(7) So termination shock is at most an argument for maintenance and governance institutions, not an argument against developing or deploying geoengineering.

(8) By this point neither the moral-hazard objection nor the termination-shock objection appears to survive as an objection, and only a diffuse worry remains about who would decide whether to deploy a technology that would, in effect, set the global thermostat.

(9) But a diffuse worry about governance can be raised about any emerging global-scale technology, so it cannot discriminate against geoengineering in particular.

(10) Therefore the standard objections to solar geoengineering fail, and opposition to it is either unprincipled or driven by the very technological optimism it claims to reject.

I should like to keep (3), because abandoning it seems to commit me to letting risk-compensation effects govern every domain of life. I should also like to keep the sense that solar geoengineering is subject to objections that are not merely an application of generic techno-skepticism, since a technology that deliberately manages the planetary radiation budget does not seem morally interchangeable with a seatbelt. But I am not sure those two commitments are jointly stable. Is the argument valid, and if so, which premise is the least costly to reject? If it is invalid, which step is the mistake, and why did it look like a safe inference?
