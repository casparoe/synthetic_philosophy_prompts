I'm a novelist, and I've realized my current book is ambiguous about something the plot can't afford to stay ambiguous about. The protagonist, Lin, is seventeen and has spent three years as the sole maintainer of an enormous half-dead legacy codebase. Around the midpoint she notices she no longer has anything she'd recognize as inner speech. When she deliberates, the deliberation just arrives as little programs in a generic Python-like language. She doesn't think "I should leave by seven"; she defines a function over `current_time`, `distance`, and `traffic_level` that errors out if `current_time >= 1900`. When she tries to decide whether to quit, she writes a type signature for the decision, the types won't unify, and she feels that mismatch as a physical sense of contradiction.

The third act changes depending on what I'm claiming when the book flirts with the idea that programming can be a language of thought. I need a defended ordering, not a survey.

Here are the six readings I keep going back and forth between.

1. Literal mentalese reading: programming is one consciously learned expression of the compositional language of thought we already reason in anyway; Lin is just introspecting code that was always running underneath.
2. Cognitive-tool reading: programming is a culturally acquired scaffolding that, once internalized, co-opts the brain's language and executive resources, so it becomes a real medium of deliberate thought without being the native one.
3. Weak-analogy reading: calling programming a language of thought is loose talk; Lin is doing ordinary problem solving and telling herself a code-flavored story about it afterward.
4. Notation-shapes-thought reading: the notation's types, abstractions, and recursion filter what Lin finds thinkable; it isn't mentalese, but it strongly shapes thought, and a different language would bend her differently.
5. Anti-language reading: thought isn't sentence-like at all; the inner programs are misleading reconstructions laid over processing that is mostly associative and patterny.
6. Normative-ideal reading: programming isn't a description of how Lin does think, but a model of how she ideally should think; it earns the title language of thought as a standard, not as a psychology.

For each reading, give me two scores out of 10: philosophical defensability and narrative usefulness for a novel where the ending depends on which reading is true. Then give an overall ranking, first to last. I care about the overall ranking most, but I want the two scores because I expect them to come apart in the middle. No reading list, no footnotes—I want the arguments so I can steal them for dialogue.
