For decades, archaeologists and historically minded social scientists have argued about how to characterize the ends of certain complex societies — the Classic Maya, the Bronze Age palatial states of the eastern Mediterranean, pre-contact Rapa Nui, and comparable cases. One side describes these episodes as collapses: comparatively rapid losses of centralized political organization, the abandonment of monumental centers, substantial population decline, and the disappearance of elite cultural forms. The other side describes them as transformations or reorganizations: political scale contracted or relocated, many ordinary lifeways continued, and later populations carried forward earlier practices; on this view, calling these episodes 'collapse' quietly assumes that large, state-level complexity is the normal or desirable endpoint. The two groups often work with the same settlement data, ceramic sequences, and climate proxies yet keep arriving at opposite lessons, which suggests to me that the impasse is not purely evidential. I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is, rather than a verdict on who is right. My working hypothesis is that 'collapse' actually bundles three disputes: an empirical dispute about magnitudes and rates (how fast, how many people, how permanent), a conceptual dispute about what it takes for the same society to persist through change, and a normative dispute about whether the loss of state-level complexity should count as a harm or failure. Evaluate that hypothesis and say what would count as progress on each component you identify.
