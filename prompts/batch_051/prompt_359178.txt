Question (midterm, markets and morals):

Reconstruct the strongest case for the view that commerce refines manners and improves character, and the strongest case for the view that commerce corrupts character. In your answer, engage the actual mechanisms proposed by each side and conclude with your own reasoned verdict.

Student answer:

Montesquieu and the eighteenth-century admirers of commerce defended what Hirschman later called the doux commerce thesis: trade brings strangers into regular, peaceful, mutually beneficial contact, which polishes away barbarous manners and softens violent passions. Hirschman's The Passions and the Interests reconstructs a sharper version of this idea. Early capitalism, on his account, does not abolish the passions but channels them into interests, and moneymaking is predictable, peaceful, and self-limiting in a way that the pursuit of glory or conquest is not. Adam Smith contributes the invisible hand: in a competitive market, each person can pursue private gain only by supplying what others want, so self-interest is made to serve the general interest. Smith's invisible hand is essentially a cleaned-up version of Mandeville's older paradox, that private vices such as greed and luxury unintentionally produce public benefits, and the two arguments stand or fall together.

On the character side, commerce is also said to make people more trustworthy: in repeated exchange, a reputation for honest dealing is an asset, so honesty pays. And since honesty pays only for those who actually possess the disposition, commercial society should over time select for, and cultivate, genuine honesty and fair dealing rather than merely outward compliance.

The critical tradition rejects this. Marx argues that capitalism alienates the worker from the product, from the activity of labor, from other people, and from the species-being, turning labor-power itself into a commodity. Sandel extends the criticism to market creep: when prices are introduced into spheres such as queue-jumping, health care, or education, market norms can crowd out nonmarket values like fairness, duty, and civic obligation. On balance, I find the critical side more persuasive, because it identifies concrete mechanisms of corruption, while the civilizing argument ultimately depends on the claim that the profit motive manufactures virtue, and I do not think that claim has been established.
