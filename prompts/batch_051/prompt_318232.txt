I keep seeing arguments about integrity and incorruptibility that start from one striking case — a judge, a dissident, a mafioso with a code — and then generalise to a full account. Before I read any more of these, I want the specification they will be measured against. Write the desiderata that any adequate account of what it is to be uncorruptible, and of how incorruptibility relates to integrity, must meet if it is to be useful for both moral judgment and institutional design.

Give me the cases it must classify correctly, with a specific case behind each requirement: the judge who cannot be bought but lets unacknowledged friendships steer her rulings; the official who serves a criminal regime and applies its own rules without exception; the whistleblower who breaks one loyalty in order not to break another; the ascetic indifferent to money but hungry for recognition; the person whose judgment is so weak or timid that he is never worth corrupting. Then specify the distinctions the theory cannot collapse: being unbribable with money versus being unbribable by threats, flattery, status, fear, or ideology; incorruptibility versus integrity; the incorruptibility of a person versus that of an office, procedure, or institution; and the disposition itself versus the mere absence of tolerable opportunities to sell out. For each requirement, say what case or consideration forces it rather than one camp's taste. Say what exactly a theory must say stays intact when someone remains uncorrupted — judgment, standards, the office, or character — and whether incorruptibility is a threshold or a matter of degree.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which could be jettisoned, and say whether any pair of them cannot be jointly satisfied. The tension I most want examined is this: can an account hold both that incorruptibility is always a good-making disposition and that a fanatic who serves an evil cause and refuses every inducement to soften, defect, or show mercy counts as genuinely incorruptible? Keep the weight on the criteria and their cases rather than on scoring named accounts; a table is fine.
