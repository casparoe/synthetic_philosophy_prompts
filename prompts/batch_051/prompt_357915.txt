Question (roughly two pages): Economists often say a rational agent ignores sunk costs: bygones are bygones, and only expected future costs and benefits count. Yet ordinary life is full of cases where people treat sunk costs as reasons — finishing a degree they've come to hate because they're three years in, refusing to sell a house or stock at a loss, carrying on with a project because of the hours already put into it. Is honoring sunk costs always irrational? If you think it sometimes isn't, give the strongest case you can and say exactly what makes it rational. If you think it always is, address what seems like the strongest argument for an exception and say what those ordinary cases are getting wrong.

Student's answer: The standard view is that sunk costs are irrelevant, and I think it's right. A sunk cost is a cost you've already paid that no future action can recover. Since it is gone either way, it should cancel out when you compare your options: only expected future costs and benefits can tip the balance. If you've paid for a movie and hate it, leaving is better than staying to 'get your money's worth,' because the ticket money is gone whichever you choose. So honoring sunk costs is always irrational, and any apparent counterexample has to be the sunk cost fallacy in disguise. The only serious objection I can think of is that honoring sunk costs might be useful as a commitment device: if people know you'll finish what you start once you've invested a lot, your past investment buys you credibility. But this fails. Commitment devices work by changing your future payoffs, and a sunk cost is, by definition, in the past. A rational person facing the same future options should make the same decision regardless of how they got there. So the people staying in the degree or the failing project are just confused about what is actually on the table now.
