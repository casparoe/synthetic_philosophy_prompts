A colleague of mine defends a strong reading of quantum indistinguishability. She says that when two electrons in a helium atom are described as indistinguishable, this is not merely a confession that we cannot tell them apart. It is, she claims, a statement about the world itself: there is no fact of the matter about which electron is which, and an electron is not an individual in the way a pebble or a planet is. I am not convinced the physics can carry that much metaphysical weight. Design the strongest test you can of her claim, using real or in-principle experiments. You might consider, for example, how the counting of possible arrangements changes when particles are treated as genuinely indistinguishable rather than merely unlabeled, or what happens when two identical photons meet at a 50:50 beamsplitter. Tell me exactly what result would favor her strong reading, what result would favor the weaker reading that the particles are individuals whose identities we simply cannot track, and then say precisely what even a decisive result would and would not settle.
