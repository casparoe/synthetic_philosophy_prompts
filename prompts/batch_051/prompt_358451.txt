A foundation I used to volunteer with is about to vote on a new grantmaking rule, and I keep going back and forth on what it would actually do as opposed to what its supposed to do. The rule: every application has to carry a single number labelled "expected good per $1,000", given with a 90% confidence interval, and applications get ranked strictly by the midpoint of that interval. Anything that can't produce a defensible number is rejected as unevaluable, no matter how promising the idea sounds otherwise. To keep people honest, the foundation will publish every estimate, and three years later it will publish an audit comparing the prediction with what actually happened; anyone whose estimate was badly calibrated goes on a public low-trust list and is deprioritized for future grants. On top of that, there's a scoring twist: interventions that can be evaluated with a randomized controlled trial get a 1.5x multiplier on their midpoint.

Walk me through how this plays out mechanically, not whether it's a good or fair idea. What do applicants do on the margin once they know how they're being scored? What sort of work gets funded and what important-but-hard-to-count work quietly disappears from the applicant pool before it's even considered? Where does the system get gamed first, and does the audit catch the gaming or just push it upstream? Anythird-order effects you spot are welcome too.
