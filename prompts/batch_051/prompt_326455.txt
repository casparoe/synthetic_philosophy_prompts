I've been working out an idea I call "resolve arbitrage," and I'd like a candid assessment of whether it's a genuine concept or just a metaphor.

The core claim is this. A lot of design is not deceptive in any ordinary sense: the costs are disclosed somewhere, no false claim is made, and the user can, in principle, decline. But some products seem to profit specifically from the predictable gap between what a person endorses when composed and what the same person will do when tired, distracted, lonely, or mid-scroll. The product does not try to change the person's standing judgment; it tries to time the decision so that the standing judgment is the one least likely to control behavior. I call it resolve arbitrage because the designer is, in effect, buying a decision at the moment when the user's resolve is cheapest and reselling it to the user's future self at a markup of regret, time, or money.

Two examples. A streaming service waits until moments after a cliffhanger and then invites you to override the screen-time limit you set for yourself. There is no concealment and no penalty for declining; the prompt simply arrives at the exact moment the offer is hardest to refuse. Or consider a subscription that can be started in three clicks but can only be canceled through a live retention call, even though the policy is fully disclosed. The company is not hiding anything; it is routing the act of leaving through a channel built to exhaust the user's resolve.

My claim is that these designs are wrong in a distinctive way. They are not wrong because they deceive, and not wrong because they force an outcome the person would never have chosen. They are wrong because their revenue model is to harvest the predictable distance between the user's first-order impulses and second-order commitments — to monetize weakness of will itself, scaled by data.

What I want is an honest verdict. Has this already been worked out under another name — is it just what people mean by dark patterns or sludge, or is it a distinct category? Is the category even coherent, or does it collapse into older ideas of exploitation, coercion, or akrasia? And where does it break? A coffee shop that places pastries by the register also profits from the gap between my plans and my impulses. Is that resolve arbitrage, and if it is, is the concept too broad to carry normative weight — or is there a principled difference I am failing to state?
