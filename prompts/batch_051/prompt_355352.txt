I recently joined the governance board of a hospital system that is deciding whether to deploy a machine-learning model to allocate scarce intensive-care resources during surge conditions. Until last winter this was an abstraction to me; then my father spent two nights in an emergency department while an earlier version of such a tool ordered patients around him, and I understood viscerally that these design choices are decisions of life and death. The committee I joined has been split for nearly a year between two camps, and I need to understand the structure of the standoff before I can vote in good conscience.

One camp argues that the system should be an expected-value maximizer: it should assign each patient a predicted probability of survival and a quality-adjusted benefit, then allocate so as to maximize aggregate expected benefit, with the trade-offs stated openly. They insist that every alternative is merely the same calculus performed in the dark — when a deontologist grants a scarce bed to the patient with the better prognosis, the deontologist is already weighing lives, only less consistently and with more room for bias. The other camp argues that the system should be built around side-constraints rather than a maximizing objective: it must not sacrifice one patient as a means to a better aggregate, must not override consent or established triage rules, and may optimize only within boundaries that respect each person's rights. They reply that the first camp is designing a machine to do what no clinician may do — deliberately let one person die because the arithmetic says two will live.

What puzzles me is that the two sides no longer contest the data, the model's calibration, or the predicted outcomes. Each can anticipate the other's replies: the maximizers say the deontologists are smuggling consequentialist reasoning in through the word "rights"; the deontologists say the maximizers are smuggling deontological boundaries in through thresholds that they insist are just "weights." Nothing has moved in months.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a first-order disagreement between two moral theories but a disagreement about what it means for an institution, rather than a human being, to "decide" — and about who is licensed to authorize trade-offs whose harms are non-repeatable and partly unknowable. I do not trust that hypothesis yet. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this dispute verbal, a hidden disagreement about risk and uncertainty, a clash of bedrock intuitions about the shape of moral reasons, or several different questions being answered under the single heading of "what should the system do"? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a formal result showing where the two views converge or cannot, a change in how the board frames its authority, or an honest acknowledgment that the deadlock should be expected to persist, and why?
