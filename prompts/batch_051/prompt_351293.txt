I write novels for a living, and that practice has slowly converted me to eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires. It has come to seem to me that the mental states I ascribe to my characters and the mental states we ascribe to one another are explanatory fictions of the same kind. Here is the argument as I would now set it down.

(1) The entire work of attributing beliefs and desires is to make behavior intelligible: we assign a person these states and then see their action as the reasonable thing for a person with those states to do.

(2) In writing fiction, I perform exactly this same operation on my characters, and it succeeds completely in explaining and predicting their behavior, even though I know there are no beliefs or desires inside the characters; there is no mental life behind the page to cause their actions.

(3) So the success of belief–desire attribution is fully compatible with the complete absence of beliefs and desires.

(4) If the success of a method of attribution is fully compatible with the complete absence of what it attributes, then that success cannot be evidence for the existence of what it attributes.

(5) The only evidence there ever was for the existence of beliefs and desires is the success of attributing them.

(6) Therefore there is no evidence that beliefs and desires exist, and we should eliminate them from our ontology.

I cannot shake the feeling that the whole argument turns on a trick in step (3) or step (4), but I cannot say exactly where. Tell me, precisely: is the argument valid? If not, which step is the first to fail, and under what reading? If it is valid but unsound, identify the false premise and what the correct version of it would be. Please answer in American English.
