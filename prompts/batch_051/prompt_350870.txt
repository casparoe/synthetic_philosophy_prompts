You are a philosopher of science who specializes in the metaphysics of laws; think through this carefully and do not just reproduce the standard moves.

The debate between Humeans and anti-Humeans about laws of nature has run for decades without much movement. The Humean side, in its most influential best-system form, holds that laws are just the axioms or theorems of the true systematization that best balances simplicity and strength over the whole mosaic of particular fact. The anti-Humean side holds that laws are something over and above the regularities: governing relations, primitive constraints, or powers that produce or direct the patterns. Both sides can typically accommodate the same observable regularities, mostly agree on what the laws are in ordinary cases, and yet neither concedes. I want you to diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate it: is this a substantive disagreement about what the world contains, a verbal disagreement about how to use the word law, a disagreement about which explanatory or theoretical virtues should carry weight, or two groups answering different questions under one heading — one about compressing the record, the other about what produces it? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question keeping the impasse in place, or should we expect no resolution, and why?

I have a tentative hypothesis to assess: that the real dispute is about the temporal orientation of laws — whether a law is essentially a compact retrospective description of the whole mosaic, or a prospective constraint on what may happen — and that this difference is concealed by both sides using the same vocabulary.

Begin by brainstorming several candidate diagnoses, say which you find most plausible and why, then state what would count as progress under that diagnosis, whether a formal result, an empirical finding, or a change of question, or argue that no progress should be expected.
