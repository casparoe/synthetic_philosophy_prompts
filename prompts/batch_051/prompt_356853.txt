In my philosophy club, two classmates argued about the Gyges story from Book II of Plato’s Republic, where a shepherd finds a ring that makes him invisible and uses it to become king. Neither disputed the example; they disagreed about what it actually shows.

Elena said the story is a fair test: if you could do whatever you wanted with no chance of being caught, you would have no self-interested reason to be just, and anyone who claims otherwise is still secretly relying on fear of punishment or concern for reputation.

Marcus replied that the story quietly leaves out the cost of becoming the sort of person who can do those things. Even if no one finds out, the shepherd has to live with himself, and a life built on constant deceit is not free or admirable, so the calculation is not so simple.

Elena answered that Marcus had only moved the calculation inward. The ring still works as a test because a rational person could accept that internal cost and still choose injustice if the payoff were large enough. So Marcus had not shown that justice is worthwhile for its own sake.

Marcus came back that the ring case treats a person as a bare chooser evaluating one act at a time. But asking whether to be just is not just asking whether a particular unjust act pays. It is asking what kind of life one should lead, and that question is not answered by weighing the benefits of an act, even with internal costs included.
