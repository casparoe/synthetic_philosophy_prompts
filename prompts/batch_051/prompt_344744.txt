I'm a novelist, and for the book I'm drafting one of my characters is a machine-learning researcher who's drifted into a quiet pessimism: he claims you can never really tell what an optimizer is trying to do, nor even what it's searching over, just from watching it. I've rewritten his argument alot and it seems to prove too much — it would make our everyday inferences about what people, bees, and chess engines are up to illegitimate — but I can't find the exit.

Here's the argument as I have it:

(1) Any behavior an optimizer produces is the joint product of two hidden things: its objective and the search space of options it can represent and consider.
(2) If you watch a finite chunk of behavior — say a robot curving around a barrier on its way to a red box — you can always explain that chunk by tweaking either hidden factor.
(3) More strongly, for any finite behavior whatsoever I can construct a new objective O* and search space S* that make the observed actions exactly optimal: O* gives top score to the precise trajectory taken and lower scores to every alternative, while S* contains only slight variants of that trajectory.
(4) So any finite stream of behavior is jointly compatible with indefinitely many different objective/search-space pairs.
(5) Since all we ever see is finite behavior, no observation can ever discriminate between a goal like reaching the red box and a gerrymandered goal like following the exact path it happened to take.
(6) Therefore behavior gives no real evidence about an optimizer's objective or search space; saying a chess engine wants to win is no better supported than saying it wants to emit the exact move sequence it emitted.
(7) But that conclusion is unlivable: we constantly and correctly infer what people, animals, and AIs are up to, and the whole project of checking whether a system is doing what we want depends on that inference being possible.

I'd rather my character be wrong in an interesting way than right in a boring one. Act as a philosopher of science who works on optimization, inverse reinforcement learning, and anthropomorphism debates, and think carefully. Before you settle on an answer, brainstorm the main places the argument could break; then say which step he should reject, what rejecting it costs, and whether the alternatives are any better.
