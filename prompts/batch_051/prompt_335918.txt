I am a junior at a public high school and serve as the student representative on a committee that is rewriting our student code of conduct. The committee includes students, teachers, and administrators, and none of us has legal training. We meet for about an hour each week, and every proposed rule must be approved by the principal and then the school board. The stakes are real: under the current code, students have been suspended under rules that different administrators interpret differently, and appeals rarely succeed because the language is so broad.

I have been reading about the doctrine that a criminal law can be struck down for vagueness. As I understand it, the underlying worry is twofold: a law is defective when a person of ordinary intelligence cannot tell what it forbids, and when it leaves too much room for arbitrary or discriminatory enforcement. I want to turn that underlying idea into something our committee can actually use when drafting and revising rules.

Please write a decision procedure for testing whether a proposed school rule is unacceptably vague. It should be a sequence of questions we can ask in order, with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than wording. Keep it usable by non-lawyers in about twenty minutes per rule, and be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on these three proposed rules:

1. A rule prohibiting conduct that disrupts the educational environment.
2. A rule prohibiting clothing or accessories that draw undue attention to the wearer.
3. A rule prohibiting remaining in a hallway, stairwell, or parking lot without a school-related purpose.

For each, say whether the procedure would flag it as vague, what specific changes would make it pass, and whether any problem with the rule is not really vagueness but something the procedure cannot fix. Try to include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer the committee is likely to resist.
