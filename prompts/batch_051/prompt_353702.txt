I'm a PhD student in cognitive science and one argument keeps gnawing at me: the claim that a materially realized, finite mind can only ever have finitely many genuinely distinct experiences, so an immortal life must eventually bottom out in repetition. The version I have in mind goes like this. A bounded brain has finitely many possible total neural or functional states — below some resolution, two states just aren't different states at all. So the space of experiences open to such a creature is itself finite. An endless life is an infinite sequence of draws from that finite set, so the number of distinct experiences it can ever contain is finite: at some point you've had the last genuinely new experience there is, and the rest is rearranging a bounded repertoire. I used to find that argument almost conclusive.

But recently I've been thinking hard about the compositional, language-like character of much of cognition. Finitely many words, infinitely many sentences; finitely many concepts, unboundedly many structured thoughts. The last few years of generative text models have made the formal point vivid too: a system with finitely many internal states — never mind whether such systems have experiences at all — can emit a never-repeating, ever-growing sequence of strings. So there seems to be a real wedge between a system's having finitely many internal states and its having finitely many experiences, at least if experiences are typed partly by the structured contents they token rather than just by the bare physical state of the vehicle. You could, in principle, keep having new thoughts forever, each one novel in content, with a strictly finite brain.

So write an essay on whether the pigeonhole exhaustibility argument survives this compositional-generative point. Pin down exactly which premise it puts pressure on and whether that premise is still defensible once you distinguish vehicle-states from contents, and state-recurrence from content-novelty. Say where you think an experience belongs in that distinction. Then say whether there is a repaired version of the argument that still goes through at some other grain — say, at the level of discriminable experiences rather than content types — or whether the worry mostly dissolves. Don't end on an "it depends"; defend a verdict.
