In preparing a lecture on how intellectual credit gets assigned, I have been struck by what looks like the same phenomenon in two rather different places. Stigler's law of eponymy says, roughly, that scientific discoveries and results are named not for their original discoverer but for a later figure—often someone more visible or better placed, as the law itself is usually taken to illustrate. Meanwhile, in the transmission of quotations and aphorisms, anonymous or obscure sayings keep migrating to famous authors: archives are full of remarks attributed to Mark Twain, Churchill, or Einstein that they never made. In both cases there is a first event (the discovery, the original utterance), a later act of naming or attribution (the eponym, the famous name), and a systematic bias toward the already salient while the actual origin is effaced. I want to know whether this is a structural identity, a loose analogy, or only a superficial resemblance. Please make the mapping between the two cases explicit, say where they come apart, identify what would have to be true of credit in science and attribution in folk transmission for mechanisms to carry over from one to the other, and give at least one prediction the analogy appears to license that is probably false. Throughout, I ask that you separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study eponymy, attribution, or cultural transmission, and that you not present a contested view as the consensus.
