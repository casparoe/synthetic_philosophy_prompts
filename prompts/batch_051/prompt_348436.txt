I've been reading Pascal's Pensées for a seminar on early modern political thought, and I keep getting stuck on his argument about justice and force. As I understand it from the section where he talks about justice and the reason of effects, he claims roughly that justice without force is powerless, and force without justice is tyrannical, so the practical solution is to make what is just strong and make what is strong just. The argument seems to assume that if justice has no force behind it, it just gets ignored, and if force has no justice or legitimacy, it eventually becomes unstable. My professor keeps applying this to the state's monopoly on violence, but I want to test it against something more modern. Consider the international human rights regime, especially in the period before the International Criminal Court had much real enforcement power, or even today when enforcement is patchy and works mostly through reputational damage, trade pressure, domestic courts applying treaty obligations, and NGO shaming rather than a central enforcer that can physically compel compliance. Is this a case where justice without force turned out to have some power after all, or does it actually confirm Pascal's point because "force" here just shows up in a more diffuse, social form? I'd like you to pull apart the premises of Pascal's argument and tell me whether it survives that development, needs repair, or should be recast in weaker terms.
