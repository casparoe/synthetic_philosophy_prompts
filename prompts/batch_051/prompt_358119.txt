You are a philosopher of science who works on explanation, modeling, and complex systems. Think through the question carefully, and begin by briefly brainstorming the considerations you will need to weigh.

I teach four criteria for distinguishing a genuine explanation from a mere simulation or prediction. A model explains only if it (a) provides a shortcut—delivering the outcome without retracing every step of the process; (b) supports counterfactual reasoning, so we can see how outcomes would change under altered conditions; (c) subsumes the case under a general pattern; and (d) yields understanding of why the outcome came about, not merely a record that it did. I tell my students that a model failing all four is a black box, however accurate it may be.

Now consider a one-dimensional cellular automaton whose update rule is three lines long but computationally irreducible: there is no general shortcut, so determining the configuration at step N requires iterating the rule through all N steps (Rule 30 and Rule 110 are standard examples). Suppose a physicist says: "The configuration after 10^15 steps is explained by the rule plus the initial condition; running the simulation merely executes the explanation." A critic replies: "That is prediction, not explanation. Until you run the very process you claim to explain, you cannot say what happens, let alone why."

Work through my four criteria one by one, saying how the physicist's proposal fares against each. Then present the strongest case for each side: that this is a genuine explanation despite computational irreducibility, and that it is not. Finally, give your own verdict on whether the proposal qualifies as an explanation, and state whether the case shows that any of the four criteria should be revised, demoted, or made more precise.
