I'm defending a paper at a workshop next month and I want a Q&A prep sheet. The paper is on lexical threshold negative utilitarianism: the view that pleasure and pain trade off in the ordinary way up to some threshold of suffering, but past that threshold, no amount of happiness or other goods can compensate, so preventing above-threshold suffering has lexical priority. My version tries to make the threshold non-arbitrary by anchoring it in the structure of experience — something like the point where the pain dominates the subject's own evaluative perspective so completely that the subject can't coherently prefer bearing it in exchange for any amount of later good. That way I'm hoping to dodge both the classic pinprick objection and the arbitrariness worry about where the line gets drawn. The audience is a general normative ethics workshop, not a suffering-focused crowd: two card-carrying utilitarians who will immediately complain that I've given up the single currency that made welfare tradeoffs tractable; a value theorist who works on incommensurability and hard choices and will press on whether I'm really defending incommensurability rather than lexical priority, and whether the view can even be represented in a real-valued utility function or forces lexicographic preferences; a Kantian-leaning political philosopher who will worry this licenses extreme interventions to prevent intense suffering at the cost of ordinary lives going well; and a formal epistemology grad student who will ask what happens near the threshold — whether a one-unit difference in pain intensity can flip something from tradeable to untradeable, and if so whether that's just a Sorites problem, and if not, how the threshold avoids being vague.

I'm planning to run the argument in three steps: threshold views are the only negative utilitarianism that survives the pinprick objection; the threshold can be anchored in the subject's own evaluative perspective rather than in a number on a pain scale; and below-threshold tradeoffs plus above-threshold lexical priority is actually more faithful to ordinary moral thought than classical utilitarianism. Give me the objections roughly in the order I'll actually get them, phrased the way these people would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
