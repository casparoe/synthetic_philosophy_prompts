I am a novelist working on a book in which a physicist becomes convinced, on the following grounds, that there can be no finite final theory of physics. I want her to be wrong, but wrong in a subtle way, so I need to know exactly where the argument fails before I put it in a character's mouth. Could you tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs?

(1) A final theory would be a finite set of fundamental laws and rules of inference from which every physical truth follows.
(2) Such a theory is a formal system.
(3) Gödel's first incompleteness theorem says that any consistent formal system strong enough to prove the basic facts of elementary arithmetic contains a true arithmetical sentence that it cannot prove.
(4) A final theory of physics must be that strong, because it has to derive quantitative measurement results, probabilities, and the ordinary arithmetic they presuppose.
(5) Therefore any final theory leaves some true arithmetical sentence unproved by its own resources.
(6) Adding that sentence as a new axiom only yields a stronger theory with a further unproved Gödel sentence, and the process never terminates; a complete theory would require infinitely many axioms.
(7) So no finite final theory is possible.

I would most like to keep (3), since it is the one piece of mathematics I am sure I have right, and I suspect the drama of the novel depends on the conclusion's being an overreach. But I cannot say exactly what the overreach is, or what a corrected understanding of a "final theory" would look like.
