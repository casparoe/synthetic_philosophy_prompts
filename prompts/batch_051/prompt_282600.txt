Okay so last night my friend and I got into a three-hour argument about whether we should deliberately slow down automation to protect jobs, and Ive been turning it over all day. He kept saying that a society that lets truck drivers and paralegals get automated away is "stealing people's work," and I kept replying that if a machine can do the thing with less human drudgery, that's just what technology is for — the work was the cost, not the good. Then he hit me with a bunch of examples I didn't have a clean answer to. Why do politicians brag about the number of jobs a project "creates" even when it looks like make-work, like building a bridge nobody asked for? Why did the same governments that spent decades subsidizing automation also run public jobs programs to put people back to work? And why did we all spend 2020 calling grocery store clerks and delivery drivers "essential workers" while paying them like they were replaceable?

The thing I actually want to ask is about a concrete practice. Imagine a government job guarantee: the state promises that anyone who wants one gets a paid job doing things like clearing invasive weeds, digitizing old records, or staffing community centers — the tasks are real enough, but the jobs are in some sense invented by the policy itself. What theory of the value of work does a practice like that presuppose? Does it presuppose that paid employment is the main way people get dignity, structure, or social recognition, so the state should be creating tasks rather than just sending checks? Or could you defend it on purely instrumental grounds — keeping people's skills warm, avoiding the stigma of being out of work — without claiming the activity is good in itself?

And here's where I got stuck: is a job guarantee consistent with also cheering on automation that eliminates jobs? If it is, can you say what seperates "real" work from the made-up work a guarantee creates, without smuggling back in the idea that some work is intrinsically valuable? My friend thinks you can't; I think you probably can but I couldn't say how. And if you do conclude that the guarantee presupposes work is good in itself, I'd want to know what a policy built on the opposite assumption — that jobs are just a cost and income and meaning should be fully decoupled — would have to change in practice.
