I've got a colleague who keeps running this argument and I can't tell if he's actually proved something or if I'm just annoyed by the conclusion. Walk me through it step by step and tell me whether the argument is valid, and if not, exactly which step fails.

(1) A three-person search committe has to decide whether to recommend a job candidate. The university's rule says recommend just in case the candidate is productive (P) and collegial (Q).

(2) Each committee member has perfectly consistent judgments about P, Q, and the conjunction P&Q.

(3) The committee uses the apparently neutral procedure of deciding each proposition by majority vote.

(4) The votes go like this. Member A accepts P and accepts Q. Member B accepts P but rejects Q. Member C rejects P but accepts Q.

(5) So by majority, the committee accepts P (A and B) and accepts Q (A and C). But it rejects P&Q, since only A accepts the conjunction while B and C reject it.

(6) The committee therefore holds the inconsistent triad P, Q, and not-(P&Q), even though the university's rule says a candidate satisfying P and Q must be recommended.

(7) No individual member's judgments are inconsistent. So the inconsistency must have been introduced by the majority-rule procedure itself.

(8) Therefore majority rule doesn't preserve logical consistency, and any body that decides each issue seperately by majority vote is committed to a contradiction.

I'm inclined to say the argument is valid and the conclusion is just uncomfortable. But maybe (8) overreaches, or maybe (5) is sneaky in how it moves from individual votes to what the committee as a body rejects. Where should someone dig in? If the argument does go through, is the fix to vote directly on the overall recommendation instead — and what, if anything, would that give up? Be specific.
