I keep going back and forth on a parallel and want a second opinion. There's a known impossibility result in algorithmic fairness that goes roughly like this: a single risk score can't satisfy all three of the following at once — (1) calibration within each group, so a score of .8 means an 80% chance among the people in that group who got .8, (2) equal false-positive rates across groups, and (3) equal false-negative rates across groups. You only get all three in degenerate cases: the base rates are the same, or the classifier is a perfect oracle. So the trilemma is pretty robust.

Here's what's nagging me. In antidiscrimination law there's the old tension between formal equality (same rule, same interpretation, applied the same way to everyone) and substantive equality (the rule's actual burdens and benefits shouldn't fall unevenly on different groups). Calibration looks like formal equality turned into math: the score has the same meaning in every group, with no group-specific correction. Equal error rates looks like one natural formalization of substantive equality: the mistakes and harms the system produces are spread evenly across groups. And then the theorem says you can't have both calibration and equalized error rates across groups unless the groups are already the same, which sounds like a very precise version of the claim that formal and substantive equality come apart, and that getting both requires the world to cooperate.

So am I just pattern-matching, or is this a real structural identity? If it's real, does anything transfer back to the legal direction — e.g., does the theorem predict that a disparate-impact-style fix will usually force you to de-calibrate, and is that what group-specific standards in law actually amount to? If it's superficial, where exactly does it break?
