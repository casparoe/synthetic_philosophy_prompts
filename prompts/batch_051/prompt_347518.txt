I'm a grad student in philosophy giving a talk to my departments political economy workshop next month, and I want to defend a Hayekian take on the socialist calculation debate. The line I'm selling is roughly this: the standard version of the debate has Hayek adding an epistemic footnote to Mises's impossibility claim, and then Lange "solving" the problem with trial-and-error pricing. I want to argue the real Hayekian point cuts deeper than that: the knowledge that market prices make usable is not the sort of thing that could be gathered up and fed into Lange's central planning board even in principle, because a lot of it is local, contextual, and not articulable as the kind of numbers a planner can process. Not a computational point, an epistemic one. The audience is mostly economists who were trained to think Lange won on the technical question — the workshop includes a couple general-equilibrium / mechanism-design people, one person in market design, and two political philosophers who are sympathetic to market socialism. I'm not worried they don't know Hayek; I'm worried they know him to well in three-line summary form.

I plan to open with this passage from Hayek's "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (American Economic Review, 1945) and make the whole paper turn on it:

"In ordinary language we describe by the word “planning” the complex of interrelated decisions about the allocation of our available resources. All economic activity is in this sense planning; and in any society in which many people collaborate, this planning, whoever does it, will in some measure have to be based on knowledge which, in the first instance, is not given to the planner but to somebody else, which somehow will have to be conveyed to the planner. The various ways in which the knowledge on which people base their plans is communicated to them is the crucial problem for any theory explaining the economic process, and the problem of what is the best way of utilizing knowledge initially dispersed among all the people is at least one of the main problems of economic policy—or of designing an efficient economic system."

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Give me the six or seven objections this specific room will actually raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way an economist or a market socialist would put it, with a two-to-three minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. I especially expect someone to say the passage just asks how dispersed knowledge is conveyed to planners and then ignores that Lange answered exactly that question with trial-and-error prices. Prepare me for that one, for the mechanism-design version of it, and for the "but modern algorithmic pricing / Walmart / Amazon?" version. Also tell me what I should concede up front, and what the one objection I should be praying nobody asks is.
