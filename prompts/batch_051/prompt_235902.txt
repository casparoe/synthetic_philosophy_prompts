I've been poking at vagueness for a column on why we can't find the exact moment a sand pile becomes a heap, and I keep getting tangled in a little argument that's supposed to show supervaluationism secretly agrees with its rival. Quick backdrop: the epistemicist says 'heap' really does have a sharp cutoff somewhere in the series from one grain to a million, we just can't know where. The supervaluationist is the one who's supposed to deny that there's any sharp boundary, by saying a sentence about heaps is true only if it's true on every acceptable sharpening of the term (the precisifications). So far so good.

But then run this:

(1) On any single precisification, 'heap' gets fully sharpened, so there's an exact last grain that counts as a heap before the series flips to non-heap.
(2) So the sentence 'there is a sharp boundary between heaps and non-heaps' is true on every precisification.
(3) By the supervaluationist's own rule, a sentence is true simpliciter just in case it's true on every precisification.
(4) So supervaluationism says that the sentence 'there is a sharp boundary between heaps and non-heaps' is true.
(5) But that sounds like exactly what the epistemicist says — that there's a sharp boundary, only with an extra layer of hedging about not being able to say where.
(6) Worse, pick any particular grain n. The claim 'the boundary is at n' is true on one precisification and false on the rest, so by the supervaluationist's lights it comes out neither true nor false.
(7) And a general 'there is' claim can't be true while each of its specific instances is neither true nor false.
(8) So either supervaluationism collapses into epistemicism, or it's flatly inconsistent.

I'd bet the supervaluationist has a move, but I can't tell wich step it should be. My gut says step (7) is humming a classical-logic tune the supervaluationist has quietly changed the words to. Is that right, and is that the same move that keeps them distinct from the epistemicist?
