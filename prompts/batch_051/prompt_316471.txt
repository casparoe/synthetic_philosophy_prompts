For an upper-level philosophy seminar on coordination problems and the governance of frontier artificial intelligence, with sixteen students split between philosophical and technical backgrounds, design a ninety-minute activity that makes participants feel the force of a multipolar trap before it is named. The intended takeaway is not the vocabulary but the experience: that a set of individually rational choices can be collectively self-defeating, that this can happen even when everyone knows the structure, and that non-binding declarations of good intent do not by themselves change the incentives. The centerpiece should be a repeated, scored game in which each student or small team runs a frontier AI lab and must decide each round how to divide a fixed compute budget between safety and capability. Structure the payoffs so that capability spending is privately dominant in the single round—it raises the lab's score while its individual contribution to shared catastrophe risk is too small to make unilateral safety worthwhile in expectation—yet make the stochastic mechanism steep enough that when most labs choose capability in most rounds, the probability that all scores are wiped out rises toward near certainty and the room can watch the trap tighten. Include at least two variants: a baseline in which choices are made privately, and a variant in which each lab makes a non-binding public declaration of its intended safety spend before choosing privately, where the declaration has no effect on payoffs. Add one mid-game maneuver, such as an optional safety consortium or a costly merger, priced so that it could in principle escape the trap but only if enough players accept a private cost. Provide the complete setup and framing, the exact payoff tables, the chance mechanism using dice or cards, the round structure, and a timeline from briefing to cleanup. In the debrief, map concrete moments in the play onto the parts of the concept—individual dominance, collective ruin, the failure of cheap talk, and the fragility of voluntary restraint—and say what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run and how the facilitator should handle it. Conclude with an honest note on which feature of an actual AI race the game cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must therefore supply afterward.
