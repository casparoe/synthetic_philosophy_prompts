In the Philosophy of Right, Hegel makes a claim that his later readers have often found troubling: not merely that war is an unavoidable feature of the relations among sovereign states, but that war performs a positive ethical function for a state. On his account, war interrupts the settling of citizens into the private pursuit of comfort and property, calls them back to the universal ends of the political community, and keeps the ethical life of a people from stagnating. This argument seems to presuppose that war is a limited and survivable event — that the community whose ethical health war is supposed to preserve can in fact survive the wars it fights.

A specific later development appears to undercut that presupposition. By the middle of the twentieth century, war between major powers can no longer be treated as a limited contest for particular ends. Nuclear weapons make a major war capable of destroying the societies whose ethical life it was supposed to renew, and industrial total war mobilizes whole populations and economies in ways that look much closer to exhaustion and trauma than to civic renewal. The mechanism Hegel relied on looks as if it has become self-negating.
