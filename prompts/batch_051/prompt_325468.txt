In my linguistics elective we were talking about homophones, and after class I went down a rabbit hole and came up with something I'm tentatively calling the ambiguity budget of a language. The basic idea is that a language only has so many sound-shapes (syllables, mostly), and each sound-shape can only hold so many meanings before actual conversation starts to wobble. So every language has to pay for its homonyms somehow, and different languages pay in different coin. Mandarin has a tiny syllable inventory (before tone there are only about 400 syllables, I think), so it pays with lexical tones and by gluing syllables into compound words, which basically mints new sound-shapes. English has a much bigger syllable inventory — we can do "splints," "thwarts," even "sixths" — so it can afford to let one form like "set" or "run" carry a big stack of meanings and people mostly keep them straight from context. My prediction is that the tighter the syllable inventory, the more a language needs tone, compounds, or something like case endings to stay under budget; a language that doesn't pay one of those ways should end up with measurably more misunderstandings or slower conversations.

I know homonymy and polysemy are ordinary vocabulary facts, but I havent seen them treated as a genuine limited resource like this. I'm also a little worried I'm confusing "why Mandarin has tones" with "why Mandarin has compounds" — maybe the tones came first and the homophony is just what happens when sound changes eat distinct syllables, and then compounding got necessary later. What I want an honest take on: is the ambiguity budget a real thing or just a metaphor with numbers? Has someone already worked it out under another name? And where does it break — are there languages with tiny sound systems, no tones, no productive compounding, and plenty of homonyms that still work fine? Also, is it even right to treat ambiguity as a cost at all, or do people like exploiting double meanings too much for it to be one?
