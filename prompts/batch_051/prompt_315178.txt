I spent the better part of four decades as a physicist, working on problems where the line between deterministic and stochastic dynamics was a practical modeling decision rather than a metaphysical one, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mind and action with the enthusiasm of a late convert. This semester I have been leading a seminar on free will for upper-level undergraduates with backgrounds in the natural sciences, and I have been asked to draft the final examination. The students know the standard taxonomy—compatibilism, hard determinism, libertarianism—but several of them keep insisting that quantum mechanics has already settled the debate, since indeterminism at the microphysical level removes the worry that our actions were fixed in advance. I find that move simultaneously appealing and confused, and I would like an exam question that refuses to let them coast on it.

Please draft one essay question for a 90-minute in-class final, worth 20 points, together with a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme. The question should force students to spell out exactly what quantum indeterminism would have to contribute to a defensible account of free will, and to assess whether microphysical randomness can supply the kind of control or authorship that moral responsibility seems to require. It should not be answerable by reciting the standard positions, and it should push students toward the problem of luck: if a neural event is genuinely undetermined, it is unclear why the outcome belongs to the agent any more than a determined one would. In the marking scheme, please identify five must-hit moves, allocate the 20 points among them, and for each move include one line on the misconception a student typically reveals by missing it—for example, treating the absence of determinism as equivalent to the presence of agency.
