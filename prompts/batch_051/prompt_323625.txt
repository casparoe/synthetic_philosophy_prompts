I teach a graduate seminar in decision theory. In a recent session on the coherence arguments for expected utility maximisation, we discussed the claim that an agent whose preferences violate the axioms is open to a money pump: a series of individually acceptable exchanges that leaves her worse off by her own lights. After the seminar, two of my doctoral students stayed behind arguing about how far such arguments really reach. I promised to think about it. I have, and I still cannot confidently assign each sub-claim to one side, so I should like a careful adjudication. The exchange, compressed from my notes, went approximately as follows.

Eleanor: Consider Aisha, who has a strict cycle of pairwise preferences over three prospects: A over B, B over C, and C over A. She is also disposed to pay up to one penny to exchange any prospect for one she strictly prefers. A broker starts her with C. He offers to exchange C for B at a cost of one penny; she accepts, because B beats C. He then offers B for A at one penny; she accepts. He then offers A for C at one penny; she accepts. She ends the afternoon exactly where she began, holding C, but three pence poorer, and there is no single exchange from her starting position to C-minus-three-pence that she would ever have accepted. A sequence of choices, each favoured by her own pairwise ranking, has produced an outcome she disprefers to where she started. That is a coherence failure, and it is exactly the kind of failure expected utility theory forestalls by requiring a single transitive ranking behind choice.

Rebecca: That shows only that a myopic Aisha can be exploited. A far-sighted Aisha with the same pairwise tastes would see from the start that accepting the first offer leads to C minus three pence, and would simply decline the first swap. The broker never gets his sequence running. The engine of the loss is not the intransitivity but short-sightedness: change the degree of foresight and the pump collapses.

Eleanor: But consider what the far-sighted Aisha declines. At the first step she holds C and is offered B for one penny; in declining, she acts as though she prefers C to B, even though she continues to avow the opposite. You have not removed the incoherence; you have relocated it. The myopic agent contradicts herself across time, but the far-sighted agent contradicts herself at the moment of refusal.

Rebecca: That is too quick. Aisha does not face a bare pairwise choice between C and B; she faces a choice between two plans — hold C throughout, or make the swaps and end with C minus three pence. She may order plans in a perfectly transitive way even while her pairwise ranking of the bare prospects cycles. When she declines the first offer, the object of her choice is not 'B versus C' but 'which sequence of choices to execute'. Plan preferences, not prospect preferences, are the right basis for sequential choice.

Eleanor: Then you have conceded that in every temporally extended context the cyclic prospect rankings are idle. Whenever a sequence is visible, she guides herself entirely by her transitive preferences over plans, so no sequence of her actions ever manifests the cycle. She is behaviourally indistinguishable from an agent whose prospect preferences were transitive all along. A preference relation that can survive only by never being acted upon is not much of a preference relation; insisting on it is a roundabout admission that transitivity is a condition on any ranking that is to guide conduct.

Rebecca: That confuses observability with coherence. There are plenty of one-off pairwise choices — two flats, two jobs, two conferences — in which no sequence is anticipated and the cyclic ranking does guide choice: Aisha happily pays the penny and takes B over C. In those local choices nothing goes wrong from her own point of view, and a theory that condemned her would be censuring choices that never lead to any dispreferred result. Her preferences are not a single all-things-considered ranking; they are choices from pairs, and choices from pairs can cycle without contradicting anything.

Eleanor: Unless the choice is made from the full set rather than from a pair. Suppose Aisha must simply choose one of the three prospects, with no sequence and no broker. Her cycle entails that whichever option she picks, there is another she strictly prefers, so every choice she could make is one she disprefers to something she rejected. There is no best element — nothing that makes her selection maximal. If rational choice requires only that one not choose an option worse than a rejected alternative, she fails that requirement directly. The broker was only a vivid way of dramatising that point.

Rebecca: But nothing in rationality requires every finite option set to contain a most-preferred item. A forced choice among several prospects can simply be a hard choice, and an agent may then select for non-comparative reasons — a habit, a default, a rule of thumb. The absence of a best element therefore does not convict her. What the money-pump argument needs is the further premise that a rational chooser must always be maximising relative to one fixed ranking. That premise is doing all the work, and in an argument about coherence it cannot simply be asserted.

They could not agree, and both are convinced the other has conceded the decisive step somewhere. Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: who is right about whether the money pump traces Aisha's loss to the cycle itself rather than to myopia; who is right about whether the far-sighted Aisha's refusal reimports the incoherence; who is right about the role of plan preferences; who is right about whether the cycle is behaviourally idle; and who is right about whether a finite choice set must contain a best element. Say whether there is anything important both parties are missing, and then say who is, overall, more nearly right.
