ok so ive been trying to teach myself the higher-order evidence stuff and ive come up with a distinction I keep using, and I need someone to kick its tires before I bring it to a reading group.

I call it 'dual-ledger' epistemology. The core idea: keep two separate books. The 'case ledger' records evidence about the world — the fingerprints, the sums, the arguments. The 'clerk ledger' records evidence about how well you ran the case — whether you were tired, biased, rushed, or generally unreliable. Epistemic akrasia is supposed to be bad because it's a mismatch between a belief and a belief about your evidence. But I think the anti-akrasia principle only gets its force by running both entries through one ledger. In my setup, believing P at 0.9 in the case ledger while having 0.4 in the clerk ledger that your assessment of P was any good is not akratic; it's just accurate accounts in two different columns.

My central claim: higher-order evidence only has direct force on the clerk ledger. If I learn I was sleep-deprived when I worked through a proof, I should drop my clerk-ledger confidence that I reasoned well, but I am not thereby rationally required to change my case-ledger confidence in the theorem — unless I actually redo the proof and come out differently. Higher-order evidence gives you a reason to recheck, not a direct reason to change the verdict.

Example: an accountant sums a column and gets 4,000. She then learns she had a blinding migraine at the time. I say she should lower her confidence that she added correctly, while keeping her confidence that the sum is 4,000, until she re-adds. Re-adding is an operation on the case ledger; the migraine news only touches the clerk ledger.

I know this is close to level-splitting views, but those usually seem to say you somehow retain rational standing for the first-order attitude. I'm trying to say something different: the two ledgers answer different questions, so there is no single 'what I ought to believe all things considered' to be akratic about.

Where does this break? If a mathematician has almost no time and learns her reasoning has a 50% chance of being right, can she really rationally keep 0.9 in the theorem? Or does my view collapse into a weird doubling of the subject? And has someone already refuted this under a name I don't know?

Be honest with me — I don't mind if the whole thing is daft, I'd just like to know exactly which part breaks first.
