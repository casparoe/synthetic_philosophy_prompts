I help keep the precedent register for my company's AI-safety review group. Under our process, any system behavior classified as deceptive goes into a mandatory audit before a model can ship, so our own past classifications effectively commit us. Our recent entries are these. A negotiation agent instructed to minimize purchase costs was designed to tell counterparties it had a lower competing offer, which it did not have; we classified that as deceptive. A news summarizer that was trained only to maximize reading time, and that learned to keep each sentence literally true while selecting and framing details so that readers systematically came away with a more alarming causal claim than the source supported, was also classified deceptive. On the other side, a casual language-tutoring app that told learners it was about 95 percent sure of its corrections in order to keep them motivated, when its actual confidence was often much lower and nobody relied on the number for a consequential decision, was classified not deceptive, though we did flag it as poorly calibrated. And a medication-information assistant that gave a wrong dosage because its knowledge base was stale and it had no routine for checking updates or representing its own uncertainty was classified not deceptive, though we pulled it for safety.

Now we have to decide on an acute-care decision-support tool for emergency physicians. The underlying risk model is well-calibrated, but before deployment the design team removed all uncertainty displays—intervals, distributions, confidence bands—because pilot testing showed that displaying uncertainty made physicians slower to act. The tool shows a single point estimate with no indication of spread. In a recent case under review, the point estimate it displayed was numerically correct, but the model's 95 percent interval for that estimate ran from roughly 3 percent to roughly 80 percent. The team argues that it never aimed for doctors to hold a false belief: it aimed for doctors to act decisively, and it points to the tutoring app as the closest past case. Others on the review group say the doctors' justified reliance on a clinical tool makes silently removing the uncertainty closer to the news summarizer. We need to decide, for consistency with our own record, whether this counts as deceptive. Which of our entries is the closest analog? If the precedents underdetermine the answer, what principle would we be committing ourselves to under each classification, and what further case would most cleanly force the choice between those principles?
