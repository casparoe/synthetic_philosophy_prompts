After a working life in the natural sciences I have come to philosophy late, and I find myself treating my immediate judgments about particular cases as data that a principle must fit. The cases that currently worry me concern what writers and filmmakers owe to real people who are dead. I have set down my verdicts; they refuse to add up.

Consider:

1. A historical novel takes a minor Roman senator, attested in a single inscription, and gives him a part in a plot against the emperor. No evidence survives either way. I think this is fine; he is nearly a placeholder, and filling him in costs no one anything.

2. A prestige biopic released thirty years after her death invents a scene in which a revered former prime minister leaked a state secret to a lover. I think this is not fine. It fastens a culpable act onto a person whose recorded life says otherwise, and her children are still alive.

3. A broad comedy depicts a real nineteenth-century naturalist as a pompous buffoon. I think this is fine, or close to it, because the form itself announces that we are watching caricature and not the man.

4. A serious drama about an enslaved woman who escaped to freedom invents a deathbed scene in which she forgives the man who enslaved her. I think this is not fine, even though I can point to no living person who is harmed. It recruits a real life into a tidy redemptive arc and speaks over her.

5. A film about a real perpetrator of a twentieth-century genocide, made while survivors are still alive, invents a subplot in which he quietly tried to halt the killings. I think this is not fine. It launders a real wrongdoer's memory and rewrites a history that still belongs to the living community he wronged.

6. A literary novel gives a real diarist who died two centuries ago a fictitious childhood trauma, in order to explain her writing. I think this is not fine, though she is long dead. It feels reductive and intrusive about someone whose actual inner life she recorded and we can still read.

7. A stage play reconstructs a final conversation between two real friends dead two hundred years, in which one confesses an envy the other never suspected. I think this is fine. It is confined, plausible as a filling-in of an unrecorded moment, and does not pretend to be the record.

8. A true-crime series stages the last minutes of a recent murder victim, with invented dialogue in which she calls for her mother. Her family says the dialogue is fabricated and asks that it be removed. I think the request should be honored.

Is there one principle, or a small set of competing principles, that captures these verdicts? Please state the principle precisely, show which cases force each of its clauses, and say honestly which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to abandon. Then apply the principle to a case I remain uncertain about: a biopic that gives a recently dead writer a mild, undocumented drug habit in order to explain her productivity.

If the question as posed rests on a mistake—for instance, because the dead cannot be wronged and the whole matter reduces to duties toward the living, or because no non-ad-hoc principle could track these verdicts—please say so plainly and tell me what I should ask instead, rather than straining to fit a principle to my list.
