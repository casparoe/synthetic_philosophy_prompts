I run a two-hour workshop for about 24 undergraduates, mostly philosophy and computer science majors, on the idea that an agent's final goals and its capability are independent of one another, while agents with wildly different final goals nonetheless tend to converge on the same intermediate objectives. Most of the students have not met the terms orthogonality thesis or instrumental convergence, and I want them to meet the phenomenon before they meet the vocabulary. Design a classroom game, playable with printed cards, tokens, and a whiteboard, in which each team of three receives a secret and deliberately arbitrary terminal goal — paperclip-like goals such as producing the most origami objects, keeping the table clear of tokens, or generating as many rhyming couplets as possible — and then plays a sequence of timed rounds in a shared environment with scarce resources. The structure should be arranged so that, before any debrief, teams find themselves taking strikingly similar instrumental actions despite their divergent goals: acquiring generic resources, protecting their continued participation, forming contracts, and investing in general-purpose capabilities. A key feature of the game should be that observing another team's behavior during play is only weakly informative about its goal: two teams with opposite goals should sometimes be behaviorally indistinguishable, and two teams with identical goals should sometimes diverge in behavior. The only public information during play should be moves, not scores or goals; scores are tallied only at the end.

In your answer, give me the exact rules, the round structure, and a minute-by-minute plan for the 120 minutes, assuming 10 minutes for setup and an 80-minute game; the full set of secret goal cards and your reasons for choosing them; the action menu and a payoff scheme that needs no real money; an elimination or deactivation mechanism that makes continued play itself a costly subgoal; and at least one action whose rationale is purely instrumental. Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the orthogonality thesis, instrumental convergence, and the distinction between terminal and instrumental goals, without me having to introduce those terms before the game is over. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure mode. Finally, be frank about which feature of the real idea this game cannot reproduce — such as the sense in which the thesis concerns possible minds and capability levels far beyond the toy case — and where I will have to supply that point myself.
