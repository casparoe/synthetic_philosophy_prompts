I am a structural engineer with no academic background in history or philosophy. Most of my work is assessing existing buildings: I decide whether a member that fails today's code should nonetheless be accepted because it satisfied the code in force when it was designed; when the fact that something was built to the standard of its day is a reasonable defence and when it is merely a description dressed up as a justification; and which later knowledge ought to be applied retroactively. For a long time I have been trying, on my own, to work out whether the habits of judgment I use on buildings carry over to a question outside my job: how we should judge historical figures such as Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus, and whether calling someone a man of his time is a sound argument or an evasion.

I would like you to restate that debate—whether it is fair to judge historical figures by present-day moral standards, and what the man-of-his-time defence is really claiming—in the vocabulary I actually use as an engineer. Where they genuinely apply, use: design codes, the standard of care, loads and safety factors, code compliance at the time of construction, grandfathering, change of use, retrofit triggers, latent defects, tolerable risk, and the distinction between prescriptive rules and performance-based standards. I am fluent in this vocabulary, so do not explain the terms; translate the moral debate into them as faithfully as possible, and do not smuggle engineering assumptions into the moral debate and call that a translation.

Then give me a residue report. Which parts of the historical-judgment debate translate cleanly? Which translate only with distortion, and what gets bent? Which parts have no counterpart at all in how I assess buildings—for instance, personal blameworthiness, the difference between explaining how a wrong arose and excusing the person who committed it, the fact that some of the relevant wrongs were contested by the person's own contemporaries, and the thought that a standard can be available even when it is not yet dominant? Treat these failures of translation as the main thing I want to understand, not as loose ends to tidy away.

Finally, tell me what you think the residue shows: that my engineering vocabulary is missing something important, or that the man-of-his-time defence is less clear than it appears? My working instinct is that I can say without contradiction that an old building is unsafe by today's knowledge without blaming the long-dead engineer, and that something similar should be available for historical figures. Is that coherent, or does the analogy make the moral problem look tidier than it is?
