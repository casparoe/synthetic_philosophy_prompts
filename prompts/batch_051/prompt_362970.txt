I want to test a structural parallel I have been turning over, and I cannot decide whether it is deep or whether I am doing nothing more than lining two diagrams up next to each other.

Start with vagueness. Epistemicism about vague predicates holds that a predicate like "heap" has a sharp cutoff — there is a particular n such that n grains make a heap and n−1 do not — and that our inability to locate that cutoff is an epistemic limitation, not a looseness in the predicate's meaning. Supervaluationism denies this: the predicate is semantically indecisive across a range of admissible sharpenings, and a borderline sentence is true only when true on all sharpenings, false only when false on all sharpenings, and otherwise neither true nor false. On one view, a borderline case is secretly settled; on the other, there is no fact of the matter until the predicate is sharpened.

Now take the status of the continuum hypothesis. CH says that every infinite subset of the real numbers is either countable or the same size as the reals: there is no cardinality strictly between the naturals and the continuum. The standard axioms of set theory neither prove nor refute CH. One philosophical response is that there is nonetheless a unique universe of sets and CH has a determinate truth value in it; the axioms are just incomplete, so the independence is a fact about our knowledge or our proof system. A second response is that there is no single intended universe of sets: there are many equally legitimate set-theoretic worlds, CH holds in some and fails in others, and absent a specification of which universe is meant, a sentence like CH is not something with an absolute truth value. Truth about sets is relative to a model.

The parallel I see is this: epistemicism is to supervaluationism as the unique-universe view is to the multiverse view. The cutoff plays the role of the intended model; underdetermination by use plays the role of underdetermination by axioms; and "true on some precisification but false on another" plays the role of "true in some universe but false in another."

What I want to know is whether the analogy survives pressure. First, is the supervaluationist's "neither true nor false" the same kind of status as the multiverse theorist's "no absolute truth value," or do the two positions merely look alike from a distance? Second, if the structure is genuinely shared, what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer — and what does the parallel predict that is false? One point that worries me is that admissible precisifications are meant to be tightly constrained by the actual meaning and use of a natural-language predicate, whereas models of set theory are constrained only by axioms and mathematical practice; I cannot tell whether that is a difference of degree within one phenomenon or a difference in kind that breaks the mapping. A second point: supervaluationists face pressure toward higher-order vagueness in the phrase "admissible sharpening." If the parallel is real, should the multiverse theorist face an analogous pressure toward a hierarchy in the phrase "legitimate universe of sets"? And does the way each side responds to that pressure show the parallel to be deep, or show where it comes apart?
