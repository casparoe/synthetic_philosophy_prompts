I'm a novelist, so I spend my working life making strangers cry over people who don't exist. My editor and I fell into an argument about what's actually happening when a reader sobs at a death scene, and she left me with this chain, and now I can't find the step out of it.

(1) A genuine emotion directed at a person needs the belief that the person exists. You can't genuinely pity a child you belive never existed.
(2) Sensible readers don't believe the people in a novel exist.
(3) So sensible readers never genuinely pity or fear fictional characters.
(4) But sensible readers plainly sob, flinch, and pace the room over fictional characters, and they'd swear blind the grief was the real thing—I bank on that for a living.
(5) The only way to square (3) and (4) is to say those apparent emotions are not genuine: they're make-believe, quasi-emotions.
(6) Now run the same thought on a parent who, while their child is safely asleep upstairs, imagines in full detail the child being hit by a car. The parent doesn't believe the child-as-imagined exists, so by (1) their terror can't be genuine either.
(7) But that terror is obviously genuine—I've felt it myself.
(8) So (1) must be false, and the whole paradox of fiction was built on a bad premise.

Where does this go wrong? Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (7) than lose (1), but I'll pay what I have to.

Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion: the candidate places the chain could break, what each move costs, and which one you'd take.
