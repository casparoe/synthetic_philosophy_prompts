I am a newly appointed judge on a state appellate court, and a recurring question before us is when a penalty may be imposed without any proof of fault—without mens rea. I want to decide an upcoming case in a way that is consistent with our own track record. The decisions I think matter are these.

First, we upheld a $1,200 fine against a licensed commercial dairy that shipped milk testing slightly above the bacteria limit. The dairy had followed every required sanitation procedure and its own tests had come back clean, so no negligence was proven. We said that a food-safety license is held on condition that the licensee guarantees the product, and that a small fine needs no showing of fault.

Second, we upheld a $750 fine against a licensed pharmacy after a technician misfilled a routine prescription. The supervising pharmacist was not on the premises, and no one showed the pharmacy's standing procedures were below the standard of care. We stressed the pharmacy's special duty to public health and the modest size of the penalty.

Third, we reversed a six-month custodial sentence for a man convicted of possessing a used car with an altered vehicle identification number. The alteration had been concealed by a prior owner, and there was no evidence the buyer could have discovered it; the offense had no knowledge element. We held that six months in custody cannot be imposed without proof of at least negligence.

Fourth, we reversed a five-year ban on keeping animals and the $12,000 penalty attached to it, imposed on a woman whose foster dog escaped through a hidden gap in a fence and injured a pedestrian. She had had the fence inspected days earlier, and no negligence was found. We held that a measure taking away an ordinary lawful activity, together with a heavy fine, requires culpability.

Fifth, we upheld a $500 fine against a licensed plumber whose soldered joint later failed because a fitting contained an internal crack that inspection could not have detected. The work had passed the required inspection. We reasoned that he held a license in a trade affecting public safety, and that a modest fine was a fair condition of practicing that trade.

The case I need to decide now is this. A licensed master electrician installed a service panel using a circuit breaker from a well-known manufacturer. The breaker later failed because of an internal defect that was undetectable by any inspection then available; the installation itself had passed inspection, and the failure caused a fire. Our state's electrical-safety statute imposes strict liability on licensed electricians for defective installations, with no proof of negligence required. The licensing authority has revoked her license and imposed a fine of $15,000, which is substantial but not by itself enough to put her one-person business under. She had no reason to suspect the breaker.

I am asking about consistency with our own decisions rather than about the best substantive rule in the abstract. If I want to be consistent with those decisions, should I vote to uphold the revocation, uphold the fine, uphold both, or uphold neither? Please lay out the candidate rules that actually fit the record above, say which rule is the best fit and why, and identify which of our earlier cases is the closest analog. If the record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say plainly what principle we would be adopting with each option. I would also like you to say how confident you are in your recommendation and to single out the point you are least sure of.
