I was talking to a friend who's just read a popular book on moral psychology, and he gave me his version of the "situationist" critique of virtue ethics. He put it like this: "Virtue ethics rests on the empirical claim that people have stable character traits that show up across situations. But the psychology says otherwise. Darley and Batson's Good Samaritan study found that whether seminarians stopped to help someone in need was mostly down to whether they were in a hurry, not how compassionate they were. Milgram and the Stanford Prison Experiment make the same point more dramatically: put ordinary people in the right situation and they'll do things that look nothing like the character they seemed to have. So there basically are no global character traits. What we call honesty or compassion is just a collection of local habits, or the situation doing the work while we tell flattering stories about character afterwards. And that means virtue ethics, which is all about cultivating exactly those traits, is empirically disconfirmed, or at best a comforting fiction."

I came away feeling this was too quick, but I cant put my finger on exactly where it goes wrong. I'd like an accuracy audit of his summary: which parts would a careful situationist actually stand behind, which parts are misrepresentations or overstatements, what a virtue ethicist would say in reply, and whether the conclusion really follows from the accurate parts. I'd also like you to separate, within the answer, what is settled among people working on this from what is genuinely contested, and not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
