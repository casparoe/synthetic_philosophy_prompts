I write a fortnightly science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and my current piece is about how languages put up with rampant homonymy. My line is that ambiguity isnt a bug the system keeps failing to fix: it falls out of using a finite stock of arbitrary signs plus inference, and speakers disambiguate the same string using context constantly, cheaply, without noticing. I want to open the piece with a short poem that makes a reader experience that process instead of reading a lecture about it.

Could you write me a villanelle where both refrain lines are built on phrases that can be read as different homonyms — same wording, unrelated senses — and where each recurrence is steered toward a different reading by the stanza around it? The form should carry the argument: the wording never changes but the sense keeps shifting with context, and the reader never grinds to a halt. 'Pitch' is the obvious candidate (throw, tar, musical note, football pitch, sales pitch) and 'tone' (sound quality, muscle tone, social tone) would do it too, but I don't want the poem just ticking through dictionary entries mechanically; the shifts should be earned by the syntax and imagery around them, the way they are in ordinary speech. It should be a proper poem, not a labelled diagram — a general reader should feel the repetitions working, and a linguist should think the picture of disambiguation is broadly right.

Keep it tight: no preface or commentary, just a title and the poem, max 19 lines. Leave the glossing to me; the poem just needs to be the demonstration.
