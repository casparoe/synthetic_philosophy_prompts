So last night a friend and I got into it about cognitive penetration, and by 2am he'd argued himself into a position that seems to prove perception never justifies any belief, even though he agrees that conclusion is crazy. He wrote the steps out on a napkin and I've been turning it over all day. It goes:

(1) For a perceptual experience to justify a belief, the experience has to be independent of that belief. If it isn't, the belief is just feeding itself through the experience, which is circular.

(2) Cognitive penetration is when a prior belief, expectation, or desire is part of what causally produces the experience.

(3) So if an experience was penetrated by belief B, then the experience is not independent of B, and it can't justify B. And really it can't justify anything in the same area either, since the arriving experience is contaminated by what you already thought.

(4) From the inside, we can't tell whether any particular experience was penetrated. A penetrated experience can feel exactly like an unpenetrated one, and the influence can run without our noticing.

(5) If you can't tell whether an experience was penetrated, you can't legitimately treat it as independent evidence.

(6) So for any actual perceptual experience, you can't legitimately treat it as evidence.

(7) So perception never gives us justification, or at least we're never rational to update on what we see. He says he knows this is absurd but he cant find the step to drop.

Where does this go wrong? Is there an equivocation hiding in one of the steps, or is it a valid argument where we just have to decide which premise pays the price? I want to keep (2)—I think penetration happens at least sometimes—and I definitely dont want to give up the idea that perception justifies most of what I believe. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked so plausible from inside the argument, and what the main positions in the debate would each have to swallow if they reject it.
