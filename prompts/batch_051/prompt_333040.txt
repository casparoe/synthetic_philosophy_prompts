I'm drafting a novel, and the character I can't stop rewriting is a retired actuary who, after his daughter dies in a ferry disaster, starts keeping a private ledger where he scores historical atrocities against one another. Not out of cruelty — he genuinely believes you can't mourn something properly until you know how much mourning it is owed. His niece, a human rights lawyer, thinks the whole project is obscene: not just offensive, but conceptually broken, like trying to rank which color is most triangular.

For the chapter where they argue about this, I want to lay out the main positions one could hold about whether ranking large-scale historical evils is possible or obscene, and then decide which ones my characters could actually sustain over 300 pages. Here are five loose positions I've sketched (I made up the labels, so don't worry if they don't map onto real schools):

1. Scaling optimism: with enough care, suffering can be compared along dimensions like deaths, duration, destruction of institutions; refusing to rank is just avoiding hard choices.
2. Singularity: at least some events resist comparison entirely; putting them on a scale doesn't merely offend, it falsifies what they were.
3. Purposive comparabilism: comparisons are unavoidable in practice, but they're only ever comparisons-for-a-purpose (prosecution, commemoration, fiction), so there's no single answer to "which was worse."
4. Category-error view: ranking events is like ranking colors by how triangular they are; "worse" is underdetermined until you specify a dimension along which to compare.
5. Moral arithmetic: only a transparent quantitative method can keep comparison from becoming self-serving; its coldness is a feature, because it makes the ledger criticizable.

Could you sort these five into tiers by how defensible they are as a working position for a novelist to explore — that is, how much moral and narrative weight each can carry over the course of a book? One sentence per item on where it lands and why is fine. If some of my positions are badly drawn or collaps into each other, say which ones and re-tier accordingly.
