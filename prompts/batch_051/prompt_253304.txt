For a seminar I teach on punishment and social sanction, I want to work through a platform-governance case. Could you act as a philosophy professor who works on law, social ontology, and platform regulation? Take your time: brainstorm three or four plausible readings of the clause, then commit to the one you'd defend and say why.

A large social platform's terms of service say:

"You may not use the Service to harass, abuse, or demean any person, including as part of a coordinated effort, even if no individual post would violate this policy on its own."

The clause was drafted in 2014, when the people who wrote it were mostly worried about abusive replies and dogpiling in the comments.

Here's the case. A group of users maintains a public, searchable spreadsheet on the platform — they call it a "ledger." For each person listed, it gives their name, employer, and links to screenshots of their public posts, quoted without comment: no slurs, no threats, no calls to action. The listed people don't have accounts on the platform. Off-platform, the group runs a forum where readers are encouraged to contact the listed people's employers; on the platform, the spreadsheet itself just sits there. Several listed people have left there jobs after their employers received waves of emails and calls that trace back to the ledger. The platform is now deciding whether to suspend the accounts under this clause.

Is maintaining the ledger "using the Service to harass, abuse, or demean" any person? Walk me through how the plain words, the purpose of the clause, what the drafters would have said if asked, and a reasonable user's understanding each come out. Then say which approach should win, and what your answer implies about whether clauses like this are rules against certain acts or de facto tools for handing out social punishment to coordinated groups.
