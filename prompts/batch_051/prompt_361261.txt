I've argued myself into a contradiction that I can't seem to get out of, and I would like a diagnosis.

(1) A rigid designator is an expression that picks out the same object in every possible world in which that object exists.
(2) "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" are rigid designators, and both of them pick out Venus.
(3) So there is no possible world in which Hesperus and Phosphorus are two different things: the proposition that Hesperus is Phosphorus is necessary.
(4) But that proposition is a standard example of an a posteriori truth: it took empirical astronomy to establish it, and no amount of reflection on the two names alone could have settled it.
(5) If a proposition can only be known a posteriori, then experience was needed to rule out some alternative that our concepts alone left open — some way the world could have been in which the proposition was false.
(6) Therefore there is a possible way the world could have been in which Hesperus was not Phosphorus.
(7) But that contradicts (3).

At least one of these steps has to go. I would most like to keep (1) through (4), because they seem to state the Kripkean picture correctly and I find that picture convincing; that directs suspicion toward (5). Yet rejecting (5) appears to mean giving up on the thought that a posteriori knowledge is exactly knowledge that rules out genuine alternatives. Which step should be rejected, and what would rejecting it cost?
