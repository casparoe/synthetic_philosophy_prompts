Consider the four-rung taxonomy of kinds of minds associated with Dennett. Darwinian creatures are those whose behavioral repertoire is fixed by natural selection: trial and error occurs only across generations, at the level of the lineage. Skinnerian creatures add within-lifetime trial and error: they generate behavior more or less blindly, and the environment reinforces or extinguishes it. Popperian creatures add an inner environment in which candidate behaviors are tried out before being enacted, so that a flawed option is eliminated before it reaches the world — the hypothesis is removed in place of the organism. Gregorian creatures add a further layer: they import thinking tools, such as words, notations, and diagrams, from the social environment and manipulate those tools offline.

Suppose a colleague claims that a particular system — a laboratory animal, or an artificial agent trained by reinforcement learning — has genuinely crossed from the Skinnerian to the Popperian rung. Her evidence is that the system reliably avoids a severe error that its training history gives no sign it ever actually made. I reply that a sufficiently well-trained Skinnerian system can also avoid novel errors, because environmental selection has shaped a policy that generalizes from past successes and failures; no inner simulation need be doing the work. She insists that the difference is real; I suspect the observable behavior may not settle it.

Design the best empirical test, or small battery of tests, you can for distinguishing the Popperian from the Skinnerian reading of such a system. Be concrete about what you would manipulate or measure, what result would favor each hypothesis, and what a positive result would actually establish. Then be explicit about what the test cannot settle. In particular, consider whether a system that merely computes and compares predicted values, without any candidate behavior's being represented and rejected, could pass your test; and whether the claim that the flawed option dies in the organism's stead is best understood as a causal-mechanistic claim, an interpretive summary of causal facts, or something in between. Where, if anywhere, does experiment stop and interpretation begin?
