I am a retired radio astronomer, and philosophy of science is a late and serious hobby of mine. I have been asked to give a physics department colloquium defending the following thesis: elegance is a legitimate tie-breaker among theories that are already empirically equivalent, and a useful heuristic for directing theoretical attention, but it is not by itself a reason to believe a theory true. The apparent historical correlation between beauty and empirical success is, I will argue, largely survivorship bias plus post hoc reclassification of failed theories as not having been truly elegant. I will also argue that the only disciplined way to let elegance enter theory choice is as a Bayesian prior whose reference class is fixed in advance and whose base rate is honestly calibrated, and that working physicists almost never do this; in practice, beauty does more rhetorical work than its logic can support.

The audience is a theory-heavy physics department with two philosophers in regular attendance, and the hazards are specific. One is a senior high-energy theorist who takes the mathematical rigidity and uniqueness of his preferred framework to be, by itself, strong evidence that nature realizes it; he will not accept that elegance is a mere tie-breaker. Another is a formal epistemologist who will press the Bayesian machinery and demand to see the reference class. A third is a philosopher-historian of science who studies how standards of elegance shifted under pressure from successful theories; she commands historical cases I have not read. There will also be two graduate students who have assimilated the standard rhetoric that a beautiful theory is likelier to be right, and a senior experimentalist in condensed matter who regards the whole question as wordplay but will attack any word that is loose.

I would like a Q&A preparation sheet. Give me the objections in the order I am most likely to face them, ordered by expected damage rather than by who speaks first. For each objection, attribute it to the relevant person and phrase it as they would phrase it, in their own vocabulary; say whether I should concede, qualify, or hold; supply a two-minute reply that works without citations; and note where that reply is softest. If an objection turns on technical apparatus such as calibration, likelihood, or overfitting, give me the one-line gloss I can offer the room before I answer. Include the objection I should most hope nobody raises, and tell me why it would hurt. Where my own argument has a structural weak point that a clever questioner will find, I want to prepare to defend it rather than hope they miss it. Six to eight objections is about right.

To help target the objections, my argument has three moves. First, the canon of successful beautiful theories—Maxwell's unification of optics and electromagnetism, Dirac's prediction of the positron, Einstein's field equations for gravity—is a biased sample, because beautiful theories that failed have been quietly dropped from the canon. Second, attributions of elegance are adjusted in retrospect: a theory is called elegant because it succeeded, rather than being selected for elegance before success. Third, aesthetic appeal therefore cannot count as evidence unless one specifies in advance a reference class of relevantly similar theories with a known truth rate, and no such class is ever specified. I am not claiming beauty is misleading; I am claiming its evidential credentials have not been earned.
