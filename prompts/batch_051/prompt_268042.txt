I'm the student rep on my city's Youth Advisory Commission, and we've been asked to send the city council a written opinion about whether to keep the Eastside Community Center open. The building is like sixty years old and needs almost two million in repairs. The parks department's plan is to close it and move its programs to the new regional rec center on the other side of the highway, which has a pool, a gym, and way better parking. The city's numbers show daily use at Eastside is low: retired folks doing cards and coffee in the morning, kids doing homework after school, an ESL conversation group, an AA meeting in the basement, a few quinceañera planning meetings. The parks people say almost all of that can just happen at the new building, and the city would save money.

But the neighborhood is fighting it, and not just because the new center is a twenty-minute bus ride away. They say Eastside is the one place where people from different ages and backgrounds actually run into each other on a regular basis. One older guy on the neighborhood council kept saying the center is a third place — not home, not school or work — and that the city is counting the wrong things if it only looks at attendance and square footage. The neighborhood's basic argument is that if the center closes, a whole community dissolves, and whatever is lost won't show up in any spreadsheet.

I'm supposed to draft the memo for the commission to send next week. Could you write it? It needs an actual bottom line — keep the center open, close it, or keep it open with conditions — plus the reasoning for it, and it has to honestly deal with the strongest point on the other side, not a strawman. I also want it to get at the weir question underneath: when people say a community has dissolved, what exactly is the loss? Is it something real and public, or just a private feeling that some people have, and should that even count in a council decision?
