Here's one I've been turning over. In 2024 the geologists formally declined to make the Anthropocene an epoch of the geologic time scale. The Anthropocene Working Group had spent years arguing for it, with a mid-twentieth-century start and a candidate golden-spike site in a Canadian lake sediment core — plutonium fallout, industrial materials, even broiler-chicken bones as markers — but the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy voted it down and the bodies above it let that stand. The Anthropocene now hangs around as an informal event rather than a formal epoch.

I want you to analyze that episode through the lense of a live philosophy-of-science fight. On one side are realists about scientific classification, who say some categories really cut nature at the joints, including maybe geologic-time units. On the other are conventionalists or pragmatists, who say a classification is just an instrument that works better or worse for our purposes. Run the analysis both directions. First: what would each side say about what the stratigraphers were actually doing, whether a boundary around 1950 can be a real joint in Earth history, and who should get to decide when the Anthropocene starts? Second: what does the episode do to the realist/conventionalist split itself — does the Anthropocene break it, expose a version of realism the old debate had no room for, or show that this whole distinction is doing less work than people think? Don't force the theory to speak where it's silent, and say plainly where it has nothing useful to say. If you think I've set the episode up wrong, or that cutting-nature-at-the-joints versus useful-instrument is the wrong lense for this, tell me that instead of politely playing along. I want a diagnosis more than a verdict, so flag your confidence and the places you're least sure about. No citations needed.
