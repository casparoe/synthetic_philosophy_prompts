In 2011 the United Kingdom held a referendum on whether to replace first-past-the-post for elections to the House of Commons with the Alternative Vote, a ranked system in which losing candidates are eliminated and their supporters' later preferences are redistributed until one candidate has a majority. The change was rejected by roughly two to one, and first-past-the-post stayed. I am not asking for the political history; I want a disciplined application of Arrow's impossibility theorem to that episode.

I have been thinking about Arrow's theorem on my own, and I understand it roughly as follows: once at least three alternatives are in play, no rule for turning any collection of individual ranked preferences into a single social ranking can satisfy all of a short list of demands that each seem almost impossible to refuse — it must work on any coherent set of rankings, respect unanimous preferences, not simply follow one voter, and not let the social comparison of two alternatives shift because of how people rank a third alternative. A two-option referendum looks at first too small for the theorem, but each of the two systems on the ballot is itself a rule for taking voters' preferences over many candidates and producing a single collective result, so the theorem seems to hang over both the referendum and what was being voted on.

Analyze the 2011 referendum through Arrow's theorem. Be strict about the mapping: what plays the role of the alternatives, the voters, and the social ranking, and whether the public was actually presented with a three-or-more-way aggregation problem or only a two-way one. Say what the theorem does and does not imply for how one should choose between first-past-the-post and the Alternative Vote, and for the campaign's treatment of voters' second preferences under AV — in particular, whether those second preferences can be treated as plain facts or as something people will revise strategically once they know the elimination rules. Then tell me where the mapping breaks: where this episode is not an instance of the theorem's setup at all, perhaps because voters' preferences over electoral systems are partly predictions about the outcomes each system would produce rather than intrinsic rankings of the systems themselves. Finally, run the analysis the other way: does the referendum confirm Arrow's lesson that every democratic procedure for aggregating preferences merely chooses which reasonable-sounding condition to violate, or does it show the theorem is too remote from a two-option constitutional choice to say anything useful about it? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
