Here's a double standard I keep running into whenever people argue about gaps in the record. Take a nineteenth-century mill town. The surviving archive has no mention of any strike among the weavers. Most historians I read would say absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence; the records were kept by the mill owners, and a strike is exactly the sort of thing that wouldn't show up in their ledgers. Fine. But then someone claims the mill owners never sold off the town's common pasture out from under the weavers, and they point out there's no record of a sale. Same archive, same silence. In the first case the silence is treated as a fact about the record-keepers—they didn't care to write it down—and in the second it's treated as a fact about the world—the sale never happened. I get that the powerful tend to leave more paper behind, so maybe their misdeeds are more likely to show up somewhere if they had happened. But that's a hunch about base rates that nobody ever actually checks in the moment. So my question is: is there a defensible principle that lets you treat archival silence as evidence of absence for the over-recorded but not for the under-recorded? Or is a silent archive just a blank check for whichever story you already wanted to tell? Give me the best case for keeping some version of the asymmetry, test it against the nearest case where it starts to fail, and say whether the whole thing should be kept or dropped.
