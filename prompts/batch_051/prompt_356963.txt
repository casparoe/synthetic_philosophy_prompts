Since I took over a small orchard, I have been responsible for pest-control decisions that kill insects in numbers I struggle to hold in my head. A friend who works in animal ethics has pressed an argument on me that starts reasonable and then seems to swallow everything, and I cannot locate the step by which it stops being right.

(1) Moral importance should track expected sentient welfare: if an action affects N organisms, each with probability p of being sentient and each suffering a harm of magnitude h if sentient, the action's expected moral cost is N·p·h.

(2) The numbers for insects make this demand drastic. Ordinary cultivation, pest control, and land use affect insects on the order of 10^18 or more at any given time; even if p for an individual insect were one in a million and h a tiny fraction of the harm to a vertebrate, the product N·p·h would still dwarf familiar human-sized and vertebrate-sized harms.

(3) So by (1) and (2), the possibility of insect suffering should dominate many decisions that we currently make without much hesitation.

(4) But there is no reason the reasoning stops at insects. The same formula applies to any group of organisms about which we hold a nonzero probability of sentience: soil mites and nematodes, then bacteria and archaea, whose populations are many orders of magnitude larger still.

(5) And we cannot honestly assign probability exactly zero to the sentience of such organisms. Every proposed necessary condition for sentience—nociceptors, a centralized nervous system, complex learning—is itself something we could be mistaken about, so a nonzero credence must extend even to structurally simple organisms.

(6) Therefore the principle in (1), together with our own uncertainty, commits us to letting the expected welfare of organisms that are almost certainly not sentient dominate our practical deliberation, with no principled stopping point between bees and bacteria.

(7) I find that commitment unacceptable. It makes moral priority hostage to the product of shaky probabilities and enormous census numbers, and it implies that a whisper of possible sentience spread across a sufficiently large population should outweigh certain suffering in front of us. So something in the argument must go.

I would most like to keep the thought that if insects can suffer, their sheer numbers matter. But I cannot see a principled feature of insects that blocks the inference to microbes without also blocking that thought. Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
