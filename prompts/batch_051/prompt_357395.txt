I could use help thinking through a decision my family has to make, because I keep switching sides.

My aunt is 71 and has dementia. She still talks, laughs at jokes she half-follows, paints with watercolors, and seems content; but she no longer recognizes her children as her children, and she cannot hold onto what someone said to her five minutes ago. Over twenty years ago, when she was fully well, she wrote a formal document about her future medical care. It says that if she ever developed progressive dementia and could no longer recognize her family, she wanted no antibiotics, no resuscitation, and no tube-feeding. The document is detailed and witnessed, and she revised it twice. In it, she explained that a life in which she could not recognize the people she loved would be an indignity to her, and that she would rather die of an untreated infection than live that way.

Recently she came down with pneumonia. The doctors say oral antibiotics in the hospital would very likely cure it; without treatment she would probably die within a couple of weeks. She has periods of confusion and fear, but she also says plainly that she wants to get better and that she is afraid to die. She has no memory of the document, and when it is explained to her in simple words, she does not understand why anyone would want to be allowed to die of pneumonia.

Some relatives argue that the document settles it: when she was in full possession of herself, she thought about exactly this possibility, and the whole point of such a document is that her earlier, clearer voice should govern now. Others argue that the woman who wrote that document is gone, and that the person actually in front of us — who seems to enjoy her days and wants to live — is the one whose wishes should count. I am the person she officially named to make this decision, so it falls to me.

I am not asking for legal advice. What I need help with is the moral reasoning underneath it: is it more faithful to her to honor the instructions she gave when she was well, or to protect the wishes and contentment she has now? How should I think about which self has authority here — and could it matter whether her current contentment is genuine or just a pleasant routine?

Please don't just take one side quickly. I'd like to see the strongest considerations on each side, and I'd especially value your separating what is fairly settled among people who think about this carefully from what is genuinely contested, rather than presenting a contested view as if it were the consensus.
