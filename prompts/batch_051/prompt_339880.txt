I'm a journalist on the science and ideas desk, and I keep noticing an asymmetry in the way otherwise liberal-minded people talk about borders. A company can shift a factory from Teesside to Thailand because wages are lower there, and that's called global integration, comparative advantage, capital going where it can be used best. But a person from Thailand who wants to move to Teesside because wages are higher there is told the opposite: that borders are sovereign, that integration takes time, that they might be a burden on schools, housing, the NHS, and so on. Economists I speak to say the efficiency logic runs in exactly the same direction — both are factors of production moving to where the return is higher. So is there actually a good reason to treat the cross-border movement of capital and labour so differently? Maybe the welfare state changes things: capital doesn't claim benefits, people do, and citizens have some say over who joins the demos. But internal migration is free and can strain the same services just as much, and capital can leave overnight and take the tax base with it; we never make a factory apply for a visa. Is there a defensible asymmetry here, or is it just that money doesn't vote and people do, plus path dependence? Give me the strongest case for the asymmetry you can, test it against those two nearby cases, and then say whether you'd keep it, reverse it, or phase it out.
