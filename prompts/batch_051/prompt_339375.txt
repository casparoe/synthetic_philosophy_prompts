I am preparing a unit on the Dutch book argument for probabilism, and I want the standard of evaluation in hand before I present any particular version of the argument. Write the desiderata for a successful Dutch book argument: the full list of requirements such an argument must satisfy if it is to license the conclusion that rational degrees of belief must obey the probability axioms. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a genuine desideratum rather than an optional nicety. Then mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a point of live dispute between camps (for example, between those who take the argument to establish a properly epistemic norm of coherence and those who take it to establish only that incoherence is pragmatically exploitable). Flag any subset of requirements that you suspect cannot all be satisfied at once, and identify the single requirement that, if honored in full, rules out the most familiar textbook formulations. Skip a scorecard of named authors and particular formulations; the criteria themselves are what I want.

Make sure the list addresses at least the following pressure points, though it should not be limited to them: the difference between synchronic constraints such as the probability axioms and diachronic rules such as updating by conditionalization, and which of these a given betting argument is even aimed at; the assumption that fair betting prices reveal degrees of belief, and what happens to that assumption under risk aversion or non-linear utility for money; the aggregation step in which each bet in the book is fair by the agent's own lights when considered singly while the sure loss belongs only to the package; whether the money can be set aside in a version of the argument that uses the agent's own values or credences as the stakes, and whether that version still supports a full norm of coherence; whether the argument requires only finitely many bets and which additivity principle a finite construction can reach, given that stronger principles may demand infinitely many bets; and whether vulnerability to such a book shows that incoherence is irrational in the full normative sense or merely that it is a practical liability.
