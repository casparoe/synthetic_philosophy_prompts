I'm a science journalist working on an explainer about Daniel Dennett's distinction between cranes and skyhooks — between explanations that build complex order through blind, local, unbroken mechanisms and explanations that lower the order in from above — together with the adjacent line between acceptable reductionism and what he calls greedy reductionism. The piece is for the ideas section of a general-interest magazine; assume a reader who can follow an evolutionary argument but has never studied philosophy, and a length of about 1,200 words, or a script of comparable running time.

Don't write the explainer for me. Plan how I should present it. I need the following.

First, the order of ideas and its dependency structure: what must be secured before what, and why greedy reductionism has to be introduced only after, and only on top of, the cranes–skyhooks distinction.

Second, the single running example that should carry the whole piece. It should be something the reader thinks they already understand. I don't want to default to the vertebrate eye or the bacterial flagellum unless you can show they earn their place over the alternatives. Tell me what this example must have and what it must not have, and what will go wrong if it is chosen badly.

Third, the misconceptions readers will bring or form, and the exact turn in the sequence where each should be headed off. I expect at least three: that a skyhook is just a religious way of talking and therefore irrelevant to a secular audience; that any bottom-up account counts as a crane; and that the attack on greedy reductionism is a retreat from scientific reduction into a soft accommodation of the humanities. Tell me which of those are right, and what other misconceptions are coming that I haven't anticipated.

Fourth, one closing question — the sort I could use as a reader-comment prompt at the end — that would show me whether someone has actually understood the distinction rather than merely repeating the definitions.

Finally, what should be left out at this length and where in the piece the omission should be acknowledged; be honest about which legitimate idea will be the first casualty of compression.

One constraint from the magazine: the piece may use Dennett's name and the two terms, but it must stand without any quotation from his books, without footnotes, and without leaning on a contested reading of a particular passage.
