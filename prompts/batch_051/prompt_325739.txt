I am a sixth-form student working on a project about the history of mathematics, and I keep returning to a question that feels more philosophical than historical: at what point does counting become mathematics? We looked at Plimpton 322, a Babylonian clay tablet from the Old Babylonian period, now usually interpreted as a table of Pythagorean triples — well over a thousand years before Pythagoras. My teacher described it as an early mathematical landmark, though we were told historians still debate what the tablet was actually for. What fascinates me is not that debate itself, but the attitude the tablet seems to presuppose: someone was investigating numerical patterns that range far beyond counting livestock, dividing fields, or administering rations.

Could you write a short story set in an Old Babylonian scribal school that dramatises the possible moment when this shift becomes visible? An apprentice scribe is copying or checking a table of numbers when he begins to notice that certain triples repeatedly satisfy a relationship that we would now write as a² + b² = c². Perhaps he tries to point the pattern out to a teacher who cares only about accuracy and legibility, so that the story turns on the difference between following a procedure and seeing a pattern for its own sake. The story should stay historically plausible and avoid anachronism; the most important thing is to dramatise the emergence of a distinctively mathematical curiosity, even if the character's understanding remains very different from ours.

Please begin with the story itself, with no preface or commentary.
