Two people in my seminar got into an argument about whether testimony could ever make it rational to believe that a miracle had occurred. They were not disputing any particular miracle report; they were arguing about the principled question, with Hume's essay on miracles in the background. I wrote down the exchange as faithfully as I can.

A: Testimony is evidence only insofar as it rests on past experience. A miracle is, by definition, a violation of a law of nature, and a law of nature is a regularity backed by the entire uniform experience of mankind. So a miracle report always starts out outweighed by the strongest possible contrary evidence, and the rational person will always discount it, because misperception, delusion, or deceit is always more probable than a genuine law-violation.

B: That proves too much. People have rationally revised extremely well-established beliefs on the strength of reports — think of the first reports of meteorites, or of an animal like the platypus. If you refused to believe anything antecedently very improbable, you could never learn surprising things from other people. The right test is not "does the event conflict with past experience?" but "is this report more likely if the event happened than if it didn't?"

A: You are comparing cases of different kinds. A meteorite or a platypus overturns a local generalization built on incomplete sampling. A miracle would overturn a law of nature, which is not merely a high-frequency summary of observations but a universal constraint on what is possible. The prior probability of a genuine miracle is effectively zero, and no amount of testimony can move a zero prior.

B: Setting the prior to zero is not an argument; it is restating your conclusion, because zero is the one value that no evidence could ever shift. And you are leaving out half the evidence. A miracle report is itself an observed event: "this witness credibly reports a miracle even though none occurred" is itself a surprising fact on your hypothesis. The proper comparison is between two complete stories — miracle-plus-report versus no-miracle-plus-report — not between the prior and the report alone.

A: Fair enough, but once you compare the full stories, the no-miracle story wins every time. We have an enormous record of false miracle reports, and no well-authenticated case of a law of nature being violated. So the probability of no-miracle-plus-report is, in any actual case, vastly greater than miracle-plus-report. The posterior is not mathematically zero, but it is always small enough that the belief would be unjustified.

B: Then you have surrendered the strong claim. "No actual report clears the bar" is an empirical judgment about the testimony we happen to have; it is not the thesis that no testimony could ever suffice. In principle, evidence can be strengthened without limit — independent witnesses, physical traces, contemporaneous documents — and at some point a small prior is swamped. The rational position is "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence," not "testimony can never establish a miracle."

A: But "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" just relabels the problem. Once you admit that testimony could overturn a law of nature, you have no principled way to dismiss the endless stream of miracle claims, because any of them might be the one with enough evidence. A surprising result inside physics is different: it suggests a new law or a new particle, which can then be probed further. A miracle points to no extension of the lawful order at all, so there is nothing to confirm beyond the report itself.

B: That is a worry about abuse, not an objection to the epistemology. The prior-likelihood framework handles both cases: a new particle gets a substantial prior because it coheres with an established theory, while a resurrection gets a tiny prior because it coheres with nothing, so the latter needs correspondingly stronger evidence. The framework does not collapse; it explains, case by case, why some surprising reports are eventually believed and miracle reports usually are not. What it forbids is settling the question before the evidence is weighed.

For each step, say who has the better of the argument, where each side relies on a hidden assumption or an equivocation, and whether the original question — can testimony ever establish a miracle? — is well posed or needs to be reformulated. I am not asking for a summary of Hume scholarship; I want the argument itself adjudicated, point by point, including what both of them are missing.
