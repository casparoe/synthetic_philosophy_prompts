I'm a novelist — literary fiction, mostly, though I keep wrecking my characters' bodies with biology — and I've agreed to lead a two-hour session at a speculative-fiction writing retreat on biological individuality: why it's genuinely hard to say whether a holobiont, a colony, or a superorganism is one organism, and not just a matter of semantics. The writers are smart but none of them have more than high-school biology, and a few already use 'holobiont' and 'superorganism' as if they were settled worldbuilding terms. I want the session to make them feel the live argument underneath. Don't explain the concept to me; plan how I should teach it. I need the order the ideas have to go in so the candidate criteria — genetic sameness, physiological integration, a shared reproductive bottleneck (the one-cell stage between generations), immune self/nonself, being the unit natural selection acts on — dont blur together, plus the one carrying example that can do the most work across those criteria, and why that example rather then, say, an ant colony or a Portuguese man o' war. I'd also like a map of the misconceptions: I'm guessing at least one person will think 'same genes' settles it, another will think 'whatever selection acts on' is the real criterion, and a third will decide the whole question is just semantics. Where in the sequence should I head each of those off, and what should I openly leave out at this level? Finally, how do I check by the end that they understand the question itself, not just that they can tell a lichen from a superorganism?
