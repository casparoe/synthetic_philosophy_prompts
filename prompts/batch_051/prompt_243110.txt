I’ve been reading Hesiod’s *Theogony* in the Evelyn-White translation, and the bit about swearing on the Styx has me in a knot. Here’s the passage:

“With nine silver-swirling streams he winds about the earth and the sea’s wide back, and then falls into the main; but the tenth flows out from a rock, a sore trouble to the gods. For whoever of the deathless gods that hold the peaks of snowy Olympus pours a libation of her water and is forsworn, must lie breathless until a full year is completed, and never come near to taste ambrosia and nectar, but lie spiritless and voiceless on a strewn bed: and a heavy trance overshadows him. But when he has spent a long year in his sickness, another penance more hard follows after the first. For nine years he is cut off from the eternal gods and never joins their councils or their feasts, nine full years. But in the tenth year he comes again to join the assemblies of the deathless gods who live in the house of Olympus.”

I’ve been trying to extract a general point from it and got an argument that seems to prove to much. (1) If a Styx oath is binding on the gods, it must be binding because of the sanction attached to breaking it. (2) The sanction is a finite suspension: one year unconscious, nine years excluded, then back. (3) For deathless beings whose lifespan is infinite, a ten-year suspension is a trivial cost. (4) So no god would keep a Styx oath simply on account of the sanction. (5) But the gods do treat Styx oaths as the strongest possible oaths. (6) Therefore either the binding force of the Styx oath comes from something other than the sanction, or the gods don’t really have a reason to keep them. (7) If the binding force comes from something other than the sanction, then a promise can bind without any penalty, which seems to imply that legal and social enforcement are irrelevant to why we should keep our word. That conclusion feels too strong—it would make the whole practical apparatus of contracts and punishments pointless. But if the binding force is just the sanction, then a Styx oath is not really an oath at all, just a fine. I’d prefer to keep the idea that swearing on the Styx is genuinely binding, but I can’t see which step to give up or how to avoid the conclusion that promises bind without sanctions. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does, and what does it cost to reject the step you pick?
