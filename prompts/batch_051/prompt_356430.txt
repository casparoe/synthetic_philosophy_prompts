I have a policy I am beginning to suspect is either epistemically defensible or a bias I have dressed up as rigor, and I would like to know which. I am a software engineer, and when I join a new project I assign zero weight to anything I have not personally verified about how the system behaves. Documentation, design docs, postmortems, and senior engineers' explanations function in my head as pointers for where to look next, never as knowledge; until I have read the relevant code path or run the system myself, I do not treat them as true. I adopted this after too many incidents in which the docs described an endpoint that no longer existed or a confident colleague described a service that had been rewritten months earlier.

Recently I started reading about Nyāya epistemology, and now I am unsure about the rule. As I understand it, Nyāya counts perception, inference, comparison/analogy, and testimony (śabda) as pramāṇas: distinct, irreducible means of arriving at knowledge, where testimony from a reliable speaker is a genuine source of knowledge and not a mere shortcut that reduces to perception or inference. That framework seems to say my policy is not just cautious but structurally wrong: it treats other people's reports as having no weight until I have personally replaced them with something I have observed in the code. Is my rule defensible as it stands, defensible only under a careful restatement, or a mistake? If Nyāya is right that testimony is an independent pramāṇa, does that refute my rule, or only my habit of stating it as a universal policy? What version of the policy would keep the protection I actually need—against stale, partial, or self-serving secondhand claims—while granting testimony whatever epistemic standing it genuinely has? Please also tell me which argument or consideration would be most likely to change your verdict, and where your reasoning is most fragile.
