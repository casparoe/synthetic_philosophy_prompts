I've been turning over that Einstein's brain book thought experiment from The Mind's I — the one where you wonder whether a book encoding a mind could itself be conscious — and I've landed on a cluster of positions I want stress-tested. Here's roughly where I've ended up:

(1) I'm a functionalist/computationalist about consciousness: if a process has the right causal/computational organization — the right state transitions, over the right timescale — then it's conscious, regardless of what material it's made of. A neuron-by-neuron simulation of a human brain on a typical computer would be conscious, or at least would instantiate some conscious mind.

(2) I also think a book that contained the complete text of that same simulation — every line of code, every state vector, every transition function, printed out and bound in the right order — would not be conscious. Not "less conscious," just not conscious at all.

(3) When asked why the book isn't conscious, my first instinct is: the book only represents the process; it doesn't carry it out. The simulation running on a computer has actual dynamics and real causal connections over time, while the book just has static marks. That difference matters.

(4) But I also think there's no extra ingredient, no élan vital or special mental force; consciousness is entirely a matter of ordinary physical/functional organization.

(5) Now here's a case that makes me uncomfortable: imagine an extremely diligent person with reams of paper who reads the book and manually updates every variable and state exactly according to the printout, step by step, over years. I want to say their hand-execution of the simulation still doesn't produce a conscious mind — even though they are in some sense carrying out exactly the same computation. But I can't give a principled reason why the hand-executed computation differs from the computer-executed one.

(6) Meanwhile I do think a sufficiently complicated mechanical device — even something made of gears and levers — could in principle be conscious if it implemented the right process, because the substrate doesn't matter.

I can't tell wether this set is coherent. Some of these look like they pull against each other, especially around (1), (3), and (5). At the same time, maybe some of the apparent conflicts dissolve if I'm precise about what "implements" vs "represents" amounts to. What I'd like is an audit: rank the genuine tensions in this set by severity, tell me which pairs are merely apparently in tension, and say what the cheapest repair would be — which commitment has to go or be modified, and what that would cost. Please answer in American English.
