Been going round in circles with this one. On one side I find the supply-side case about housing basically convincing: rents are absurd because we spent decades making it illegal to build homes where people actually want to live, and the fix is to legalise building, including market-rate flats, because new supply filters down and because the people who would live in that housing never make it to the planning committee. Give sitting residents a veto and you are just handing a scarce asset to incumbents, who get to watch it appreciate while everyone else gets priced out. I suspect most talk of preserving the character of a neighbourhood is a polite way of saying current residents should get to keep the city to themselves.

And yet. When a council estate is demolished and rebuilt at higher density, and the original tenants are decanted out with no right to return, somthing is lost that will not show up in a supply-demand diagram. People make the place: they look after the old bloke on the landing, they have buried their parents there, their kids go to the school down the road. It seems mad to say their claim is just to a quantity of housing rather than to their place.

So I have a theory with a clean decision procedure, build and treat local vetoes as incumbent selfishness, and an intuition it flatly contradicts: that long-term residents have a claim to continuity that is not reducible to being housed somewhere else. Lay out the strongest version of each and then tell me which you would give up. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, and what would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes? Is there a reading of a right to the city on which building enough and protecting the right to stay are compatible, or are they genuinly in tension?
