A friend gave me this argument and I can't shake it, even though the conclusion seems way too strong. (1) If someone rationally believes p, she's entitled to assert p. (2) So if someone rationally believes p while also rationally believing that her total evidence doesn't support p, she'd be entiled to assert both p and "my total evidence doesn't support p." (3) So she'd be entitled to assert the whole conjunction: "p, but my total evidence doesn't support p." (4) But that assertion is Moore-paradoxical in the same way "p, but I don't believe p" is: the first conjunct presents you as taking p to be true on the basis of your evidence, while the second presents you as taking your evidence to fail to support p. (5) Since no one can rationally make a Moore-paradoxical assertion, epistemic akrasia isn't just irrational — it's impossible. I'd like to keep the idea that epistemic akrasia is at least a coherent state someone could be in, but maybe I'm wrong. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Tell me which step you'd reject and what rejecting it costs for the broader debate about whether a rational person can believe something while believing her evidence doesn't support it.
