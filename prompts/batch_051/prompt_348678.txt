Here's an assymetry I don't know how to justify. A 62-year-old signs an advance directive saying that if he develops dementia and later needs antibiotics to survive pneumonia, he wants them withheld. At 82 he has moderate dementia, but he isn't miserable: he likes his routines, recognizes his kids, laughs at jokes. When the pneumonia hits and the hospital starts to withhold antibiotics, he objects clearly and repeatedly and says he wants to live. The mainstream view—and most advance-directive practice—says the earlier signature wins; the 82-year-old's preferences don't count because the 62-year-old was the last competent him.

Now take a 25-year-old who signs a contract committing his 45-year-old self to stay in a religion he later loses, or to give a kidney to a stranger, or to never marry. Everybody says the 45-year-old can tear the thing up, and that the 25-year-old had no business trying to bind a future person he hadn't met. So we let a person bind a future self who has lost most of the memory and continuity that made him who he is, but we refuse to let a person bind a future self who is, in any ordinary sense, much more the same person.

Is there a principle that delivers both verdicts, or is the dementia case just a superstition about 'who he really is' dressed up as respect for autonomy? If the hinge is capacity, say why the 82-year-old's clear, repeated, happy-sounding preference to keep living isn't enough to make capacity do real work here. I want the strongest candidate principle and an honest verdict on whether it actually holds.
