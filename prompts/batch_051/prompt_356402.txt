After a long argument with a friend about whether a person can knowingly act against his better judgment, I drafted the following exam question for my students and now need to grade one of the answers. Please assign a grade and provide specific, point-by-point comments justifying the grade.

[Exam question]

Read the following passage from Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VII.2, translated by W. D. Ross:

"There are some who concede certain of Socrates' contentions but not others; that nothing is stronger than knowledge they admit, but not that no one acts contrary to what has seemed to him the better course, and therefore they say that the incontinent man has not knowledge when he is mastered by his pleasures, but opinion. But if it is opinion and not knowledge, if it is not a strong conviction that resists but a weak one, as in men who hesitate, we sympathize with their failure to stand by such convictions against strong appetites; but we do not sympathize with wickedness, nor with any of the other blameworthy states."

Answer the following:

(a) Explain the view Aristotle reports in this passage and the role the knowledge/opinion distinction plays in it.

(b) Reconstruct the criticism of that view that Aristotle develops in the surrounding discussion.

(c) Apply that distinction to a standard hyperbolic discounting model: an agent at t0 prefers larger later reward A at t2 to smaller sooner reward B at t1, but at t1, when B has become immediate, prefers B to A. Does the agent at t1 act from opinion or knowledge in the sense of the passage? Does the model support the position Aristotle reports or his own response to it?

[Student's answer]

The passage reports a group who accept part of Socrates' view. They concede that nothing is stronger than knowledge, but they deny that a person can act against what has seemed to him better. To explain the appearance of akrasia, they say that the incontinent man is not mastered by pleasure while he has knowledge; he has only opinion. Aristotle accepts this solution because opinion is a weak state, like the state of men who hesitate, and can be overpowered by appetite, whereas knowledge cannot be dragged about. A hyperbolic discounter initially prefers A, but when B becomes immediate her preference reverses and she chooses B. At t1 she is fully informed about the options and her own discount function, so she knows what she is doing. This means she acts from knowledge, not opinion. The hyperbolic discounting model therefore supports the Socratic side: the earlier preference was really only a weak opinion about what was best, while the choice at t1 reflects the agent's knowledge of what is best for her at that time.
