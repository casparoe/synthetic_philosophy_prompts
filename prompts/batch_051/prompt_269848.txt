I've been teaching research ethics this term and keep getting stuck on a counterfactual, so I'd like to think it through with you instead of re-running the textbook clinical-equipoise debate. Suppose the Health Research Authority introduces this rule for all publicly funded randomised controlled trials from 1 January 2030. A trial may open only if a standing panel records genuine community-level uncertainty about the arms, much as now. But then, at every randomisation, the treating clinician has to enter anonymously their personal probability that arm A is better than arm B for that patient. If the number lies outside a pre-registered equipoise band of 40 to 60 per cent (with a narrow exception for patient-specific contraindications), that patient cannot be randomised by that clinician and recieves standard care or is referred elsewhere. A trial is automatically suspended once more than 25 per cent of otherwise eligible patients are excluded on these grounds, and audits are meant to catch clinicians who just enter a compliant number regardless of what they actually believe.

I want a reasoned prediction, not a story. Think through the next twenty years: what research gets proposed and funded; how trial designs shift (eligibility criteria, sample sizes, non-inferiority margins, surrogate endpoints, platform and adaptive designs); how clinicians form, report, and possibly game their probabilities; and whether the evidence that emerges is more or less trustworthy than what we get under the present system. For each step, say whether it is a robust effect or a guess, and what assumption would have to fail for it to be wrong. Tell me which single prediction you are most confident in, and what would have to be true for that prediction to be wrong.
