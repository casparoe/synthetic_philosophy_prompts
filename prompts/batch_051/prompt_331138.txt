I help run a member-governed organization of about forty people, part housing cooperative and part community space. We make recurring decisions among more than two options: how to allocate the annual budget across proposals, which building renovation to pursue, and which changes to our internal rules to adopt. I have spent enough time on rationalist forums to treat voting procedures as an engineering problem, and we have now tried three systems in two years.

We began with score voting: each member rates every option from 0 to 5, and the highest total wins. In practice people exaggerate. Many members bullet-vote, giving 5 to their favorite and 0 to everything else, and the outcome tracks how tactically each faction voted rather than how members actually rank the options. We moved to approval voting. It removed the numeric spread but introduced what our members call threshold stress: each person decides privately which options to approve, and approving a disliked compromise to block a worse option can produce a winner very few people actually want. We then tried instant-runoff voting. It feels more expressive, but the more numerate members have documented cases where raising a candidate in one's ranking makes that candidate lose, and other cases where burying your true favorite and ranking a compromise first gets a better outcome. Once people know that, they vote tactically again.

I have begun to suspect the problem is structural: with three or more options and voters whose true rankings differ, there may be no deterministic, non-dictatorial method under which every voter can always do at least as well by reporting their sincere ranking as by reporting anything else. I do not need a literature review, and we are not going to become a voting-theory reading group. I want to know two things. First, is my suspicion essentially correct, or is there a method we have missed that actually satisfies it? Second, if it is correct, what should a small organization like ours actually adopt once it accepts that every method can be gamed in some way? Our priorities, in order, are: minimize the incentive and opportunity to misreport; avoid outcomes that flip because of irrelevant alternatives or arbitrary procedural choices; and keep the process simple enough that members can verify the result by hand. Please tell me what you would recommend and why. Because I expect the answer to turn on contestable trade-offs, also say explicitly which consideration would lead you to a different recommendation, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
