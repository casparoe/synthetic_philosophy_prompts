I'm a PhD student in decision theory, and I keep oscillating about a point that has never stopped bothering me. I am drawn to Good's theorem, which looks watertight: for an agent who maximizes expected utility, updates by conditionalization, faces no strategic opponent, and is free to ignore any signal, costless information cannot have negative expected value, since at worst the agent does exactly as well by ignoring the signal. That yields a cleaner principle than almost anything else I know: a rational agent should never pay to avoid information she can simply decline to use. And yet I cannot persuade myself that there is anything irrational, or even subtly mistaken, in refusing to learn a fact one already knows would only bring pain and could not alter any decision, such as carrying a marker for an untreatable and unpreventable disease, or learning exactly how long a loved one suffered. That refusal does not feel like a failure to see that the information is free; it feels like a correct sense that some beliefs are themselves burdens, not merely inputs to choice. I want a negotiated verdict rather than a winner. Present the strongest case for each side, and then explain what it would take for the intuition to be debunked and dropped, for Good's theorem to be read as carrying a scope restriction that excludes such cases, or for both to be preserved by a distinction between decision value and the value of a state of mind. In particular, assess whether folding the pain of knowing into the outcome set is a genuine reconciliation or only a way of restating the theorem's assumptions so that the apparent counterexample is defined away. Please write your assessment in continuous prose, as you would in an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
