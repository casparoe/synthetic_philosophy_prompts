You are an expert philosopher of linguistics with a solid working command of linguistic typology and grammatical theory. I am organizing the conceptual groundwork for an essay on whether grammatical categories are natural kinds, and I need you to do original conceptual design rather than survey or cite existing work. Before you write anything, brainstorm the hard cases and pressure points that any candid answer would have to confront; only then write the final answer in careful, orderly prose.

The question I want the groundwork for is this: are categories such as noun and verb, case, and preposition or postposition natural kinds in any substantive sense — real joints in the structure of language — or are they convenient descriptive labels, language-particular artifacts, or family-resemblance clusters? I want the desiderata before the theories. That is: what would an adequate theory that grammatical categories are natural kinds have to deliver, and what would an adequate deflationary account have to explain away? Do not give me a reading list or citations; the substance should stand on its own.

Write the requirements list. For each requirement: state it precisely; give the case or consideration that forces it, with concrete cross-linguistic examples rather than generic ones; and say whether it is negotiable and, if so, under what pressure. Address at least these candidate pressures, but treat them as candidates rather than automatically as desiderata; where you think one is merely a prejudice of a particular camp, say so explicitly and reject it with reasons:

- Coverage under serious typological diversity: isolating languages with little bound morphology; polysynthetic languages that package many meanings into a single word; languages with no clear adjective class; and languages with flexible word classes in which a single lexical item can function as a noun, verb, or modifier depending on its syntactic position.
- The distinction between language-particular categories and cross-linguistic comparative concepts, and why a natural-kind claim must amount to more than a claim about the latter.
- Mixed and hybrid categories: participles, gerunds, nominalizations, and other forms that exhibit properties of two classes at once.
- Covert categories: groupings with no dedicated morphology that nonetheless constrain grammatical behavior.
- Gradience, prototype effects, and fuzzy membership: whether a natural-kind view can tolerate them, and what it must say if it cannot.
- Acquisition: any innate or universal category constraints must be learnable from primary linguistic data without the child's being given the categories in advance.
- The semantics temptation: why definitions such as noun-equals-person, place, or thing fail, and what the theory must do with the partial semantic correlations that make such definitions seductive.
- The morphology–syntax boundary: whether case is one kind when it is expressed by affixes in one language and by adpositions or clitics in another, and similarly whether prepositions and postpositions form a single kind.
- Diachrony: the well-attested historical pathways by which content words become function words and function words become affixes; a natural-kind view must not make these category changes impossible.

Then flag any pairs of these requirements that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify which requirement is doing the excluding.

Finally, briefly score the main existing families of view against your list — innatist feature-based accounts, comparative-concept accounts, and constructional or prototype accounts — and say which single requirement knocks out the most of them.
