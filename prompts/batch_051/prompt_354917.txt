I'm a philosophy professor teaching a grad seminar on rational choice and conflict this semester, and for the last few weeks I've been sitting in on a political scientist colleague's working group on Fearon-style explanations of war, extended to AI development races. I've been keeping notes on how she classifies cases, and I want to go into Thursdays meeting with a prediction. Her track record so far runs like this. (1) The silent breakthrough: one lab has a private lead and the other has private information about its deadline pressure; negotiations collapse because neither can reveal its private information without losing its bargaining leverage, and they end up duplicating work a joint schedule would have avoided. She called that a private-information problem. (2) The safety protocol: both labs agree a slower shared schedule would leave both better off, but each knows that whichever lab gets the capability first will face pressure to defect from the protocol and the loser will by then have lost its leverage, so they race. She called that a commitment problem. (3) The unshareable dataset: two labs negotiate access to a uniquely located resource that is useless if split, has no substitutes, and each side's domestic audience refuses monetary compensation, so no reachable bargain exists. She called that issue indivisibility.

The new case I want to predict her on is this. Two national labs are racing to build the first general-purpose scientific research model. There is exactly one 'first mover' slot, and it looks genuinely indivisible: whichever lab is first sets the standards, captures the talent, and gets the reputational windfall. But a side deal is available in principle: the winner could license the model cheaply to the loser, co-brand the deployment framework, and split the revenue, and both sides genuinely prefer that to racing, if only it were enforceable. The trouble is that once one lab has the artifact it can stall on the transfer, the other side has no leverage left, and this is common knowledge, so nobody signs.

What should I expect her verdict to be? I can see two candidate principles consistent with her record: one says the new case is indivisibility because the prize itself cannot be shared; the other says indivisibility only controls when side payments are impossible, and here side payments are possible but unenforceable, so it is a commitment problem. If you were aiming for consistency with her earlier cases, which principle fits her better, what would her verdict be, and which feature of the new case actually does the work? Then describe the cleanest further case that would settle between the two principles, and say what someone pushing the other principle would have to say about her third case to keep that reading alive.
