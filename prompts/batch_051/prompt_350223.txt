I teach a graduate seminar on the epistemology of testimony and miracle reports, and next week we are working through Part II of Hume's essay "Of Miracles," from *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Section X. One passage has been troubling my students, where Hume writes:

"The passion of *surprize* and *wonder*, arising from miracles, being an agreeable emotion, gives a sensible tendency towards the belief of those events, from which it is derived. And this goes so far, that even those who cannot enjoy this pleasure immediately, nor can believe those miraculous events, of which they are informed, yet love to partake of the satisfaction at second-hand or by rebound, and place a pride and delight in exciting the admiration of others."

I want to use this passage as a springboard for thinking about how Hume's psychological mechanism would apply, were he writing today, to the modern discourse on pseudoscience, and in particular to the genre of popular books and documentaries about lost civilizations such as Atlantis. The type of claim I have in mind is not religious miracle testimony in the usual sense, but structured pieces of pseudoarchaeology: reports of ancient high technology, sunken continents, or deliberately suppressed evidence. These are not attested as single anomalous events by a handful of witnesses; they are presented as a cumulative alternative picture of the past, often with an elaborate apparatus of citations, underwater photographs, and appeals to academic conspiracy.

I would like you to prepare me, the seminar instructor, for the discussion by anticipating the strongest and most likely ways a thoughtful student could resist applying Hume's mechanism to this case. The students have read Hume carefully and are not hostile to his general project, but they have read more philosophy of science than Hume did and will be alert to disanalogies between eighteenth-century miracle reports and modern pseudohistory. They will worry, for example, that Hume's account presupposes a settled background of uniform experience against which a miracle stands out as contrary, whereas the Atlantis enthusiast rejects that very background. They may argue that the pleasant emotion of wonder is no more explanatory here than the pleasures of puzzle-solving in legitimate science, so the mechanism overgenerates. They may also press the point that a modern pseudohistorian does not merely report an event but offers an alternative methodology for evaluating evidence, and that Hume's framework, which is fundamentally about testimony to a particular occurrence, is too narrow to capture this.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the six or seven objections I should actually expect from this particular seminar, each phrased in the way a philosophically sophisticated graduate student would put it, ordered by how likely the objection is and how much damage it would do, with a suggested reply for each and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Include the objection I am hoping nobody raises. Please do not simply defend Hume; I want to see where the extension to pseudohistory genuinely strains, and where the passage still earns its keep.
