I'm a behavioral econ PhD student, and I keep hearing the same line in our bounded-rationality reading group whenever someone's research plan gets challenged: "don't let the perfect be the enemy of the good." We've been working through satisficing and search costs for weeks, and everyone nods like that sentance settles it, but I've started to think it's doing a lot of different work depending on who says it. Last week in lab meeting, one of the postdocs used it to shut down my suggestion that we keep working on a fully identified structural model of the experiment. Her argument was that the data's noisy and the identifying assumptions are shaky, so we should just report the reduced-form treatment effects and call it a day. When I asked whether the reduced-form estimate actually answers the question we wrote in the preregistration, she said the perfect is the enemy of the good.

I can see at least five different claims hiding in that sentence in this context. (1) A search-cost claim: the extra time and effort needed to get the "perfect" model isn't worth the improvement over a good-enough estimate. (2) An opportunity-cost claim: time spent on identification would be better spent on another paper. (3) An aspiration-level claim: our real question only needed an estimate above some threshold, so "good enough" is really just "above the bar we should have set in the first place." (4) A second-best-style claim: given how messy the data are, the structural model might not just cost more but actually give a worse answer than the reduced form. (5) A psychological claim: perfectionism is why papers in this lab never get submitted. I'd like you to lay out the precise theses this slogan could be expressing, tell me which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false or at least unproven, and then say which reading the postdoc actually needs for her argument to go through. And one more thing: is there a reading on which the slogan isn't answering the question at all but quietly changing it from "what's the true effect" to "what's a defensible, publishable estimate"? Because if that's what's happening, the nod is doing a lot of unspoken work.
