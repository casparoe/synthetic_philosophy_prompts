I'm on the three-person academic-misconduct panel in my department and last week we hit a case that's been bugging me ever since. A student submitted a paper with chunks clearly lifted from the internet. Two questions matter: (1) is the text actually plagiarized? and (2) did the student know they were doing it? The rule we're told to apply: expel only if the panel answers yes to both.

Our votes came out like this. Alice: yes on 1, yes on 2, so expel. Bob: yes on 1, no on 2, so don't expel. Carol: no on 1, yes on 2, so don't expel.

Here's the problem. On question 1 the panel majority says yes (Alice and Bob). On question 2 the panel majority says yes (Alice and Carol). So the panel as a group has accepted both factual premises. But on the final vote the majority says don't expel (Bob and Carol). Three individually consistent people, three straight majority votes, and the group ends up in a position no sane individual could hold: both conditions are met and yet the consequence doesn't follow.

A colleague says this proves issue-by-issue majority voting is just broken, and that we therefore have to give up either majority voting on the separate questions, group consistency, or the idea that the group has a single coherent view at all. That conclusion feels too strong, but I can't find the step to attack. His argument, numbered:

(1) Each panelist's individual judgments are perfectly consistent.
(2) The panel's position on each question should just be whatever a majority supports.
(3) The panel should keep applying the rule — expel iff both 1 and 2 are yes — so the group's overall judgments should be just as consistent as each member's are.
(4) But applying (2) to our actual votes yields 'yes' to 1, 'yes' to 2, and 'no' to expel, which violates (3).

So (1), (2), and (3) all seem mandatory, but together with the votes they produce a contradiction. Which premise should I give up, and what do I actually lose by giving it up? I'd rather keep majority voting if I can, but not if it forces the panel into an incoherent pair of judgments. Are fixes like voting only on the conclusion, or aggregating the premises but not the conclusion, real fixes or just ways to hide the problem? And please seperate what's actually settled here from what's still genuinely contested among people who know this stuff, and don't pass off a contested view as the consensus.
