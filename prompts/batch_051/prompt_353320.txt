I am an undergraduate in a philosophy of technology course, and this semester I have been interning in my city's Office of Innovation and Performance. The office convenes a monthly review of proposed municipal technologies and capital projects — traffic systems, public-engagement platforms, bridge and underpass designs, energy upgrades — and right now the default assumption is that design and engineering specifications are politically neutral unless someone happens to object. That assumption keeps failing. A bridge clearance gets presented as a cost-saving decision and then turns into an access-and-exclusion fight; a vendor's 'neutral' ranking algorithm turns out to determine which residents the city actually hears. My supervisor has asked me to draft a reusable screening procedure, grounded in what we have been reading about whether artifacts have politics, that staff without any philosophy background can run in about 45 minutes per proposal before the monthly meeting, with final sign-off from the planning director.

The course has covered the two sides of the debate. On one side: a technical arrangement can itself settle a political question in a particular social context — for example, a clearance height can decide who reaches a public resource without anyone ever stating an intent to exclude — and some technologies seem to have an affinity for a certain structure of authority, such as centralized expert-run systems versus dispersed small-scale ones. On the other side: technologies are usually flexible enough that their political meaning is not fixed by the artifact alone; it gets fixed through surrounding rules, ownership, procurement, and patterns of use. I need the procedure to preserve what is right in both positions without dissolving into 'everything is political' or 'nothing is.'

Please take the role of a philosopher of technology who has actually advised public bodies, and write the procedure for the staff. Begin with a short brainstorming pass over the hardest cases and likely failure modes, then present a numbered checklist or decision flow with, for each step: what question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and which way the procedure moves. It should mark explicitly where it stops and returns 'ordinary engineering decision,' where it returns 'do not approve as designed,' where it sends the proposal to elected officials or a broader public process because a value choice is embedded in the design, and where it says the artifact matters less than the surrounding governance, so the committee should redesign the rules rather than block the gadget. Flag every step that is a judgment call dressed up as a technical test. Then apply it to three proposals: (1) an underpass with a clearance that will exclude city buses from a newly opened waterfront; (2) a vendor's algorithmically ranked platform for public comments on zoning changes, sold as a neutral filter; and (3) a choice between a centralized municipal energy facility and distributed rooftop solar on public buildings. Make sure at least one outcome is one the committee will not like, and close with an honest statement of what the procedure cannot detect no matter how faithfully it is run.
