For the past several months I have been part of a team designing an oversight system for high-stakes decisions. The system is deliberately engineered so that it cannot act on its own judgments: it examines plans, assigns evaluations, and formulates recommendations, but its outputs are entirely advisory. A separate group of human operators decides what, if anything, to do, and the system has no effectors, no capacity to initiate action, and no ability to appeal or reverse the operators' decisions. We built it this way on purpose — we wanted a judge, not an actor.

The team has nevertheless become stuck on a question that recurs and never resolves: is this constrained system a moral agent? One camp says no. On their view, an agent is something whose evaluations actually figure in the production of outcomes; a system whose judgments can never be causally connected to what is done is closer to a thermometer with opinions than to an agent, no matter how sophisticated its internal deliberation is. The other camp says the morally relevant thing is the system's normative cognition: it identifies options, reasons about what ought to be done, and forms intentions or verdicts, and it merely lacks efficacy. They argue that a human being handcuffed by someone else does not cease to be a moral agent, and that the block on our system is external to it in the same way; the agency lies in the judgment, not in the actuators.

Everyone agrees on the architecture and on every fact about how the system operates. The analogies in each direction feel decisive to their respective partisans, and yet nobody has moved in months. My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a disagreement about the system at all, but about what 'moral agency' is being asked to track: whether it is a property of an internal process of judgment and intention, or a property of occupying a functional role in an action-feedback loop that changes the world. I also suspect the two camps have different paradigm cases silently fixing their interpretations of the term — the imprisoned plotter versus the pure advisor.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal disagreement about the phrase 'moral agent,' or several questions — about agency, responsibility, and blameworthiness — running under one heading? Which hidden commitment is doing the largest part of the work? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a thought experiment that separates the relevant variables, an empirical finding about the system, or an argument that the question as posed is ill-posed and should be replaced by some better question? If you think it is ill-posed, say what the better-posed question would be.
