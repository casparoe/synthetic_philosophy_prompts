I'm in a legislation class and we keep coming back to a doctrine I find genuinely weird, and I'd like a careful account of what it presupposes. Here's the practice as I understand it. Courts normally treat the text of a statute as the law: if the words are clear, a judge follows them, and if the result is harsh, unfair, or just bad policy, the standard response is something like "that's the legislature's problem, go get it amended." But there's a long-standing exception, usually called the absurdity doctrine. When a court decides that the literal application of clear text would produce a result that is not merely unwise or harsh but absurd — something no minimally rational legislature could plausibly have wanted — the court will refuse to apply the text as written. It will read in an exception, or pick a strained construction the words don't naturally bear, while still insisting that its interpreting rather than rewriting. So the same body of judges can say in one case "the text is clear, my hands are tied," and in another "the text is clear, but applying it would be crazy, so it can't mean what it says."

I'm not asking whether the doctrine is good or bad. I want to know what view of statutes — of what a statute is and where its meaning comes from — this practice presupposes. Does it presuppose that the real object of interpretation is legislative intent, with the text as a fallible clue? Does it presuppose that judges are partners in a shared enterprise with legislators rather than faithful agents carrying out orders? Or something weaker, like a background principle that legal texts are written by reasonable people and should be read against a default of reasonableness? And how much is actually settled by the practice: could an intentionalist, a purposivist, and a textualist all consistently apply some version of the doctrine, or does applying it commit you to one of those views whether you admit it or not? Tell me what would have to change in the practice if we adopted the theory that best rationalizes it, and whether that would make the doctrine narrower, broader, or just more honest about itself.

Finally, mark the points where your reconstruction is shakiest: which claim about what the practice requires would you drop first, and what kind of consideration — a different account of what judges are doing when they call a result "absurd," or a clean example of an absurdity ruling that fits none of the available theories — would change your mind.
