I am a retired ecologist who, after a career spent thinking about selection pressures and the limits of field evidence, has recently taken up philosophy of biology. I keep returning to a methodological quarrel in and around evolutionary psychology, and I would be grateful for a careful analysis.

Please adopt the role of a professional philosopher of biology with a good working knowledge of selectionist inference. Think carefully, and before committing to your final answer, spend some time working through the strongest version of each position and the most serious objection to it; I care more about the quality of your reasoning than about a brisk verdict.

I should like you to compare two methodological standards, or meta-standards, for deciding when an evolutionary explanation of a human behaviour is genuinely warranted. I shall call them the engineering licence and the historical audit.

Under the engineering licence, a proposal that some behaviour is an adaptation is respectable if three conditions are met: (i) one can specify a recurrent ancestral problem with independent plausibility; (ii) the behaviour shows design-like fit to that problem's solution, meaning features that are not easily explained as incidental by-products or as chance; and (iii) the proposal yields novel, falsifiable predictions that could not simply be read off the original observations. On this view, functional analysis may licence reverse engineering even where the ancestral record is thin, because successful novel prediction is doing the confirmatory work.

Under the historical audit, by contrast, the decisive facts, such as ancestral selective regimes, the heritability of the behaviour, and historical fitness differences, are rarely if ever known independently. A story about a specific behaviour is therefore warranted only when there is independent historical or comparative evidence, such as phylogenetic reconstruction or palaeoanthropological data, before functional fit is allowed to count. The crux is that narrative fit is cheap: almost any observed behaviour can be made to sound like the solution to some posited ancestral problem, so fit alone cannot turn a story into science.

There is a natural thought, which I should like you to assess: perhaps these two standards are not genuine rivals, because the engineering licence is a standard for generating hypotheses whereas the historical audit is a standard for confirming them, and the familiar charge that evolutionary psychology deals in just-so stories rests on conflating the two. Is that diagnosis right?

I would also like the comparison to be concrete rather than abstract. Apply both standards to an invented case: a long-isolated human population displays a strong, stable, and partly heritable tendency to share food preferentially with non-kin who have previously shared with them, and a researcher proposes that this tendency is an adaptation for reciprocal exchange in small foraging bands. Show how each meta-standard would evaluate this proposal, and indicate what each standard would regard as the next step in investigation.

Finally, please give a clear overall verdict: which meta-standard, or which combination of them, better captures how behavioural adaptation claims ought to be assessed? Then make the self-critical move explicit: tell me what evidence, argument, or consideration would change your mind, and tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail, for instance by identifying the premise of your preferred position that you regard as most vulnerable.
