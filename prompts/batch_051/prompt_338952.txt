After forty years in a lab I've started sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the library, and last week two of the regulars, Pete and Ruth, got stuck on risk and consent and wouldn't drop it. Pete (a retired actuary) said: every time I drive to the shops I impose a tiny chance of killing a pedestrian who never agreed to that risk. If I walked up to someone and said "I'd like a one-in-a-million shot at killing you, but it gets me to the shops five minutes faster," they'd refuse, and doing it anyway would obviously be wrong. So what changes when I do the same thing from behind a windshield, just because I can't see their face? Ruth (a retired nurse) said that principle can't be right, because then walking down the street, lighting a wood stove, or mailing a parcel would all be wrong too — we'd need consent from everyone for everything. She said the real question isn't consent but whether the risk is reasonable: small enough, tied to a worthwhile activity, and spread around fairly. Pete came back that "reasonable" just means the imposer deciding for the victim what risks are worth taking, which is exactly what consent was supposed to stop. Ruth said consent is a non-starter at that granularity — you can't get informed consent from every pedestrian on your route — so demanding it is just a backdoor way of banning ordinary life. Pete then said fine, but then we've swapped a right for a cost-benefit calculation, and at least admit that's what were doing; and anyway a thousand people each carrying a one-in-a-million risk is not the same as one person carrying a one-in-a-thousand risk, even though the expected body count is identical.

I stayed out of it, but I've been chewing on it ever since. Who's right about which specific points, and what are both of them missing? I'd want a point-by-point verdict on the moves they actually made, not a general essay on risk.
