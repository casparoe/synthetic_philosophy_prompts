I am preparing a two-hour seminar for final-year physics-and-philosophy undergraduates on whether rotation is absolute or merely relative, built around Newton's bucket and carried forward into the modern contrast between spacetime substantivalism and relationism. The students know Newtonian mechanics and special relativity but have not studied general relativity. I want a teaching plan for the seminar, not the seminar itself.

The plan should specify the order in which the ideas must be introduced and make the dependency relations explicit at each step — for example, why the distinction between inertial and accelerating frames has to be established before the bucket can be used as evidence, and why the idea of a standard of rest has to come before either the substantivalist or the relationist position can even be stated. It should choose the one concrete setup that will carry the whole session — the bucket itself, Newton's two rotating globes, or an equivalent — and say what that setup must include and what it must be kept free of. It should map the misconceptions these students predictably bring or form and say at which point in the sequence each should be headed off and how: expect at least the idea that relativity has already refuted absolute rotation, the idea that the water rises because it rotates relative to the bucket, and the idea that Mach's principle quietly settles the question in favor of relationism. It should include a check that would show the distinction between absolute and relational motion has genuinely landed rather than merely been repeated, and it should identify what will deliberately be left out at this level and announced as such, such as the bearing of general relativity on the dispute.
