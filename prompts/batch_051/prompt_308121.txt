At a reading group last month we were going through I.J. Good's 1965 paper Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine. The discussion turned on the passage where Good argues roughly that once you have a machine that can far surpass the intellectual activities of any human being, and machine design is one of those activities, the machine could design an even better machine; this, he says, would set off an intelligence explosion and leave human intelligence far behind. The two people who knew the material best split immediately.

Dan said Good's argument is essentially a proof of fast takeoff: the moment you have a system just a little better than the best human AI researchers at the relevant design task, it starts doing the R&D itself, and the bottleneck on further progress stops being human cognitive speed. Meera said that doesn't follow. She said Good's argument only shows there is a positive feedback loop, not how fast it runs; a machine could be superhuman at theoretical insight but still need to wait on data, compute, fabrication runs, or safety testing. Dan answered that if those bottlenecks exist, they are themselves intellectual design problems for the machine to optimize, so they cannot impose an external speed limit. Meera said he was confusing optimization of a chip design with the physical process of building a fab; if the machine needs new hardware to run the next generation, it may have to wait on supply chains, construction, and energy infrastructure, so the speed is governed by the same forces that govern human technological progress. Dan replied that even a decades-long transition from human-level to radically superhuman AI would count as an explosion on any historical timescale. Meera said at that point the original argument has stopped doing any work and just describes ordinary technological change where each generation of tools helps build the next.

I couldn't figure out whether Dan was extending Good or abandoning him. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: identify what each got right and wrong at each step, where the disagreement actually lies, and who is more right overall about what Good's argument genuinely establishes regarding takeoff speed.
