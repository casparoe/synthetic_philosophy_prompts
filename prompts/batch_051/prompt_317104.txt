I've been going back and forth on Skolem's paradox for years — the kind of thing that comes up on the forum every few months and everyone acts like it's obvious once you see it, but I still can't locate the exact bad step. I wrote the argument out in five steps to force myself to either accept it or find the crack. Every step looks true on its own, but the conclusion is absurd. Here it is:

(1) If ZFC is consistent, it has a countable model M. Downward Lowenheim-Skolem theorem: ZFC is a first-order theory in a countable language, and if it has any model it has an infinite one, so it has a countable one.
(2) M satisfies "there exists an uncountable set," because ZFC proves there exists an uncountable set — for instance, the powerset of the natural numbers, by Cantor's theorem — and M is a model of ZFC.
(3) But M is countable. That's just step 1.
(4) So the set that M says is uncountable is itself countable: its elements are elements of M, and M has only countably many elements, so any subcollection of M is countable.
(5) Therefore that set is both countable and uncountable, which is a contradiction.

I know the standard move is to say "the bijection that enumerates it isn't in M," but I need to know which numbered step actually breaks, and whether it breaks because it's false or because the word "countable" changed meaning between two steps. Spell out what "countable" means at steps 2, 3, 4, and 5, and point to the first place the argument stops being valid. If you think the whole thing is valid and I should just accept the conclusion, say so.

Then one calibration question: if instead I considered a countable model of some simpler theory that satisfies "there is a bijection between the naturals and the reals," would the same contradiction arise? Why or why not?
