Here's an argument that's been bugging me, and I'd like to know exactly where it goes wrong, if it does.

(1) An epistemic bubble is just a structural lack of exposure to relevant counterevidence.
(2) An echo chamber is more active than that: its members discredit outside sources, so simply putting the evidence in front of them won't break it.
(3) For someone inside an echo chamber, the only way out is to extend epistemic trust to at least one source outside the chamber.
(4) But any outside source is going to fail the chamber's internal trust-tests, since those tests were built to identify outsiders as unreliable; and any argument that says "actually, trust this source" will itself be evaluated by those same tests.
(5) So in a sufficiently tight echo chamber, there's no non-question-begging argument that can get the person out.
(6) And if that's right, then our judgment that the person is irrational isn't really a flaw we've found in their reasoning—it's just our trust network grading theirs.

I think (6) is the suspicious step, but I can't put my finger on why it doesn't follow. Is the whole argument valid? If not, wich step fails, and what's the most charitable version of that step that would still support (6)?
