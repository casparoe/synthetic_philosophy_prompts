I help coordinate a small research group that occasionally reaches results any one of us could release on our own, such as a dataset, a preprint describing a new method, or code, and we have been arguing about how to govern cases where a few members sincerely think release is justified but the group, if it pooled everyone's judgement, would likely conclude it is not. I gather this is called the unilateralist's curse. Our current rule is unanimous consent, but one colleague says that merely selects for the most stubborn optimist and blocks harmless work; a second wants each of us to commit private probability estimates in advance and permit release only when the pooled estimate clears a high threshold; a third thinks every such procedure simply relocates the error and that we should appoint a single accountable director who decides. We obviously cannot run a controlled experiment on our own governance, and I suspect part of the disagreement is about what the group's view even amounts to once individual estimates are averaged.

Could you rank the following six proposals according to how well each would make our collective behaviour track the collective's own considered judgement, acting when that judgement would endorse release and refraining when it would not, from best to worst? The proposals are: requiring unanimous consent before any member may release; holding a binding supermajority vote with release requiring prior approval from, say, two-thirds of members; collecting independent private probability estimates from every member and permitting release only if the aggregate meets a preset evidential threshold; imposing no prior gate but requiring the releasing member to justify the decision before the whole group afterwards; delegating the decision to a single trustee instructed to implement the group's pooled estimate; and imposing escalating sanctions on members who release unilaterally against what was, or reasonably should have been inferred as, the group's judgement. Please give a sentence or two of reasoning for each placement.

Finally, I would like a note on where your ranking is most likely to fail, and which empirical finding or argument would be most likely to change it. Please write the whole response as continuous prose in the style of a short email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
