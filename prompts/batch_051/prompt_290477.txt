I need a thought experiment built to order for a handout on social choice, so the scenario has to be boring and realistic rather than science-fictiony. You are a professional social choice theorist. Please start by brainstorming a few candidate scenarios before you commit to one, then present the best one and work through it carefully.

The target is Sen's liberal paradox. Here is the set-up I want. There are two people, and each is sovereign over a distinct pair of alternatives that differ only in what that person does with their own body: if the person prefers one member of the pair to the other, society must rank it that way, no matter what the other person thinks. On top of that we add weak Pareto: if both people prefer one alternative to another, society ranks it that way too. The outcome should be the classic cycle — society ranks x above y, y above z, and z above x.

Now the twist. I want the three alternatives to be orderable along a single natural axis of how much or how intensely something gets done (e.g. not at all / moderately / a bit more intensely), and I want both people's preferences to be single-peaked with respect to that axis. So the cycle is not a run-of-the-mill majority-rule cycle caused by messy non-single-peaked preferences; I want it to be generated by the two sovereignty claims plus Pareto, and I want you to show that.

Constraints: no books, no courtship, marriage or sexual examples, no Lady Chatterley's Lover. Make it a mundane flatshare, family or workplace decision. And do not just relabel Sen's Prude and Lewd — the preferences should come from different motivations, and at least one person should be using their sovereignty to stop themselves doing something rather than to force a choice on the other.

After you have set the scene, do the check. State the three alternatives in their order on the axis. Give each person's full preference ranking and confirm it is single-peaked relative to that axis. Say what each person is sovereign over and why its plausibly their own business. State the Pareto claim. Then derive the social cycle step by step, flagging at each step whether a sovereignty right or Pareto is doing the work. Finish by saying which structural feature of the case actually produces the cycle, what happens if you delete each of the two sovereignty pairs in turn, and how a defender of minimal liberalism and a defender of the Pareto principle would each try to block the case from counting.
