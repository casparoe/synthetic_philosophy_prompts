In my social choice course we just finished Sen’s liberal paradox (the impossibility of a Paretian liberal), and I keep flip-flopping about which side of the clash should give way. On the one hand, I’m pretty sold on the framework because each condition looks close to unassailable. Unrestricted domain says a social decision procedure shouldn’t be built so that it only works once we’ve already declared certain preferences illegitimate. Pareto says if everyone prefers x to y, ranking y above x is just refusing a free improvement. And minimal liberalism says each person should be decisive over at least some connected part of their own life—what they read, who they associate with, whether they take a job. The proof is mechanical once you have the Lewd/Prude example: Prude prefers that nobody reads Lady Chatterley’s Lover, but if someone has to read it, he prefers himself to Lewd; Lewd most wants Prude to read it, and second wants to read it himself. Give each person a right over the pairs “they read it” vs. “nobody reads it,” add Pareto, and you get Lewd > nobody > Prude > Lewd. No best option. So at least one of the axioms has to go.

But I also can’t shake the intuition that a society can obviously respect individual liberty without making itself unable to capture unanimous improvements. It seems like a decent society doesn’t literally face this choice, and that the real culprit here is the meddlesome character of the preferences: Prude cares whether Lewd reads, Lewd cares whether Prude reads. So the paradox looks like an artifact of letting nosy preferences into the social calculus. The problem is that as soon as I try to formalize “only count rights-respecting preferences,” it feels ad hoc, and I can’t state the exclusion rule without presupposing exactly the liberal conclusion it’s supposed to protect.

I want your bottom line as your first sentence, then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. But don’t just tell me wich side wins. I want you to say what would have to be true for the intuition to be the thing that gets debunked and dropped—that is, for me to accept that liberty and Pareto can really conflict, and bite the bullet on giving one up. And what would have to be true for the social-choice model of rights to be the thing that goes—say, redoing rights as constraints on admissible action or game forms rather than decisive power over outcomes. And whether there is a reading on which both survive: the theorem only shows that “rights as decisiveness over alternatives” is too crude to capture what liberty is, not that liberty actually conflicts with Pareto. Which move do you find most justified, and what makes the alternative moves cost more?
