In my seminar on the political philosophy of education, we have been discussing school choice proposals, and I keep being struck by a feature of the debate that our readings treat only in passing. Consider a state that operates, in effect, two separate systems. In its district public schools, the state is heavily involved: it sets curricular standards, tests students, requires teacher credentials, and funds schools through formulas governed by enrollment and need. The same state runs a universal voucher or education savings account program under which any family can direct public money toward private or religious school tuition, and in some versions toward home-schooling materials, tutors, and other educational services. Those receiving institutions face no parallel curricular requirements, no comparable testing, no equivalent certification rules for instructors, and only minimal obligations to report what children are actually being taught. The stated rationale is that funding should follow the child, that parents are the best judges of their children's education, and that the state should remain neutral among educational approaches. Yet the state continues to enforce compulsory attendance and continues to speak of an educated citizenry as a public interest.

I am trying to work out what philosophical commitments this two-track arrangement presupposes. Does it presuppose a particular view of what education is for—for example, that education is at bottom a private good that parents are entitled to direct, rather than a public good the state must guarantee on behalf of the community? Does it presuppose that governments can be meaningfully neutral among families' comprehensive worldviews even while funding them, or that parental authority simply outweighs the state's interest in civic formation? Or is the arrangement underdetermined, compatible with several rival theories, so that the official neutrality story does more rhetorical work than the policy strictly requires? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, identify where the two tracks presuppose inconsistent commitments, and say what would have to change in policy if the state instead presupposed that civic formation and equality of educational opportunity are non-negotiable public interests.
