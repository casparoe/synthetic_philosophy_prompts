I've been working through Spinoza's Ethics after a long career in the natural sciences, and I recently read a short introductory book that tries to digest the whole system for nonspecialists. The author summarizes Spinoza's position roughly as follows:

"Spinoza demonstrates that absolutely everything happens necessarily. God could not have produced the world in any other way or in any other order, because the world follows from the divine nature with the same necessity as the properties of a triangle follow from its definition. Human beings therefore have no free will: our actions are determined by prior causes, and the feeling of being free is merely ignorance of the causes that determine us. Spinoza's famous pantheism amounts to saying that God is simply the universe, understood as the totality of physical things and the laws governing them. The intellectual love of God is the highest state of the mind: it arises when we understand things as necessary consequences of God's nature, and it constitutes a kind of blessedness. Since God is not a person, petitionary prayer is pointless, and there is no hope of personal immortality."

How fair is this as an account of what Spinoza actually argues? Please work through it claim by claim, distinguishing what a careful reader of the Ethics would accept from what they would qualify or reject, and flag any places where the summary blurs distinctions Spinoza takes care to draw. As a retired scientist I am inclined to think the causal-necessity part is correct, but I do not know how much weight to place on the rest. End your answer with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that gives your one-sentence overall assessment of how fair the summary is.
