I'm taking a philosophy of criminal law course this term, and our week on blackmail left me unsettled. The puzzle, as it was presented, is that blackmail seems to be assembled out of ingredients that are each independently lawful in many contexts: threatening to disclose some information, and demanding something in exchange for remaining silent. Yet the combination is almost universally condemned. What I find missing in the discussion is a precise account of what blackmail actually is—as a moral wrong, not merely the offense defined in any particular statute—that explains why some disclosure-threat-plus-demand combinations are condemned while structurally similar ones attract no blame at all. I have been trying to build a working definition, and I have collected a set of cases that I want the definition to sort correctly.

The cases are these: (a) a man threatens to send photographs of his colleague's affair to the colleague's spouse unless the colleague pays him $10,000; (b) a parent tells his teenager that he will lose his phone for the weekend if he does not finish his homework before dinner; (c) the victim of an embezzlement tells the thief to repay the stolen money by the end of the month or she will report him to the police; (d) a customer who received spoiled food tells the vendor to refund the purchase and the shipping cost, or she will post a truthful negative review and contact the health inspector; (e) a sister tells her brother that if he does not tell their parents about his recent drunk-driving arrest by Sunday, she will tell them herself; (f) an attorney's settlement letter states that unless the recipient pays $300,000 within two weeks, the firm will file a harassment complaint on behalf of its client, which would then become publicly accessible.

I am confident that (a) is blackmail and that (b) is not. I want to say that (c) and (d) are not blackmail, or at least are far less wrong than (a), but I cannot articulate what makes a demand for restitution or a refund different. I genuinely do not know what to say about (e), and (f) looks like blackmail on paper yet is treated as ordinary legal practice. I would like you to propose a definition of blackmail that gets these cases right, or that explains convincingly why some of my confident verdicts are wrong. Please run the definition through all six cases, say explicitly where it surprises you or parts ways with my initial take, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. I am less interested in the law itself than in the underlying moral structure: which features—the truth of the information, what one is entitled to demand, whether the threatened disclosure is independently permitted or required, who benefits, who the target is—are actually doing the work, and which are only correlations.
