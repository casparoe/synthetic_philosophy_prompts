I'm a retired chemist who only recently started reading philosophy, so forgive the homemade vocabulary. I've been mulling over Goodman's grue and I've formed a little theory I want someone to stress-test. I call a predicate load-bearing if the split it draws would still show up in our best causal/explanatory picture of the world, rather than only in a description we happened to learn. My claim is that a predicate is inductively legitimate only if it's load-bearing. So 'green' is load-bearing: whatever physical story explains emeralds, that story would still mark off green from blue. 'Grue' isn't, because its only machinery is a clock someone pasted onto the world—green until t, blue after—so it's electron-chasing without the electron. I know about projectibility and natural kinds, but I think those are about our history of using words, wheras mine is meant to be about the joints in the world itself. Is this a real distinction or just a metaphor? Has someone already made this move under a name I should know? And where does it break—for example, could a gruesome predicate be load-bearing if the best causal story itself contains a sharp temporal asymmetry? Be candid: tell me what it gets right and where it stops being distinctive. Don't bother with citations. And at the very end, make the final line a single sentence starting 'Summary:' that gives your overall assessment.
