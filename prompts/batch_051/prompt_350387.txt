ok so I've been going down a void-for-vagueness rabbit hole and I can't figure out what the doctrine actualy presupposes about law. In American criminal law, a statute can get struck down for vagueness when it fails to give ordinary people fair notice of what's illegal, or when it invites arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement—even if the legislature clearly could criminalize the conduct in question. So the defect is supposed to be in how the law is written, not in what it bans. But those two tests seem to assume completly different things. Fair notice assumes the law is basically instructions from the legislature to citizens, something you could in principle comply with. The anti-arbitrariness test assumes the law is more like a leash on police and prosecutors, a check on official discretion. Those can pull apart: a statute banning dishonest conduct gives lousy notice but it's exactly the sort of open standard courts are expected to sharpen case by case, while a hyper-specific rule could give great notice and still be enforced arbitrarily. And courts routinely save otherwise-vague statutes by announcing a limiting construction, which means the defendant was supposed to know the law as construed even though the text she read never said any such thing. So what view of law, legal obligation, and the intended audience of criminal statutes does this whole practice commit us to? I want it sorted out: which of these are strict presuppositions, which just happen to fit comfortably, and where the doctrine is genuinely underdetermined between rival pictures like law-as-guidance, law-as-discretion-allocation, or law-as-coercive-threat. Then tell me what would have to change in the doctrine if we took the guidance picture seriously and dropped the anti-arbitrariness rationale entirely—or the other way around.
