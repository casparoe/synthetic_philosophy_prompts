I'm a phd student working on cross-country wellbeing measurement, and I've developed a rule I'm starting to worry about. I no longer beleive the big 'happiest countries' rankings, because they're basically all built on the Cantril ladder — the question where you rate your life on a 0-to-10 ladder. My policy is: unless a study reports actual experienced affect (yesterday's enjoyment, stress, worry, sadness, that kind of thing), I won't draw any conclusion about how happy a country is. I go straight to the affect data in the appendix and ignore the life-evaluation score. My supervisor says I'm throwing away the field's most validated measure, and that life evaluation isn't a noisy proxy for my pet construct — it's a legitimate sense of 'happiness' in its own right, maybe even a better one. And honestly part of me knows I'm stipulating that happiness just is how a life feels from the inside day to day, and treating the ladder as contaminated by expectations, social comparison, cultural response styles, and so on. But is my policy actually defensible? Should I be discounting the evaluative measure all the way to zero, or should I just be careful about what claim I make and keep some weight on it? What would I need to see in the data to tell whether the ladder is being warped by those factors rather than tracking real differences in how good people's lives feel?
