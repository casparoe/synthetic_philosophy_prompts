I have been struck by an apparent structural parallel between two sorts of failure that cannot be blamed on a single culprit. On the Duhem–Quine picture, a false prediction never refutes one hypothesis in isolation: the core theory can be retained while an auxiliary assumption about instruments, background conditions, or idealisations is revised instead, so the evidence underdetermines where the error lies. A failing end-to-end test in software engineering seems to have the same character: it shows that the system as a whole has failed, but not whether the fault lies in the application code, the test harness, a third-party library, the compiler, or the hardware, since any one layer can be blamed while the others are held fixed. Is this a genuine identity of structure, or only a loose analogy trading on the metaphor of blame? If it is genuine, what would have to be true on both sides for the standard software practice of bisecting a sequence of changes to isolate a regression to carry over as a procedure for adjudicating among rival auxiliary hypotheses in science? If it is superficial, where precisely do the two structures come apart?
