I retired a few years ago after a career in physical chemistry—spectroscopy, mostly—and I've spent a fair bit of the time since reading philosophy of mind. Mary's room keeps pulling me back. You know the set-up: Mary has seen only black and white her whole life but knows every physical fact about color—wavelengths, photopigments, opponent processing, the whole machinery. Then she steps out and sees a ripe tomato.

I'm trying to figure out what I actually think, so I've written down my gut reactions to some variants. My tentative line is that what Mary gains is acquaintence with a quality, not a new fact, so physicalism survives untouched. But my own cases keep snagging on that line, and I'd like you to tell me either what principle my verdicts actually add up to, or how to repair the acquaintance principle, or which verdicts I should drop. I'm not sentimental about any single verdict—I want the neatest view that does the least violence to what I feel.

Here are the cases.

1. Classic Mary. She sees the tomato. Verdict: she learns something new, in the ordinary sense of "learns." Not just a new ability. I can't state the proposition she'd add to her knowledge, but saying she doesn't learn anything feels like a dodge.

2. The spreadsheet simulation. Before leaving, Mary gets to inspect the complete output of a biophysical simulation of her own visual system, run on the exact type of input the tomato will later produce. Every firing rate, every synapse, every concentration, for as long as she wants. Verdict: no—reading those numbers doesn't give her what she later gets by seeing red. She still learns something on release.

3. The electrode. Still in the room, someone activates her color cortex with the same spatiotemporal pattern that seeing red would produce. She's told, at the time, "that's red." Verdict: yes. This seems to give her the same new knowledge as the tomato, with no red object anywhere. She can now say "so that's red," which she couldn't do from the spreadsheet.

4. The magnetic belt. A normally sighted person wears a belt that vibrates toward magnetic north for six months and eventually reports that they just know where north is, the way you know where your hand is. Verdict: I keep going back and forth. I want to say they've gained a new what-it's-like, but I also suspect it's just old tactile sensations repurposed into an ability.

5. The fourth cone. An adult trichromat gets gene therapy that adds a working fourth cone photopigment with peak sensitivity between her existing red and green cones. Afterward she reliably sorts surfaces that look identical to everyone else and says there's a new color she can't place anywhere in the old color space. Verdict: I think she learns a genuinely new quality, not just finer discrimination—though I'm not sure whether this is real science or a philosopher's daydream.

My problem is cases 2 and 3. In both, Mary has full physical information about the very same neural pattern that realizes seeing red. In 2 she stands outside it and knows it as data; in 3 she undergoes it. If acquaintance with a quality is the thing, why should undergoing the pattern make an epistemic difference like that? And if it doesn't, I have to either take back my verdict in 3 or admit my verdict in 2 is just prejudice.

So: give me the principle, stated as precisely as you can, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. Please say explicitly which of my verdicts the principle has to overturn, and what it predicts for a case I haven't listed because I can't settle it: someone who knows the complete neuroscience of pain but has never felt pain. If the right answer is a small menu of candidate principles rather than one winner, I'll take that too.
