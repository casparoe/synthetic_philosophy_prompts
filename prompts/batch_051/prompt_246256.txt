I've become convinced, in roughly the Zahavi–Grafen spirit, that the handicap principle is the right general account of honest signaling: signals stay honest because they are differentially costly, so only high-quality individuals find them worth producing. I keep reaching for it to explain human cases like conspicuous consumption and credentialing as well as the usual peacock-and-gazelle examples. I want to know what would count against it as a general account, with defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Which apparent counterexamples are genuine problems rather than cases the view can absorb by widening what counts as a cost—unfakeable indices such as body size, signals between parties whose interests align, cheap conventions kept honest by reputation or third-party enforcement? If I allow strategic, social, and opportunity costs into the notion of cost, have I insulated the principle into unfalsifiability, and is that a defect or a reasonable feature of an explanatory framework? What empirical or formal results should move me most: a stable signaling equilibrium with no differential cost at all, or an experiment in which an expensive signal conspicuously fails to track quality? And what should a committed handicap-principle theorist themselves concede counts against the view, as opposed to what critics merely assert? End with your overall confidence in the assessment and the single point you are least sure of.
