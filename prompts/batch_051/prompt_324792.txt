I am a PhD student in philosophy of religion working on divine impassibility, and I keep getting stuck on a specific problem: whether the claim that God is compassionate can be reconciled with the claim that God cannot be affected by anything. Anselm’s Proslogion, chapter VIII, is the classic attempt to hold both at once. In Sidney Norton Deane’s translation, the central passage reads:

“But how art thou compassionate, and, at the same time, passionless? For, if thou art passionless, thou dost not feel sympathy; and if thou dost not feel sympathy, thy heart is not wretched from sympathy for the wretched; but this it is to be compassionate. But if thou art not compassionate, whence cometh so great consolation to the wretched? How, then, art thou compassionate and not compassionate, O Lord, unless because thou art compassionate in terms of our experience, and not compassionate in terms of thy being. Truly, thou art so in terms of our experience, but thou art not so in terms of thine own. For, when thou beholdest us in our wretchedness, we experience the effect of compassion, but thou dost not experience the feeling. Therefore, thou art both compassionate, because thou dost save the wretched, and spare those who sin against thee; and not compassionate because thou art affected by no sympathy for wretchedness.”

What draws me to this passage is that Anselm seems to concede the affective point — God does not experience the feeling of compassion — while insisting that nothing important is lost, because the creature still experiences the effect of compassion. I am inclined to think this distinction does real work. My advisor, by contrast, thinks Anselm has saved the word “compassion” by evacuating it: if the attribute is defined purely by effects on the recipient, then an agent wholly unmoved by another’s suffering could be called compassionate just in case the relevant effects happened to console, and that seems to break the concept.

I would like your credence, given as a number between 0 and 1, in the following proposition: Anselm’s distinction — that God is compassionate in terms of our experience but not in terms of his own being — is sufficient to preserve a substantive, not merely verbal, doctrine of divine compassion while maintaining impassibility. Please decompose the number. In particular, assign rough probabilities to these sub-questions and say how they combine: (a) whether the core meaning of “compassion” includes an affective state in the agent; (b) whether effect-based or analogical predication can carry the normative content of a moral attribute; (c) whether there is independent reason to affirm divine impassibility, so that an Anselmian reconciliation is motivated rather than ad hoc; and (d) whether the actual consolation of the wretched requires anything more than a fitting causal ordering of effects. Then identify which sub-question my advisor and I most likely disagree about, and say what evidence or argument would most move your overall number.
