I've been going back and forth with a friend about what makes a mathematical object "legitimate," and I think the history of geometry really messes with the intuitive answer. Ancient geometers had a hierarchy of problems: some solved by straightedge and compass, some by conics, and the "linear" ones needing more complicated curves. Descartes in his Geometry tried to draw a sharp line: he excludes curves like the spiral and the quadratrix because they can't be generated by a single, continuous motion of one point under simple constraints, and he says only curves drawn by such motions are genuinely geometrical. But that line felt principled to him and less principled to later mathematicians, once analytic expressions and infinite series let you write down all sorts of wild curves. So here's my question: write an essay on whether there is any stable, non-arbitrary critereon for counting a mathematical object as legitimate, or whether legitimacy is always relative to a particular stage of mathematical practice. Weigh constructibility, representability by finite or infinite expressions, and consistency/utility as candidate criteria. Don't end fence-sitting — defend a verdict, even if it's something like "legitimacy is practice-relative, but that's not arbitrary." Also, as you go, separate the parts of this that are settled historically from the parts that people who actually work on philosophy of mathematics genuinely disagree about, and don't make a contested interpretation of Descartes sound like the consensus.
