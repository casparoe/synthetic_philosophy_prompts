Suppose a colleague defends the following claim: there is no number n, however large, of barely noticeable, momentary irritations — a dust speck in the eye, one per person — that are together morally worse than one individual's being tortured for an hour. The colleague says very small harms cannot add across persons into a large harm, and that anyone who would avert the dust specks rather than the torture is committing a moral-arithmetic error. I suspect this claim is, depending on how it is read, either a conceptual stipulation or an empirical conjecture about the psychology and structure of value. I want to know whether any observation could actually bear on it. Set out the strongest test you can construct of the proposition that very small harms fail to aggregate across persons into a harm comparable to a single severe harm. Specify the comparison, the measure, the result that would count for aggregation, the result that would count against it, and then say exactly what a positive result would and would not establish — in particular, whether the residual disagreement would be psychological, conceptual, or moral. Present the answer as an exactly five-item numbered list, each item one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
