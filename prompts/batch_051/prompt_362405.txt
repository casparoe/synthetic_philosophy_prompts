I am drafting a paper on whether a finite binary string can properly be called random, and I keep returning to an argument with a colleague in statistics. He maintains that we can settle the matter empirically for any given finite string by running the usual test batteries—frequency, runs, autocorrelation, and so on—and that the first one hundred binary digits of the fractional part of π would be shown to be random, or at least statistically indistinguishable from random. I have been reading the algorithmic randomness side of the question, where infinite sequences are called random when they avoid all effectively describable null sets, and finite strings are graded by something like Kolmogorov complexity: a finite string is random to the extent that the shortest programme that outputs it is not much shorter than the string itself. On that view the π digits are not random at all, because a very short programme generates them, even though they may sail through every ordinary statistical test. My question is not which side is right; it is what a test could possibly settle. Design the strongest test you can of the claim that a particular finite binary sequence is random. Be concrete about how one would operationalise it, say what result would count for each reading, and say what a positive result would and would not show. In particular, I want to know whether running a statistical battery tests randomness itself, or tests something weaker such as present-day unpredictability or the absence of a pattern we already know how to describe. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on algorithmic randomness, rather than presenting one school's view as the consensus.
