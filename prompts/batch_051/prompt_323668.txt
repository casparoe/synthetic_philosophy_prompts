I'm teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar on practical rationality this term, and the material has recently become unexpectedly personal. A few months ago I had to choose between two postdoctoral positions: one at a highly regarded department in a city I knew I would dislike, and another at a less prominent department in a town where my partner and I could live near family. I kept trying to rank the two options the way the standard machinery of decision theory seemed to require, but neither option was clearly better, the options did not feel equal, and the difficulty did not feel like mere uncertainty about the facts or about what I wanted. It felt as though the options resisted comparison on any single scale, and that experience has made me acutely aware that much of this semester's material on incommensurable values and hard choices turns on distinctions a student can repeat in an essay without ever actually using them.

For the final exam, I want one essay question, pitched for a 45-minute in-class answer, that cannot be answered by reciting the taxonomy we covered: the alleged trichotomy of comparative relations (better than, worse than, equally good); the distinction between incommensurability, understood as the absence of a common cardinal scale on which both options could be measured, and incomparability, understood as the failure of the usual comparative relations to apply; and the proposal that hard choices are explained by a fourth relation such as being on a par. I would like the question to force students to work through a live issue we only touched on, namely whether what shows up as parity in a hard choice could instead be explained by vagueness plus the agent's uncertainty about her own values, and what follows for how the agent should treat the choice — for example, whether it would be rational to let a coin flip settle it. Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme on a 20-point scale. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain, and should flag one or two characteristic errors, such as conflating incommensurability with incomparability or treating being on a par as if it simply meant being equally good.
