Consider a familiar move in debates about free will: if the brain were deterministic, our choices would be fixed by prior states; but some have argued that neural events such as the gating of ion channels or the release of synaptic vesicles are quantum-mechanical, so the brain is not deterministic, and the door is reopened for agents to do otherwise. I want to know whether this move has any empirical content and, where it does, how it could be tested.

The claim comes in at least two strengths. On the weak reading, quantum indeterminism is simply irreducible noise: some close calls are settled by genuinely chancy events, so no complete physical account could have predicted them, but the agent neither directs nor controls the chance. On the strong reading, the agent's will or some top-down neural process exploits the indeterminism, raising the probability of the selected outcome relative to what unfettered quantum mechanics would predict, or coordinating many chancy events so that the outcome is not merely left to noise.

Design the strongest concrete test you can that would distinguish the weak reading from the strong reading, and either from a purely deterministic account. Say what you would measure (a forced choice between nearly matched options, the reported moment of intending, the trial-by-trial statistics of the choices), and spell out which pattern of results would favor each reading. Be explicit about the obstacle posed by the fact that a brain cannot be reset to the same microstate, and say what invariant or aggregate features of the data could nonetheless carry the signal. Then say what a decisive win for the strong reading would still not show—in particular, whether the agent would thereby be free or merely subject to an additional physical control parameter—and identify the part of the original claim that remains untestable in principle, along with whether that residue should be treated as meaningless or as a conceptual question to be settled by other means.
