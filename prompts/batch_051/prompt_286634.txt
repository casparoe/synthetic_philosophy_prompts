So my school has this joint CS and philosophy club thing, and last week we got into whether a self-driving car that has to choose between two bad outcomes is actually "making a moral decision" or just running code. The discussion was really getting somewhere until a senior basically ended it by announcing that "you can't get an ought from an is." His argument was that any machine built out of sensors, facts, and code is stuck on the is side, so no matter how much data it has, it can never actually do ethics — it can only describe the world and compute, never judge it. I've heard the line before, but in this context I don't actually know what it's supposed to prove. I get that a pile of facts about the world doesn't by itself tell you what to do, but couldn't you just build the ought in as an explicit premise — like a rule that says minimize expected harm — and then the machine is doing ought-in, ought-out rather than deriving an ought from an is? And another kid said the car isn't reasoning at all, its just physics plus code, which feels like a totally different use of the slogan. I don't want the senior to win just because the line sounds deep.

What I'm looking for: lay out the seperate precise claims that sentence could be making — about logical derivability, about facts vs values, about what can motivate an action, about whether an algorithm can be moral — and for each one say whether its actually plausible and whether it would get him his conclusion that a machine can't do ethical reasoning. Can you do it in four sections: (1) Readings, the main section, a numbered list of the distinct things "you can't get an ought from an is" might mean here, with a short explanation of each; (2) Scorecard, a quick verdict on each reading (platitude / genuinely defensible / just false); (3) Does it get him the conclusion?, where you say which readings would actually support his shutdown and which wouldn't; and (4) Confidence, a short paragraph saying how confident you are in the overall analysis and singling out the point you're least sure of.
