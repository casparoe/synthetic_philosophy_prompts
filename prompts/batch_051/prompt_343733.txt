I've been doing those card puzzles—I think they're called Bongard problems—where you get a dozen little drawings, six in one box and six in another, and you have to guess the rule that sorts them, like the left cards all contain a triangle. When someone gets the rule from just those twelve examples, we call it good pattern recognition. But if a person does the same thing with people—meets six people from one office, sees they all shirk, and concludes that office is full of shirkers—we call it prejudice, even though the rule fits the six examples just as well. I get that the stakes and the subject matter are different. But if we set those aside—same amout of evidence, same confidence, same fit, both rules pretty simple—is there anything left that makes the card-puzzle inference smart and the people inference bad? Or is the only real difference that the puzzle has an answer sheet, and that's what's doing all the work? If it is, does that mean we've just been calling guesses smart whenever we already know the answer? What actually justifies the difference, and does your answer survive a version of the puzzle where the rule is just as vague and loaded as a social one, like 'the faces on the left look untrustworthy'?
