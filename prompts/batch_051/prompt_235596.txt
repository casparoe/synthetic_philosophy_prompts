I teach an upper-level philosophy of language seminar with a unit on prescriptivism and descriptivism. The session I need help with has sixteen students and runs for 75 minutes. The central question for the session is: Is there such a thing as bad grammar, or only grammar that is socially marked in a given context? I want the students to stop treating descriptivism as the obviously correct, comfortable view, and I also want them to stop treating prescriptivism as self-evident common sense. By the end, they should see standard written English as one variety among others—a real variety with real norms, but one whose authority is social and contextual rather than intrinsic to the language itself.

Design a runnable 75-minute activity for this session. The activity should make students feel the force of the problem before it is named. The rules, roles, or materials must be arranged so that participants first enact a prescriptive judgment—or better, two competing prescriptive judgments—then discover that their own judgments are unstable, context-sensitive, or socially loaded, and only then have the prescriptivism–descriptivism distinction introduced explicitly in the debrief.
