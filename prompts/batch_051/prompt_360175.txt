I'm a novelist, and I've been doing research for a book set in a big urban hospital. The plot turns on a quality-improvement program that starts publicly reporting surgeons' mortality rates, and pretty quickly the whole thing rots — surgeons avoid hard cases, coding tricks, risk-adjustment gaming, all of it. So I started reading about Goodhart's law, and here's the thing I keep bumping into. The theory, as I understand it from Goodhart's 1975 paper on monetary management, is something like: once you put pressure on a statistical regularity by using it for control, that regularity stops being a regularlity. I'm paraphrasing, becasue I don't have the paper in front of me, but I think that's the core claim. Campbell made a similar point about social indicators, though I'm even fuzzier on his exact wording.

Now here's my problem. That theory feels almost too powerful. It seems to predict that any metric you use for accountability will eventually be corrupted, so all you've really done is move the corruption around or push it deeper. But my intuition refuses to go along. My grandfather ran a small grocery for forty years and kept honest scales even when the inspector wasn't looking. Surgical checklists seem to actually reduce infections without just shifting the lying somewhere else. Baseball on-base percentage still tracks something real even after front offices started optimizing for it. So which should yield? Should I treat Goodhart's law as a universal law about measurement and write my novel as a tragedy where every improvement metric is doomed, or should I treat my intuition that some numbers stay honest as evidence that the theory needs boundaries? Walk me through what each side costs. Is there a reading where Goodhart is right about what pressure does and my intuition is right that some measures stay reliable — maybe because some metrics are structurally harder to game, or because some people have non-strategic reasons to keep them honest — or is one of these just wrong?
