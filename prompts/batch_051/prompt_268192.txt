A recurring move in a seminar I'm taking on modern critiques of religion is to reach for Freud and present his account as an analogy: an individual's obsessional neurosis is to that person what organized religion is to a community. The comparison is being asked to do real argumentative work, not just illustrative work. It is supposed to reframe the question of whether religious claims deserve intellectual engagement at all: once you see ritual washing, scrupulous repetition, avoidance rules, and guilt as the marks of a shared neurosis, the appropriate response looks like diagnosis and treatment rather than argument, evaluation, or even respectful disagreement.

I'd like you to audit how far the analogy actually holds. First, give me the correspondence table as precisely as you can: what in religious life is supposed to map onto the obsessive act, the anxiety, the moral scrupulosity, the sense of being observed, the rigidity, and the apparent irrationality of the neurotic symptom. Then work through where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely incidental. In particular, I want to know whether the analogy quietly imports three things it should be made to earn: that the religious practitioner is the patient and the critic is a neutral clinician; that religious practices are irrational in the way neurotic symptoms are, even though they are shared, taught, publicly rule-governed, and usually not experienced as alien intrusions; and that a collectively inherited practice can be pathological in anything like the sense in which a private compulsion is. Finally, say whether the diagnosis-and-treatment conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether you can offer a better analogy that does less smuggling.
