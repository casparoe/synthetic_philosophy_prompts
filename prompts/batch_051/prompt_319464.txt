Give the specific comments that justify the grade: say what the student got right, what's wrong or conflated, and where they under-argued. Don't just give a general impression.

Exam question (10 points):
Explain the difference between the practice view and the expectation view of promissory obligation. (For reference: the practice view says promissory obligations are constituted by the rules of a social practice; the expectation view says they are generated by the expectations the promisor knowingly leads the promisee to form.) Then apply both views to this case: on her deathbed, Marisol makes her nephew Luis promise that, ten years from now, he will scatter her ashes on a particular mountain. She dies the next day, and no one else in the family knows about the promise or cares. For each view, say whether Luis is obligated to perform, and explain why. Which view handles the case better, and why?

Student answer:
The practice view says promising is like a move in a game: saying "I promise" only puts you under an obligation because there is a standing social practice with rules that say people who sincerely say those words must perform. So the obligation comes from the practice itself, not from the promisee's expectations or from any harm done. This view has a clear advantage in the deathbed case: once Luis said "I promise" in the right circumstances, he invoked the practice rule, so he remains obligated to scatter the ashes even after Marisol is gone.
The expectation view, by contrast, says the wrong in breaking a promise is that you knowingly created expectations in the promisee and then disapointed them. The important thing on this view is the promisee's reliance: by promising, Luis led Marisol to rely on him, and breaking the promise would harm her by frustrating that reliance. This is a nice feature because it explains why the duty is owed to Marisol rather than just to society. But in this case, by the time performance comes due, Marisol is dead, so she no longer has any expectations, cannot rely on him, and cannot be harmed. It follows that on the expectation view Luis has no obligation to perform. So the practice view clearly handles the case better: it says he still must do it, while the expectation view lets him off the hook.
