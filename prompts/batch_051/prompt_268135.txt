For decades the most influential argument against reducing the special sciences has been the multiple realizability argument. In its canonical form it runs roughly as follows: a single kind recognized by a higher-level science—say, a particular belief or a state of fear—is realized by many different physical configurations across individuals, species, and times; but if a higher-level kind is identical with a physical kind, it must be identical with one physical kind; therefore the higher-level kind cannot be reduced to or identified with any physical kind, and the higher-level science keeps its autonomy. The argument's load-bearing empirical premise is the heterogeneity of the underlying realizers.

I have been wondering whether that premise is still doing the work it used to do. The argument took shape when the 'physical level' was individuated mainly in terms of single cells, anatomical regions, or gross physiological events. Contemporary systems neuroscience routinely treats mental states instead as points, trajectories, or equivalence classes in a high-dimensional space of population activity: a state may be decodable from patterns spread across very many neurons, and the neural kind is defined by the population-level geometry rather than by any list of participating units. If one individuates the realizer at that population level, apparent realizer heterogeneity can collapse—what looked like different physical states may be the same kind described at a coarser or more appropriate grain.

Does the multiple realizability argument survive this shift, and if so, exactly which premise is now carrying the weight? One possibility is that population-level kinds are themselves higher-level functional or representational kinds, so the reduction would no longer be to an unambiguously physical target. Another is that the argument never depended on actual cellular heterogeneity, only on the claim that the higher-level kind is realizable in multiple ways relative to any fixed physical vocabulary. My interest is in the fate of the argument itself, not in scoring a point for reductionism: is the argument weakened, forced up a level, or essentially untouched, and what would a repaired version have to say to remain convincing?
