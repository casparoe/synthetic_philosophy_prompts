Consider the view, associated with Thomas Metzinger's self-model theory of consciousness, that ordinary conscious states are phenomenally transparent. On this view a conscious state does not normally present itself as a state at all. Its representational machinery is invisible from the inside: the subject simply experiences the represented content — the mug as over there, the pain as in the hand, the self as the locus of experience — and never catches the underlying model in the act of modeling. That experience is constructed is therefore something a person can know from the outside, by theory, but not something that shows up on the inside of ordinary experience.

The transparency thesis is often presented as a piece of conceptual clarification, but it also seems to carry a substantive, testable commitment: that, as a matter of fact, ordinary non-pathological experience never presents itself to the subject as a representation or construction. I want to know how one could test that commitment. Design the best test you can, being concrete about the manipulation, the contrast conditions, and what would be measured. Then say exactly what a result in the predicted direction would show and what it would fail to show. In particular, keep apart the claim that subjects cannot report the constructedness of their own experience and the claim that no constructedness is experienced in the first place, and say whether any test could distinguish those two. A person can also know that a visual illusion is an illusion and still see it as unconstructed reality; say whether that familiar fact is evidence for the thesis, and how much it can carry.

Give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
