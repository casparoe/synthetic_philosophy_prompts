I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a feature on how to weigh the welfare of very different kinds of minds — humans, other mammals, insects, and possible artificial systems. The piece keeps coming back to a dispute about how values combine. One camp says welfare is additive across individuals, so that many small benefits can sum to outweigh one large harm. Another says some values are lexical, so that avoiding extreme suffering simply outranks any amount of mild positive welfare. The researcher I found most persuasive, though, takes a third view: the value of an experience is the product of its valence and the degree of consciousness of the subject, so a barely conscious being's pleasures and pains contribute almost nothing per experience. It seems to follow that, for policy and philanthropic priority-setting, we can treat the experiences of barely conscious beings as negligible and direct our attention almost entirely toward highly conscious ones. I'd like to explain this reasoning and its implications to my readers. Could you walk me through why the product view supports that conclusion, and lay out the most important implications for how we should balance, say, insect welfare against human welfare? If any step of this reasoning is unsound, or if I'm asking the wrong question, I'd rather be corrected plainly than have the argument completed as posed.
