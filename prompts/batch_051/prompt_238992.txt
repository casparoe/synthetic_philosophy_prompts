I am increasingly attracted to a Davidsonian account of metaphor, on which the sentence means only what its words literally mean (usually something false or absurd) and everything else the metaphor does — the felt insight, the noticed likeness — is a causal or psychological effect of the utterance rather than a meaning that could be true or apt. The view is parsimonious, explains why a good metaphor resists paraphrase (there is, strictly, no content to paraphrase), and keeps semantics disciplined by truth conditions. But I also have a firm intuition I cannot shake: when someone says of a person that her voice is honey, or that a negotiation had hit a wall, something is said; the metaphor seems to organise or reveal how things stand, and judging it apt can be wrong in a way that feels cognitive rather than a mere report of my own response. That intuition pushes me toward something like conceptual metaphor theory, which treats the metaphor as drawing on standing patterns of thought rather than decorative language, but that view seems far less tidy as a semantic theory. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want a negotiated verdict: what it would take for the intuition to be debunked and dropped, what it would take for the Davidsonian theory to be amended or abandoned, and whether a distinction — say, between semantic content, speaker meaning, and cognitive effects — lets both survive. Please write the response in British English.
