I am part of a working group on comparative privacy and state information practices, and at our last meeting someone floated a scheme that I want analyzed as a mechanism rather than judged in advance. Suppose a jurisdiction introduces a monthly "opacity budget." Each adult receives, say, one hundred opacity units per month. Spending a unit allows a person to lawfully refuse a single specified state request for personal information — a census question, a demand that a platform turn over location metadata, a police demand for identification that is not backed by a warrant or equivalent judicial order, a question on a benefits form — and the refusal itself may not be treated as evidence of anything. Units expire at the end of the month and may be traded on a regulated exchange. The state itself may not buy units; instead, ten percent of every monthly allocation is diverted into a public reserve, and an independent board may grant units from that reserve to an agency that demonstrates urgent need, publishing every grant. To keep the market from being smuggled into other relationships, it is illegal for employers, insurers, landlords, and service providers to ask about a person's balance or trading history.

I would like to know how this would actually behave in three settings: a German-style data-protection regime, where control over information about oneself is treated as a constitutional matter and non-collection is the default; an American-style openness regime, where transparency is the default and concealment tends to need a justification; and a Chinese-style social-credit regime, where collection is pervasive and access to services is commonly tied to compliance. Walk through who would sell opacity and who would buy it; how citizens, agencies, and intermediaries would game it; what happens to the social meaning of hiding once concealment has a posted price; and whether the scheme drifts toward coerced participation. The question that interests me is less whether privacy ought to have a price than what pricing concealment would do to who hides, from whom, and under what pressure. End by naming the failure modes you consider most likely and saying which, if any, can be fixed without abandoning the mechanism. Throughout, please separate what is comparatively settled about the incentives from what is genuinely contested among people who work on privacy and state power, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
