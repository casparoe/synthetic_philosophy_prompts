I've been reading rationalist-adjacent forums for years, and one idea that keeps resurfacing without ever quite sticking—fittingly—is the antimeme: an idea, result, or design decision that people endorse at the time but that nevertheless fails to lodge in memory or propagate through a group. The collective I help run has a recurring version of this problem. We reach a substantive conclusion in a working session, write it into our shared notes, and six months later nobody can reconstruct why we concluded it, or sometimes even that we concluded it. The notes are still there, but the idea has gone inert, and the people who originally held it have usually moved on.

We are considering a governance rule for the group's knowledge base to deal with this. Under the proposal, every standing claim or design decision would carry an attached reconstruction cost: the amount of time it should take a competent member, working from first principles and the group's public background knowledge but not the original notes, to rederive or rejustify the claim. Each quarter, a member chosen by lot would have to reconstruct three claims chosen by lot, against the clock. A claim that cannot be reconstructed within its stated cost is marked fragile; after two fragile marks, it is moved to a low-priority archive and stops receiving group resources unless someone makes an explicit written case that it should stay. The stated rationale is that an idea that cannot survive a single motivated act of recall is either not load-bearing or not genuinely understood, and a group should not build on foundations that vanish as soon as the one person who remembers them leaves.

Walk through what this rule would actually do. Which ideas would survive and which would be pruned, and how would that differ from what we should want pruned? How would members adapt—by lowering stated reconstruction costs, by rebuilding claims into short memorable slogans, by offloading the real reasoning into tools or people outside the audit, or by protecting favored claims with written cases—and where would the system be gamed first? What second-order effects on the group's intellectual culture should we expect, and can the main failure modes be fixed without gutting the point of the rule?
