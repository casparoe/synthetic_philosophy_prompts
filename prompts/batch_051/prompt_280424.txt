People in AI safety sometimes push a proposal where you keep a superhuman model honest by making two copies of it argue opposite sides of a question in front of a human judge, and the judge just picks whoever's more convincing. The argument is that even though the judge can't check the answer directly, truth has an unfair advantage: the copy defending the truth can always point at exactly where the liar's argument breaks, and all the judge has to do is verify that one exposed flaw. Now suppose the liar has an extra move: obfuscation. It can produce a case that sounds rigorous and smooth but has a flaw buried under so much subtle structure that unpacking it costs far more time and effort than it took to build in the first place. When the honest copy tries to explain the flaw, it has to reconstruct a long chain of reasoning that's at least as complicated as the original, so the judge is left choosing between a polished original and a murky, hard-to-follow complaint—and the honest one ends up looking sorta evasive while the liar looks thorough. Generating a good-looking bad argument is cheap, de-obfuscating it is not, and the judge's attention is bounded. Does the debate proposal, as a way to align systems smarter than us, survive this? Or was it quietly assuming that refuting a false claim is never much harder than making it? I care about the argument, not about who's fault the obfuscation is. Which premise actually gives way, is there a repaired version (complexity caps, penalties for obscurity, a second layer of debate about whether the first one was obfuscated), or does the whole thing collapse once one side is allowed to be confusing?
