When a company, politician, or public figure is caught in wrongdoing, the standard performance in many countries is the public apology. The person appears before cameras, reads from a prepared statement, declares that they take full responsibility, expresses deep regret for the harm caused, voices sympathy for those affected, and promises to make amends and to do better. They almost never dispute that the act was wrong; if they do, the public typically treats the apology as a failure. And, crucially, the apology is staged before an audience: the public is invited to scrutinize the person's face and voice and to judge not only what is said but whether the remorse is genuine.

What theory of moral repair, and of the difference between guilt and shame, does this practice presuppose? Please address the following. First, does the core of the apology — acknowledging that the act was wrong and committing to repair it — presuppose a guilt-based picture, in which wrongdoing is a violation of a norm the agent already accepts and the remedy is acknowledgment, repair, and recommitment? Second, does the public staging of the apology presuppose something in tension with that: a shame-based picture in which exposure before an audience is itself part of the sanction, and the deficient self, not just the act, is the object of concern? Third, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; several theories may license it. Fourth, briefly describe how the practice would change if its underlying picture were replaced by a fully shame-based one. Then say whether the practice as it stands is coherent, and which of the two emotions it effectively treats as the more important one.

Begin with a one-sentence statement of the practice's central presupposition. Keep the entire response to at most 450 words.
