I have recently taken over responsibility for a working group on family policy in my organisation, and I have been reading Mill's The Subjection of Women alongside the group's papers. The group contains a dispute that has persisted for years and no longer moves at all. Roughly, one side holds that the existing distribution of caring responsibilities between men and women is natural, and that schemes meant to redesign it amount to disregarding the grain of human life. The other side, explicitly indebted to Mill, replies that what the first side calls natural is just long-standing custom that has come to seem inevitable, and that this is exactly the move Mill diagnoses when he examines how the subjection of women sustains itself. Both sides agree on the broad facts about who does what and on many underlying values; what they do not agree on is what would settle the word natural. I have begun to suspect that they are not really having one disagreement. Diagnose this for me: is it a hidden empirical disagreement about the origins of the relevant differences, a conceptual disagreement about what nature means, a normative disagreement about whether naturalness counts as a reason, or a disagreement about which facts are allowed to count as evidence? How would Mill's analysis of the appeal to nature bear on your diagnosis? And given that diagnosis, what would count as progress in this dispute — a distinction, an agreed empirical test, a reformulation of the question — or is the impasse a sign that the question is badly posed? Please write your answer in British English.
