Here is a thesis I would like stress-tested. I call it "doxastic emergency powers". Clifford's principle is that one should not hold a belief on insufficient evidence; James's will to believe replies that where a choice is live, forced and momentous and the intellect cannot settle it, the passional nature may properly decide. I propose that we treat those Jamesian exceptions not as permissions to believe, but as a kind of emergency power, analogous to temporarily setting aside ordinary constitutional restraints in a crisis. On my account, believing on insufficient evidence is never justified as settled belief; it is justified only as a provisional and publicly accountable act. Three conditions must hold: the option must be genuinely live, forced and momentous; the proposition must be adopted as a working assumption rather than asserted as knowledge; and the believer must accept that she will later owe a retrospective justification to the epistemic community, much as emergency decrees are ratified after the fact. This, I think, dissolves the dispute: Clifford is right about beliefs and James is right about actions. Give me a candid assessment. Is this a real solution or a category mistake? What are the three strongest objections, and has the position already been defended under another name, for instance as the idea that one may act on a proposition without believing it? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
