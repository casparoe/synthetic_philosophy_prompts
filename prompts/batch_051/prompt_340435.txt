I am a novelist at work on a book whose narrator must decide how to judge the historical figures who pass through the story, and I have been testing different formulations of the man-of-his-time defence against Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus. I have arrived at a view I call the Asymmetry of Historical Excuse. It holds that the defence really bundles two claims that should be separated. One claim is about moral sentiment: we should not expect a historical agent to feel the wrongness of an act with the fully developed emotional responses of a later century, because visceral disgust, guilt, or shame can be absent when a culture has made an atrocity ordinary. The other claim is about moral inference: we can expect a historical agent to draw a conclusion from considerations that were present in his own world and that his mind demonstrably used elsewhere. The era excuses the first but not the second. On this view, Jefferson’s slaveholding is not excused by his time, because the inference from a shared human nature and natural rights to the wrongness of one group’s domination of another was one his own political reasoning already carried out against a different object; what his time blunted was the feeling that this inconsistency ought to torment him, not the availability of the inference itself. The same structure applies to Columbus’s treatment of the Taíno, where the inference from their evident humanity and political organization to the wrongness of mass enslavement was available in the moral and legal vocabulary of his own world, and to Churchill’s defence of empire and his declarations on racial hierarchy, where the relevant considerations were part of the liberal and strategic reasoning he deployed elsewhere. I do not want a further indictment or exoneration of these three men; I want the principle itself tested. I realize the sentiment-inference distinction may be less stable than it looks, and that the principle may simply smuggle present-day standards in through the back door while pretending to do otherwise. I would like the three strongest objections to the Asymmetry of Historical Excuse, a candid verdict on whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable, and some sense of whether the view already exists under a better name or collapses into something familiar.
