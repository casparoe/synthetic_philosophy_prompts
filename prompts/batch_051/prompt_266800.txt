You are a political philosopher who works on early modern theories of sovereignty and corporate personality. I want you to think carefully about an institutional practice and reconstruct what it presupposes. In early modern Europe, rulers frequently chartered private trading companies whose shareholders were in it for profit, and gave them powers that today would be called sovereign: making war and peace, coining money, administering civil and criminal justice, and, in some cases, collecting taxes and ruling territory and subject peoples. This was not a rare improvisation but a normal instrument of governance for a long period. Yet the juristic theory of the same period often insisted that sovereignty is indivisible and can reside only in a single supreme authority. That looks like a plain contradiction: the one power that was supposed to be indivisible was routinely partitioned and handed to commercial firms. Before settling on your interpretation, distinguish two or three ways of understanding what it means for a power to be sovereign, and see how the practice looks under each. Then answer this: what view of the state, of sovereignty, or of the difference between public and private must the practice have presupposed in order to be coherent? As you answer, mark carefully which of those commitments the practice strictly requires, which it merely fits well with, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, explain what would have to change in our picture of political authority if we still permitted arrangements of that kind today. Please write your answer in continuous prose, as you would an email or a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
