I've been turning over a parallel between automata theory and an argument about immortality, and I can't tell whether it is deep or superficial. In automata theory, a deterministic finite automaton with no input is forced by the pigeonhole principle to revisit states and settle into a cycle; the pumping lemma for regular languages is the recognizer's version of the same fact — any sufficiently long accepted string must contain a loop. I've heard a structurally similar argument about finite minds: a being with only finitely many possible total experiences cannot live forever without its experience becoming repetitive, and on some versions, tediously so. Is the immortality argument genuinely the same formal situation as the finite automaton, or is the resemblance only verbal? If the analogy is sound, does the theorem transfer — must an immortal finite mind eventually cycle through exactly the same total states? If it isn't, where does the mapping break: is the mind not really finite-state, are experiences not really its states, is the world's input doing work the no-input automaton ignores, or something else? I keep getting stuck on one point in particular: a finite stock of experiential atoms is not the same as a finite stock of total experiences, since a finite alphabet supports infinitely many distinct strings. So the argument seems to need the stronger premise that the mind has finitely many total states. Is that stronger premise defensible for a mind that composes, remembers, and reinterprets its experiences, or does the compositional character of experience make the pigeonhole argument idle even if the mind is finite in some sense? And is the step from repetition to tedium part of the same theorem, or a further move that would have to be argued separately?
