I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training. I was drawn into social choice theory while working on a ranked-choice voting system, and Sen's liberal paradox now looks to me like an unsatisfiable specification: Pareto efficiency and minimal individual liberty each seem non-negotiable, yet the theorem says no social decision procedure can satisfy both together. I want a document that states the conflict exactly, separates substantive disagreement from terminological confusion, and could serve as a shared design note that both sides would sign.

Write the joint statement that a committed Paretian social choice theorist and a committed rights-based social choice theorist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on Sen's liberal paradox.

Use the following shared formalism so every claim is precise. There is a finite set of individuals and a finite set of at least three alternatives. Individual preferences are complete and transitive. A social decision procedure takes any profile of individual preferences and returns a complete, transitive social ranking. Pareto says that if every individual strictly prefers x to y, the social ranking must not rank y above x. Minimal liberalism says that there are at least two individuals, each of whom is decisive over at least one pair of alternatives, in the sense that the individual's strict preference between that pair must be reflected in the social ranking. Both parties accept Sen's theorem: no social decision procedure satisfies Pareto, minimal liberalism, and unrestricted domain together.

The two positions to reconcile are these. The Paretian holds that the theorem locates the fault in minimal liberalism as an unconditional output constraint: when a unanimous preference conflicts with an individual's protected choice, the procedure must be allowed to let the unanimity win. On this view, rights are better modeled as restrictions on the alternatives or preference profiles that get put before the group, or as priorities the right-holder may waive, rather than as absolute output constraints. The rights-based theorist holds that the theorem locates the fault in Pareto: Pareto is attractive only as a guard against avoidable collective self-defeat, and a preference about how someone else lives a protected choice is not an admissible input to the collective decision in the first place. On this view, rights are side constraints on what may be aggregated, not output constraints available to be overridden by unanimous preference.

Produce the following, formatted as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list both parties could sign. Cover: the theorem and the structure of its proof, including the strict-preference cycle it produces; what each party counts as a resolution rather than a restatement of the problem; what both agree is wrong with naive resolutions; and any points about the formalism that both accept but that outsiders often get wrong.

2. Disagreements. A table whose rows state the exact remaining disagreements in wording each side would accept for its own view. For each row, give the Paretian wording and the rights-based wording, and indicate whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal.

3. Cruxes. For each row, identify the sub-question such that a settled answer would move at least one side. I expect at least these to belong on the list, but revise or add to them as needed: (a) whether a unanimous preference over another person's protected choice has standing as an input to social choice; (b) whether a protected-pair decisiveness can be waived by the right-holder, and under what conditions, without the view collapsing into the other side's; (c) whether the paradox is best repaired by restricting the admissible domain or by weakening one of the output conditions.

4. Tests and predictions. For each crux, describe a concrete case or argument that both sides accept as bearing on that crux, and record each side's prediction about it in advance. Make the cases simple enough to run as thought experiments, and the predictions specific enough to be falsifiable.

Close with a short statement of what, if anything, both parties agree would actually change their minds, and identify any part of the debate that is purely verbal.
