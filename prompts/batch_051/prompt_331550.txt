I've been trying to sort out when a bigger population is better, and my gut verdicts keep failing to line up behind either of the obvious rules — "more total well-being is what matters" or "average well-being is what matters." I want you to tell me if there is one principle behind my actual verdicts, and if there isn't, which ones I should give up. Here are my cases and how I react to them.

1. Casey and Morgan have two kids and a good household. They're thinking about a third child. The third kid's life would be clearly worth living, but things would get tighter: each of the five people would end up a bit worse off than the four are now — less room, less money, less attention — though nobody's life gets bad. I think having the third kid is a fine outcome, maybe even slightly better than stopping at two. Not required, but not worse.

2. City A: 100,000 people, all at a high level of well-being, call it 90. City B: 130,000 people, all at 86, with the same background as A. I'd call B a bit better than A. I don't feel any urge to protect that four-point average at the cost of 30,000 good lives.

3. Now compare City A (100,000 at 90) with City C: 1,000,000 people, all at 30 — lives that really are worth living, with genuine pleasures and projects, but plainly much worse than the lives in A. The total is much higher. I still think C is a worse world than A. The sheer number of people doesn't make up for how much worse each life is.

4. City D is just City A plus 50,000 extra people whose lives are net bad — bad enough that they'd be better off not existing. The original 100,000 are unaffected. I'd call D worse than A, no question. I include this as an anchor because nearly everyone shares it.

5. Elk case. Future P: 400 elk, all thriving, call it 85. Future Q: those same 400 untouched, plus 40,000 more elk living scraggly but net-positive lives in other ranges, call it 5. Total elk well-being is much higher in Q. I'd prefer P. I won't call Q better; the fact that there are many more elk eking by doesn't make the future better.

What's bugging me: in 1 and 2 I'm willing to trade away some average for more good lives, but in 3 and 5 I refuse, even when the original people/elk aren't made worse off (5) or when the total dwarfs the alternative (3). And 4 says adding bad lives makes things worse. Maybe the difference is just degree — how good the new lives are and how big the average drop is — and maybe a principled line can be drawn there, but it feels like I'm reverse-engineering a curve to save my verdicts.

So: is there a defensible principle (or a small set of candidates) that captures these? State it precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Then tell me honestly which of my five verdicts it keeps and which it can't accommodate, and what it says about a case I keep flip-floping on: City H (150,000 people at 60) vs City I (90,000 people at 85). I want the principle to settle that one, not my gut. Also say whether the principle treats the elk case any differently from the city case, because I suspect I'm being inconsistent about animals. Lead with the principle itself in a short paragraph, then go through the cases; keep the whole thing under 1,000 words.
