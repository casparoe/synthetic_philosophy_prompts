I'm a novelist who teaches fiction in an MFA program. For three years my department's curriculum committee has been stuck, in a polite and strangely symmetrical way, on whether perseverance ought to remain the organizing virtue of our fiction curriculum. The older faction treats staying power as the thing that separates writers who develop from writers who don't: rejection is constant, progress is slow, and the capacity to keep revising through uncertainty is precisely what a degree in writing should build. They want the program to say so openly, in course objectives and in the language of feedback. The other faction holds that this vocabulary, under current conditions in the literary economy, functions as ideology: it takes a system of adjunctification, consolidated publishing, and inherited support and teaches young writers to experience its costs as private failures of character. On their view we are training students to call burnout a moral weakness and to misread precarity as a test of worth.

The strange part is that the two sides agree on nearly all the local facts. They agree that some of our students are stopped by avoidable self-sabotage and others by plain material hardship; that some books take a decade; that persistence is sometimes admirable and sometimes self-punishing. Yet every meeting ends in the same place, each side repeating a version of "you're confusing the individual with the structure" and "you're confusing a virtue with its misuse."

I suspect that what is really going on is that one side is answering a first-person practical question about what to cultivate in an agent, while the other is answering a third-person question about what this language does as a social practice, and that the dispute only looks like a factual disagreement because both are talking about "grit" as if it named one thing. But I don't want to flatter myself with a diagnosis that dissolves the problem too easily.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this: verbal, structural, empirical, or a real moral difference? What would actually count as progress for my committee, given that diagnosis? If the two sides are not talking about the same thing, is there a distinction or reframing that could let the debate take a step forward rather than merely rename it? And if it is a genuine moral or empirical disagreement, what would have to be true for one side to be wrong? I don't want a survey of the resilience literature or a reading list; I want the structure of the stalemate and an honest account of whether the disagreement is as deep as it feels.
