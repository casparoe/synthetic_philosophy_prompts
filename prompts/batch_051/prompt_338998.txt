Here are the mechanisms I've collected for why people overbid in the dollar auction, where a dollar is auctioned off and both the winner and the second-highest bidder have to pay, the runner-up getting nothing. I'm after something new: a mechanism that isn't just a rewording, a special case, or a combination of these.

1. Sunk cost: the bids already paid are gone either way, so they shouldn't matter at the margin, but treating writing them off as a fresh loss makes bidding on feel like it rescues the earlier spending.
2. Loss aversion: once both people are in, dropping out is a certain loss of everything bid so far, while one more bid is a risky chance to recoup some of it; presented with a sure loss people get risk-seeking.
3. Self-justification: persisting quietly validates the original decision to enter, while quitting means admitting the earlier bids were a mistake.
4. Face-saving: quitting in front of a rival or an audience is a public loss, staying in signals resolve.
5. Competitive arousal / goal substitution: the prize switches from the dollar to beating the other bidder, so paying more than the dollar is worth stops feeling like the point.
6. Entrapment: each extra increment is small compared with what's already sunk, so the choice is always between a tiny next step and a big accumulated total, and the whole path never gets re-evaluated.
7. Anticipated regret: dropping out now and then watching the other person win for only slightly more than your last bid feels more vivid and aversive than the abstract downside of overpaying later.
8. Local rationality / war of attrition: conditional on the other bidder still being in, one more bid can look like the best move at every stage—if they quit next round you win, and if they don't you're only one increment deeper—so two locally sensible people chain themselves into a terrible outcome.

If you can't, say why this list might be complete.
