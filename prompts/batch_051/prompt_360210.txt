As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have been struck by how selectively we use Aristotle's old suspicion of money-making. In the Politics, he describes coinage as a device invented to facilitate exchange among households, then argues that the kind of money-making which aims at increasing money itself is a corruption; of all such practices, he says, interest is the most contrary to nature because it makes money, which was introduced to be a medium of exchange, function as a breeder of more money. Today a pension fund compounding at four per cent is just good stewardship, and a central bank setting interest rates is ordinary macroeconomic management, but when a payday lender or a currency speculator profits without creating a product, many of us reach for a similar charge of unnatural predation. Is there a principled difference between interest that is treated as legitimate and interest that is treated as parasitic, or is the difference only one of degree, respectability, and time horizon? Put Aristotle's argument to work: if his claim that money is naturally barren is false, does the moral judgement survive the failure of the biology? Please answer in British English.
