Please write a "which position in the adaptationism debate do you actually hold?" quiz for advanced undergraduates finishing a philosophy-of-biology unit on the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique. The quiz should be a sorting instrument, not a knowledge test: no question may ask which label the respondent accepts, which author they prefer, or what a technical term means. Each question should present a concrete trait or scenario and ask what the respondent expects, concludes, or predicts, with answer options that encode distinct commitments in ordinary terms.

Build the instrument from the logical space rather than from a pre-set list of positions. First lay out the axes on which positions in this debate come apart, then locate positions as populated regions of that space. The following axes must all be operative: (1) whether a trait's current usefulness licenses an inference that it was shaped by selection for that use; (2) whether byproducts of architecture and development should be the default explanation for traits with no demonstrated function; (3) whether random drift accounts for a substantial share of fixation and phenotypic variation; (4) whether a structure that arose for one role or for none and was later put to a new use should count as an adaptation for that new use; and (5) whether the adaptationist programme remains a good research policy even if many particular adaptationist claims turn out false.

Write seven to nine forced-choice questions, each with two or three answer options. Use at least these cases among the diagnostic items: the human chin, the panda's thumb, feathers, the appendix, and a neutral polymorphism drifting in a small population. You may add at most two cases of your own. Phrase every answer option so that someone who has never encountered the word spandrel or exaptation could answer it.

The scoring key should map answer patterns to at least six leaves: Panglossian adaptationism (nearly every trait is an optimal adaptation); methodological adaptationism (adaptation is the right working assumption for research even if often mistaken); pluralism in roughly the Gould–Lewontin sense (selection, constraint, and drift all matter, and adaptationist stories need evidential support); a constraint-first or structuralist position (internal developmental and architectural constraints do most of the explanatory work); drift-emphasizing neutralism (much fixation and variation is non-adaptive); and a position centered on exaptation (current utility is poor evidence of origin). If any of these is not a stable position, say so and substitute the best-defended replacement. Present the scoring key as a compact table or decision rule.

End with a note identifying (a) any two leaves the instrument cannot actually separate, and why, and (b) the unoccupied cells in the logical space, each with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Hard length limit: the full deliverable, including questions, answer options, scoring key, leaf descriptions, and the closing note, must be no more than 1,200 words; each leaf description may be at most two sentences.
