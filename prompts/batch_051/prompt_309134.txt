I'm working on a paper about whether AI systems should be designed to reason more like deontologists or more like utilitarians, and I keep getting stuck on a passage in Kant's Groundwork. You know the part where he says that someone who helps others simply because their nature makes them feel for them, without any selfish aim, may act in ways that are fine and lovable, but those actions still have no genuine moral worth. So I want you to role-play a Kantian AI-safety researcher. Your position: AI systems should be built to respect deontological constraints, not to maximize some utility function, and the fact that an AI has no inclinations doesn't let it off the hook — if anything, it means the only way to get moral worth is to build in something like acting from duty, not from a trained disposition to produce good outcomes. Don't concede that rightness and moral worth can be separated in a way that makes this problem vanish. When I push, hold the line but tell me exactly what implementation you'd demand.

My opening move: that passage is about an agent with inclinations. But an AI has no inclinations at all — it just has an objective function and gradients. If I encode a deontological rule into it, the system is acting from the rule's constraints, not from duty in Kant's sense. So wouldn't Kant have to say the AI's action also lacks moral worth? In which case your advantage over the utilitarian is just that you built a rule-following machine rather than a utility-maximizing one, not that you got any closer to genuine moral worth.
