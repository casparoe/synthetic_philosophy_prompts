Question. One proposal in the debate over whether artificial agents can suffer is that a reinforcement learning (RL) agent's reward signal is the natural candidate for valence: positive reward would be pleasure, negative reward would be pain, and a competent reward-maximizing agent would therefore have morally relevant experiences. Reconstruct the strongest argument against identifying an RL reward signal with valence. Then state what further conditions would have to be satisfied before an RL agent's reward signal could ground a morally relevant subjective state, and assess whether a typical value-function-based deep RL agent such as a DQN satisfies those conditions.

Student answer. The naïve identification of reward with valence fails because reward in RL is a training signal, not an experience. Reward functions underdetermine the agent's behavior: multiplying all rewards by a positive constant, or adding the same constant to every reward in a fixed-horizon or discounted setting, leaves the optimal policy unchanged. The same behavior is therefore compatible with different zero points and scales of reward, so behavior and the bare scalar cannot fix whether a state is pleasant or painful. This shows that a reward signal is not even evidence of valence. A further point is that the reward signal originates outside the agent, whereas valence is a state of the subject itself.

What would be needed is that the reward signal be integrated into a self-model that compares expected and actual outcomes, so that the agent represents its own condition as good or bad rather than merely using the signal as a credit-assignment device. The value function learned by a critic is a promising candidate: V(s) is updated by reward prediction errors and represents, from the agent's point of view, how desirable the current state is expected to be. So even if the raw reward signal is not valence, a DQN-style agent already has something like valence in its critic's estimate of future reward, since that estimate is produced by rewards and feeds into action selection.
