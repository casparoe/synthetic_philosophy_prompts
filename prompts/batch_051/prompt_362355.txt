I've been trying to make sense of revealed preference theory, and I keep getting stuck on the kind of case where a person's own choices seem to point in two directions at once. Let me lay out the criteria I'm using for saying a choice reveals a preference.

A choice of x over y reveals a preference for x over y when: (1) the agent could have had y instead, (2) the choice isn't made under coercion or by some obvious mistake, and (3) the agent's pattern of choices is stable enough that we'd bet on it to predict what they'll do next. Utility, on this picture, is just whatever number represents the ranking those revealed preferences give us — no hidden hedonic stuff behind it.

Now the case. Imagine someone with a serious smoking addiction. Every morning she says she doesn't want to smoke, she's signed up for a quit-smoking program, she pays for therapy, and she's asked her partner to hide any cigarettes they find. But by mid-afternoon, most days, she buys a pack and smokes it. She's not being coereced by anyone else, and it's not that she's mistaken about what's in the pack or what it'll do to her health.

Does her afternoon choice to buy and smoke reveal a preference for smoking over not smoking? It seems to satisfy my three conditions: not smoking was available, nobody forced her, and her behavior is depressingly predictable. But her morning choices — enrolling in the program, paying for therapy, telling people to hide her cigarettes — look like choices too, and they seem to reveal the opposite preference. So either she has two preference rankings (one in the morning, one in the afternoon), or one of these sets of choices reveals her real preference and the other doesn't, or my criteria are missing something.

Which is it? Walk through the case criterion by criterion, and tell me whether my criteria need to be changed — maybe by adding something about when the choice is made (craving versus not), about second-order preferences, or about whether her choices cohere into a single ranking at all. I care less about the addiction itself than about whether revealed preference can carry the weight I'm putting on it when utility is supposed to tell us something about a person's good.
