I've got a recurring argument in my research group that has basically ossified. One faction says you keep a research programme alive as long as it keeps generating novel predictions, and that when core assumptions run into trouble you adjust the protective belt rather than junking the whole thing, because that is just how Lakatos said science actually works. The other faction says that is unfalsifiable post hoc rationalisation: by the time you have decided a programme was degenerating you are reading the outcome back into the story, and the real explanation for sticking with a dying programme is sunk costs, grant capture, and people who built their careers on the hard core. We agree on the historical record, mostly, and we agree on what actually happened; we just dont agree on whether what happened was rational. My working diagnosis is that the two sides want different things from a methodology: one wants a rule that could have told you in advance when to abandon ship, and the other has given up on that and now wants a causal-sociological explanation of why fields actually change, so it treats any rational reconstruction as a story we tell afterwards. If that is right, the disagreement is less about whether Lakatos was correct than about what a methodology of science is even for. Is that the right diagnosis? If not, what kind of disagreement is it actually — a hidden empirical question about how people really change fields, a difference in standards of evidence, bedrock intuitions about rationality, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress, or is this one of those disputes that should be dissolved rather than settled? I want a diagnosis with reasons, engaged with those two positions, not a summary of Lakatos. Please write it as continuous prose like an email, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables.
