ok so in my law-ish elective we were reading Warren and Brandeis's 1890 article The Right to Privacy, and there's this one passage I kept rereading: "The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury."

If Im reading them right, theyre giving a picture of what makes something a privacy violation: the thing exposed has to be part of someone's private or domestic life, it gets put out there to other people, and the real damage is a kind of mental pain or distress, like worse than getting physically hurt. So I came up with a case that doesn't fit neatly. Imagine a guy who takes a drug that eliminates all negative feelings about being watched—he genuinely cannot feel embarassed, creeped out, anxious, nothing—and without his knowledge someone installs hidden cameras all over his apartment, including his bed and shower, and live-streams the feed to a small anonymous online forum. The people watching don't know his name or where he lives. He consented to the drug but obviously not the cameras, and he never finds out about the stream. Does this qualify as a violation of his privacy under Warren and Brandeis's criteria? Go through each criterion honestly. If the mental-distress one fails, does that show their account is missing something—like privacy is really about control over who sees you, not just about feeling bad—or is it fine to say his privacy wasn't violated because he'd never care? Also, does the fact that the viewers can't identify him change anything?
