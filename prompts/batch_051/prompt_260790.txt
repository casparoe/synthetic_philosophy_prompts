At a decision-theory reading group I have attended for years, two regulars spent most of an evening arguing about the Ellsberg paradox, and neither would concede. The setup they used was the standard one. Urn A contains 100 balls, 50 red and 50 black. Urn B contains 100 balls, each either red or black, but the proportion is unknown. You must name a color, a ball is drawn from the urn you select, and you win if it matches your color. Most people prefer to bet on urn A whether they name red or black, and expected-utility theory holds that these two preferences cannot be rationalized by any single probability assignment: preferring urn A on red implies assigning red in urn B less than 0.5, and preferring urn A on black implies assigning black in urn B less than 0.5, which cannot both hold. I know enough of the surrounding decision-theory discussion to have followed each move, but not enough to referee it, so I am reconstructing the exchange below.

(1) Sam opened with that expected-utility argument and called the pair of preferences a straightforward inconsistency. (2) Rhea answered that expected utility presumes the agent already has a unique subjective probability over every relevant event, and the lesson of the paradox is precisely that a rational agent facing urn B need not have one: she can hold a set of possible proportions, and choosing urn A for both colors is not an incoherent point-valued probability but a refusal to gamble with unquantified odds, since symmetry does not force a 50/50 prior. (3) Sam replied that a set of probabilities is not itself a decision rule; once you adopt a rule for choosing in the face of such a set, such as maximizing minimum expected utility, you reproduce the Ellsberg preferences, but the rule carries serious pathologies — it is driven entirely by the single most pessimistic distribution, it can assign negative value to information, and it can be dynamically inconsistent across a sequence of choices. (4) Rhea countered that those pathologies are artifacts of importing a one-shot rule into sequential settings: there are coherent ways to update a set of priors by updating each member of the set on the observations, and the alleged money pumps require the agent to walk into a foreseeable bundle of trades. She added that precise probabilities are the exception in real decisions, so a norm requiring a unique prior ends up hiding a point guess inside the formalism. (5) Sam responded that the Ellsberg urn is the artificial case: the composition is already fixed, and the only thing missing is the description of it, so being averse to the unknown is not robustness to a real feature of the environment. In real problems with base rates and data, refusing to quantify is not caution but a sophisticated-sounding license for status quo bias. (6) Rhea said that in practice most problems look more like urn B than urn A, and choosing robustly across the models one cannot rule out is ordinary good practice rather than a pathology; the alternative, completing one's preferences arbitrarily whenever probabilities are not handed to us, is what actually smuggles guesswork into decisions.

They stopped there, agreeing only that the argument turns on whether rationality requires a unique probability over every relevant event. I genuinely could not tell who was right at which step. Then give an overall verdict, including what each side is missing. I am less interested in which of them wins than in locating exactly where the disagreement is still live.
