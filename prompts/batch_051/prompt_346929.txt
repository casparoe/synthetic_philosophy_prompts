Construct a decision tree that sorts answers to the question: what makes an evaluative attitude—loving, admiring, revering, being devoted to, or treating something as an end—go wrong? Treat "idolatry" as the umbrella term for the whole phenomenon of valuing something the wrong way, in religious and non-religious cases alike. The tree should be built from the logical space of possible answers before it is built from a list of familiar positions: first work out the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they make available, then locate the actual positions in that space, and note the empty cells.

Audience and purpose. The tree is for a reading group of advanced undergraduates in value theory who have not read the specialized literature on idolatry or on fitting attitudes. It will be used as an instrument for each member to identify what they actually believe, so every diagnostic question must be stated without technical vocabulary or position labels: answerers should be sorted by their answers to concrete cases and plain claims, not by which named view they recognize themselves in.

Required cases. The tree must use the following five cases as the material for its diagnostic questions (you may use a case in more than one question, and you may add cases only to separate leaves that the five cannot separate):
(a) a woman whose romantic relationship is the sole source of meaning in her life and who cannot function when it wobbles;
(b) a man whose devotion to a football club is entirely groundless—he inherited it and has no reason to support this club rather than any other—yet stable, joyful, and harmful to no one;
(c) a manager who comes to treat a performance metric as the thing that matters, because it is what gets measured and rewarded, thereby reversing means and ends;
(d) the members of a movement who treat their leader as beyond criticism and owed unlimited deference;
(e) a person who values money for its own sake, knowing it will never buy him anything he wants, and hoards it only to watch the number rise.

Required positions. The following positions must appear as leaves (give the leaf a neutral name if one exists, and write "unnamed" if the position has no standard label):
- the fitting-attitude position: a valuation is wrong when it fails to fit the value-making properties of what it is directed at—loving the unlovable, admiring the not-admirable, revering the merely finite;
- the ordered-love position: the error is not that a finite good is loved, but that it is loved out of its proper order, as though it were the highest good;
- the single-currency position: all value reduces to one underlying thing (such as well-being or preference satisfaction), and error is a miscalculation of how much of that single thing something delivers;
- the structural position: the defect lies in the form or role of the valuing itself—for example, reversing means and ends, objectifying a person, or letting one local good absolutize itself over all the rest;
- the consequentialist position: a valuation is misplaced when holding it predictably worsens things, including by undermining itself or crowding out better attachments;
- the recognition position: the defect is relational—granting to something a deference, trust, or standing that is owed only to persons (or, in a theological version, only to God);
- the no-error position: there is no standard of correctness external to valuing, so "misvaluation" is at most rhetorical, and things have exactly the value they are given.

Branching constraints. The first split in the tree must be the existence question: whether there can be such a thing as valuing wrongly at all, independently of what anyone happens to value. For the branch that says there can, the second split must be the question of where the defect is located: in the object valued, in the valuer's reasons or evidence, in the form or role the valuing plays in a life, in its downstream consequences, or in a relation of recognition. Further splits are yours to design, but each branch must be labeled by the concrete claim or case that induces it.

Unoccupied combinations. After locating the positions, go back through the full combinatorial space generated by your questions and identify the cells that no live position occupies. For each empty cell, say whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, coherent but unattractive and therefore undefended, or coherent and merely unexplored. Do not invent a position to fill a cell for the sake of completeness.

Separability. Where two required positions cannot be separated by any question in the tree, flag this explicitly: say which leaves merge, explain what would distinguish them in principle, and state why no question that can be answered without the labels manages to separate them.

Format and size. Aim for 9 to 13 diagnostic questions and 7 to 10 leaves. Present the result as (i) a nested text outline of the tree, with every branch labeled by the claim or case that splits it, and (ii) a table or list in which each leaf carries its name, the path of answers that reaches it, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment—the case or consequence its own defenders would most like to soften.
