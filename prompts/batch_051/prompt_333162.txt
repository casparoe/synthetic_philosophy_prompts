I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been trying to work through the foundations of Bayesian inference on my own. A slogan keeps appearing in discussions and documentation as though it were self-evidently true: "When you have no information, the rational prior is uniform." Recently a colleague used it to settle a disagreement about whether a model with no informative prior knowledge should place a uniform prior over a rate parameter or over its logarithm. Her argument was that since genuine ignorance cannot favor one value over another, the uniform distribution is the unique rational representation of ignorance, and so our disagreement was not a real one.

I suspect that this one sentence packs several different claims together, and I want them separated before I either accept or reject the conclusion she drew. What distinct precise claims could "When you have no information, the rational prior is uniform" be making? It might, for instance, be a claim about symmetry among possible values, about expected information or maximizing entropy, about invariance under reparameterization, about betting odds and indifference, about worst-case behavior, or about something else I have not considered. For each reading: Is it trivial, defensible, or false? Does it survive changing variables, such as moving from a rate to its logarithm or from a probability to its log-odds? And which reading, if any, actually supports the conclusion that the uniform prior is the uniquely rational representation of ignorance? I would like the readings laid out as a menu rather than a single answer, with a verdict on each, and I would also like to know which of them, if any, extend to the maximum-entropy principle when some partial information (for example, a known mean) is available.
