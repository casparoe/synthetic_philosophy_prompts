I have been piecing together a view on distributive justice over several years, and I want to know whether the commitments I have accumulated form a coherent whole. They are as follows:

(1) I hold a Nozick-style entitlement theory: justice in holdings is historical, not patterned. A distribution is just when, and only when, it has come about through just acquisition and just transfer, with rectification for past violations; whether it satisfies any structural ideal is irrelevant.

(2) The Wilt Chamberlain argument settles the case against patterned principles: once people are free to make voluntary transfers, any favored pattern will be upset, so a state that insists on a pattern must continuously interfere with liberty. I therefore reject all end-state and patterned principles of distributive justice.

(3) Because existing holdings descend from expropriation, conquest, and slavery, the rectification condition reaches nearly all present wealth; very few current titles are clean.

(4) Rectification is individual and counterfactual: it asks what particular holdings would now exist had particular wrongs not occurred, and restores those holdings, rather than moving toward some independently attractive distribution.

(5) Since the relevant counterfactual histories are almost never knowable, I also accept that rectification in practice should proceed by a statistical proxy: transfer resources until no group's current distribution of wealth reflects the expected legacy of past expropriation.

(6) A bequest of property that was itself legitimately acquired is a paradigm case of a just transfer, and taxing such a bequest for redistributive purposes is unjust.

(7) A steep tax on large inheritances is nevertheless justified as a way of financing rectification.

(8) The state may use coercion only to enforce individual property rights and to carry out rectification, never to bring about a distributive pattern as such.

I cannot tell which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to. Please audit the set rather than lecture me on it: identify the real tensions, rank them by severity, say which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and state the least costly revision that would make the whole set coherent, including what that revision would cost me. I need discriminations, not a list of every possible conflict.

At the very end, on its own line, state the single most severe tension in the form \answer{...}, using the numbering above (for example, \answer{5 vs 1,2,4}).
