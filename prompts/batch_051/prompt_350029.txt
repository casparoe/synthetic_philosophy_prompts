Two of my labmates got into it at journal club about Alan Turing's 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence, specifically the section called The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception. Neither of them could agree on what Turing is doing there, and they asked me to referee, but I honestly couldn't.

The passage as I remember it goes roughly like this: Turing says these objections against machine thinking are all things he can answer, but the one from ESP is a real problem. He claims the statistical evidence, at least for telepathy, is overwhelming. If telepathy exists, he says, then in the imitation game the interrogator's thoughts could leak to the contestant, so the only way to keep the game fair would be to put the interrogator in a telepathy-proof room. He also says something like that telepathy would explain some otherwise puzzling facts about miracles and faith healing, and that he'd like to see the statistical findings repeated before changing his mind much.

Anyway, the argument went like this.

Maya said this is one of the rare places where a major scientist takes anomalous evidence seriously instead of just ruling it out by prior. She said Turing's move is methodologically elegant: he doesn't endorse telepathy, he treats it as an unchecked channel of information and proposes a control for it. If you're testing whether a machine can pass as a person, you should close every channel an opponent could use, even ones you think are unlikely. That's just sound experimental design, not credulity.

Sam said no, the section is an embarrassment precisely because Turing ranks ESP among genuinely serious objections to machine intelligence. He said Turing's line about the statistical evidence being overwhelming is just slumming in parapsychology, and by 1950 the evidence was already contested and non-replicated. Worse, a telepathy-proof room isn't a real experimental control because there's no way to specify what would make the room telepathy-proof or to verify that you've achieved it. It's a fantasy control dressed up as engineering.

Maya shot back that Sam was confusing a prior probability with a practical precaution. She said you don't have to believe telepathy is real to guard against it in a test any more than you have to believe your opponent is cheating to demand that they keep their cards above the table. Turing's move is conditional: if telepathy exists, the imitation game as originally set up is compromised. That's true regardless of how low you set the prior.

Sam replied that no, the card-table analogy fails because vision is a known channel with known physics and known countermeasures, while telepathy is a channel whose existence isn't established. So there is no operational difference between a room that is telepathy-proof and one that isn't. He also said Turing's comment that telepathy would explain miracles is armchair theology, not science, and that the correct response to bad parapsychology evidence is better replication, not folding ESP into the design of the imitation game as if it were a live option.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of Maya's and Sam's points, say whether it is clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, and explain briefly. Also say what both of them are missing about what Turing might actually be doing in that section.
