Your task is to draft the joint statement that a committed memeticist and a committed cultural attraction theorist could both sign at the close of a good-faith adversarial collaboration on this question: do cultural variants replicate, and is "meme" more than a metaphor?

Open with a brief prospectus. It should name the two positions and state the question in terms neither party would reject. The memeticist's position is that cultural variants—tunes, stories, words, skills, rituals—are discrete replicators that differ in longevity, fecundity, and copying fidelity, so culture evolves by a Darwinian process acting on variants. The cultural attraction theorist's position is that transmission is transformative reconstruction shaped by cognitive and ecological attractors, so resemblance across generations is largely convergence rather than faithful copying, and the meme picks out no causally real unit. The prospectus should also identify the shared phenomenon the collaboration examined: a serial-reproduction chain in which forty adults each hear a brief narrative once and reproduce it for the next participant; across the chain, one formulaic sentence survives nearly verbatim, two sentences are paraphrased into local idiom while keeping their gist, one surprising sentence is repeatedly replaced by a familiar proverb, and the story shortens and grows more conventional. Both parties accept this as a fair stylized description of such chains.

Then give two sections.

Section 1, Agreed claims. A numbered list of at least eight claims that both parties can sign. Include (a) the shared descriptive facts about the serial-reproduction chain, phrased without either side's explanatory vocabulary; (b) broader points both accept, including that cultural variants exist and change through transmission, that some variants spread while others die out, and that human cognition biases what is transmitted; and (c) methodological commitments both accept, such as distinguishing drift from selection and requiring models to track individual transmission episodes rather than merely matching aggregate frequencies.

Section 2, A disagreements table with six rows. Columns: (i) label; (ii) the issue as the memeticist would word it; (iii) the issue as the cultural attraction theorist would word it; (iv) the crux, as a sub-question whose answer would move at least one side; and (v) a case, experiment, or argument both sides accept as bearing on the crux, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. The rows:

1. Replication or reconstruction. Is ordinary cultural transmission best modeled as copying of discrete variants with occasional error, or as inferential reconstruction in which each recipient produces a new token?
2. Fidelity and cumulative adaptation. Is transmission fidelity high enough at the grain of the variant to support cumulative, selection-driven adaptation?
3. The unit of selection. Is there a principled way to individuate memes across a chain of transmissions?
4. Selection or attraction as the main engine. In the serial-reproduction chain, is change better explained by differential replication among discrete variants or by convergence toward stable, easily reconstructed attractors?
5. Predictive purchase. Does Darwinian vocabulary generate predictions unavailable to an attraction-based model, or does it mainly redescribe the same outcomes?
6. The verbal residue. One disagreement the collaboration should uncover as at least partly verbal; state what dissolves under clarification and what, if anything, remains.

In the table, no entry may be vacuous, slanted, or a caricature. Each side's wording must be something its own proponents could sign without strain.
