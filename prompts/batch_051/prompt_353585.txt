I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy; I read in it on my own and mostly in the evenings. I recently read Augustine's On Lying (De Mendacio), and a passage near the beginning has unsettled me. He writes:

"Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie."

If I understand it, Augustine is claiming that whether an utterance is a lie is settled entirely by the speaker's inner state — what he believes and what he means to convey — not by whether the sentence itself is true or false.

That is elegant as a definition, but set against ordinary practice it exposes an asymmetry I cannot account for. Consider two colleagues. Amara tells the team, "The build will pass," believing it will; the build fails. Everyone says she was mistaken, not lying. The falsehood of the words is set aside because her mind was not divided; that tracks Augustine. Now Ben tells the team, "The build will pass," believing it will fail and intending to keep the team working; by luck, the build passes. Here ordinary judgment, in my experience, flips to the other rule: people say he "technically told the truth," and he is treated much more gently than he would have been had the words been false — although on Augustine's criterion Ben's mind is exactly the mind of a liar.

So the truth-value of the sentence is ignored in Amara's case and made decisive in Ben's. In each case it is invoked in whichever direction produces the softer verdict: for Amara, the false words matter little because the mind was honest; for Ben, the dishonest mind matters little because the words happened to be true. That looks like a standing bias toward leniency, implemented by two contradictory rules.

My question is whether any principle generates this pair of judgments, or whether it is an inconsistency we merely tolerate because each verdict feels humane on its own. The best justification I can construct is epistemic: other minds are not directly observable, and the truth or falsity of the words can be a serviceable proxy, so we might reasonably lean on signs rather than inner states. But that does not seem to justify the direction the practice takes. In Ben's case the inner state is stipulated to be known, and still the true sentence is allowed to soften the verdict; in Amara's case we do not let the false sentence harden the verdict, even though the same proxy logic might. I would like the strongest case that this asymmetry is rational. If it cannot be made, should we adopt a single rule instead — and if so, should it be Augustine's, so that Ben counts as a liar and a deliberately misleading true statement is judged by the speaker's intent, or is some other unified rule better? Please test the candidate justifications against nearby cases, and end with a verdict: keep the ordinary practice, revise it, or abandon it.
