I keep chewing on a parallel and can't tell whether it's deep or just a verbal similiarity. Take the debate over state apologies for historical wrongs—slavery, colonial massacres, that sort of thing. The standing objection is that nobody alive today did the deed, so the apology is either empty or it's making me confess to someone else's sin. But that's basically the intuition behind the old doctrine of corruption of blood: the idea, banned in the US constitution for treason cases, that a convict's descendants are tainted and can be stripped of their inheritance. So here's the worry: when a government apologizes for a wrong from generations ago, is it quietly re-importing a kind of inherited collective guilt that liberal legal systems threw out on purpose? Or does the analogy break down fast, because an apology is expressive and creates no liability, and because states (unlike persons) are treated as continuous across generations—they inherit treaties and debts, after all? I'd like the answer to lay out the mapping carefully: what would have to be true about state identity, collective responsibility, and the difference between apologizing and punishing for this to be a real structural parallel rather than a loose vibe. And name a prediction the parallel would make that turns out false. Also say how confident you are in the overall verdict, and single out the point you're least sure of.
