I'm a law student, and my roommate and I keep comeing back to an argument about statutes of limitations. The law seems to treat time as mattering in wildly different ways depending on what was done and how. In most US states and plenty of other jurisdictions, murder has no limitation period at all, and over the last few decades many places have also removed the limit for rape and some other serious felonies. But embezzlement, fraud, assault, and defamation all have limits, often short ones — a couple of years for defamation, three to six for fraud. Civilly, the clock usually runs from discovery of the harm for fraud or medical malpractice, but defamation runs from publication, and some jurisdictions have statutes of repose that cut off claims after a fixed number of years even if nobody could have discovered the injury in time. The clock is also tolled for minors and for people who are mentally incapacitated, and sometimes while the defendant is out of state. So time doesn't just pass: it expires some wrongs, freezes others, and doesn't touch a few at all.

I'm less interested in the doctrinal details than in what this whole practice presupposes about the moral relevance of time. Does it commit the law to the view that wrongdoing decays — that a claim loses force as it recedes into the past? Or is the practice better read as a pragmatic bundle (evidence goes stale, defendants need repose, factfinders can't reconstruct distant events) that doesn't actually presuppose any particular moral view about time? Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and flag places where the pieces seem to presuppose inconsistent things. Then tell me: if someone held that time is morally irrelevant and a wrong is a wrong forever, what would they have to change in this body of law, what would they not have to change, and is the current law closer to that view than it looks?

Please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this — don't present a contested story as the consensus. And lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, without any preamble or restating of my question.
