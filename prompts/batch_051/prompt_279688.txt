I am a high school junior and the student representative on my school district's Character Education Review Committee. Next week the committee votes on a recommendation to the school board, and I will circulate a short written decision memo before the meeting. Please draft that memo.

For the past two years the district has piloted a character education program in two middle schools. The program teaches six core virtues — honesty, respect, responsibility, fairness, compassion, and perseverance — through weekly lessons, adult mentoring, and a character points system that will eventually appear on students' permanent records. Supporters point to improved student self-reports and teacher ratings of classroom conduct. The district's own spot checks, however, found no difference between the pilot schools and matched non-pilot schools on several objective measures: detected cheating on unproctored online quizzes, reported hallway and bus bullying, and rates at which borrowed sports equipment was returned. Students who earned the most character points also showed no observable advantage on these measures over students who earned none.

A competing proposal from the district counseling office would retire broad character instruction and replace it with situational interventions: proctored quizzes, honesty confirmations administered at the moment a student submits work rather than pledges made weeks earlier, redesigned hallway and cafeteria layouts, and teacher training in small environmental prompts. Its advocates argue that behavior tracks immediate circumstances more strongly than it tracks global traits. Opponents answer that this treats students as systems to be managed rather than as people to be formed, and that it surrenders the long-run goal of building character.

The committee is deadlocked between a virtue-first faction and a situation-first faction. Underneath the policy question is a philosophical dispute: whether there are stable traits such as honesty or compassion that schools can cultivate, or whether much of what looks like character is in fact a response to immediate situations. I need the memo to take a clear position: recommend expanding the program district-wide, recommend the counseling office alternative, or recommend a narrower middle course — for example, keeping the virtue language but certifying situation-specific habits rather than global traits — with explicit conditions. The memo should give the school board a concrete course of action with a time frame and criteria for judging success in future years. It should also engage the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously rather than caricaturing it.
