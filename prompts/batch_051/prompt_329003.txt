I'm a software engineer and I've been turning over a double standard about copying that I run into constantly. If I sit in a design review and someone describes a clever pattern—say, a way to batch writes to a database or a state modeling trick—and I go implement my own version of it, with my own variable names and file layout, everyone calls that competence. Copying a competitor's feature is basically the industry. But if a diff ever shows that I pasted someone's actual class or function from a public repo into our codebase, suddenly its a serious offense: lisence problems, "stealing code," maybe getting fired. The underlying insight and the effort that produced it are the same; the only difference I can see is how far down the ladder of specificity I copied.

Attribution doesn't seem to be the real issue either, because even if I leave a comment saying where I got the code, it's still treated as a different kind of act than copying the idea and reimplementing it. The law seems to draw a line between an idea and its expression, but that line looks strange to me because in my day job I'm constantly moving between abstraction levels: any concrete implementation is just an instance of a more general idea, and any idea is almost useless until you make it specific enough to run. So what looks like "the expression" is just the idea at a lower level of abstraction. Is there actually a defensible reason for this asymmetry, or is it a convention we've stopped noticing? If you defend it, I'd want you to deal with the version where I rewrite the thing—new names, new comments, reordered functions—but keep the same logic. At that point what's left of the moral difference?
