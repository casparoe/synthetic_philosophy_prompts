I help run a philosophy-and-medicine discussion group, and we're putting together a consent workshop for a mixed room of medics, law students, and people who just enjoy a fight. We want to open with a "which account of consent do you actually hold?" handout quiz, the kind where readers answer a bunch of concrete cases before they've seen any labels, and then get sorted into a position they didn't know they held. We've noticed people sometimes hold a stricter line on being lied to about a partner's age than on being left in the dark about what a procedure will actually do, or the reverse, and we want an instrument that exposes the pattern rather than letting people pick the label that sounds most principled. So none of the questions should ask anything like "do you think consent must be informed?" — they should all be cases.

Could you write the quiz? Nine or ten questions, each a short concrete scenario (e.g., a surgeon omitting a rare but serious side effect before someone signs the form; a partner lying about being single; a landlord heavily implying the tenant has no real choice about letting him in; someone agreeing because they didn't know they could refuse; someone saying yes while wery drunk; a care worker getting consent from a resident with early dementia). Each question should offer three responses: "fully valid", "valid but the consent-seeker has done something wrong", or "invalid". Then provide a scoring key. Please make sure the key can sort people into at least these five positions, marking any that are best left "unnamed": (1) a mental-state view on which consent is fundamentally an inner act of agreeing, so hidden facts matter only if they change what the person took herself to be agreeing to; (2) a communicative or performative view on which consent is a public act, and private reservations don't undo it; (3) an informed-authorisation view on which omissions matter as much as lies whenever they're material; (4) a token-act view on which consent is always to the particular act performed, so being misled about anything that matters for that act means the person consented to something else, not to what happened; (5) a stricter hierarchical view on which background power imbalances can vitiate consent even when nobody lied or threatened. If the answer combinations your questions allow contain cells that don't map onto any of these, name the empty cells and say in a sentence whether each is empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. And if two of these positions can't actually be separated by the cases you've written, flag that rather than papering over it.

Finally, please write it in British English, and add a short afterword for the facilitators noting what is settled about all this and what is genuinely disputed by people who work on consent, so we don't accidentally pass off one contested account as the consensus.
