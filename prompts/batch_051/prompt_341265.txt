I'm writing a novel about a game designer who quits a big studio to make a serious little game about a war, and as research I've been playing a lot of war games and writing down my gut reactions. The problem is my reactions probably don't add up. A fast, polished single-player shooter set in a real-ish modern war, where every kill pops up on a scoreboard and the game hands out medals for killstreaks, genuinely repulses me; it turns a real kind of suffering into a sport. A dumb mobile defense game with little cartoon tanks that explode into confetti doesn't bother me at all, even though it's also war-as-fun. A game where you play a medic who triages the wounded, loses patients, and never gets to turn any of it into spectacle feels to me not just okay but important. A very grim game whose most-discussed scene forces you to use white phosphorus on what turns out to be a crowd of civilians, and then makes you walk through the burned bodies afterward without rewarding you for any of it, I would defend; it wanted me to feel complicit, on purpose. A multiplayer Second World War shooter where you can queue as the German side but both teams are mirror images, no ideology anywhere, just uniforms and gun stats, leaves me mildly grossed out, but I can't say why. A wacky cartoon war game with ragdoll deaths and silly quips, but set in the actual Vietnam War with real unit names and real battles, I find more wrong than the confetti-tank one, maybe worse than the scoreboard one. And a drone-operator game where you fire on blurry thermal shapes from a trailer and then go home and watch TV between shifts: it's dull and flattening and awful to play, but it feels like it's telling me something true about modern war, and I can't call it glorification.

Is there a single principle about what makes a war game glorify war, take it seriously, or defuse it into harmless play that gets all of these, or nearly all of them? I don't just want a taxonomy. I want the cleanest principle (or two candidate principles, if you can't pick one), which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and what it predicts about two games I haven't played: a gentle, awarding-winning puzzle game built around a real concentration camp, and a children's game where you dig cartoon trenches and lob silly green gas clouds at each other. And tell me how much work is being done by the line "it's fine because it's not real."
