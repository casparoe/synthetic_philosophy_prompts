I have spent the last few months arguing with two friends about how values combine, and the disagreement has produced a view I want checked before I keep defending it. One friend holds a straightforwardly additive view: the value of an outcome is the sum of the values of its experiential parts, so enough mild pleasures can always outweigh any single bad. The other holds a multiplicative view: the value of an experience is something like its degree of consciousness multiplied by its degree of happiness, so a vivid moderate pleasure can outrank a faint intense one, and the trade-offs come out differently from the additive picture. I have ended up defending a third position, which I call threshold lexicality.

The claim is this. The value of a composite state is additive only above a threshold of acceptability; below that threshold—when one component is an experience the subject could not, in full information and on reflection, endorse undergoing again—its negative value becomes lexical, so no amount of merely mild goods can compensate it. Above the threshold, goods and bads trade off roughly additively, with diminishing marginal returns. The view is meant to be neutral between hedonistic and preference-based accounts of value, and to apply both within a single life and across lives. I am aware that it sits near lexical-priority and negative-leaning positions, but the two-tier structure is supposed to do work those views leave undone.

I would like an honest assessment in four parts. First, is the view coherent as stated, or does the 'acceptable to undergo again' test smuggle in the very lexical judgement it is meant to explain? Second, has this already been worked out under a name I should know, and what, if anything, distinguishes my version from existing lexical or critical-level views? Third, where does it break—across persons, over time, under risk, or in the gap between mild chronic discomfort and brief acute agony? Fourth, are any of the strongest objections fatal rather than merely uncomfortable, and does the view give plainly wrong predictions for cases such as a small chance of catastrophe or one pinprick against a lifetime of bliss? Please write your answer in American English.
