Here is an argument I have been developing for a paper, and I want to know whether it goes through or exactly where it breaks. The paper's thesis is that quantum indeterminism cannot supply what libertarians (those who hold that free will is incompatible with determinism and that we nonetheless act freely) need from physics, because replacing determination with chance only makes the action less attributable to the agent, not more. The argument runs as follows.

(1) An action is free and the agent is responsible for it only if the agent controls the action.
(2) Suppose a decision depends, at the neural level, on a genuinely indeterministic event E: the complete physical state of the agent's brain immediately before E does not determine whether E occurs.
(3) If the complete state of the agent's brain immediately before E does not determine whether E occurs, then nothing about the agent — her beliefs, desires, values, and intentions — settles whether E occurs.
(4) If nothing about the agent settles whether E occurs, then whether E occurs is, so far as the agent is concerned, a matter of chance.
(5) If whether E occurs is a matter of chance, then the agent does not control whether E occurs.
(6) If the agent does not control whether E occurs, and the decision depends on E, then the agent does not control the decision.
(7) Therefore, a decision that depends on a genuinely indeterministic neural event is not one for which the agent is free or responsible. Quantum indeterminism, far from making room for free will, turns the decision into a lottery.

I have a vague sense that something shifts meaning between (3) and (4), or between (4) and (5), around the phrase "a matter of chance," but I cannot make the two readings precise. Is the argument valid? If it is, which premise is the least costly to reject, and what does rejecting it commit one to? If it is not, identify the exact step at which it fails, name the term whose meaning shifts, and give the corrected version of the step under the reading the argument needs.
