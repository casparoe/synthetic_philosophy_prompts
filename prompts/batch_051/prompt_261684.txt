A line of argument I keep hearing in debates about AI deception runs like this: calling a large language model a liar is like calling a GPS a liar when it sends you down a wrong street. A GPS has no beliefs about where you are, no understanding of the instruction it emits, and no aim to make you believe anything; it simply outputs an inaccurate route. So, the argument concludes, a model that produces falsehoods is at most erroneous, not dishonest, because deception requires a kind of representation and intent these systems lack. I want you to audit the analogy itself. Give the correspondence table: what plays the role of the map, the route planner, the spoken instruction, the driver, the destination, and the road network? Then lay out the disanalogies that matter: what features of the GPS case does the analogy quietly import that the model case lacks or changes, such as the driver's background understanding that the device is not asserting in the way a person does, or the fact that the GPS's errors are not reinforced by its interactions? Finally, say whether the argument's conclusion survives once those disanalogies are on the table, and propose a better analogy if this one cannot carry the weight.
