I have a habit that keeps annoying my family : whenever someone says a generic thing like ducks lay eggs or mosquitoes carry malaria, I immediately want to know what the exception structure is — which ducks, how many, under what conditions. My brother, who did some philosophy in grad school, tells me this is a category error: generics aren't universal claims, so treating them as though they need a "most" or a "typically" to be true is just refusing to speak ordinary language. But the reason I got this habit is that I once acted on a generic-sounding warning and it went badly: a neighbor told me, sincerely I think, that bats carry rabies, and I panicked and called animal control on a grounded bat when actually the crucial thing was whether this bat, the one on the ground, was behaving rabid. The exception structure mattered for my decision.

So my policy now is: I don't accept a bare generic as a reason to act until I either know something about the domain or the cost of being wrong is low. If I can't investigate, I treat it as true-for-practical-purposes only when the action is cheap and reversible. My brother says this is using a linguistic fact to justify a blanket skepticism about other people's ordinary speech. I think I'm extracting the only usable evidence a generic actually gives. Is my policy defensible? Is there a more principled way to get evidence out of a bare generic, and when, if ever, should I just believe one? Also, can you separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who study generics, because my brother presents his view as the obvious consensus and I suspect there's a live argument there.
