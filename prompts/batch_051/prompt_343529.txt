You are a professor of moral philosophy who specializes in moral psychology and the theory of action. Please think carefully and do not settle for the first principle that comes to mind. Begin by distinguishing two or three plausible accounts of what makes a right action reflect well on the agent—for example, accounts that stress the agent's own judgment, accounts that stress the features of the situation that actually justify the action, and accounts that stress the motives the agent is really moved by. Then work through the case below using those accounts, and only then give your final judgment. Be explicit about where your reasoning is solid and where it is tentative.

I manage a small nonprofit that serves clients regardless of their background. One of my most reliable staff members, Rhea, was raised in a religious community that teaches that helping members of a certain group is wrong, because it allegedly rewards weakness and interferes with God's judgment. We recently took on a client from that group. Rhea recognized the client, did not ask to be reassigned, and quietly spent several weeks giving the client extra assistance—extended appointments and some of her own money toward a housing deposit. Later, in a supervision meeting, she told me she believes she did something wrong. She said she did not help because helping was the right thing to do; she helped because the thought of this particular person going without made her feel so terrible that she could not stop herself. If she had been stronger, she said, she would have refused. By any ordinary standard, her actions followed our rules and genuinely helped the client.

I need to decide whether this episode should count in Rhea's favor. My question is not about organizational policy. It is this: can an action deserve moral praise—can it show something good about the agent—when the agent herself sincerely believes the action was wrong, and when she describes her own motive not as the action's rightness but as an inability to bear the alternative? If you had to state the principle behind your answer, what is it? And does your answer change if the thing that made her unable to turn the person away was the very thing most of us would cite as what made helping right: the client's evident need?
