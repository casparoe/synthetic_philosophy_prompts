I am a PhD student in cognitive neuroscience, working on the relation between the sensory and affective dimensions of pain, and I have been collecting cases and recording my intuitive verdicts. But I cannot extract the principle behind the verdicts, and I suspect some of them may have to go. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher with expertise in the philosophy of mind and the theory of well-being, and enough background in the neuroscience of pain to follow the cases. Please think carefully and do not simply assign my cases to a familiar label. Before settling on an answer, brainstorm several candidate principles and test each one against the cases below, noting exactly where it fails or has to be stretched. Then present the best candidate, or a small set of finalists, stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. Be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and say what it predicts about at least one further case I have not listed. I suggest the masochist case at the end.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. Asymbolia. A patient of the kind described in the pain-asymbolia literature, after a small stroke, is tested with a noxious stimulus such as cold-water immersion or a firm pinprick. She identifies the sensation at once as sharp and burning, localizes it accurately, and rates its intensity 8 out of 10. But she does not grimace, withdraw, or complain. Asked whether it hurts, she says, roughly, that she feels a strong pain-like sensation but that it does not hurt or bother her, and that she would not pay to avoid it. My verdict: she is still in pain. I would not say the stroke extinguished the pain and left only a pain-like tactile sensation, even though what she calls 'hurting' is missing. I also want to say that her state is not bad for her in the way pain normally is — perhaps not bad at all.

2. Morphine. A post-operative patient given morphine reports that the pain is still there, just as intense and in the same place, but that it no longer bothers him: he can feel it, he says, but he does not mind it anymore. My verdict: the pain persists and is genuinely less bad; the drug has not merely lowered the intensity but has detached the badness from the sensation.

3. Hypnotic dissociation. In a laboratory, a volunteer rates a noxious heat pulse as 8 out of 10 for intensity and 7 out of 10 for unpleasantness. Under a suggestion aimed only at the affective dimension, her intensity rating stays 8 out of 10 while her unpleasantness rating drops to 1 out of 10; she insists the sensation feels exactly as strong and exactly as burning, only no longer aversive. My verdict: it is the same pain, and it is much less bad; the two rating scales are tracking two distinct components of pain.

4. Congenital insensitivity. A child with congenital insensitivity to pain feels touch, pressure, and vibration, but has never experienced burns, cuts, or fractures as painful. She is repeatedly injured because she does not favor or protect an injured limb. My verdict: she lacks pain altogether, not merely the unpleasantness; the distinctive sensory quality of pain is missing as well.

5. The athlete. A footballer finishes a match with a fractured wrist. He reports that during the match he felt almost nothing from the wrist, although he kept using it; minutes after the match, intense pain appears in that same location. My verdict: the pain began after the match, not as an unnoticed pain during it. Nociception was occurring throughout, but nociception is not yet pain.

6. Wanted pain in labor. A woman in unmedicated labor says the contractions are among the most intense and unpleasant sensations she has ever had, but she also says she would not trade the experience away, that the pain felt meaningful, and that it was not bad for her in the way a toothache of equal intensity would be. My verdict: her pain is intensely unpleasant in the narrow, phenomenal sense, but it is not bad for her in the all-things-considered sense. The two senses of bad can come apart.

7. Bad news and chronic pain. A chronic low-back-pain patient rates his pain 6 out of 10 for intensity and 5 out of 10 for unpleasantness on Monday. On Tuesday, after being told that the pain reflects progressive damage to the spine, he still gives 6 out of 10 for intensity but now gives 8 out of 10 for unpleasantness, saying that the sensation feels exactly the same but is harder to bear. My verdict: the pain became more bad without any change in its sensory character; the badness of a pain is sensitive to what the pain is believed to signify.

8. The bristle. The same asymbolic patient from case 1 is touched firmly with a stiff brush. She rates the sensation as sharp and intense but says it is not painful, and she says she would have said the same before her stroke. My verdict: this is not pain, even though it is sharp, intense, and does not matter to her at all. So a sharp sensation one does not mind is not yet pain.

My working hypothesis has been a two-component view: pain is a sensory-discriminative state with a distinctive pain quality, and its badness is a separable affective-evaluative layer. Case 4 seems to show the sensory quality is necessary, case 1 seems to show the evaluative layer is not necessary for pain, and cases 2, 3, and 7 seem to show the layer can be damped or amplified independently of intensity. But I doubt this hypothesis is fine-grained enough. It does not explain the difference between cases 1 and 8: if the pain quality is characterized independently of unpleasantness, I cannot say what it is; if it is characterized partly through its normal link to unpleasantness, then the asymbolic patient may turn out not to have pain after all, against my verdict. It also does not handle the two senses of bad in case 6, and it makes the evaluative layer look at once bodily, as in cases 2 and 3, and cognitive, as in case 7.

Please repair or replace this two-component principle, or show me which of my verdicts I must give up. And please apply your account to at least one further case: a competent adult masochist who seeks out painful stimulation during consensual activity and reports that the sensations are both painful and unpleasant and that he wants them precisely as unpleasant. Is his pain bad for him, and in which sense — if either? What does your principle say, and does that force me to revise any of the verdicts above?
