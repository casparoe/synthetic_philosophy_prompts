I have been playing with a distinction I'd like checked. I call it "conferred inheritance" versus "transmitted inheritance." Conferred inheritance is the part of a parent's economic standing that is delivered to a child before death, through choices the parent makes that the child does not directly own: schooling, neighborhood selection, enrichment, introductions, co-signing, and the way the child's preferences and expectations are formed. Transmitted inheritance is the part that passes afterward through probate, trusts, and estate instruments. My central claim is that a well-designed tax on transmitted inheritance will fail to equalize opportunity almost exactly to the extent that conferred inheritance is doing the real work, because the tax arrives after most of the competitive advantage has already been delivered. I also suspect that conferred inheritance is not merely timing-shifted bequests; it has its own logic, since parents can observe the child and meter spending in response to the child's effort. So the two should be analyzed as different mechanisms, not one transfer with a clock. Is this distinction already in the literature under another name, and is the central claim actually true? Push back if the question is badly framed.
