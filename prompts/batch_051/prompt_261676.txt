I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas desk and I cover AI. Roughly once a week a lab or startup pitches me some version of the same story: they moved from outcome-based supervision to process-based supervision, and that's why the model is now safer, more capable, or less prone to reward hacking. I'm tired of nodding along.

What I can realistically do with a pitch: read a preprint or technical blog post, send the lab a handful of questions, and spend maybe 90 minutes before I have to tell my editor whether there's a story. I dont get access to training data or internals, and half the time the evals I'm handed are obviously cherry-picked. What I want is a reusable decision procedure — a checklist or a flowchart in words — for deciding when a process-based-supervision claim is doing real work and when it's just outcome supervision with extra steps, or a safety story dressed up with the word "process."

Some knots the procedure needs to handle. Process supervision is expensive: you need step-level labels or a trained process reward model, and somebody has to decide what counts as a good step. Outcome supervision gets sparse and noisy when you only learn the outcome much later, if ever. Process rewards can be gamed by plausible-looking reasoning that doesn't actually produce the outcome. And the sharpest version I've heard is that the "process" being supervised might just be the step-by-step text the lab chooses to surface, not whatever computation actually caused the behavior — in which case you can end up training the model to write tidy justifications while the underlying behavior barely moves.

I suspect the right call depends on things like whether the outcome is cheap to check, how long the verification lag is, whether the process itself is part of what the user consumes, and whether the process features you're rewarding are genuinely correlated with the outcome rather than merely looking correlated. Turn that into tests I can run in order, flag the hard cases, and mark honestly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. I need the procedure to tell me when to kill a pitch, when to send a specific followup question, and when a lab deserves the benefit of the doubt even if I don't like them.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical pitches: (1) a lab says it trained a math-reasoning model with per-step process rewards and beat its own outcome-reward baseline; (2) a company says it now supervises a deployed customer-service agent by having a second model critique each step of the conversation instead of only checking whether the customer's problem got resolved; (3) a lab says it uses process supervision to train a research assistant that does weeklong literature reviews whose final quality nobody can score for months. Make at least one of these come out in a way I won't like. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do — the claims it simply can't adjudicate from where I sit.
