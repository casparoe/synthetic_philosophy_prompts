Last night my two housemates, Mara and Jonas, got into it over dinner about our friend Priya, who just came back from a ten-day silent meditation retreat. Priya had told us she'd come to know something she couldn't put into words. Mara thought we should take that seriously; Jonas thought the whole idea was confused. I was stuck at the table while they went back and forth. Here's roughly how it went.

Mara said: "Priya isn't pretending to have a theory. There are plenty of things you know without being able to say them. You know how to keep a bicycle upright, you know your mother's face, you know what a mango tastes like. None of those reduce to a sentence. So 'I can't say it' doesn't by itself mean 'I don't know it.'"

Jonas said: "But all of those are checkable by someone else. I can see you stay on the bike. I can hold up a photo and ask if it's your mother. The mango, fine—two people can eat the same mango. What's Priya's check? If she can't say what she knows, then neither she nor we can distinguish knowing something from just having a vivid feeling. That's the problem, not that she lacks a theory."

Mara: "That standard is too high. You know the layout of our kitchen; you've never once described it, and you'd have to stop and think to do it. Does that mean you don't know it until you describe it? Knowing isn't the same as verbalizing. And Priya isn't unchanged: she's stoped treating every setback as a catastrophe, she's calmer without being spaced-out. That's a public difference."

Jonas: "But if the difference is public and describable, then the so-called ineffable content is doing no work. We can just say she learned to take setbacks better. That's a fine, ordinary, effable thing. Once you move the evidence to her behavior, you've stopped defending 'she knows something that cannot be said' and started defending 'she changed,' which is much weaker."

Mara: "You're mixing up the content and the effects. She can point at the effects without being able to state the content itself. Think about knowing what it's like to be heartbroken. Nobody can hand you that in a sentence, and it's 'private' in exactly the sense you're worried about, but you don't say a heartbroken person knows nothing when they know that."

Jonas: "Knowing what heartbreak is like isn't a claim about how the world is; it's just having undergone an experience. If Priya only meant 'I had an experience I can't fully describe,' I'd accept that. But she's claiming something stronger—that she came to know some truth. And for a truth to count as known, there has to be a difference between getting it right and getting it wrong. I don't see where the 'wrong' is here."

Mara: "There is a wrong: you can fake the calm, or mistake a pleasant emotional state for insight, and it'll fall apart under pressure. Priya's claim is that what she's seen won't fall apart that way. The test just unfolds over months rather than in a sentence. That doesn't make it not-knowledge; it makes it hard to assess from the outside."

Jonas: "That might make her claim more respectable, but notice what you just did: you gave criteria. You said it should survive stress. So it isn't unsayable at all—it's just hard to put into words right now. And if that's all 'ineffable' means, I've got no quarrel; she's just in the early stages of articulating something. But that's not the mystical reading she seemed to mean."

Mara: "Maybe you're right that she meant something stronger than 'not yet articulated.' But I think the stronger thing is still coherent. Some knowledge is a way of seeing that can only be shown in a life, not stated as a fact. The demand that she state it as a fact is what's doing the work in your objection."

Jonas: "And I think 'shown in a life' either is a slow, public kind of saying—in which case we're back to ordinary knowledge—or it's just a way of saying she's different now, and being different isn't knowing. So we keep going around the same circle."

They both looked at me and asked me to referee. I honestly couldn't; every time I thought one had it, the other would say something that moved me back. You're a professional philosopher. Please think through the whole exchange carefully, and give each argument its strongest version before you decide. Lay out each separate claim made in the argument and assess it one by one. Then say who is right about which specific points, who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing about the relationship between saying, knowing, and showing.
