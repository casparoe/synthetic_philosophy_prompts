I am a law student who has been following a single judge on my state's intermediate appellate court—call her Judge Voss—because her votes in cases about strict liability and mens rea first looked like plain strict-liability enthusiasm, but now seem to me to be balanced between two different principles. I want help working out which principle actually drives her, and then predicting how she would vote in a pending case.

Over the past four terms she has voted this way. In a food-purity prosecution she voted to uphold a $300 fine against a bakery that shipped bread with an undeclared preservative after its manager skipped the usual supplier-change check; she reasoned that the statute exists to protect consumers, and that a licensed food business cannot convert its own carelessness into immunity from a fine. In a motor-carrier case she voted to uphold a fine against a trucking company whose dispatcher relied on the shipper's stated tonnage instead of weighing the truck, contrary to the company's own written policy; she said the company was the party best placed to prevent the violation and had failed to do so. In a pharmacist's prosecution for selling a misbranded drug, she voted to reverse a conviction where the mislabeling originated inside a sealed manufacturer's carton and no retail inspection could have discovered it; the statute authorized up to a year in jail, and she reasoned that the legislature should not be read as attaching a jailable offense to conduct no amount of care could have prevented. In a zoning case she voted to uphold a $150 ticket against a homeowner who rented a room without a permit after twice ignoring mailed city notices. In an auto-parts case she voted to reverse a retailer's conviction for selling a brake part with an internal defect traceable to the manufacturer, because the retailer could not have discovered the flaw and the offense carried possible incarceration.

The case now on its way through the lower court is this. A beekeeper moved her operation to a new county and attempted to comply with the state's apiary-registration statute, which makes a first failure to register a strict-liability civil offense punishable by a flat $250 fine. She completed the online form, paid the fee, and received an on-screen confirmation number. Because of a database error on the county's side, the registration was never recorded for the new county, and her hives sat unregistered for seven months before the mistake came to light. She is fined. The record establishes that she did everything a reasonable person would have done to comply. How should I expect Judge Voss to vote on the fine if the case reaches her?

I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the reasoning. Please lay out the principle or principles that best explain her prior votes, explain any competing principle that fits those votes just as well, say what each candidate principle requires in the beekeeper case, give your best prediction about her vote and identify the closest analog among her earlier cases, and then propose one further hypothetical case whose facts would cleanly separate the candidate principles. Write your answer as a continuous email or letter, in prose only—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
