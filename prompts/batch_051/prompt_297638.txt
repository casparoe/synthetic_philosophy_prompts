I am a teaching assistant for a course on the ethics of artificial intelligence. A student has given me the following argument, which she built from a standard intentionalist account of deception. The conclusion seems plainly unacceptable to me, but I cannot decide which step she should give up, and I do not want to grade her paper by feel. Please work through the argument and tell me exactly where it should be resisted, and what that resistance costs.

(1) An agent A deceives an audience by producing a statement p only if A produces p with the intention that the audience come to believe p, where p is false.

(2) A language model has no beliefs or intentions of its own: for any output p, there is no fact of the matter about whether the model takes p to be true, and no aim in emitting p beyond the token probabilities that generated it.

(3) Therefore, no output of a language model is something the model itself asserts, and no output is the model's own deceptive act.

(4) The human agents who develop and deploy the model also fail condition (1) with respect to any particular false output p. Before deployment they cannot intend that a user come to believe p, because they cannot anticipate which false sentences the system will emit; and at the moment of emission no human is selecting p. They may know that the system will sometimes emit falsehoods, but that is a general expectation about a class of outputs, not an intention with this or that false proposition as its content.

(5) So, for every false p that a deployed system actually emits, neither the model nor any human developer or deployer satisfies condition (1) with respect to p.

(6) Hence even a system that reliably emits false statements dressed in the syntax and citations of medical competence — for example, fabricated drug-interaction cautions — deceives no one. The claim that the system deceived the patient is, on this argument, literally false.

(7) If that claim is literally false, then honesty and deceptiveness are not properties such a system could have. The only evaluative dimension left would be accuracy: the rate at which outputs match some external standard.

(8) But honesty is not reducible to accuracy. A system can be highly accurate yet misleading in what it surfaces or omits, and a less accurate system can be honest in the way it flags uncertainty. Since we do and should evaluate systems along this second dimension, the conclusion in (6) and (7) must be wrong.

Which numbered claim should be rejected, and why does it look forced by the ones before it? What exactly does rejecting it force us to give up — for instance, in how we distinguish deception from accidental misleadingness or mere unreliability, or in how we attribute psychological states to systems and the institutions that deploy them? I am most reluctant to give up (1), because it is what separates deception from being misread; but I also cannot accept the conclusion in (6) and (7). I need a precise, step-by-step diagnosis rather than a general impression.
