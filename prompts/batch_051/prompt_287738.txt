I'm a philosophy professor, and I've been kicking around an insitutional design for a constitutional commission and want to see it stress-tested. It's a supreme court with three chambers: Non-Interference can strike a law only for negative-liberty violations (new coercive interference with a protected sphere of action); Self-Mastery can strike only for positive-liberty violations (leaving people too ill-fed, unschooled, or manipulated to run their own lives); Non-Domination can strike only for republican-liberty violations (creating or entrenching unaccountable power over someone). Any chamber can strike, but Self-Mastery and Non-Domination can jointly override a Non-Interference strike when they show the interference is necessary for self-mastery or non-domination—not the reverse. Pretend you're a political philosopher who works on liberty and institutions. Don't just give a verdict: think carefully, brainstorm the incentives for legislators, litigants, and chambers, then work through how the thing would actually behave—who files first, how interests redescribe one law in all three vocabularies, where it gets captured or gamed. Then use one or two concrete policies (a minimum wage, a teen curfew, campaign finance) to show where the three concepts genuinely collide, and say whether the override is an honest compromise or just papering over the fact that they're not three species of one thing. Is the system stable? Can its failures be fixed without collapsing the three into one metric? Keep it American English, please.
