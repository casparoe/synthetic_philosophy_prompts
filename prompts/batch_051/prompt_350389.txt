I have spent years collecting a friend's verdicts about public officials and whether they kept their integrity or became corruptible, and I would like to work out what principle she is actually applying. She is not a philosopher; she spent a long career in public administration, and her judgments are quick but impressively consistent. Her earlier verdicts, as I have reconstructed them from our conversations, are as follows. She called a city comptroller corrupt for voting to renew a contract with a software company in which the comptroller secretly held shares, on the ground that the comptroller stood to gain from her own decision and concealed it. She called a procurement officer corrupt for accepting a week's stay at a supplier's holiday home while the supplier's bid was one of two finalists for a contract the officer would recommend, because the benefit came from a party with a live matter before him. She called a school board member corrupt for accepting a substantial gift card from a textbook publisher the week before the board voted on adopting that publisher's series, and then voting for the adoption without disclosing the card. She called a regulator corrupt for accepting tickets from a trade association while her agency was drafting rules that would bind the association's member banks, and then sitting through a meeting where the association pressed its position. But she said a planning commissioner had done the right thing and remained clean when he recused himself from a vote because he had once worked for the developer years earlier, on the ground that he had nothing to gain from the vote and removed himself from it. Now I have come across a newer case. A public health official leads a drug approval process to its final decision. The day after the decision is issued, the manufacturer's representative sends her a bottle of wine worth about sixty dollars with a note thanking her for handling the process fairly. She reports the gift to her agency's gift registry the next morning, as required. There is no other matter pending between her office and the manufacturer, and she had no prior arrangement with the representative and no reason to expect the gift. I can see several principles that would make her earlier verdicts hang together, and they seem to part company on this case. What would she most likely say about the official if she is trying to be consistent with herself, which of her earlier cases is the closest analogy, and if her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, what principle would I be attributing to her under each choice, and what further case would cleanly separate those principles? Please answer in continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
