I have a policy for forming a view about how much a species can suffer when I dont have time to look into the actual science: I use brain size, specifically brain size relative to body size, as the default. If a species has a lot more brain than I'd predict from its body mass, I treat it as probably having more going on subjectively—more pain, more richness of experience, more of whatever makes a creature matter—until I see a reason to change my mind. My friend says this is just cranial chauvinism, a tape measure doing my ethics for me. He points out that brain size is at best a noisy proxy for the underlying properties that actually matter, and that it fails in predictable ways: birds have tiny brains by mammal standards and some of them seem plenty sophisticated, and a blue whale has a brain several times the size of mine without, I'm guessing, being several times more sentient. I can't deny any of that. But I also can't investigate every species before I act, and the alternatives all look like lazy proxies too—cuteness, fur, having a face, genetic closeness to us. I'm not saying brain size is itself the thing that matters; I'm saying it might be the rational thing to act on when I'm ignorant and somebody has to make a call. Is my policy defensible, or is it just bias with a ratio attached? Under what assumptions about how brain size correlates with the morally relevant properties would it be the right shortcut, and is there a version of the rule that keeps whatever is good about it while dropping the chauvinism?
