I am a PhD student in political science working on technology competition, and I have been trying to adjudicate an argument that came out of a seminar on the rationalist theory of war. The framework begins from the puzzle that war is ex post inefficient: since fighting destroys resources, there should always be some settlement that both sides prefer to actually fighting, so rational actors with common knowledge of the relevant payoffs and the ability to commit credibly should never come to blows. The framework then accounts for observed wars by identifying the preconditions whose failure makes fighting rational despite its wastefulness: private information with incentives to misrepresent, credible commitment problems such as preventive war or first-strike advantage, and issue indivisibility. A colleague has now extended this framework to competition over advanced AI, and he has produced the following argument, which seems to me to prove too much, though I cannot locate the fault myself.

(1) If the parties to a conflict are rational, and their payoffs, beliefs, and the structure of their interaction are common knowledge, then they will not end up in an outcome that is Pareto-dominated by some feasible agreement they could all credibly commit to. (2) An all-out AI race is Pareto-dominated in exactly this way: the same frontier capabilities could be reached through a slower, coordinated, jointly financed research program with less duplicated effort, lower deployment risk, and a smaller chance of catastrophic accident, leaving every major party at least as well off as it would have been under the race. (3) So rational developers should reach and keep such a bargain rather than race; on the rationalist framework, racing is intelligible only when one of the three standard failure conditions—private information with incentives to misrepresent, credible commitment problems, or indivisible stakes—actually obtains. (4) But contemporary AI competition appears to be a case in which those conditions are absent: the costs and risks are publicly documented, coordination is not blocked by any technical impossibility, and leading labs do not obviously need to misrepresent their capabilities. (5) Hence either the rationalist framework gives the wrong account of technological competition, or the participants in the AI race are systematically irrational.

His conclusion strikes me as stronger than the premises entitle him to, since the same line could be used to explain away any costly competition whatsoever. I want to know exactly where the argument goes wrong: which step should be rejected or reformulated, what the corrected version of that step would say, and whether the rationalist framework, properly applied, actually predicts that AI races can be a rational response to a strategic situation rather than a refutation of the framework.
