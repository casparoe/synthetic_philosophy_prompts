In "Theoretical Impediments to Machine Learning with Seven Sparks from the Causal Revolution" (arXiv:1801.04016, 2018), Judea Pearl defends a three-level hierarchy of causal inference: association, intervention, and counterfactual. He writes:

"The second level, Intervention, ranks higher than Association because it involves not just seeing what is, but changing what we see. A typical question at this level would be: What happens if we double the price? Such questions cannot be answered from sales data alone, because they involve a change in customers behavior, in reaction to the new pricing."

Large language models are trained by a purely associative objective—next-token prediction over a corpus of text—yet they now answer many interventional and counterfactual questions fluently, including questions they were never explicitly trained on, and researchers increasingly use them to propose causal hypotheses and experimental interventions. Does Pearl's hierarchy claim survive this development? Identify which premise the LLM case bears on: is next-token prediction over text really training on "sales data alone" in Pearl's sense, or does text encode rung-2 and rung-3 information in a disguised form? If the argument needs repair, state the repaired claim; if it survives unchanged, say precisely why the apparent counterexample fails.
