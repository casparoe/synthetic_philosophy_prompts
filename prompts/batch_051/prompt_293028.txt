I retired a few years ago from a career in experimental physics and have since been reading seriously in the philosophy of artificial intelligence. I have been asked to help mark a short-answer question for an introductory philosophy of technology course, and I would welcome a careful second opinion. Below are the question and the student's answer.

Question.

Some researchers have proposed that an artificial agent's side effects could be kept in check without a full specification of human values. The idea is to define a quantitative measure of the agent's impact on its environment and to penalise the agent for impact beyond what its assigned task requires. Discuss whether "avoiding side effects" can be made precise in this way without a full specification of value. Your discussion should engage with (i) the choice of a baseline against which impact is assessed and (ii) the choice of a metric for comparing states of the world.

Student's answer.

Conceptually, a side effect is a change in the world brought about by the agent that is not part of its assigned task. The appeal of impact measures is that they appear to bypass the hardest part of value alignment: one need not specify a utility function over complete histories, only a penalty for moving the world away from an appropriate baseline, such as the state immediately before the agent acts, or equivalently the state that would have obtained had the agent done nothing. By penalising the number of state variables that change, or the loss of future options for acting in the world, an agent can be discouraged from, say, breaking the crockery in order to wash it faster. This is plainly weaker than full value alignment, since it requires only a comparative judgement that one state departs further from the baseline than another. At the same time, the approach is not value-free. Any distance metric must weight the variables of whatever representation is used, and the designer's choice of representation is itself evaluative, since moving a cup and altering a patient's morphine drip will be treated as commensurable unless the metric says otherwise. It follows that an impact measure cannot succeed unless the designer already possesses an explicit specification of the things that matter, which is exactly a specification of value. The baseline also comes apart, because the state immediately before the agent acts was itself produced by earlier agents and earlier side effects, so any choice of baseline starts a regress with no principled stopping point. Even so, if the aim is only to suppress large, irreversible changes, a coarse impact penalty may remain useful as a regulatory stopgap, even if it cannot resolve the underlying philosophical problem.
