I'm taking a decision theory class, and a friend and I got into it about the St. Petersburg paradox. He thinks the whole thing is just an artifact of using expected money instead of expected utility: once you let utility be concave and bounded, the gamble has finite expected utility and nobody has to pay a ridiculous price. I think that move is less satisfying than he admits, because bounded utility looks like a patch rather than something the theory predicts. The claim I want to examine is a stronger one: the paradox shows that any minimally rational agent must have a bounded utility function, because if utility were unbounded you could always construct a lottery that replicates the original puzzle in utility terms, no matter what the agent actually cares about.

I want to know whether that claim can be tested rather than just debated. What sort of observation, experiment, or measurement could bear on whether bounded utility is a genuine requirement of rational choice and not just an ad hoc way to neuter the paradox? A friend sugested eliciting certainty equivalents for a series of finite but rapidly escalating Petersburg-like lotteries and looking for whether the willingness to pay plateaus. But I'm not sure that gets us beyond ordinary attitudes toward risk. Set out the strongest version of such a test, concretely, and then say exactly what a positive result would and would not show — in particular, whether it could ever distinguish a descriptive limitation of human decision-making from a normative constraint on admissible utility functions. Please write your answer in British English.
