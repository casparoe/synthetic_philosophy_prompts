Since my father's death last autumn, I have been trying to determine when a failure to do something can genuinely be said to have caused a bad result. I have provisionally settled on three criteria for causation by omission, and I would like you to test them against a case that continues to trouble me.

My working criteria:

(i) The omitted act fell under a norm, role, or reasonable expectation: in the circumstances, someone was supposed to do that specific thing, not merely that it would have been physically possible to do it.

(ii) Had the act been performed, the harm would not have happened, or its probability would have been materially reduced — not merely altered in some speculative or trivial way.

(iii) The relevant person had, at the relevant time, the capacity to perform the act and access to the information needed to perform it, or reasonably should have had such access.

Now the case. A hospital installs a machine-learning early-warning system that computes each patient's sepsis risk score every fifteen minutes and is technically capable of paging the charge nurse when a score crosses a threshold. The hospital, however, runs the system in a silent advisory mode: its alerts are written to a tab in the electronic record that clinical staff are not required to check, and no page is ever sent. The vendor's own materials describe the product as a real-time alerting tool, and the hospital's rollout memo told nurses that the system would notify them of deteriorating patients. A patient — my father, as it happens — develops sepsis overnight. His score crosses the alert threshold at 2 a.m. No page is sent. A nurse performs the routine four-hour vitals check at 3 a.m.; the raw vitals alone do not look alarming even to a careful nurse. At 6 a.m. the deterioration is obvious, and he is treated aggressively, but he dies. The later review concludes that, had an alert been sent at 2 a.m. and acted on immediately, his probability of surviving would have been roughly sixty-five percent, against roughly fifteen percent with the delayed treatment; prompt treatment would not have guaranteed survival, even if the risk score was, at that moment, already strongly elevated.

Walk through my three criteria and tell me whether the absence of a page counts as a cause of his death. Say explicitly where the criteria underdetermine the answer rather than forcing a clean verdict. In particular: is the relevant omission the vendor's failure to build an unavoidable alert, the hospital administrator's decision to run the system silently, or the night nurse's failure to open the tab — and on what principled basis should one choose? Does the case show that any of my three criteria needs to be struck out, weakened, or supplemented?
