Suppose a large public organisation must divide a fixed annual improvement dividend of £2,000,000 between two divisions, and it adopts the following procedure. Each division head privately submits two figures: a fallback, meaning the smallest allocation they would accept before walking away from the scheme, and an ideal, meaning the largest allocation they could plausibly justify in negotiation. The figures are not audited against any objective benchmark. If the two fallbacks sum to more than the dividend, the dividend is split equally. Otherwise the rule selects the split that gives each division the same fraction of the gap between its fallback and its ideal: in symbols, there is a single multiplier λ such that Division A receives its fallback plus λ times (its ideal minus its fallback), Division B receives its fallback plus λ times (its ideal minus its fallback), and the two allocations exactly exhaust the dividend. For example, fallbacks of £300,000 and £200,000 together with ideals of £1,400,000 and £1,100,000 yield λ = 0.75, so the awards are £1,125,000 and £875,000. This is a declared-aspirations version of the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution.

Analyse how this arrangement would actually behave, not whether it is fair in principle: what figures sophisticated division heads would learn to submit, how the rule would be gamed, where it would fail, what second-order effects would emerge across successive years, and whether any of those failure modes could be fixed by changing the rule. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
