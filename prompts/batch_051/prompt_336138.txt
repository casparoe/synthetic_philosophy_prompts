I'm teaching a module on medieval political thought, and we've just done Aquinas on tyrannicide. The line I've been emphasising is that tyranny is a corruption of rule because it's ordered to the ruler's private good rather than the common good, but that this doesn't by itself permit killing the tyrant: often it's better to endure a bad ruler than to risk the chaos that comes from trying to remove him, and in any case the remedy is meant to come through public authority — the community, or a higher authority where one exists — not through the private judgement of someone with a knife. He also distinguishes between a man who is a tyrant because he usurped power and one who became a tyrant by abusing a power he held legitimately, a distinction I've allways thought was interesting but undertested.

I want to use Shakespeare's Julius Caesar as a way of testing this in class, and I'd like a model answer to think through myself before I set the exercise. The conspirators are exactly the private persons Aquinas says shouldn't take this upon themselves: they kill Caesar not because he is a tyrant but because they expect he will become one once he is crowned. Brutus says, more or less, that he has no personal quarrel with Caesar, only a fear of what the crown might do to his nature. And the play spends its second half showing the fallout — the funeral oratory, the mob, the civil war, the proscriptions. So here's what I want you to write: a reading of Julius Caesar through Aquinas's treatment of tyrannicide that takes both the play and the position seriously and doesn't reduce the play to an illustration. Where does the Aquinian framework illuminate the political logic of the play — for instance, the difference between a tyrant and a tyrant-in-waiting, or the insistence that the remedy be public? Where does the play resist the framework or expose a blind spot in it, especially if the public remedy turns out to be instituionally impossible in the Rome the play gives us? And could you say something about whether the usurper / legitimate-ruler-turned-tyrant distinction maps onto Caesar's case, since that seems to me to be where the reading gets difficult? It'd also help to say what the play adds to the philosophical debate that the treatise can't say.
