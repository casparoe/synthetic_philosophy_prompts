I've been messing with a variant of the classic Makropulos argument about immortality being unbearably tedious, and I want to know whether a new premise actually changes the standard analysis or just relocates it. The original line, as I remember it, goes: an immortal life eventually runs out of the categorical desires that give you reasons to go on, at which point either you repeat the same activities until you're bored out of your mind, or you change into a person with new desires so different that it's no longer clear the earlier person survives. Here's the twist. Add the premise that a finite mind can be in only finitely many discriminable total experiential states, so there's a hard cap on the variety of experiences available to that mind, not just a psychological tendency to get stuck in a rut. Then an infinite life guarantees that some experiential state-type recurs, and recurs infinitely often. On the face of it this turns the tedium conclusion from a bit of armchair psychology into something like a theorem. But I'm not convinced it doesn't undercut the original argument instead. If a state recurs exactly and your fully absorbed in it, you may not experience it as a repetition; the badness of boredom seems to require memory of the earlier occurrence and some anticipation of novelty, which is a separate claim. And if the same state-type can occur at two different times and each one is lived as if for the first time, maybe the argument was never really about the objective size of the space of experiences but about how a life is held together by memory and desire. Run the analysis with the new premise in place. Does it strengthen the original conclusion, does it weaken it, or does it shift the real weight of the argument from the exhaustion of desires to questions about memory, anticipation, and whether value attaches to experience-types or experience-tokens? And which of the standard replies to the original tedium argument survive the finite-experience cap, and which ones get blocked?
