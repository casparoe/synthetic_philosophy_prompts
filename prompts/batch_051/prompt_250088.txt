I am a high school student, and I have been reading, in a somewhat scattered way, about two ideas that keep being described as inversions of ordinary reasoning. Darwin showed that the appearance of design in living things can be produced by natural selection, a process with no foresight and no understanding of what it builds, and Turing made the analogous point that a machine can carry out an intelligent-looking task such as arithmetic without any grasp of what it is doing. I have started using a distinction of my own to make sense of this, and I would like an honest assessment of it. I call it the difference between first-order competence and second-order competence. First-order competence is doing a task reliably; second-order competence is being able to explain how and why the task is done. A classmate who can solve quadratic equations by applying the formula has first-order competence; a tutor who can say why the formula works has second-order competence. My central claim is that what we ordinarily call comprehension is nothing over and above second-order competence. Explaining is itself a task, so comprehension would just be competence at the explanation task, and the much-discussed gap between competence and comprehension would not be a difference in kind but a difference in which task we happen to be testing.

This seems to me to dissolve the puzzle rather than solve it. A language model can write a coherent paragraph without understanding it, but so, in a way, can evolution build an eye without understanding optics; the difference is only that the language model has first-order competence at writing while lacking second-order competence at explaining its own writing. I suspect my distinction already exists under a more technical name, and I am unsure whether it collapses into something trivial. I would like a candid assessment. Is the claim that comprehension is just competence at explaining coherent, or does it lead to an infinite regress, because explaining an explanation would require a further competence? Does it wrongly classify cases where someone can recite an explanation without understanding it, or cases where someone understands something but cannot yet put it into words? And where does the distinction do the most work before it stops being distinctive?
