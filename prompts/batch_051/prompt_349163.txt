I work as a software engineer on products built around large language models, and I have no formal philosophy background. A claim I keep running into in policy discussion is that transformative AI is best treated by analogy with nuclear weapons: the Manhattan Project and the later nonproliferation regime supposedly show that a small set of capable actors can keep a dangerous technology concentrated, monitorable, and contained. I want to work out whether that analogy survives an important disanalogy. In the nuclear case, there were hard physical bottlenecks — fissile material, enrichment, the need to conduct tests, and observable test signatures — so spreading the underlying scientific knowledge was not, by itself, enough to acquire the weapon. In the current AI case, however, a growing share of frontier capability is available as open-weight models or is specified openly in technical reports; once produced, the capability can be duplicated at near-zero marginal cost and cannot be verifiably recalled. Does the nuclear-weapons analogy for governing transformative AI survive this difference? Identify the specific premise or background assumption of the analogy that this development puts under pressure, explain what that premise would need to be true in this setting, and state whether a repaired version of the analogy can still support the governance conclusions people draw from it, or whether it should be retired.
