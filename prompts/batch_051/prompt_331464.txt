Write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps in the foundations of Bayesian statistics. Camp A, the washing-out camp, holds that the standard convergence results — Doob's consistency theorem, Blackwell–Dubins merging of opinions, and Bernstein–von Mises — jointly establish that prior choice is asymptotically irrelevant: once the likelihood is shared and the technical conditions are met, rational agents converge, so lingering prior disagreement is a finite-sample nuisance rather than a foundational problem. Camp B, the prior-sensitivity camp, holds that this overstates the theorems. Doob's theorem holds only up to prior-dependent null sets; Blackwell–Dubins merging concerns predictive distributions and says nothing about rates or finite samples; Bernstein–von Mises requires regular, fixed-dimensional parametric models with a well-behaved prior; and under misspecification or in nonparametric and high-dimensional models, the prior can affect both the limit (a KL projection, not a shared truth) and the rate at which it is approached.

Produce the document the collaboration would issue. Begin with a numbered agreed-claims section, written so both camps can sign: exactly what each theorem proves, under which conditions, and what each leaves open. Then give a disagreement table with three columns: Camp A's position in wording Camp A accepts, Camp B's position in wording Camp B accepts, and a neutral statement of what would settle that row. Then identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp; I suspect, but do not require, that they include whether prior-dependent convergence rates and finite-sample sensitivity refute washing out, whether misspecification is the normal case or an edge case, and whether merging-of-opinions results are normative claims about rational agents or descriptive claims about posteriors. For each crux, specify a concrete test, simulation, or theorem both camps agree would bear on it, and record each camp's prediction in advance. Close by identifying any part of the dispute that is verbal rather than substantive, with the neutral restatement both can accept.
