You are an expert in early modern epistemology, especially Hume’s mitigated skepticism, the problem of the criterion, and the contrast between Humean naturalism and Cartesian doubt. Take your time and think carefully before giving the procedure: first identify what in Hume’s moderate skepticism can actually be operationalized, and what in it resists operationalization.

I direct a small internal review unit in a state public health department. Our analysts receive about a dozen requests a month from officials who need to know whether an empirical claim is solid enough to act on: a vendor’s claim that a screening program saves lives, a modeling claim about outbreak projections, an assertion that a school-based intervention reduces depression, and similar policy-facing claims. We write a one-page assessment with one of three verdicts: act, do not act, or need more evidence. We are not philosophers, but we can use a clear rubric. The stakes are medium-to-high: a bad “act” can waste public money or harm people; a bad “do not act” can block useful policy. Analysts have about 90 minutes per case and cannot run their own studies.

I want you to turn Hume’s moderate or mitigated skepticism into a reusable decision procedure my analysts can follow when deciding how much evidence is enough. The procedure must take seriously the following passage from David Hume, An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section XII, Part I:

It must, however, be confessed, that this species of scepticism, when more moderate, may be understood in a very reasonable sense, and is a necessary preparative to the study of philosophy, by preserving a proper impartiality in our judgments, and weaning our mind from all those prejudices, which we may have imbibed from education or rash opinion. To begin with clear and self-evident principles, to advance by timorous and sure steps, to review frequently our conclusions, and examine accurately all their consequences; though by these means we shall make both a slow and a short progress in our systems; are the only methods, by which we can ever hope to reach truth, and attain a proper stability and certainty in our determinations.

Here is the problem the procedure must solve. On one side, the Cartesian thought that we should doubt our faculties until some original infallible principle certifies them is useless in our setting; if we wait for that, we will never sign an “act” assessment. On the other side, Hume says the correct method is to begin with clear principles, advance by timorous steps, review conclusions, and accept that we will make only slow and short progress. We need to know what that means operationally when the subject matter is empirical policy claims, not philosophical systems.

The procedure should include: the ordered questions an analyst should ask; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; at what point the procedure returns each of our three verdicts; where the analyst must exercise judgment rather than simply apply the rule; and what the procedure explicitly forbids, such as demanding Cartesian certainty before acting. It should also address the problem of the criterion as it shows up in our work: analysts sometimes say they cannot judge a study without first having an agreed general method for judging studies, and then cannot agree on that method. Give them a Humean way to start anyway. Finally, the procedure should respect Hume’s naturalist point that belief and action are not simply suspended by philosophical doubt, so “need more evidence” must not become an indefinite pause or a disguised “do not act.”

Then apply your procedure to two worked cases: (1) a randomized trial of a workplace wellness program showing a small but statistically significant reduction in sick days, with several failed replications; and (2) a well-conducted observational study using administrative data suggesting that a local heat-warning system sharply reduces heat-stroke deaths among elderly residents, with no randomized evidence. Make sure one of your applications returns a verdict the analysts would not like. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: the point at which Hume’s mitigated skepticism stops being a method and becomes a temperament, and where our unit must hand the decision to a person because the remaining doubt is about values or politics, not evidence.
