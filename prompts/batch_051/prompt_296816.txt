Two housemates of mine got into a long argument last night about a thought experiment one of them had been reading about, and I'd like a move-by-move verdict because I honestly can't tell who's winning.

The setup they were arguing about: a field linguist arrives in a community whose language she's never encountered, notices that whenever a rabbit runs past the locals say gavagai, and writes down gavagai = rabbit in her notebook.

(1) The first one said the evidence can't settle that. Pointing at a rabbit is also pointing at the undetached parts of the rabbit, and at a brief temporal slice of a rabbit, since all of those are there whenever the rabbit is. And the obvious later checks won't help either: whatever she'd be willing to assent to if gavagai meant rabbit, she'd also be willing to assent to if it meant undetached rabbit part or rabbit-stage, because those are just the same stuff carved up differently. So rabbit is underdetermined by the evidence, and at bottom theres no fact of the matter about which translation is the correct one.

(2) The second one said that's too fast. There are tests, she said. Point at the same animal this evening and ask whether it's the same gavagai as this morning. Ask how many gavagai there are when two rabbits are sitting in the field. The rabbit reading says same, and two. The stage reading says not same. The undetached-part reading says dozens. So the translations make different predictions, and the linguist's job is to watch how the locals count, reidentify and talk about one-and-the-same thing, not to give up after one pointing session. You don't need a hidden dictionary; you just need more evidence.

(3) The first one came back: those tests don't settle it either, because they use exactly the words that are up for grabs — same, is, one, two, the plural. A rabbit-stage manual can be adjusted in how it translates those, so it still comes out that there are two things in the field, or that the evening sighting is a later slice of the same rabbit-process. The two manuals end up empirically equivalent no matter what the locals do, and since theres no hidden dictionary in anyone's head to compare the manuals against, there's no fact of the matter which one is right. Picking one is like fitting a curve to a set of points: lots of curves go through the data.

(4) The second one said even if that's granted, the conclusion is less radical than it sounds. If the two manuals really have been adjusted so they agree on all possible future practice — counting, reidentifying, the lot — then calling them rival translations is misleading: theyre just two notations for the same competence, like giving the same position in two coordinate systems. If instead they don't agree on all future practice, then some observation could in principle settle it. Either way, it doesn't follow that translation is arbitrary, or that the linguist may as well flip a coin.

Please walk through the four moves in order and, for each, say whether it's mostly right, mostly wrong, or mixed, with a sentence or two on why. Then give an overall verdict: who's right about which specific points, and what, if anything, are the two of them jointly missing? Could you write the answer in British English?
