I am trying to work out what principle, if any, captures my standing judgments about punctuality, reliability, and small promises. Here are the cases, with my gut verdicts.

1. I promise a friend who is having a crisis that I will call her at 8 p.m. I get absorbed in something else and forget. I judge that seriously wrong, even though nothing material followed from the missed call.

2. I tell a colleague I will send comments on her draft by Thursday. I send them Friday at noon. She had no deadline and was not waiting on them. I judge that mildly wrong.

3. I am 15 minutes late to a 40-person party that is already underway, where no one is waiting for me. I judge that not wrong.

4. I am 15 minutes late to dinner with one friend at a restaurant, and I do not send word. I judge that genuinely wrong.

5. Same dinner, but at the appointed time I text her to say I am running 15 minutes late. I judge that barely wrong, perhaps not wrong at all.

6. I agree to water a neighbor's plants for a week. I skip one day, reasoning that the soil is still damp; two plants die. I judge that seriously wrong.

7. I cancel a lunch on the morning of because a more attractive invitation comes in. I judge that clearly wrong.

8. I cancel the same lunch because I wake up with a fever. I judge that not wrong.

9. I am five minutes late to a weekly one-on-one with a colleague who is himself always about five minutes late. I judge that not wrong.

I am not looking for a list of factors. I want the principle stated precisely enough to decide cases: a rule, or a small set of candidate principles, with each clause motivated by the cases it handles. The two tensions I most want the principle to resolve are these. First, cases 3 and 4 both impose roughly the same 15-minute delay, yet I judge one not wrong and the other genuinely wrong; is waiting, or being the only person affected, doing the work? Second, sending the text nearly erases the wrong in case 4 (case 5), but I doubt that an apology the next morning would have nearly erased the wrong in case 1; how much difference should notice make, and why?

Please also apply the principle to four cases I have not settled:

(a) a colleague who always answers non-urgent messages after three days, though his role implies he will answer within a day;
(b) someone who arrives 20 minutes late to a two-person lunch but texts at the appointed time, without apologizing, that he is running 20 minutes late;
(c) a friend who cancels a coffee two days ahead because she no longer feels like going;
(d) a colleague who routinely shows up 10 minutes late to meetings, but so predictably that everyone now plans around it.

Tell me which of my listed verdicts your preferred principle preserves, which it forces me to abandon, and what it says about the unsettled cases. If no single principle gets all of the listed verdicts, give me the smallest set of candidate principles and say explicitly what each one costs me. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the principle or candidate principles you defend — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
