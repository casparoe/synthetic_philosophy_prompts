I am a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on value pluralism and the ethics of compromise, and I also coordinate a small student mediation program in our residence life office. We see about one dispute a week between student organizations with sharply different value commitments — religious, political, environmental, dietary. Our recommendations influence which organizations receive space, funding, and recognition, so the stakes are modest but real, and the Dean of Students' office signs off on our memos.

The seminar has been developing a picture of compromise I find persuasive but hard to make operational. On this picture, because rival value systems do not rank the same goods the same way, two groups can sometimes trade concessions so that each ends up better off by its own lights than it would be under the status quo or after a winner-take-all outcome. Compromise of that kind need not be a failure of conviction; it can be a way for each side to realize more of what it actually values. But the picture also marks off several failures: a compromise can be coercive (extracted by power rather than genuinely consented to), corrupt (a side trades away a commitment it holds to be non-negotiable), exploitative of third parties, or merely strategic (a truce that keeps the peace without either side gaining anything it values).

I want a decision procedure my mediators can run. It must be teachable in a ninety-minute training session to undergraduates who have not taken the seminar and runnable in under twenty minutes before a mediation. It should return one of three outputs: (a) this is the kind of compromise the seminar's picture endorses; (b) this should not be pursued as a moral compromise; or (c) the procedure cannot decide and the matter should be escalated to the Dean of Students' office, with the reason stated.

Please give me: (1) the procedure itself — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it returns a "no" or an escalation, and which distinctions from the theory must survive in the procedure and which can be dropped for usability; (2) an explicit marking of the judgment calls your procedure cannot eliminate, and where it is likely to misfire — compromises it will wrongly approve and ones it will wrongly block; (3) a definite recommendation on each of three cases below.

Case 1. An environmental organization and a group advocating for low-income students clash over a proposal to raise the mandatory meal-plan fee to source sustainably produced food. The emerging compromise phases the increase over three years, exempts students on full financial aid, and applies the new sourcing standards only as existing contracts come up for renewal.

Case 2. A free-expression society has invited a speaker whom a coalition of student groups says attacks their members' standing on campus. The proposed compromise lets the event proceed in a smaller pre-registered venue with a required moderator and requires the society to co-fund a counter-event, while the coalition agrees not to disrupt the talk.

Case 3. The student senate offers a small religious group a long-sought permanent prayer space in exchange for the group's public endorsement of a budget line the group has, on record, called contrary to its teachings. I am including this because I find the trade appealing — the group gets something it deeply needs and the senate gets a vote it needs — and I suspect your procedure will tell me I should not like it. If it does, show me exactly which question in the procedure catches it.

Finally, say what the procedure cannot do even when followed well. I am asking for a committed answer: take definite positions on each design choice and each case, and defend them, rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me.
