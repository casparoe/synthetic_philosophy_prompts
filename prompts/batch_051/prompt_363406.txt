Here's something I keep tripping over, and I'd like the implicit theory dragged out of it. American law and practice draw a line between markets that are fine and markets that are repugnant, but I can't state the line. A plasma center can pay you forty bucks for an afternoon hooked to an apheresis machine, and the plasma is then bought and sold like any other commodity; pay someone for a kidney and you've committed a federal crime. A fertility clinic will pay an egg donor several thousand dollars, but a contract paying a surrogate is unenforceable or banned outright in several states. You can sell your hair, your sperm, and—after a federal court decided the organ-sale ban didn't cover them—stem cells filtered out of your blood, but not your kidney or a lobe of your liver. Meanwhile, buying someone's vote is a felony, while a candidate who promises a tax cut to a room full of donors in exchange for their support is just campaigning. My question: what view of commodification does this pattern presuppose? I mean the implicit theory—what money corrupts when it enters, which things can't be alienated, which exchanges degrade the parties or the thing traded. Is there a single principle that draws the line in all these places at once, or is the practice quietly running on two or three incompatible principles? If you can give me a principle, state it precisely and test it on a case I didn't list. If you can't, tell me which parts of the practice are pulling against which other parts, and what would have to change—legally or in our self-understanding—if we replaced the implicit view with the best single explicit alternative you can defend. Along the way, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with.
