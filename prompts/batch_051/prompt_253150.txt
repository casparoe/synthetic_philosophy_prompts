Take the repugnant conclusion in population ethics. In its standard form it is reached by summing welfare across lives: for any population of, say, one million people living at a high welfare level, there is some vastly larger population whose members each have lives barely worth living, and the larger population is ranked better because its total welfare is greater. Now suppose a consequentialist tries to avoid this by replacing the additive rule with a multiplicative one: let the value of a population be the product of its average welfare and the natural logarithm of its size, so that each additional life adds value only at a diminishing rate. Work through the new arithmetic carefully. If the baseline is one million people at welfare level 100, how many people at welfare level 1 would the product rule need before it overtakes the baseline? Does the rule block the repugnant conclusion generally, or does the conclusion return for some other choice of baseline and low-welfare population? And does the rule introduce a different absurdity, such as making it wrong to add a person whose life is worth living but whose welfare is below the current average? Say which of the original arguments for total utilitarianism survive the change and which do not. Please answer in British English.
