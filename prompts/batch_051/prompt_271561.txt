The practice I have in mind is judicial review as it operates in the United States and similar constitutional systems. An elected legislature passes a statute after deliberation and a majority vote, often after its members have themselves considered whether the statute is constitutional. A court whose members are appointed, not elected, and largely insulated from electoral accountability, may then strike that statute down as unconstitutional; the court's view prevails unless the constitution is formally amended or the court reverses itself. The same legal order is ordinarily justified in democratic terms: political authority comes from the people, and those subject to the laws ought to have a say, through their representatives, in making them.

What theory of political legitimacy, constitutional rights, or institutional competence does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can. In particular: does the practice strictly presuppose that constitutional questions have objectively right answers and that courts are more likely than legislators to find them, or is it compatible with a purely procedural account of democratic authority? Distinguish what the practice presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and note any points where its parts presuppose inconsistent things. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the practice if one adopted a thoroughgoing majoritarian view, or whether a majoritarian could retain judicial review for other reasons. If the question itself is misposed, perhaps because "presuppose" is doing too much work or because the practice is too varied for a single implicit theory, say so plainly instead of making the task come out clean. Please answer in American English.
