I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I've found myself applying an interpretive rule in seminars, reading groups, and honestly in arguments with friends about what some adopted public text "really" means — a constitution, a party platform, a collective statement. The rule is that private evidence about what the drafters wanted is out: no letters, no diaries, no convention debates, no "he later admitted." The only admissible evidence is the text and how the relevant public would have read it at the time of adoption. I got the rule from Frederick Douglass's 1860 Glasgow address, "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?", where he argues:

"Again, it should be borne in mind that the mere text, and only the text, and not any commentaries or creeds written by those who wished to give the text a meaning apart from its plain reading, was adopted as the Constitution of the United States. ... It would be the wildest of absurdities, and lead to endless confusion and mischiefs, if, instead of looking to the written paper itself, for its meaning, it were attempted to make us search it out, in the secret motives, and dishonest intentions, of some of the men who took part in writing it. It was what they said that was adopted by the people, not what they were ashamed or afraid to say, and really omitted to say."

It came up concretely last week, when a friend used Madison's private convention notes to settle what the First Amendment meant, and I rejected the move before I had even thought about what the notes actually showed. My advisor says I've turned a tactical move from a political fight into an epistemology, and that my rule lets me seperate texts from the ugly compromises that produced them. I can't decide whether the rule is a defensible epistemic policy — after all, for a ratified public document, the public text and its public context are often the only thing the adopting public could have meant — or whether I've built myself a shield against looking at the history. When does a rule like this actually fail, and is there a repaired version that keeps the anti-"secret motives" insight without becoming a way of protecting texts from their own origins?
