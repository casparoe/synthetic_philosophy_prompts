Imagine a country where the ruling party has spent a few years capturing the courts and the election commission, and the remaining opposition parties are genuinely scared they'll be banned or their candidates disqualified before the next vote. So they adopt a mutual-defense pact. The rule: if the government bans any signatory party, bars its candidates, or prosecutes it's leader on charges that the other signatories, by a two-thirds vote, judge to be political, then every other signatory must withdraw from the next parliamentary election and tell its voters to spoil their ballots or stay home. The pact has no legal force; the only enforcement is that a party that stays in the race anyway gets expelled and publicly shamed as a collaborator.

Analyze how this arrangement would actually behave, not whether it's noble. What does it incentivize for the government: does banning one party become more attractive because it clears the field, or less? What does it incentivize for each party individually, and for the smallest party in particular? Where are the free-rider moves, the bluffing opportunities, and the chances for the government to trigger the pact on purpose? What second-order effects does it have on voters, on the credibility of elections, and on the meaning of a boycott if the government cheerfully wins with 80%? Finally, say whether the obvious failure modes can be patched without making the pact toothless, or whether the whole device is inherently self-defeating.
