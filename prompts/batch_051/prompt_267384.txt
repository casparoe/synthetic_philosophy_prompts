so ive been reading hayeks essay 'The Use of Knowledge in Society' on my own (not a student, just fell down a rabbit hole), and this passage near the start keeps nagging at me:

"In ordinary language we describe by the word "planning" the complex of interrelated decisions about the allocation of our available resources. All economic activity is in this sense planning; and in any society in which many people collaborate, this planning, whoever does it, will in some measure have to be based on knowledge which, in the first instance, is not given to the planner but to somebody else, which somehow will have to be conveyed to the planner."

He seems to be saying the whole problem isnt really whether planning happens but how knowledge that starts out scattered around gets to whoever is deciding. And that got me thinking: maybe you could keep central planning but fix the knowledge-conveying part with better technology, instead of just letting prices do it.

My idea is sort of a national reporting-and-pricing app. everyone (managers, workers, even households) has to log the local stuff they notice: which inputs are running low, which machines are idle, what skills are going unused, where queues are forming, what people are hoarding. A central algorithm turns all these reports into daily shadow prices for every good and service, and state-owned firms are required to buy and sell from each other at those prices. To stop people from lying or slacking off on reporting, theres an accuracy bonus: if your report turns out to predict a shortage that later shows up in the official aggregates, you get paid extra; if your reports look like noise or manipulation, you get docked. The idea is that the bureau isnt planning from its own guesses—its planning from everyones local knowledge, aggregated in real time.

I want to know how this thing would actually behave, not whether its a good idea in the abstract. Who games it and how? Can the prediction bonuses be manipulated—say, by coordinated reports that help create the shortage they predict, or by reporting true-but-useless stuff that happens to score well? Does it actually capture the kind of knowledge hayek is pointing at, or does the algorithm still end up as a single mind that only sees a flattened, reported version of things? Which failure modes could be fixed without effectively turning the whole scheme back into a market with extra steps?
