You are a cognitive scientist who works on analogy-making and on the phenomenon sometimes called conceptual slippage: the way the units, relations, and categories an analogy appears to use shift under contextual pressure. I am going to describe a set of my own pretheoretic judgments about analogies in a tiny artificial letter-string domain, and I want you to do careful reflective equilibrium on my behalf. Think step by step: first brainstorm several candidate principles and test them against each of my cases before choosing one; then state the best principle precisely, apply it to new cases, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and why.

The domain. Problems have the form X : Y :: Z : ?, where terms are lowercase letter strings over the usual alphabet, written without spaces, and the task is to supply the most natural completion. Successor means the next letter of the alphabet; do not assume wrap-around unless the cases force the question.

Here are my verdicts.

1. abc : abd :: ijk : ?
My completion is ijl, with no felt effort. The natural rule appears to be: replace the rightmost letter by its alphabetic successor.

2. abc : abd :: aabbcc : ?
My completion is aabbdd, not aabbcd. What advances is the whole final pair 'cc', replaced by 'dd'. So the thing being operated on is not a letter but a repeated chunk, and the rule from case 1 has quietly changed its unit.

3. abc : abd :: iijjkk : ?
My completion is iijjll, for the same reason as case 2: the rightmost chunk 'kk' becomes 'll'. I would not give iijjkl.

4. abc : abd :: abcc : ?
My completion is abcd, and this is the one that bothers me. If 'cc' was a single unit in cases 2 and 3, it should be a unit here, and I should say abdd. But abdd feels wrong in a way that aabbdd did not. Here I treat only the final letter as moving even though it is part of a pair.

5. abc : abd :: abbcc : ?
My completion is abbdd, and it feels nearly forced. At first glance this puts me in direct tension with case 4: both targets end in 'cc', but here the pair is treated as a unit and there it is not. A difference that might matter is that in abbcc the length-2 pattern recurs — 'bb' and 'cc' — while in abcc it occurs only once. But I cannot yet say why recurrence should settle the unit.

6. abc : abd :: xyz : ?
I catch myself wanting to say xya, wrapping around from z to a. But strictly z has no successor, so the original rule is undefined at the edge of the alphabet. This feels like the same sort of move as cases 2 and 5: rather than keep the concept 'successor' fixed, I let it slip under pressure. Maybe that is right, or maybe I am merely manufacturing determinacy.

What I want from you.

(a) State a precise principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures my verdicts, noting for each component which case requires it.
(b) Say which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate, and which simple candidate is cheapest to keep. In particular, decide whether the right treatment of case 4 is that I should abandon it, or that the unit of operation depends on the string's global structure and is governed by something like recurrence; and say whether case 6 belongs to the same account or is a separate pragmatic convention.
(c) Apply your best principle to cases I have deliberately not settled — aaabbbccc, aabcc, abccc, abccdd, and abcd — and give your predicted completions.
(d) Finally, tell me where my judgments are genuinely principled and where they are ad hoc, and exactly which of my verdicts I should revise.

Be explicit about the choices your principle makes. If it saves all six verdicts, say plainly that it does so only at the price of a more complicated unit-selection rule, and defend that price. If it overturns one or more of my verdicts, say which and why. Your rule should be precise enough to score a new case without hand-tuning.
