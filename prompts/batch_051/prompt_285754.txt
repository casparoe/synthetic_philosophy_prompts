I work on governance mechanisms for decentralized protocols, and I have been reading about sortition — filling public office by random lot rather than by election or appointment. The usual pitch is that sortition blocks the formation of a permanent political class: everyone has a turn, no one builds a career out of office, and the selected body ends up resembling the population it governs. From that reading, I have assembled five criteria that a mechanism would seem to need to meet in order to count as sortition.

1. Selection is genuinely random, not election or appointment.
2. Every member of the eligible pool has a known chance of selection, and in the canonical case the chance is equal.
3. The selected body exercises binding decision-making power, not merely advisory power.
4. The pool from which the draw is made is the community that will live under the decisions.
5. Terms are short and re-entry is restricted, so that service is rotation rather than a career.

Now I want to test these criteria against a case I am working on. A decentralized lending protocol with roughly 40,000 token-holding addresses currently governs itself by token-weighted vote, and in practice a handful of wealthy professional delegates behave as career politicians who never leave power. The proposal is a Sampled Council of 60 seats. Every quarter, an on-chain random beacon draws council members from the set of addresses holding the governance token. An address's probability of being drawn is proportional to the square root of its token balance, which is meant to mute whales without ignoring stake entirely. One person can control many addresses, and the draw does nothing to prevent that. Selected addresses serve a single 90-day term, cannot serve consecutively, and are compensated. The council sets risk parameters and allocates grants with binding effect, though token holders can overturn its decisions by gathering 15% of circulating tokens in a seven-day veto vote.

Is the Sampled Council sortition in any sense that matters? Work through my five criteria one by one, and wherever the answer is "no," tell me whether that is a defect in this mechanism or a sign that my criteria are doing real work — or that the criteria themselves need changing. In particular, I want to know how much weight to put on equal chance and on the character of the eligible pool: if "equal chance" becomes "chance proportional to stake," and "citizens" becomes "token-holder addresses," have we kept sortition and merely adapted it, or have we kept a lottery while discarding the thing sortition was supposed to do?
