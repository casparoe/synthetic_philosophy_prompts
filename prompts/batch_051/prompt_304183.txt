Two people from my reading group got into an argument over Odysseus and the Sirens, specifically over whether Odysseus's decision to have himself bound to the mast should be classified as temperance in the virtue sense, as mere prudence, or as repression. They were treating the episode as a case study rather than as literature.

The facts of the case, as they took them: Odysseus wants to hear the Sirens' song, and he knows in advance that anyone who hears it will be so overpowered that he will try to steer the ship onto the rocks unless he is prevented. He therefore has the crew stop their ears with wax and bind him to the mast, with orders not to release him no matter how much he begs, and to bind him tighter if he struggles.

(1) Priya opened by saying it clearly wasn't temperance. Temperance, she said, is a settled disposition to feel and act in moderation; Odysseus doesn't moderate the desire at all. He leaves the desire at full strength and lets a length of rope and other people's obedience do the resisting for him. That can be prudent forward planning, but it isn't the virtue of temperance.

(2) Tom replied that this is artificially narrow. The temperate person, he said, is exactly the one who sees that she cannot be trusted later and arranges her surroundings so that the decision she endorses now is the one that gets carried out. Once you admit that temperance is a trait for coping with recalcitrant desires, you have to allow that its exercise can include physical and social scaffolding, not just an inner moment of resistance. The whole arrangement — mast, wax, ropes, standing orders — is the temperate act.

(3) Priya came back that, if that is sufficient, the smoker who asks her partner to hide cigarettes, or the person who takes a drug that suppresses appetite, is already behaving temperately, and we have lost the distinction between being temperate and merely being made to behave temperately. A virtue that can be outsourced entirely to chemistry and other people's vigilance has stopped being a state of character and become an engineering problem.

(4) Tom said that was not a reductio but the point. The line Priya wanted to draw between the self and the device presumes that the will is a special internal organ rather than one causal system among others. If the person reflectively installs the device and endorses what it does, that is self-government rather than something done to the self from outside. Insisting that the resistance must occur inside the skull is a leftover from an honorific and somewhat mystical picture of agency.

(5) Priya answered that endorsement is doing all the work and is cheap. Odysseus could equally endorse a plan to have the crew knock him unconscious at the first note of the song, and by Tom's criterion that would count as self-government too; yet no one wants to call that temperance. She said she had no difficulty calling the original case prudent, since it is a sensible engineering fix for a foreseeable failure. The problem is that Tom's analysis would entitle a great deal of repression to the same honorific, because repression is precisely the maneuver of leaving the desire intact while assigning someone else to keep it from issuing in action.

(6) Tom replied that not every desire deserves a seat at the table. For a desire the agent regards as wholly alien, the reasonable response is to make it causally irrelevant rather than to confront it. And the binding was itself a moment of accurate self-assessment: Odysseus judged correctly that his later self would be a poor judge, and acted on that judgment. Repression would be refusing to admit that the desire existed; planning around it is the opposite.

(7) Priya finished by saying that Tom's own description divides Odysseus into a ruling self and a ruled self, and licenses the ruler to use force against the ruled on the basis of what the ruled self will do. She said she did not see how that differs in kind from the repressive structure the rhetoric of temperance was supposed to avoid — except that the jailer and the prisoner happen to share a body.

At that point neither would budge, and they turned to me. I could see that each was right about something and wrong about something, but I couldn't referee it to my own satisfaction. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the seven moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right (intermediate ratings are fine), briefly justify each rating, and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing.
