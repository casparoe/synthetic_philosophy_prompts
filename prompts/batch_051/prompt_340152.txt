In Fitch's paradox of knowability, let Kp mean that someone at some time knows that p, and let ◇p mean that it is possible that p. The knowability principle says that for every truth p, p → ◇Kp. But take any truth p that is not actually known: p ∧ ¬Kp is true, so by the principle ◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp). Since knowledge is factive and distributes over conjunction, in the possible situation where someone knows the conjunction we get Kp and K¬Kp; by factivity K¬Kp implies ¬Kp, giving Kp ∧ ¬Kp. The principle therefore collapses into the claim that every truth is already known.

Now vary the argument by replacing individual knowledge with common knowledge in a fixed, nonempty group G. Write C_G p for the usual iterative notion: everyone in G knows p, everyone in G knows that everyone in G knows p, and so on. Say that a truth p is G-commonly knowable just in case it is possible that C_G p, and suppose every truth is G-commonly knowable. Run the Fitch argument on the sentence p ∧ ¬C_G p, where p is true but not common knowledge in G. Does the contradiction go through? If it does, state exactly what conclusion the argument now forces. If it does not, identify the first step that fails and say whether weakening that step would rescue a plausible version of the principle. Conclude by assessing, on the basis of this variation, whether the engine of Fitch's paradox is factivity, closure under conjunction elimination, or something else.
