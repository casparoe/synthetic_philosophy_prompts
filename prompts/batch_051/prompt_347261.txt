In arguments about workplace surveillance, the interesting question is not whether monitoring occurs, but what kind of power is operating when it does. I want to use Foucault's account of disciplinary power to test a borderline case. On that account, disciplinary power is distinguished from ordinary command-and-obedience monitoring by several features working together. First, hierarchical observation: a field of continuous and asymmetrical visibility, in which those who are watched can never be sure when, or by whom, they are being observed. Second, normalizing judgment: conduct is measured against a standard or an average, and small deviations draw small, corrective penalties rather than dramatic punishment. Third, the examination: observation and normalization are fused in an apparatus that records an individual as a case with a score, rank, or profile. Fourth, the production of a certain kind of subject: persons become simultaneously more useful and more obedient, and eventually take over the watching themselves, so that the power runs even when no supervisor is present. Fifth, the operation of power is productive rather than merely repressive: it generates knowledge, capacities, comparisons, and identities, not only prohibitions.

Now the case. A fully remote software company requires each employee to install a tracking tool that continuously records keystrokes, mouse movements, active-window time, and idle time. The tool converts these streams into a weekly focus score. Each employee sees their own score and the team's distribution, and everyone's scores are visible to the whole team. Managers review the distribution in a weekly meeting and hold calibration conversations with anyone whose score is more than one standard deviation below the team mean. The conversations are explicitly framed as supportive and are said not to affect pay or promotion. Over time, employees adapt: some keep a document open and tap keys while thinking, some avoid long stretches of reading, and some use small programs to simulate keyboard activity. No one is punished outright for a low score.

Taking those five features as the criteria, assess whether this arrangement qualifies as an instance of disciplinary power. Work through the criteria one by one, arguing in each case whether the feature is present, absent, or only partially realized, and identify any respects in which the criteria leave the verdict underdetermined. Then address the reverse question: does the case show that the criteria themselves need amendment—for example, because the visibility here is partly symmetrical among peers, or because the subjects game the metric rather than internalize the norm, or because the threat of punishment has been replaced by a separation between the metric and formal personnel decisions? Conclude with a definite, defended verdict on whether the case counts.
