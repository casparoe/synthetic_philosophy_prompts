You are a professional philosopher with expertise in epistemology and in the thought of Wang Yangming. Think carefully before answering. Begin by briefly sketching the strongest considerations on each side, then give a final assessment.

I follow a policy in my intellectual life that I want evaluated. I keep a private ledger of claims I am tempted to preface with "I know that..." Whenever a claim has practical content — about what I should do, what would be good for me, what a situation demands — I apply a rule: the claim counts as knowledge only if my conduct has caught up with it. If I can state it accurately, find it obvious on reflection, and even argue for it, but I have not acted in accordance with it, I demote it to "I can recite this" or "I verbally endorse this." The rule is inspired by Wang Yangming's doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action, as I understand it: knowing and acting are not separate stages, and someone who knows what is right but fails to do it does not yet truly know it, because the relevant kind of knowing is completed only in action.

Concretely, the policy requires me to say that I did not know my last relationship was harming me until I actually ended it, though I had said "I know it is bad for me" for months. It requires me to say that I do not know a political cause is urgent until I have done something concrete for it beyond arguing about it. It requires me to say that I do not know I should exercise until I exercise. I adopted the policy because I noticed that I used "I know" to close questions my conduct showed were still open, and because it seemed to mark the difference between understanding a claim and being changed by it.

Evaluate the policy itself, not a caricature. Does it defend a real epistemic distinction — for example, between possessing a practical judgment and merely being able to produce it — or does it blur the difference between knowing-that and acting, which should be kept apart? Is it a reliable guard against self-deception and performative conviction, or does it simply let weakness of will, fear, or circumstance revoke my knowledge? Does it work only for a special class of practical and self-involving claims, and misfire for mathematical, historical, or perceptual knowledge? Could a revised version keep the benefits without making my ascriptions of knowledge hostage to how disciplined I happen to be? And is a private ledger enough to make such a policy trustworthy, or is it likely to flatter me while leaving my self-image intact? Say when the policy is reliable, when it fails, and what the best corrected version of it would be.
