I cover philosophy and psychology for a magazine, and through a weird series of favors I've ended up grading a short-answer question in an intro ethics course I helped design. I need a second read on one answer before I send the marks back. Below is the question and the student's answer. Give it a grade out of 10, and write the specific comments you'd leave that justify the grade — point by point, not just a overall impression.

Question: Can forgiveness properly be unconditional, that is, granted before the wrongdoer repents or apologizes? If it can, does it ever become obligatory? Defend your answer.

Student's answer:

"It's easy to think forgiveness can't be unconditional because forgiving someone who hasn't apologized looks like saying the wrong didn't matter. But that confuses forgiveness with excusing or condoning. To forgive is precisely to keep the judgment that the act was wrong and then forswear resentment toward the wrongdoer for it. Since resentment is the victim's to abandon, forgiveness can be unconditional: a victim can drop the resentment without waiting for repentance, for instance because holding onto it is destroying her. What unconditional forgiveness can't do is repair the relationship on its own — reconciliation still needs amends from the other side.

On whether it's obligatory: resentment is bad for the person who carries it, and forgiveness is the standard way to give it up. So in cases where a victim can't move on any other way, forgiveness is something she owes herself, under a general duty of self-care. That makes forgiving a repentant wrongdoer sometimes obligatory, not just supererogatory. And because this duty is only to herself, the wrongdoer never has a right to be forgiven, so the victim can't be blamed for not forgiving. This gives a coherent picture: a victim can owe it to herself to forgive unconditionally, and if she holds out for an apology anyway she is failing that duty."
