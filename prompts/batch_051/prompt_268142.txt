Edward Gibbon, the Enlightenment historian of Rome's decline, is often taken as the exemplar of a certain kind of narrative historical explanation: the fall of Rome emerges, on his account, from a long and textured interplay of institutions, mores, religion, military arrangements, and personality rather than from a single master variable. Now imagine a modern forecasting researcher, trained on reference classes and base rates, asks him to evaluate a working paper that treats his own subject matter differently. The paper sorts past empires into reference classes, computes age-specific base rates of collapse for each class, then issues probabilistic forecasts for new cases while deliberately withholding most case-specific narrative detail. Your task is to predict, in concrete terms, how Gibbon would respond to this paper. What would he find genuinely useful in the outside-view treatment of imperial survival, and where would he resist its three central moves: the construction of the reference class, the anchoring of forecasts to base rates rather than case histories, and the rule that case-specific information may move the base rate only if it passes some explicit skeptic's test? Which of Gibbon's known intellectual commitments give the response its shape? As you answer, mark explicitly which assertions about Gibbon are well established, which are contested among scholars (for example, how central his treatment of Christianity really is to his causal story), and which are speculative extrapolations from his known views. Do not present a contested reading of Gibbon as if it were the consensus, and do not include a bibliography or citations; reason from the ideas.
