Me and a friend got into an argument about whether you can design a job so that the person in it can't be corrupted, rather than just hoping they won't be. He proposed this, and I keep turning it over. The city makes the Director of Procurement a post filled by lot from among volunteers who pass an accounting exam. On taking office, the director's personal assets go into a locked trust run by the runner-up from the same draw — the person who just missed the job — and they can't touch or even look at the trust for seventeen years. Their salary is fixed at the city median, and any income from a city contractor over the same period is forfeited to the city. The runner-up gets payed a fixed fee for running the trust, plus ten percent of any concealed assets they prove. Then the twist: once during their seven-year term, the director may cancel any one contract, for any reason or no reason, with no council override, and they can be removed only for taking money, not for using the cancellation. The idea is that bribery becomes pointless, because a supplier who pays for a contract can have it canceled next week by the very person they paid.

I'm not asking whether the scheme is a good idea. Walk through it as a mechanism: who volunteers and who stays away, how contractors, the runner-up, and the council actually play it, where the corruption migrates, and whether the institution produces an incorruptible official or just one whose corruption is harder to see. Also say whether the one-time cancellation power is itself a corruptible asset. Keep the whole answer under 800 words.
