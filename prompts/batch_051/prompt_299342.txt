I joined the three-person board of my housing co-op last spring, and we are currently deciding on a renovation proposal. Our rules say a proposal is approved exactly when it is both within budget and permitted by the building code, so for each proposal we answer three questions separately: (A) Is it within budget? (B) Is it permitted by the code? (C) Is it approved? We decide each question by simple majority.

One of the other board members showed me a pattern of votes that has been worrying me ever since. Suppose our three members vote like this. Member 1: yes on A, no on B, no on C. Member 2: no on A, yes on B, no on C. Member 3: yes on A, yes on B, yes on C. Each member on their own is perfectly consistent with the rule that approval requires both conditions: the first two reject because exactly one condition fails, and the third approves because both hold. But the majority tallies come out as A yes (2–1), B yes (2–1), and C no (2–1). So the board's official answers say the proposal is within budget, permitted by the code, and not approved — directly contradicting our own rule that such a proposal is approved.

The board member who showed me this laid it out as an argument I cannot shake: (1) each member's votes are consistent with the rule C exactly when A and B; (2) the board aggregates by majority vote on each question; (3) that procedure, applied to these votes, yields A yes, B yes, C no; (4) those collective answers violate the rule; (5) so proposition-by-proposition majority voting does not guarantee that a group's position stays consistent; (6) therefore majority rule itself is an incoherent way for any group to decide several connected questions, and we must either let one person's consistent set of answers stand for the whole board or accept that the board's official position can contradict itself. I cannot find an arithmetic mistake, yet the conclusion seems to prove far too much — plenty of ordinary committees use majority rule on connected questions without being incoherent. Which step should I reject, and what would rejecting it cost?
