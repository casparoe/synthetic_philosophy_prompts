After forty years in neuroscience, I've recently gotten into philosophy of psychiatry and have been trying to work through the two-factor account of monothematic delusions. It has always struck me as exactly how a lab scientist would set the problem up: one factor produces the anomalous datum (in Capgras, the usual autonomic response to a familiar face fails, so the spouse no longer "feels" familiar), and a second factor explains why the patient then adopts such an implausible interpretation instead of rejecting it, which is usually glossed as a deficit in belief evaluation. Without the second factor you'd have a odd experience and an intact reality check; without the first you'd have a general credulity but no particular delusion. So the delusion looks like a two-part machine.

Lately I've been reading the predictive processing material and it seems to me it dissolves the boundary the two-factor argument leans on. If perception is itself top-down hypothesis testing — if experience is the brain's best guess, with prediction errors weighted by expected precision — then the "missing feeling of familiarity" is not an independent signal that gets handed to a separate evaluator. It is already part of the same inference in which a high-level hypothesis like "this person is an impostor" might be generated and then held, perhaps because precision has been misassigned somewhere. On that picture, the second factor starts to look less like a second module and more like a redescription of aberrant precision-weighting.

So my question is whether the two-factor argument actually survives this development, or whether predictive processing kills the distinction the argument depends on. I'm trying to pin down exactly which premise is under pressure: the claim that there is a separable experience-generating factor, the claim that accepting the delusional explanation requires a special second deficit, or the broader modular picture behind both. And if it doesn't survive as stated, is there a repaired two-factor claim that still earns its keep — one cast in terms of prediction error versus precision, or something like that — rather than just collapsing into a single-factor account? I also can't figure out whether this makes the older question of whether delusions are genuine beliefs easier or harder to answer.
