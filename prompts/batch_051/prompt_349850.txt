I hold a view in the advance directives debate and I want you to map out what would realy count against it. The view, roughly: when a competent adult signs an advance directive refusing, say, antibiotics or tube feeding if she later develops severe dementia, and she then develops dementia and in that state seems to want to live — to eat, to stay in her routine — her earlier directive should still be respected. I don't just mean legally; I think the earlier self has the moral authority to bind the later one, because the earlier self is the one whose values and whole-life plans should set the terms for what happens to her. Call it the authority-of-the-earlier-self view, the sort of thing people call precedent autonomy.

I'm not after the usual objections, like 'she's changed her mind' or 'the later person has rights too.' I want the deepest posible account of what would have to be true for my position to be wrong. Which considerations should actually move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward? In particular: (1) what does the view commit me to about personal identity — does it need the later self to be the same person, and what breaks if identity turns out to be a loose bundle of overlapping psychological capacities rather than the persistence of the agent who signed the form; (2) where does the earlier self's authority run out — can she bind only future decisions made while competent, or can she override a later self who is contented and has clearly different but stable preferences; (3) what should I make of evidence that people are bad at predicting how they will value a life with dementia; (4) are there defeaters that defenders of my view would themselves concede count against it, as opposed to ones only critics bring up? I'd personally find the identity considerations most persuasive if they cut against me, so give those your best shot. And tell me honestly whether the view can insulate itself by saying the later self's preferences are inauthentic, and whether that insulation is itself a problem — I don't want to hold a position that can't lose.
