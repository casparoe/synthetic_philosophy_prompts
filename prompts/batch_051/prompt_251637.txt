A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about the claim that human violence has declined dramatically over the centuries, and we ended up unsure which sub-dispute was actually doing the work. My credence is around 0.7 that the decline is a real feature of the world — in the sense that an average person's annual risk of dying from intentional interpersonal or organized violence is substantially lower now than it was a few centuries ago — rather than being largely an artifact of how violence is defined, counted, or normalized. My friend's credence is around 0.3.

Give me your own number for that proposition, but more importantly, decompose it. Please assign rough probabilities to at least these sub-questions: (i) that the per-capita framing, rather than absolute numbers of victims, is the appropriate way to state the underlying claim; (ii) that the available evidence for periods before roughly the nineteenth century is good enough to support any confident trend judgment; (iii) that the decline survives once violence is broadened beyond battle deaths and homicide to state violence, structural violence, or nonlethal violence; and (iv) that there is a determinate fact of the matter here at all, as opposed to a partly verbal choice about what to count as violence. Show how these combine, say which term your final number is most sensitive to, and say where a 0.7 and a 0.3 person most likely diverge. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
