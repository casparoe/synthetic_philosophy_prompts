Suppose a driver on an unfamiliar road at night begins to wonder whether there is ice ahead. No one would say the sensible response is to estimate how probable the ice is and then drive on at full speed because one is slightly more confident that there is none. The sensible response is to slow down, or stop and look. Now consider a person who is genuinely torn about a moral question, such as whether it is permissible to buy and eat meat from factory-farmed animals. That same person will often, while still torn, simply settle on whichever answer she finds a little more plausible and then order the meal or refuse it, without slowing down, without gathering further evidence, and without doing anything that would be reasonable whichever answer turned out to be right. In other words, we seem to treat uncertainty about facts as something to manage, but uncertainty about morals as something merely to resolve, even when the stakes are no lower. Is there a principle that justifies treating these two kinds of uncertainty so differently, or is it just a habit we have never examined? I want the strongest justification you can find, tested against nearby cases (for instance, someone genuinely unsure whether a promise still binds, or whether a joke is cruel), and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
