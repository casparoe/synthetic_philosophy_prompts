I'm a journalist covering tech and political rhetoric, and I keep coming back to this paragraph from Orwell's *Politics and the English Language*:

“But an effect can become a cause, reinforcing the original cause and producing the same effect in an intensified form, and so on indefinitely. A man may take to drink because he feels himself to be a failure, and then fail all the more completely because he drinks. It is rather the same thing that is happening to the English language. It becomes ugly and inaccurate because our thoughts are foolish, but the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts. The point is that the process is reversible.”

I get the general gist, but the causal story is frustratingly slippery: he seems to say both that bad political language is caused by bad politics, and that the language itself becomes an independent cause, and then that the whole thing is reversible if individuals clean up their prose. I want to understand precisely how those three moves fit together.

Could you act as a philosopher of language and political rhetoric, start by brainstorming a couple of plausible readings of the causal claim, and then walk through the paragraph sentence by sentence? After that, pressure-test the argument with a modern case. Suppose a party's communications team fine-tunes a language model on decades of press releases, so it generates phrases like "streamlining service delivery" or "right-sizing" not because anyone is trying to deceive, but because those strings are statistically probable. Would Orwell's feedback-loop picture class that as the same disease, a different one, or a case where his explanation stops applying? Try to say what his position commits him to predicting about machine-generated political speech, and be explicit about how confident you are in the reading and what point you are least sure of.
