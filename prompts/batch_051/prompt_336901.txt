I'm putting together a handout for a sixth-form taster day on philosophy of perception, and I'd like to finish with a poem instead of the usual diagram of a stick half-submerged in water. Could you write me a sonnet about the argument from illusion and the disjunctivist reply? I know its a weird ask, but I want the form to do real work, not just decorate the topic.

Use the octave to run the argument from illusion: the bent stick in the water, the way hallucinating and veridically seeing can seem exactly alike from the inside, and then the slide to the thought that the two cases must share a highest common factor, and from there to sense-data. The volta should be the disjunctivist's objection — the turn where the poem notices that being subjectively indistinguishable doesn't make two experiences the same kind of mental state. The closing couplet should land the positive view: veridically seeing a cup and merely seeming to see one are different sorts of state all the way down, not two versions of a single underlying state. Make the octave confident and a little seductive, so the turn actually feels like a turn. It should be accurate enough that someone who teaches perception wouldn't wince, but readable enough that a bright sixteen-year-old who has never heard of disjunctivism could get something out of it.
