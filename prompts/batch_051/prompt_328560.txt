In my comparative health law seminar we've been on reproductive markets, and there's an asymmetry I can't get to sit still. Almost every country I've looked at allows paid blood plasma donation, and many let men sell sperm; the same societies are completely fine with people being paid for genuinely dangerous and body-grinding work—commercial fishing, mining, phase I drug trials, eldercare that wrecks your spine. But when the thing being given is an egg, or the service is a nine-month pregnancy, a lot of Europe shuts the door: egg donors are expected to be altruistic (or egg donation is banned outright), and commercial surrogacy is illegal, unenforceable, or at least heavily restricted, while the US has donor price lists and six-figure surrogacy agencies. So the line seems to be that blood, sperm, and risking your body in a mine are sellable, but eggs and gestation are too special to sell.

I want you to act as a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of markets, commodification, and the body, and think through this carefully. Start by brainstorming the strongest candidate justifications for treating eggs and surrogacy differently from plasma, sperm, and dangerous labor: exploitation of poorer women, the fact eggs are non-renewable, the length and intimacy of pregnancy, the idea that a child results, dignity or commodification worries, the unequal burden on women, or just a moralized taboo around motherhood. Then stress-test the most promising ones against nearby cases—if exploitation justifies banning paid surrogacy, why doesn't it justify banning paid care work or mining?—and give me your honest verdict. Is there a principle that keeps the asymmetry, or should one side move, and which one?
