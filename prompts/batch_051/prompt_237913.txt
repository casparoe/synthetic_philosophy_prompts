I keep going back and forth on a parallel, and I'd like someone to help me figure out whether its deep or just a nice metaphor. I cover science, and I've done enough reporting to develop a distrust of stories that are too tidy: when a source gives me an account where every loose end is tied off, each quote lands on the theme, and the whole thing has a clean moral, my alarm bells go off. In journalism, a perfectly clean narrative is often a sign that someone shaped it — either the source selected and smoothed, or I did. Reality is supposed to have jagged edges. But in mathematics and much of physics the opposite seems to hold. When a proof feels inevitable and short, or a theory turns out to have compact equations, deep symmetries, and no adjustable knobs, that elegance is treated as evidence for the theory, a sign we've found something real rather than built something that happens to fit. So here's the thing: both trades are using an aesthetic response to tidiness as a guide to truth, but one treats tidiness as a warning and the other treats it as a virtue. Is there a principled reason for the flip, or is one of these habits of thought just wrong? Is the physicists' elegance heuristic anything more than the reporter's suspicion of the too-neat story, run in reverse? And if the analogy is superficial, where exactly do the two situations come apart? When you answer, I'd like you to mark which parts of this are settled among people who work on the topic and which parts are genuinely contested — and please don't present a contested view, like whether elegance is actually good evidence, as if it were the consensus.
