I've been going in circles on embedded agency — the idea that an agent is a physical part of the world it's trying to model, rather than a ghost outside the causal graph pulling levers. The version that's been bugging me is about information-gathering: deciding whether to pay for some information when the act of gathering or computing it is itself a physical process inside the system you're reasoning about. I want a case built to spec.

Construct a thought experiment where two approaches to decision-making come apart on whether to acquire a costly piece of information. The first approach — call it the Cartesian view — treats information-gathering as epistemically transparent: it updates the agent's beliefs but doesn't change the distribution of outcomes except by changing what the agent eventually does. The second approach — the embedded view — treats the agent as a node in the world, so the physical footprint of measuring or computing (power drawn, heat given off, time taken, the instrument perturbing the thing measured, the analysis changing the agent's own later state) can itself alter the outcomes being predicted. Both views get the same utility function and the same prior; the disagreement has to come entirely from whether the agent's own measurement or computation is included in the model of the world. The scenario should be realistic (a hospital, a factory, a data center, a small buisness — not a trolley, not science fiction), with no other agents who hold beliefs about the decision-maker, no perfect predictors, and no copies or simulations of the agent. The point is to test whether the agent/environment boundary is ever load-bearing in an ordinary choice, so the divergence has to grow out of the engineering details rather than being stipulated. I want the less obvious direction: the Cartesian view concludes 'don't gather' while the embedded view concludes 'gather.' The embedded view's reason must trace to something beneficial about the measurement process itself that the Cartesian view is forced to ignore — not to a different utility function or risk preference, and not to some unrelated windfall like the waste heat happening to warm the office.

Then do the check: walk through what each view recommends and why, in terms of the causal structure of the case. Say exactly which feature of the scenario is carrying the disagreement, and what would happen if you removed it. Finish with the strongest move a defender of the Cartesian view could make to show the case is rigged or irrelevant. Oh, and write the answer in American English.
