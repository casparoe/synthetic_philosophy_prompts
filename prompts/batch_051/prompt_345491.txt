I spent thirty years as an analytical chemist, which may be why I keep wanting to break philosophy down into what-you-need-before-what. I've got to run a session for a local reading group—mostly retired scientists and engineers, sharp but no formal philosophy—on Nozick's experience machine. I've read enough to know the usual moves, but I don't want to present it as a story with a moral. I want to teach it as the diagnostic instrument it is: the way it seperates hedonism, desire-satisfaction, and objective-list theories of well-being, and why it presses hardest on the first of those without actually refuting anything.

So plan the session for me. Don't give me the explanation; give me the order of ideas and what depends on what. What has to be in place before the thought experiment can do any sorting work? What features of the machine case are load-bearing—what must it have and what must it not have—if it's going to sort the three families of theories rather than just make people uneasy? Then the misconceptions. I'm expecting at least these: that the machine proves hedonism wrong; that "what you'd choose" and "what's good for you" are the same thing; that refusing to plug in is just status quo bias or squeamishness about the machine's creepiness; and that since we already spend whole weekends inside films and games, we're being inconsistent if we wouldn't plug in. Where in the sequence should I head each one off, and how should the sequence absorb them rather than fight them? I keep getting tangled in one sub-debate myself: whether a desire-satisfaction theorist can just say people want to be genuinely in touch with reality, not merely to have the experience of being in touch with it. Should I include that as a reserve move or leave it out at this level? Finally, what question could I ask at the end that would show me someone has got the idea that the machine is a instrument for locating values, not a gotcha for hedonists?
