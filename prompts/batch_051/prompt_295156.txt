ok so over the last few years I've pieced together a view about emotions and tranquility from three directions at once—some CBT-informed self-work, an on-and-off Stoicism phase, and a strong reaction against the idea that the goal of all this is to become a placid, low-affect person. I want a consistency audit before I put any of it in writing, because I have a creeping sense it doesn't all fit together.

Here are the commitments, as close as I can state them:

(1) Being at the mercy of anxiety, anger, and craving is one of the worst ways to live, and equanimity—not being knocked around by those states—is a real good.

(2) The main way to get that equanimity is by correcting beliefs. Most of the emotions that disturb me rest on false claims about what matters, and when I work through the false claim, the emotion lets go.

(3) But I also think grief at the death of someone you love is not an error and not something to treat away. Someone who didn't grieve would be missing something important, and I'd find the advice that you shouldn't grieve because death is outside your control kind of monstrous.

(4) I also worry that the whole equanimity project points toward a flattened life. It seems like the same openness that makes you vulnerable to grief and anxiety is what makes love, humor, and being moved by music possible, and I don't know whether you can have that without the bad parts.

(5) I think moral outrage at real injustice is often the appropriate response, and that calm acceptance of injustice is not equanimity but complicity. I don't want my equanimity to turn into looking away.

(6) I'm fairly convinced that the emotions in question are judgments or appraisals rather than blind drives—thats just why working on beliefs works at all.

(7) And I still think there's something right in the old idea that the way to stop being hurt by things outside your control is to genuinely stop caring about them, not just to pretend to.

So: does this package hang together? I can feel some friction somewhere but I can't pinpoint it, and I don't want to be one of those people who holds each piece because it sounds good in isolation. Which commitments are actually pulling against each other, which pairs only seem to be, and how would you rank the real tensions? And if I have to revise, whats the smallest change that leaves the most standing, and what does that change cost me?
