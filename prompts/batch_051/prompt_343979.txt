I'm the ideas editor at a general-interest magazine, and once a month I run a short methods session for our junior writers on a concept they keep encountering in the work of scientists and philosophers. Next month's twenty-five-minute session is on intuition pumps: the vivid thought experiments and stylized cases that writers use to make a controversial conclusion feel obvious. My goal is not to explain what any particular thought experiment claims, but to give the group a durable way to separate a case that earns its conclusion from one that mainly persuades by concreteness, framing, or emotional pull. Plan the session's explanatory arc for me: the conceptual distinction that has to be in place before the idea of an intuition pump can even be usefully discussed; the one worked example that should carry the whole session, and what features that example must have and must lack; the misconceptions writers predictably bring—I suspect they will treat vivid cases as mere illustration, or treat their own felt conviction as evidence—and the point in the sequence where each should be defused; what to leave out at this level; and a closing check that would show me they have understood the distinction rather than just learned the term.
