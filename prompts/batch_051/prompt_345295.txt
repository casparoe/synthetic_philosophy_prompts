A public health system funds treatments by explicit cost-effectiveness: a treatment is covered only if its cost per quality-adjusted life-year falls below a fixed threshold, where a QALY assigns one unit of value to a year lived in full health, fractional units to years lived with illness or disability, and adds those units across all patients, present and future, with no weight for who any of them are. A drug that buys one QALY for $40,000 is approved; a drug that would buy the same QALY for $400,000 is refused, even when the patient in question will otherwise die.

The very same system practices what is commonly called the rule of rescue: when a named, present patient is in imminent danger — the child with an ultra-rare genetic disease, the trapped miner — it spends millions with low expected benefit and applies no cost-effectiveness test at all. A $2 million attempt with a 5 percent chance of saving one identifiable child is treated as an imperative, while the QALY rule would have rejected it out of hand.

Reconstruct the theory of value, and of how individual benefits and burdens may be weighed, that each practice presupposes. Separate what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say which rival views each one quietly rules out. Then consider whether the two practices can be licensed by a single coherent theory. Do this specifically against Scanlonian contractualism: the view that an act is wrong if it is disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject, with its familiar resistance to summing small benefits across persons to outweigh a grave burden to one. Does the QALY practice strictly presuppose the interpersonal aggregation that view rejects, or is it compatible with that contractualism once budget scarcity is taken into account? Does the rule of rescue presuppose the opposite commitment — that a claim by an identified individual cannot be outweighed by aggregate opportunity costs — or only a bias toward the identified and the vivid? Say plainly where the two practices are philosophically underdetermined and where they are flatly inconsistent; then say what the health system would have to change if it adopted strict anti-aggregation throughout, and if it adopted strict aggregation throughout.
