I am a PhD student working on the boundary between proof theory and philosophy of mathematics, and I keep returning to a tension in how the discipline now treats computer-assisted and unsurveyable proofs. On one side, a proof is still officially supposed to be the kind of argument a competent reader can check line by line; deference to authority is thought to be alien to mathematics, and a referee's job is to verify, not to trust. On the other side, the field accepts as settled theorems the four-color theorem and the Kepler conjecture, whose proofs rest on exhaustive computational case analyses far too long for any human being — or any realistic team of human beings — to survey. In the four-color case, the essential enumeration was done by a computer program, and accepting the result seems to require confidence that the code did what it was claimed to do. In the Kepler case, as I understand the history, the referees eventually acknowledged they could not fully verify the computational portion, and epistemic confidence has since migrated toward the fact that a machine-checkable formalization of the argument exists — which moves the trust to proof assistants, compilers, and hardware rather than eliminating it. Neither theorem is treated as merely experimental or provisional; both are called theorems, taught as settled results, and used as premises in later work.

What I want to know is what conception of proof — and of mathematical knowledge more generally — this practice actually presupposes. Does it presuppose that a proof is a surveyable argument that produces understanding in a human reader, or that a proof is the existence of a mechanically checkable formal object, or that a proof is something the relevant community accepts as such? Those look like rival accounts, yet the practice seems to want all three at once. I am especially interested in what the practice strictly presupposes as against what it merely fits: must a mathematician who accepts the four-color theorem thereby commit to testimonial or probabilistic knowledge of artifacts and software, even if she would never describe herself that way? Is the practice coherent, or does it quietly shift the meaning of "proved" between the human-checkable ideal and the machine-checkable fallback? And if the implicit epistemology were made explicit and followed consistently, what would have to change in refereeing, in training, in what counts as understanding a result, and in how much trust the discipline places in formal verification?

Please approach this as a philosopher of mathematics with a good grasp of formal proof and the epistemology of computation. Think carefully and do not settle for the first plausible account: begin by sketching two or three distinct reconstructions of the implicit theory of proof, then defend the one you judge best as an interpretation of the actual practice. Where the practice is genuinely underdetermined between rival views, say so explicitly; where its parts pull in inconsistent directions, identify the inconsistency precisely. Keep the useful distinction between "the community is warranted in accepting the theorem" and "the theorem is proved in the traditional surveyable sense" — and tell me which of those, on your reconstruction, the practice is really tracking.
