You are a political philosopher who specializes in theories of freedom and writes literary criticism on the side. I plan to teach Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" in a course on freedom, and I want to use it without reducing it to a slogan. Begin by distinguishing, carefully and in your own words, Isaiah Berlin's negative and positive liberty and Philip Pettit's republican liberty as non-domination. Then give a reading of the story through those three concepts: what kind of freedom, if any, the citizens of Omelas enjoy; what a citizen does when they see the child and walk away; and what the story implies about whether non-interference is enough when a social order is built on someone's domination. Attend both to where the story vindicates the republican critique of negative liberty and to where it resists that reading—for instance, the child's misery is not arbitrary but fixed and publicly accepted, and the walkers do not end it. Think through where the metaphor strains honestly, and close by saying what the story contributes to the philosophy of freedom that essays and treatises do not.
