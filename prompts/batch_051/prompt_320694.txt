Ok so I just spent like an hour arguing with a friend about sovereign immunity, and we kept talking past each other on whether the state can actually do wrong or just whether there’s a remedy for it. He kept treating the maxim the king can do no wrong as a purely procedural thing about courts, but then I was reading Blackstone’s Commentaries on the Laws of England (Book I, Chapter 7) and found this quote from Sir Henry Finch that Blackstone uses:

“The king hath a prerogative in all things, that are not injurious to the subject; for in them all it must be remembered, that the king’s prerogative stretcheth not to the doing of any wrong.”

I’ve read this a dozen times and I still can’t figure out what the argument is. Is Finch saying that if an act is wrongful, it’s automatically outside the king’s prerogative, so it just isn’t a real act of sovereign power? Or is he saying the prerogative is limited by some independent standard of wrong, and the king can in fact do wrong if he violates it? Those seem like opposite readings. And then Blackstone approvingly quotes this after discussing how the law limits the king, so maybe he’s trying to hold both: the king is under the law, but the law also defines the king’s powers so narrowly that he can’t wrong anybody through them. But that sounds like it would make the whole idea of sovereign immunity collapse into a question of how you describe the act, not whether a wrong occurred. My friend thinks I’m overcomplicating it and that the point is just that the king has no legal power to authorize injury, so the remedy runs against his agents, not him. But Blackstone’s wording here seems more ambitious than that. Can you help me parse what Blackstone is doing with this passage? I’m particulary interested in whether, on this view, the state is capable of legal wrong at all, or only moral wrong, or neither.
