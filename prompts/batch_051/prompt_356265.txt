I started this a few years ago and I can't tell if its clever or a bias with good branding. For each of maybe six core beliefs—politics, religion, what I should do with my life, a couple about people I'm close to—I keep a dated note that spells out in advance what would change my mind, and by how much: not just "new information," but "if X happens by Y date, I drop this belief," or "if I catch myself making that same excuse three times, I reopen the question." When the specified thing happens, I update, even if it feels wrong in the moment, and I'm not allowed to re-litigate unless the note itself permits it. Last month one of them fired: I had written that if a friend kept a promise for a full year I would stop treating him as unreliable, and he did, and I dropped the belief, and it honestly felt like I was lying to myself. When something surprising shows up that wasn't on the list, I mostly discount it—my rule is that if it were real evidence, I would have predicted it—and I wait for the annual review of the notes.

I described this to someone as a Ulysses contract for beliefs: the calm past self who did the thinking binds the tired future self who wants to wriggle out of the update. He said that has it exactly backwards. I've built a constitutional amendment procedure for my own head that entrenches whatever I happened to believe when I was feeling unusually clever, and now the least-informed version of me—the one with the least experience—has a veto over the most-informed one. So my question: is the policy actually defensible? Under what conditions does it make sense to bind a belief the way you might bind a preference, and when is it just freezing overconfidence into a constitution? And is there a repaired version—say, one where the note's predictions have to earn trust over time rather than being obeyed on first use—that keeps the anti-wriggling benefit without turning me into my own ancestor's puppet?
