I have come to think that the ancient atomists — Leucippus, Democritus, Epicurus, and their Roman expositor Lucretius — are most charitably read not as proposing an early empirical microphysics that modern science has refuted, but as articulating a concept of what it is to be matter: to be a body is to be solid, impenetrable, and free of void, where solidity means fullness of being, while the void is not-being, which nonetheless must exist if there is to be motion and plurality. On this reading, the later discovery that chemical atoms are divisible and mostly empty space belongs to a different level of inquiry and does not refute the atomists' concept; the atomists and the physicists are not making rival claims at the same level. I want an honest map of what would count against this reading and against the underlying concept of matter. Which arguments, thought experiments, and developments in metaphysics or philosophy of physics would a defender have to concede as counting against the view, and which could they fairly dismiss as changing the subject? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, identify the costs a proponent should themselves acknowledge, and then say whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that is a defect of a position of this kind.
