I have no training in philosophy or law, so I will describe the thing that puzzles me as plainly as I can. In the city where I live, a property owner who wants to tear down a small house and replace it with a single-family house three times the size can usually proceed as a matter of right: no notice to the neighbors, no public hearing, no discretionary approval by an elected official. If the same owner wants to keep the house and add one small second unit over the garage, or convert the basement into an apartment that someone else would rent, the city requires mailed notice to every nearby property owner, a public hearing, and a discretionary finding that the project will not harm the character of the neighborhood; in practice, a handful of neighbors speaking against it can get it denied. The person who might live in the new apartment has no right to notice and no standing to speak at the hearing. Nobody claims the large house is less disruptive than the small apartment, and nobody denies that the real trigger for the process is that the second unit would house someone who does not own the property.

I understand the surface talk about parking, noise, and light, but it seems applied so selectively that I want to understand the deeper picture. What view of a neighborhood and its people does this practice presuppose? In particular, whose preferences count, whose absence from the process is simply treated as invisible, and what would neighborhood character have to be for this to count as a legitimate reason? Reconstruct the implicit theory of who has a right to the city, distinguishing what the practice strictly commits to from what it merely happens to be consistent with. Then say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and what would have to change if the underlying view were replaced by one in which future and would-be residents carried the same weight as current owners. Please answer in American English.
