After a career in experimental physics, I am used to derivations that either go through or stop at a particular line, and I want the same precision from philosophy. I have been puzzling over an argument concerning the knowability principle — the claim that every truth can in principle be known. The argument purports to show that this innocent-sounding principle collapses into the far stronger claim that every truth is actually known by someone. It runs as follows.

(1) Assume the knowability principle: for every true proposition, it is possible that someone knows it. (2) Let p be a true proposition, and suppose for reductio that p is not actually known by anyone. (3) Then the proposition 'p and no one knows that p' is true. (4) By the knowability principle, it is possible that someone knows that conjunction. (5) Whoever knows a conjunction knows each conjunct, so in that possible situation one and the same person knows that p and knows that no one knows that p. (6) Knowledge is factive: if a person knows that no one knows that p, then in that situation no one knows that p. (7) But that same person also knows that p, so in that situation someone does know that p. (8) Contradiction. Therefore the supposition that p is true but unknown is impossible, and since p was arbitrary, every true proposition is actually known.

I am told the standard response is to accept the proof as valid and to restrict the knowability principle. That strikes me as the philosophical equivalent of saving a failed theory by carving off the recalcitrant data, so I am suspicious unless the restriction can be stated in a principled way. Adding to my unease, when I rerun the derivation with 'justifiably believed' or 'supported by adequate evidence' in place of 'known', the conclusion is plainly false, which suggests something structural is going wrong rather than a missing side condition. Give me a committed diagnosis. Is the original proof valid? If not, state exactly which step fails and under what reading. If it is valid, state precisely which premise or assumption must be rejected or restricted, give the strongest non–ad hoc formulation of that restriction, and then explain whether and where the belief/evidence variant fails — and whether its failure shows that factivity was doing all the real work in the original argument.
