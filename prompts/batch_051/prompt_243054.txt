I have been trying to sort out exactly what a widely used test for the permissible public use of religious arguments commits us to, and I have a case that seems to sit right at its edge. Here is the test as I understand it; call it the public-justification test.

The test has four criteria:

(1) Public justification is required only for coercive law touching constitutional essentials or matters of basic justice; private persuasion, internal religious discourse, and noncoercive advocacy fall outside its scope.
(2) The argument is offered in the public political forum — a legislature, an election debate, or public advocacy about a law — not only inside a religious community.
(3) The reason offered must be accessible: a citizen who does not share the speaker's religion must be able to understand it and assess its force without first accepting a theological premise.
(4) A public reason must be sufficient to justify the proposed measure on its own. A religious reason may be offered in addition, but the public reason must be what bears the justificatory weight; the religious reason may accompany but not carry it.

I am deliberately not including a sincerity or motivation requirement, because I know there is a live dispute about whether the test contains one; part of what I am asking is whether this case forces the issue.

Now the case. A legislator, M., in a religiously plural democracy sponsors a bill to fund a network of end-of-life facilities — homelike residences for the dying that provide palliative care outside hospitals. In her floor speeches and committee testimony she offers only a public-health and fiscal argument: the facilities reduce expensive hospital deaths, improve patient and family satisfaction, relieve pressure on acute-care beds, and include safeguards against abuse. That argument, taken alone, would plausibly support a range of designs. Some of those designs permit physician-assisted death on the premises where it is lawful, and several allow terminal sedation under fairly broad criteria when pain is otherwise unrelievable. M.'s bill adopts the strictest plausible design: assisted death is prohibited on the premises, and terminal sedation is permitted only under narrow criteria that exclude cases where sedation might shorten life, even if it would otherwise be indicated for pain.

In a newsletter sent to her co-religionists, M. writes that the bill's restrictions are there because she believes end-of-life suffering can have redemptive value and that a Christian legislator should not facilitate acts that may hasten death. In public, when the restrictions are challenged, she does not mention redemption or the value of suffering; she defends the sedation restriction on patient-safety grounds and on the need to respect the conscientious objections of care providers.

So the public reason supports the general idea of such facilities, and perhaps supports each restrictive clause individually under some safety rationale, but the specific package — the strictest design rather than any broader one — appears to have been selected by the religious view.

Does M.'s bill satisfy the public-justification test as stated? Work through it honestly, criterion by criterion, and then say whether the case shows that the test itself needs repair — for example, by adding a motivation or sincerity requirement, by clarifying what "sufficient" means when a public reason supports a range of policies but not the exact one chosen, or by dropping or revising one of the four criteria.

Please structure the response in the following sections, in this order:

2. Criterion by criterion (450–550 words): apply each of the four criteria to the bill; for each, say whether it is satisfied, violated, or whether the criterion does not settle the matter.
3. Where the criteria go silent (150–200 words): identify the exact point at which the criteria stop determining an answer, and name the additional principle — such as a motivation test, a sincerity requirement, or a clarified notion of sufficiency — that would be needed to resolve it.
4. Settled versus contested (200–250 words): separate what is broadly settled among people who work on public reason from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. In particular, flag whether a sincerity or motivation requirement is part of the standard test or a controversial addition.
5. Bottom line (100–150 words): give a verdict on whether the case qualifies as a permissible use of religious reasons under the test as stated, and state whether the case shows that the criteria themselves need to be revised, and if so how.
