ok so I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about the KK principle and whether any mental state is luminous. She kept saying: if you're in pain, you're automatically in a position to know you're in pain, so pain is luminous. I kept trying to run the anti-luminosity argument on her — near the borderline between pain and not-pain there will be cases where you're in the state but not in a position to know it. We talked past eachother the whole time, and I think the real sticking point is that we never agreed on what "being in a position to know" amounts to. She thought it just means the fact is available through your senses or memory; I don't think mere availability is enough, but I couldn't say why without a better definition.

So: give me a working definition of "being in a position to know" (not of knowledge itself). Test it against these cases:

1. You're looking directly at a large, clearly lit clock.
2. You're asleep in a room while a radio next to the bed quietly broadcasts a true sentence in a language you understand.
3. You once learned a friend's birthday and would say it immediately if asked, but you're not currently thinking about it.
4. You guess on a multiple-choice question and happen to be right, with no sense that you are.
5. A tone is played just below your discrimination threshold while you're awake and paying attention; you report hearing nothing.
6. A complicated theorem follows from things you know, but the proof is too long for you to work through.

Run the definition through all six, say which verdicts it gets wrong or leaves unclear, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
