I'm giving a colloquium talk next month at a philosophy departement that is mostly mainstream epistemologists—evidentialists, a couple of contextualists, one reliabilist—plus a senior Wittgenstein scholar who is friendly to On Certainty but thinks the hinge reading has been overblown. My thesis is that On Certainty offers a genuine answer to Cartesian skepticism: the skeptical doubt is not refuted but shown to be idle because it would undermine the very hinges—e.g., that there is an external world, that my memory is broadly reliable—that make any doubt or inquiry possible. I lean on the passage where Wittgenstein says, roughly, that some propositions stand fast for us and that what we do with them is not to test them but to treat them as the axis around which our questions turn. I’m worried the Q&A will split between people who think I’ve just dressed up old-fashioned foundationalism in new language, and people who think I’ve made epistemic standards unacceptably relative to a form of life. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the actual objections this audience will raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging, phrased the way they would say it, with a short reply to each and a note on where that reply is soft. Include the objection I’m hoping nobody asks. Write the answer in American English.
