Consider the debate over whether the relation "better than" is transitive, as pressured by spectrum arguments: a chain of small improvements can lead from an option that is clearly better than another to one that is clearly worse. This forces a choice among rejecting transitivity, denying some suppressed premise about each adjacent comparison, or accepting that the endpoints can stand in a non-transitive relation. Which unresolved question in this area is most worth settling first, in the sense that the largest number of downstream issues depends on it? Evaluate at least these candidates: whether betterness should be modeled on one evaluative dimension or several incommensurable dimensions; how best to handle the interaction between barely noticeable differences and the semantics of comparative predicates; and whether apparent non-transitivity can be explained by constraints on rational preference cycles rather than by abandoning transitivity of value itself. Defend a prioritization among these or other questions: identify which are upstream of which, which are most tractable with current formal tools, and which you would deprioritize because resolving it would leave the core spectrum problem mostly untouched. Assume the aim is philosophical progress in the transitivity debate rather than designing a decision procedure for practical choice.
