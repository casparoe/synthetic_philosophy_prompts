I live in a pretty big shared house, and last month we had to vet someone for the spare room. One of my housemates—who, for the record, is pretty skeptical of open borders—kept leaning on the same analogy the whole time. He said: if the five of us get to interview someone and veto them because we'll have to share the kitchen and the bathroom and the awkward small talk with them, why would it be any different when a country decides who gets to cross its border? His conclusion was that a country's right to exclude is basically the same kind of thing as a household's, so it doesn't need any special justification. I don't even know if I disagree with him on immigration; the problem is I can't tell which parts of the comparison are doing the real work.

I want the whole thing audited, not just vibes. Work it out item by item: the tenants, the landlord, the spare room, the interview, the trial period, the lock on the front door, the way everyone in the house gets a veto. Then show me where it starts to tear and do the seperating: which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion he's trying to draw, and which are just little bumps in the analogy? We chose each other over the years, while citizens mostly never chose each other or the people being kept out; and we could leave and start another flatshare down the road, but someone shut out of a country can't exactly go found a rival one next door. If the comparison can't carry the weight, is there a better analogy for whatever truth is in it, or should the whole thing be retired? Please put on your professional-philosopher hat, think from first principles, and maybe start by brainstorming the correspondences before you tear into them. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the overall verdict and single out the one point your least sure about.
