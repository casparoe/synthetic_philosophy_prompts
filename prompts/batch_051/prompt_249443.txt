so I've been going down a rabbit hole with the two-envelope paradox and I want to check a connection before I embarass myself at standup. The paradox, as I understand it: two envelopes, one contains twice as much money as the other. You pick one and see it has x. The other one, you reason, is equally likely to have 2x or x/2, so its expected value is (2x + x/2)/2 = 1.25x. So you should switch. But this argument works for any x, which is weird—it seems to say you should switch no matter what you see, and even before you open, which can't be right because the setup is symmetric. From what I can gather the fix has something to do with there being no uniform prior over all possible amounts, so the "equally likely" part is doing more work than it's allowed to.

Here's the thing. At work I keep hearing what sounds like the same argument for rewriting our services in whatever new stack is trending, or moving to a new database. It goes: the new thing could be twice as good as what we have, or it could be half as good, and since the upside is bigger than the downside the expected value is positive, so we should switch. Notice that this doesn't depend at all on how good the current system actually is—just like the envelope argument doesn't depend on x. The current system's measured performance is like the x in the envelope, and the "twice as good or half as good" is the 2x or x/2. It smells like the same sleight of hand: you can't just assume the two outcomes are equally likely; that would be like assuming a uniform prior over all possible system qualities, which isn't a real distribution.

Is this a genuine structural parallel or am I just pattern matching a fun puzzle onto my job? If it is genuine, the mistake in both cases would be the same—pretending an "equally likely" exists where no well-defined base rate does—and the fix would transfer: the switch-migration argument is only valid relative to some actual distribution over how migrations tend to go, and absent that it's not an argument at all. But maybe it breaks: in engineering there kind of is a real distribution—postmortems, benchmarks, failure rates of past rewrites—so the prior isn't impossible, just missing, whereas in the envelope case it's provably impossible. I can't tell whether that's a difference that makes the parallel superficial, or just a difference in how bad the situation is.
