I have been working through the problem of evil, and I have noticed that the standard discussion is heavily weighted towards human suffering: moral wickedness, war, and the harms people inflict on one another. What troubles me far more is animal suffering, particularly the suffering of non-human animals during the hundreds of millions of years before human beings existed: predation, parasitism, starvation, disease, and mass extinction. That material looks resistant to the usual appeals to human free will, because no human choice was involved and the creatures in question could neither sin nor be morally educated by their pain.

I would like you to take a definite position on how well the main theodicies handle this pre-human animal suffering. Consider at least these six: the free will defence (including the claim that natural evil is caused by the free choices of supernatural agents), the soul-making theodicy, the natural-law or regularity theodicy, the cosmic-harmony or aesthetic theodicy, the no-best-possible-world theodicy, and sceptical theism. Rank them from most to least successful with respect to this specific evidence, and assign each a percentage representing the share of the evidential force of that suffering which, in your assessment, the theodicy can plausibly be said to blunt; take 100 per cent to mean that it removes the whole difficulty on its own, and let the six figures sum to no more than 100, with any shortfall representing the residual problem that survives all of them. Then defend the ordering in a few sentences per position. Please answer in British English.
