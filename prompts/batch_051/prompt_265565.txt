So I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm putting together an explainer on the nonidentity problem. I want to avoid the standard distance-future, climate-policy, near-extinction examples you always see, and I also don't want the version where the child's life isn't worth living — that lets people off the hook by saying the real problem is just that the life is bad. I need a thought experiment built to order.

Here's the spec. I want a same-number nonidentity case: the decision affects which child comes into existence, not how many children exist, so the child who ends up existing would not have existed under the other option. Make it ordinary-life — say, a couple deciding about the timing of conceiving or some already-routine reproductive-medicine choice, no science fiction, no far-future disaster. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph. The child they actually have must have a life that is clearly worth living, and indeed the kid is on the whole glad to exist; but the parents passed up a cheap, easy alternative that would have produced a different child with a substantially better life, and the reason they didn't wait is something pretty trivial, like impatience. I want a competent, fully informed adult reader to feel the choice they actually made was criticizable — maybe even wrong to the child — even though it's hard to point to any particular person who was made worse off by it. And no cheating by adding some independent harm to an existing person, like the couple breaking a promise to each other, that would do all the moral work.

Then do the check for me. State the two options and the two possible children, call them A and B. Walk through why a narrow person-affecting principle — the idea that an act can be wrong only if it's bad for some particular person who exists or will exist — says the parents didn't wrong the child they actually had, and, if you think a merely possible person can't be wronged, that they didn't wrong A either. Then show why a wide person-affecting view or a same-number quality principle (when the same number of people would exist either way, an outcome is worse if the people in it are worse off than the people who would have existed in the alternative) says the outcome they chose really was worse. I want you to be explicit about wich single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the judgment if you remove or soften that feature — say, the child's life is only barely worth living, or the alternative child would've been only slightly better off — and what a defender of the narrow person-affecting view would say to resist the intuition.

And one last thing, since I might actually build an explainer on top of this: tell me how confident you are that the case isolates same-number nonidentity rather than some other issue, and single out the point in your construction you are least sure about.
