Next month I am presenting a paper at a methods workshop titled “The State as an Experimental Problem: Dewey on Democracy, Inquiry, and the Limits of Expertise.” My thesis is that Dewey’s experimentalism about democracy is not a metaphor for piecemeal policy adjustment but a substantive claim about the social conditions of intelligent inquiry: democratic publics are required because only they can register the indirect consequences of associated action that specialized inquirers are structurally bad at noticing. I lean heavily on the opening of *The Public and Its Problems*, where Dewey says: “If one wishes to realize the distance which may lie between ‘facts’ and the meaning of facts, let one go to the field of social discussion.” I read this as setting up Dewey’s later argument that facts about social consequences do not interpret themselves, and that the experimental method in politics therefore requires a public capable of identifying and testing the meanings of consequences—not simply a population managed by experts.

The audience is genuinely cross-disciplinary. About half are political theorists, most of them sympathetic to deliberative democracy and deeply suspicious of anything that smells like technocratic “experimentalist governance.” A quarter are philosophers of science who work on the social organization of inquiry and will press me on whether Dewey’s view collapses into a bad sociology of science or a banal point about peer review. The rest are public law scholars who know the American administrative state well and will want to know what Dewey’s experimentalism implies for judicial review and agency discretion. Please write me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections this audience will actually raise, in roughly the order they are likely to come and also ranked by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way a person from that subfield would phrase it. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply, a note on what I should concede versus hold, and a candid note on where my reply is weakest. End with the question I should be most afraid of, the one I hope nobody asks. Keep the whole prep sheet under 1,200 words, not counting the quoted passage.
