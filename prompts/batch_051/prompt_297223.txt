I am taking a course in philosophy of criminal law, and my term paper defends a position about the void-for-vagueness doctrine. The doctrine is usually presented as having two separate requirements: a criminal statute must give potential offenders fair notice of what it prohibits, and it must not invite arbitrary or discriminatory enforcement by leaving too much discretion to police, prosecutors, and judges. I want to defend the view that these are not two independent requirements at all. My claim is that there is really a single requirement: a criminal statute must supply standards usable by the people it governs and by the officials who enforce it. Failures of fair notice and risks of arbitrary enforcement are, on my view, only two symptoms of that one underlying defect, so nothing is lost by collapsing the two prongs into one.

I know this view stands against the way the doctrine is usually framed, and I worry that I may have made it unfalsifiable. So my question is not whether you agree, but what would count against it. What would have to be true for the standard two-pronged reading to be right and my one-requirement reading wrong? Which observations about the doctrine, hypothetical statutes, or empirical findings would be decisive rather than merely awkward, and where does my view risk nothing at all? I would find it most persuasive to be shown a case, real or constructed, in which the two prongs come apart: a statute that plainly satisfies fair notice but plainly invites arbitrary enforcement, or one that guides officials precisely but leaves citizens unable to tell what is forbidden. I would also want to know whether my notion of usable standards is doing so much work that no pattern of decisions could embarrass it, and whether that is a defect in my position or something one should expect of any account of vagueness.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order and roughly at these lengths:

1. The position stated (150–200 words). Restate my claim precisely and say what it must predict if it is to be testable at all.

2. Decisive defeaters (250–300 words). Describe the strongest things that could be true and, if true, would require me to abandon or significantly revise the one-requirement view. Distinguish these from milder problems.

3. Merely awkward evidence (200–250 words). What findings or cases would raise costs for my view without being fatal, and how would I most naturally try to absorb them?

4. What defenders would concede (150–200 words). What must someone defending my position grant, and is that concession damaging or harmless?

5. Verdict (150–200 words). Give an honest assessment of how exposed the view is, whether its flexibility is a sign of insulation, and what the strongest version of it can still be.

You need not name cases or cite anything; reasoning from the structure of the doctrine and from invented statutes is enough.
