You are a moral philosopher who specializes in the ethics of loyalty, whistleblowing, and betrayal, and you are known for reconstructing implicit principles from a person's case-by-case verdicts. My colleague, a moral philosopher who works on whistleblowing and the ethics of disclosure, has given me her verdicts on a series of cases over the years. I will list them as I remember them, then ask you what she would say about a new case. The cases all concern when breaching a duty of loyalty or confidentiality is justified.

Her earlier verdicts:

1. A plant safety officer at a chemical company discovers that the company is discharging untreated solvents into a public river, in violation of its permit. She raises the matter internally for months and is ignored, then sends documents to the state environmental regulator. My colleague's verdict: the disclosure was not only permissible but required; continued loyalty to the firm would have been the greater wrong.

2. A manager at a small nonprofit sees a coworker billing personal dinners to the organization as donor cultivation. Without speaking to the coworker, the manager forwards the receipts to the finance director, triggering a formal investigation. My colleague's verdict: the manager did wrong. The scale of the wrongdoing did not justify bypassing the coworker, and the first move should have been a direct conversation.

3. An intelligence analyst learns that his agency has been collecting domestic communications without legal authorization and has falsely denied the program to its oversight committee. He complains through the agency's internal inspector general process; the complaint is closed with no action, and he is told not to pursue it. He then contacts a reporter and provides a limited set of documents. My colleague's verdict: the leak was justified despite the non-disclosure agreement, because internal oversight had already failed and the secrecy itself was being used to undermine oversight.

4. A man discovers that a close friend has been having an affair and tells the friend's spouse. My colleague's verdict: this was an unjustified betrayal. The man was not protecting anyone from an ongoing hidden harm of the kind that overrides loyalty, and the disclosure was more self-righteous than protective.

5. A graduate student discovers that her supervisor has fabricated images in two published papers and continues to publish related work. The university investigates but clears the supervisor without examining the lab records. The student sends the raw evidence to the editors of the journals. My colleague's verdict: the student did the right thing. The journals were the appropriate audience, and scientific integrity is the kind of public trust in which concealment is itself a serious wrong.

Now the new case:

An engineer at a company that makes home insulin pumps finds a software fault that can, under a rare but predictable sequence of inputs, cause a fatal overdose. Several hundred affected devices are already being used by patients. She reports the problem to the company's quality assurance lead; the company opens an internal assessment but will not commit to a timeline and does not stop shipping or warn patients. That evening, without waiting, she sends documentation to a tech journalist. She has not contacted the relevant medical-device regulator, and she has no direct evidence that the company plans to suppress the problem, but the devices remain in use and she believes delay could cost lives.

Predict my colleague's verdict on the engineer's decision to go to the journalist. More importantly, give me the principle she seems to be running. Specifically: identify at least two or three candidate principles that fit her earlier verdicts equally well; say what each candidate principle would predict about the new case; identify the closest earlier case and explain why; give your best guess about her verdict and the principle behind it; and finally describe one further case that would cleanly separate the leading candidate principles from one another.
