I am a doctoral student in philosophy, working on personal identity and the authority of the earlier self. I have been re-reading Mill's *On Liberty*, and the dedication has been bothering me. Mill writes: "Like all that I have written for many years, it belongs as much to her as to me; but the work as it stands has had, in a very insufficient degree, the inestimable advantage of her revision; some of the most important portions having been reserved for a more careful re-examination, which they are now never destined to receive." What strikes me is that Mill treats the published text as having a standing that survives the absence of a revision he himself thought was important. That seems to me to be the right shape for thinking about advance directives.

I have been calling my view the principle of deferred revision. The idea is this: when a person writes an advance directive, the later self is best understood not as an owner who revokes a contract, but as the only surviving artefact of an unfinished project. The earlier self is like Mill issuing the text; the later self is like the book that had to go to press without Harriet Taylor's revision. The later self has no obligation to remain identical to the earlier self, but it does have a custodial obligation to the project whose revision can no longer be supplied. That is why a dramatically changed later self can still be bound by a directive: not because the earlier self's preferences are authoritative, but because the later self is the only remaining site where the earlier self's project could be completed or honestly abandoned. I know this may be merely a metaphor dressed in Mill's dedication, and I want it tested hard.
