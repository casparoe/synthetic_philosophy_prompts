I keep coming back to an asymmetry in how we police testimony, and I want it either justified or abandoned. If a colleague tells me the highway is jammed and I don't bother checking a traffic app, nobody thinks I've been epistemically reckless. But if I move my retirement into a single stock on the say-so of a broker I'd just met, I'm a textbook case of gullibility, full stop. In both cases I'm believing unsupported testimony, so what's actually separating them—stakes, the speaker's incentives, institutional role, the fact that one is cheap to check and the other isn't? Does the distinction line up with the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate about testimony, or does it cut across it? Give the best justification you can for the asymmetry, test it against a nearby case where the stakes are high but the testifier is a close friend, and then say whether the asymmetry stands, should be reversed, or is just a double standard we're comfortable with.
