I have been rereading Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, Book III, chapter 6, and the following passage has unsettled what I thought I believed about courage:

"Now the most terrible thing of all is death; for it is the end, and when a man is dead, nothing, we think, either good or evil can befall him any more. But even death, we should hold, does not in all circumstances give an opportunity for Courage: for instance we do not call a man courageous for facing death by drowning or disease. What form of death then is a test of Courage? Presumably that which is the noblest. Now the noblest form of death is death in battle, for it is encountered in the midst of the greatest and most noble of dangers. And this conclusion is borne out by the principle on which public honors are bestowed in republics and under monarchies. The courageous man, therefore, in the proper sense of the term, will be he who fearlessly confronts a noble death, or some sudden peril that threatens death; and the perils of war answer this description most fully."

Aristotle ties courage proper to facing a noble death, especially death in battle, and explicitly denies the name to facing death by drowning or disease. That makes sense for his world, but I live in a society where battle death is very rare and the military is a specialized profession. I am trying to figure out what I actually think about courage as a virtue in a peaceful society, proceeding from my gut on cases. Here are my verdicts:

1. A firefighter who enters a burning building to save a child, knowing there is a real chance of death: clearly courageous.
2. A dissident who publishes criticism of an authoritarian regime, knowing imprisonment or execution is likely: clearly courageous.
3. A cancer patient who endures brutal treatment without complaint and maintains hope for the sake of their family: not exactly courage—something like fortitude or endurance, but not the same.
4. An alpinist who repeatedly takes fatal risks on difficult climbs for no social purpose: not courage; more like thrill-seeking, even though death is possible and skill is required.
5. A corporate whistleblower who reports fraud and loses their career, home, and marriage, but never faces physical danger: I want to call this courage, though it does not fit Aristotle's death-in-battle paradigm at all.
6. A soldier in a modern army who follows orders in a war and is killed: this seems closer to Aristotle's paradigm, but I am not sure I feel more admiration for it than for the whistleblower, and I am not sure the soldier exercised the relevant sort of choice.
7. Someone with severe social anxiety who gives a best man speech despite feeling terrified: I do not call this courage; I call it overcoming fear, but it feels wrong to put it in the same category as the firefighter.
8. A person who refuses to flee a city under bombardment but does nothing to help others and waits passively: not courage; something like stubbornness or resignation.
9. A surgeon who takes a high-risk operation that could destroy their career, where the risk is professional death rather than physical death: I lean toward calling this courage, but I am uneasy about whether it stretches the concept too far.

These verdicts leave me with a puzzle. Aristotle identifies the paradigm case of courage as facing a noble death, particularly in battle, for the sake of the noble. Several of my verdicts involve no physical death at all, and some involve facing death but do not strike me as courageous. Yet dropping Aristotle's paradigm and just saying "courage is acting well despite fear" would force me to count the social anxiety speech and the alpinist as courageous, which I resist.

I would like you to extract from my verdicts the best principle you can, or the smallest set of candidate principles, stated precisely, and tell me which of my verdicts it preserves and which it forces me to give up. Where Aristotle's passage would support or conflict with a candidate, say so explicitly. Finally, apply your preferred principle to two cases I have not listed: (a) a scientist who runs a highly risky experiment without safety protocols because of personal ambition, and (b) a parent who risks their life by donating a kidney to a stranger, knowing the surgery has a low but real mortality risk. I do not need you to settle these two by fiat; I need to know what your principle says about them and whether it exposes a fault line in the principle itself.
