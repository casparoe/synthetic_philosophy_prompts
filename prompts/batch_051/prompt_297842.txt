I'm giving a paper next month to the ethics and political philosophy workshop at my department, and I want to walk in with a prep sheet rather than just hope the Q&A goes fine. The paper is on the paradox of deontology. My thesis, put bluntly: the familiar paradox isn't really about value at all; its about the structure of moral reasons. The standard move—go agent-relative and say that of course I have stronger reason not to kill one person than to prevent five killings by other people—doesn't dissolve the problem, it just relocates it. The same tension reappears one level up when you ask why a rational agent should treat her own agency as specially significant in the first place. My positive view is that deontological constraints are better understood as constitutive norms of the deliberative standpoint of someone who holds herself and others to a practice of mutual accountability, not as reasons grounded in the goodness of states of affairs. I think this dissolves the original paradox, but it forces me to admit that the constraints have no grip on a reasoner who refuses that standpoint, and a chunk of the paper is about why that isn't fatal.

The audience matters here. It's a mixed workshop. Two regulars are committed consequentialists: one old-school act-utilitarian who thinks the paradox is simply a reductio of constraint-based deontology, and one younger person who thinks the right reply is to consequentialize—build agent-relative value into the ranking of outcomes and stop worrying. There are two Kantians: one works on doing/allowing distinctions and trolley variants, and one is a constructivist who will immediately ask why anyone should care about a deliberative standpoint they don't already occupy. There's also a decision theorist who wants everything stated as a ranking over options, and about six general philosophy faculty who don't know the niche literature but are excellent at spotting gaps in arguments.

Act as a senior colleague who has chaired this workshop for years and has a nasty talent for predicting exactly where a paper will get attacked. Think carefully; start by brainstorming the full space of objections before you rank them. Then give me a prep sheet, not a defense of my view: the objections roughly in order of how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way those particular people would phrase them, each with a short reply I can actually give in two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede outright, what I should hold hard, and how to answer the decision theorist when he asks for the formal statement. Most importantly, include the one objection I'm clearly hoping nobody raises, and explain why it's the dangerous one. I want you to be committed: tell me "concede this, fight that," not a menu of possibilities.
