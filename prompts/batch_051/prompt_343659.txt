I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the design of institutions and organizations, around twenty-two students, and we have one eighty-minute session to spend on metrics and perverse incentives. I want that session to make students feel Goodhart's law — the way a measure stops tracking the underlying thing it was introduced to measure once people are rewarded on the measure itself — and the related phenomenon of specification gaming, in which agents satisfy the letter of a rule while missing its spirit. The session should not open with a definition or a lecture; students should run into the dynamic themselves inside a game or simulation, and the vocabulary should be introduced only afterwards, in the debrief.

The activity should place students, in teams of four or five, inside a setting where they are producing or judging something they can genuinely care about — quality, safety, learning, or helpfulness — while being scored on a public, legible proxy for that good. The setup should make it individually rational over the course of the game to begin trading off the real good for the proxy, and there should be a tournament or comparison across teams so the pressure is felt rather than merely described; no grades or real money should be at stake, if that can be avoided. Ideally, by the end, most teams will have quietly done the very thing the session is about, and at least one team that tried to stay honest will have felt the cost of doing so.

Please give the full runnable design as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, covering: (1) the setup, materials, and formation of teams; (2) the rules of play and the scoring system, including the underlying good and the specific proxy; (3) the eighty-minute timeline from start to finish; (4) the debrief, including the explicit mapping of what happened onto the original goal, the proxy, the first trade-off, and the pressure on a team that attempted to stay honest; and (5) a frank note on what the activity cannot reproduce about real-world specification gaming and what the facilitator must supply — together with what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run and how to handle it.
