I'm a novelist, so I've always been privately obsessed with Walter Benjamin's idea about what makes an original original. In "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" he says: "Even the most perfect reproduction of a work of art is lacking in one element: its presence in time and space, its unique existence at the place where it happens to be." His point is that the original object has a history — its smudges, its previous owners, its exact spatiotemporal track — that no copy can share, no matter how exact.

Here's a borderline case that's been bugging me. Suppose a writer drafts her novel in a single notebook: revisions, coffee rings, a pressed leaf, a whole chapter stitched in later. The published book is set from it. Years later the notebook is scanned at extremely high resolution, and then — for reasons of privacy, say — she burns the thing. The only survivor is the digital scan. A scholar then uses the scan to produce a physical facsimile on archival paper, bound to match, with the stains and scribbles reproduced. To the naked eye, and even under a good loupe, it's indistinguishable from the notebook as it was. The original no longer exists, so there is no side-by-side to compare.

Apply Benjamin's criterion to this. At first pass, the facsimile clearly lacks presence in time and space: it's history begins later, at a print shop, not in the author's study. But since the original is gone, what exactly does the facsimile lack? Can something lack the original's spatiotemporal history when there is no actual object that has that history? Is the facsimile now the original manuscript, or is it a copy of a nonexistent thing, or something third? And what, if anything, has been lost from the work itself — as opposed to a fetish object?

Then add a twist: suppose, unbeknownst to everyone, she actually burned a blank dummy and the real notebook survives in a bank vault. Does that hidden fact change what the facsimile is, even though no one can ever check the difference? Argue whether the concept of an original depends on the world or on our epistemic position, and say whether Benjamin's criterion needs to be revised for cases where the original is destroyed or unknown.
