I co-edit my high school's philosophy blog, and I want to publish an interactive quiz called "What do you actually think about lying and misleading?" The quiz should have eight to ten questions and a scoring key, and it should sort readers by the judgments they make about concrete cases rather than by which label they prefer.

The underlying question is whether there is a moral difference between telling an outright lie and misleading someone with a statement that is technically true but intended to produce a false belief. I want the scoring key to distinguish at least these five positions:

- no-difference: misleading is morally as bad as lying whenever the aim and the predictable effect are the same;
- what-is-said: only asserting something one believes false is wrong in that distinctive way, so true-but-misleading speech can be wrong only for weaker or merely instrumental reasons;
- reliance: misleading is wrong exactly when the speaker has invited the other person to rely on them for the whole truth, and bluffs in plainly adversarial contexts are typically permitted;
- virtue: both lying and misleading are vices of truthfulness, but lying is the graver vice because it directly cuts what one says off from what one believes;
- consequentialist: neither form is wrong in itself; each is wrong only insofar as it causes or risks harm.

The quiz must use at least these five cases: (i) the murderer-at-the-door case, where someone gives a true but evasive answer to a would-be murderer asking where a friend is; (ii) a poker player who bluffs by making a literally true statement about their hand; (iii) a doctor who tells a patient a true but incomplete test result that the patient predictably takes as an all-clear; (iv) a politician who answers a reporter's question with a literally true statement that everyone will misread, sometimes called paltering; and (v) a friend asking for an honest opinion about a new haircut, where a misleading answer would be motivated by kindness. Do not replace these five, though you may add cases if the design requires them.

Each question should present a short, concrete version of one of these situations and offer three answer options that track different underlying principles, such as "exactly as wrong as a lie," "wrong but less wrong than a lie," or "not wrong in this situation" where that fits. Never ask questions like "do you think lying and misleading are morally equivalent?"

Then provide the scoring key. Build it from the logical space of answers, not from the list of names: work out which combinations of case-judgments are possible, locate each of the five positions in that space, and mark any boxes that are logically possible but have no real defenders, with a sentence on whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. Where a leaf has a standard name, use it in the scoring key; where none exists, mark it "unnamed" and add one sentence on that view's most awkward commitment. Include at least one question in which the lie or misleading statement is harmless and never discovered, so that a pure consequentialist is forced apart from the other positions. Finally, flag any two positions that the quiz cannot cleanly separate with these cases, and say what a further question would need to test to split them. The aim is for a reader to discover from their own answers that their view lies somewhere they had not expected.
