I've been rereading the part of Peirce's "The Fixation of Belief" where he distinguishes four ways people settle opinions: tenacity, authority, the a priori method, and the method of science. The key move—if I'm reading him right—is that the method of science is the only one whose success doesn't depend on controlling what people think or on what seems natural or reasonable to a particular community. It assumes there are real things whose characters are independent of what we think about them, so inquiry will eventually converge on one answer.

I've caught myself using a policy that seems like a modernized version of this. I'm pretty quick to discount beliefs that are maintained mostly by group loyalty or by appealing to what's "obvious" to people like us. My heuristic: if a claim can't be checked against something independent of our attitudes about it—physical evidence, predictions, records, that sort of thing—then I treat the belief as less likely to be knowledge and more likely to be a social fact. Not false exactly, but more about the community that holds it than about the world.

Is that defensible? Peirce wrote before a lot of debates about whether even science is socially constructed, whether all observation is theory-laden, and so on. Does my heuristic just recast his method of science as a prejudice? Where does it break down—say, in math, ethics, or historical interpretation—and can I restate it to keep the benefits without those failures? Also, is Peirce himself maybe overclaiming when he says the scientific method is the only one that can genuinely fix belief? What about relying on authority for good reasons, like deferring to an expert consensus? I want to know if I'm being unfairly dismissive or whether I've just absorbed Peirce's scientism without noticing.
