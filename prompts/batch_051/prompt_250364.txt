At lunch last week, two of my friends, Ren and Theo, spent nearly an hour arguing about our classmate Daniel. Daniel has spent the past year practicing a kind of radical thrift: he patches his own clothes, eats the same bagged lunch every day, walks rather than paying bus fare, and keeps a spreadsheet of every dollar. He is also determined not to "preach" any of this, and he recently declined to help run the school's financial-literacy club, which teaches budgeting to younger students, because he said he is not a lifestyle preacher.

Ren said Daniel has frugality backwards. The point of being frugal, she argued, is not a tidy bank account; it is a response to a culture that wastes too much, so it only does its real work when it spreads and changes how other people live. Daniel's thrift is invisible and unshared, which Ren called a private hobby wearing the shape of a virtue. Theo answered that frugality is a discipline you exercise over your own choices: the frugal person is the one who lives within his means, not the one who campaigns about it, and an audience is simply not part of the question. Ren replied by asking us to imagine two people with Daniel's exact discipline, one frugal behind closed doors and one who lives the same way but also teaches budgeting and lets people watch how he does it. The second produces more frugality in the world, she said, so if waste is the problem, the second does more of what actually matters, and Daniel's privacy is a refusal to do the part that helps. Theo said that turns every virtue into advertising, since a kind person is not less kind because no one saw the kindness. Ren answered that kindness unobserved still reaches the person it helps, whereas frugality unobserved reaches exactly one person, the frugal person himself, and its effects are trivial unless it spreads. Theo then asked whether Ren was really willing to say that a dishonest presenter who fakes a frugal lifestyle on stage is a better steward of resources than Daniel quietly living the discipline, and added that Daniel's self-mastery is a good in itself, not merely a tool for moving consumption statistics.

They eventually framed the disagreement as one question: is the value of frugality about being frugal, or about promoting frugality? I could see the force in both positions and did not know how to referee. Please go through the exchange point by point and adjudicate it — say where each of them is right, where each argument goes wrong, what both of them are missing, and then give an overall verdict about which of them is more right. Keep your answer to at most 300 words.
