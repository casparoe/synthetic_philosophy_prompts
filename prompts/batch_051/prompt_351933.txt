I am taking an upper-level law course on international humanitarian law, and a philosopher friend keeps pressing me on something I had not noticed before. The law of armed conflict treats combatants symmetrically: a soldier fighting for an aggressor state and a soldier fighting in self-defense are both lawful targets, both have the legal privilege to use lethal force against opposing combatants in regular hostilities, and if captured both are entitled to prisoner-of-war status rather than being prosecuted simply for having taken part in the war. The same body of law also imposes identical rules of conduct on both sides and prosecutes individual soldiers, regardless of which side they fight for, when they deliberately kill civilians or wounded or surrendering combatants. So the practice appears to say that a soldier's legal standing and permissions do not depend on whether his side's cause is just, but they do depend on how he fights. I want to understand what philosophical commitments that practice presupposes. Does it presuppose that combatants on both sides are genuinely moral equals, or only that the law must, for practical reasons, treat them as equals even if they are not? Is it compatible with the asymmetric view that soldiers fighting an unjust war lack a moral right to kill at all, their legal privilege being a pragmatic convention justified by the need to restrain the conduct of soldiers who cannot reliably assess the justice of their own side? And how does the practice's willingness to punish individual soldiers for wrongful conduct fit with its refusal to punish them for wrongful participation in an unjust cause? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of combatant responsibility as carefully as you can, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and then say what would have to change in the law of armed conflict if one adopted a strongly asymmetric moral view of combatants. I would like the response in continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
