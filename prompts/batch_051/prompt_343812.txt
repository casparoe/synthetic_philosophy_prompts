I'm a novelist a year into a novel set in a desert city, and I've ended up inventing the city's language almost from scratch. It has fifteen words for the smell of dry earth after rain, each pinning down a different grade of dust and heat and sweetness, and no word at all for privacy. Watching my workshop argue about this made me notice a double standard in how people around me talk about language and thought — the Sapir-Whorf thing. When a language makes more distinctions than mine, everyone treats that as evidence the speakers experience the world more finely: the wine vocabulary proves they taste wine differently, the color words prove they see color differently, and my fifteen rain-smell words got read, with no objection, as proof that my desert people live in a richer world of smells. But the moment I suggested the missing word might cut the other way — that maybe my protagonist doesnt hold privacy as a separate mental compartment because her language never handed her the handle — the same people corrected me: of course she can still have the concept, language is not a cage, only a crude determinist thinks a missing word is a missing thought. So extra words are allowed to shape a mind, but absent words are not allowed to leave it unshaped. Is there anything that actually justifies that asymmetry, or is it only the half of Sapir-Whorf we find flattering? I can see how a principle might go — extra words make certain thoughts easier to reach rather than newly possible; a missing label just means more work to hold the concept together — but I want to know whether that genuinely saves the asymmetry or just redescribes it. If it does not save it, which side should ordinary talk give up, and does that change how I should write this character? And yes, I am aware of the irony: a novelist asking whether words have real power, and bothered that the unflattering answer might turn out to be the consistent one.
