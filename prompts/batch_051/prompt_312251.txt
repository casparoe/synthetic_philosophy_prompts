I'm teaching a unit on why promises bind, and I want a problem that forces students to separate two families of views that classroom examples usually keep tangled together: assurance or expectation-based accounts, on which the promisor's obligation arises from leading the promisee to form a justified expectation of performance, and wronging or authority-based accounts, on which the obligation arises because the promisor has conferred on the promisee a right to performance that survives the promisee's expectations, reliance, and losses. Most stock cases separate these in the easy direction, by removing the promisee's reliance or benefit while leaving their expectation intact; I want to test the hard direction.

Construct a thought experiment in an ordinary-life setting (workplace, family, or a consumer transaction) in which someone makes a sincere, voluntary promise and later breaks it, and the breach is clearly wrong even though the promisee, at every point from the making of the promise through the breach, had no predictive expectation that the promisor would perform, never relied on the promise, suffered no loss from the breach, and no third party or general social practice is harmed. Do not stipulate the wrongness; it should fall out of the structure of the promise itself, not out of independent background duties the promisor already owed. Avoid science-fiction devices (no amnesia, no convenient comas, no surveillance gaps). Keep the scenario under roughly 150 words. After the scenario: (1) state as exactly as you can what grounds the obligation in your case; (2) walk through what an assurance-based account and a wronging-based account would each say about it, and why they diverge; (3) identify the single feature of the case that does the separating work, and say what happens to both verdicts if that feature is removed; (4) give the strongest reply an assurance theorist could make to your case, and answer it. Hard limit: 700 words total.
