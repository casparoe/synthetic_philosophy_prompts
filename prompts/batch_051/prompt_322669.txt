I've been turning over a puzzle I read (I think it's called the self-torturer) and I can't figure out where my version of the argument breaks. The setup: a guy has a dial attached to him, settings 0 through 1000. At 0 there's no pain and no money; for each single notch he moves up, he gets an extra ten thousand dollars a year, but the pain increases by an amount so small he can't notice it step to step. The dial only goes up, never down. By 1000 he's in serious, daily pain—the kind he says he would never choose in one shot over 0, even with all the money.

Here's the argument I keep getting stuck on:

(1) For any setting n below 1000, setting n+1 is rationally preferable to setting n: an extra ten grand a year is worth taking on an increase in pain you can't even feel.
(2) Rational preference is transitive: if A is rationally preferable to B and B is rationally preferable to C, then A is rationally preferable to C.
(3) So, by applying transitivity over and over, setting 1000 is rationally preferable to setting 0.
(4) But setting 1000 is not rationally preferable to setting 0—he'd refuse that trade if offered it directly.
(5) So the assumptions can't all be right, and it looks like either the step-by-step reasoning or transitivity has to give.

Walk me through it: is the argument valid as writen? If it is valid, which premise or premises are false, and what's the corrected principle I should put in its place? If it isn't valid, point to the exact inference that fails and say why.
