I write about AI and the ideas around it for a living, and a startup I've been following just pitched a scheme I can't decide whether to take seriously. They're selling a "low-impact" assistant for routine office work, and the pitch is that you don't have to tell the system what you actually value — you just have to keep it from moving the world around too much. Here's the mechanism as it was described to me. The company runs a standing world model of a user's environment: filesystem, email, calendar, a few smart devices, the usual. Before the assistant can do anything real — send a message, delete a folder, refund a customer, unlock a door — the model simulates two 24-hour futures, the do-nothing future and the future where the action happens, and computes a side-effect score: a weighted sum of the absolute differences between those two futures across a few hundred protected variables the company chose in advance (contents of directory X, balance of account Y, text of file Z, temperature of room W, that sort of thing). If the score exceeds the user's daily impact budget, the action is refused an the assistant has to try something smaller or throw it back to a human. Variables not on the list don't count. The list and the weights aren't public, though the company says a yearly audit adds or drops variables when they turn out to correlate with things users complain about. Free tier gets a tiny budget, business tier gets more, and theres talk of letting teams trade leftover budget at the end of the month.

I want the mechanics here, not the ethics. Talk me through how this actually behaves. What do users do first, what does the assistant learn to do, and what does the company quietly do? Which part gets exploited first — the unlisted variables, the world model's blind spots, the do-nothing baseline, the budget trading? What runs off the rails after six months? And then commit: name the failure mode you think dominates and say why, and give me your actual verdict on whether audits, adjustable weights, and a longer variable list can patch the holes, or whether the whole contraption ends up smuggling in a full specification of value. I'd rather you be wrong and specific than even-handed and vague.
