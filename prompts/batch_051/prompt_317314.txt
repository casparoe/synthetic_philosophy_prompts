In a seminar on social ontology that I have been running with a colleague, we have spent the term asking how standing social regularities should be classified: when is a regularity a convention in Lewis's sense, when is it a norm, and when is it neither. My colleague keeps the ledger, and over the term she has recorded verdicts on the following cases.

Driving on the left, in a country where left is the established side, she marks as a convention. Her gloss is that either side would have been as good in itself, and the reason to conform is simply that others conform.

Using hello as the standard English greeting among colleagues she also marks as a convention. Someone who switched to a different greeting, she thinks, would seem eccentric but not transgressive.

Waiting in line at the campus coffee cart she marks as a norm. The person who cuts the line is not merely failing to coordinate; strangers resent it and correct it.

Wearing dark, subdued clothing at a funeral she marks as a norm. She grants that the specific code is culturally arbitrary and could have been white, yet within the relevant culture a violation is read as expressing disregard and is socially punished.

The hand signals used in a local road-cycling club she marks as a convention, even though a missed signal can cause an injury. Her view is that the wrong signal produces dangerous confusion rather than a moralized transgression.

Wearing a heavy coat in winter she marks as neither convention nor norm. The regularity, she says, rests on independent self-interest and would persist even if no one expected it.

The next case on her agenda is the folder structure in a shared laboratory drive. The structure was initially arbitrary: any number of naming hierarchies would have served, and no one can reconstruct why the chosen one was adopted. The group has nonetheless used it for years. In the last year or two, however, new members who file materials in the wrong place have not merely been redirected; senior members describe them as careless and inconsiderate, and admit that this impression colors whether they want to collaborate with that person. She must say whether the shared folder structure is a convention, a norm, both, or neither.

I am less interested in the label than in the implicit rule she is running. Predict her verdict. In your answer: reconstruct the rule or test that best explains her earlier verdicts; identify at least two other rules that fit those earlier verdicts just as well, and say what each would predict for the folder case; state which candidate rule you think she is most likely applying, with your reasons; and specify the further case, actual or easily imagined, that would most cleanly discriminate among the candidate rules.
