I chair my town's tercentenary comittee, which is a awkward position since I moved here six years ago and now everyone expects me to know what the town has always done. Our historical society's 1902 charter contains this clause: 'The Society shall hold annually on the twenty-second day of June a public ceremony commemorating the landing of the first settlers in the year 1725; and the date of said ceremony shall be fixed and not altered, the said twenty-second day being the true anniversary of that landing as established by the memory of the oldest inhabitants.' A researcher just sent us a stack of settlers' diaries found in an attic in a neighboring county, and they plainly record the landing as May 30. She also found letters from 1879 in which a town boosters' association agreed to start a summer fair on June 22 and 'call it Old Settlers Day so people think we have a history.' It looks like the 1902 drafters copied the date from the boosters without checking. Half the committee says the charter was written to commemorate the real landing, so the 300th celebration should be moved to May 30. The other half says 123 years of June 22 ceremonies have made June 22 the real tradition, and the invented origin no longer matters. The town lawyer keeps saying this is what Hobsbawm and Anderson were on about, but he won't tell us what to do. Write the opinion I should bring to the committee: does the charter require keeping the June 22 date, moving to May 30, or something else? Argue from the text, from the purpose of the clause, and from what the 1902 drafters would likely have said if shown the diaries, then tell me which approach should control and why.
