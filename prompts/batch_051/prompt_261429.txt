I am drafting a historical novel set in Lisbon in the years around the great earthquake, and one of my central characters is a clergyman committed to something close to Leibniz's doctrine that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds. I want his position to be intellectually serious, not the flattened version Voltaire satirized in Candide, so I need to understand what the doctrine actually commits a thoughtful person to and where it would come under real strain in the aftermath of 1 November 1755, when the earthquake struck during All Saints' Day Mass, destroyed much of the city, and was followed by fire and tsunami, with tens of thousands dead.

Analyze the Lisbon earthquake as a test case for best-of-all-possible-worlds theodicy. Begin by saying precisely what the view does and does not claim — in particular, what a careful Leibnizian is committed to about local horrors, the distribution of suffering, and God's reasons for permitting this particular disaster rather than some other or none. Then move through the episode feature by feature: which aspects of the disaster the doctrine can absorb without strain, which it can handle only through moves that would sound evasive or hollow to a grieving survivor, and which, if any, rise to the level of a genuine anomaly for the view. End by saying what Lisbon shows about the theory itself: whether it refutes the doctrine, refutes only a popular caricature, forces a narrowing of its claims, or reveals that the doctrine was never in the business of consoling particular sufferers in the first place. I am less interested in the familiar verdict that Voltaire won and Leibniz lost than in a precise account of exactly where the pressure falls — and whether the clergyman could go on affirming the doctrine after the earthquake without self-deception or retreat into vacuity.
