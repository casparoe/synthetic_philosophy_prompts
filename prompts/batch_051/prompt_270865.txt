ok so ive been turning over this puzzle about abandoned stuff. If I leave a chair on the curb, the law basically says I abandoned it and anyone can take it. If I stop using a patch of land and somebody else openly farms it for long enough, they can end up owning it. But if a novel's author died forty years ago and nobody can track down who inherited the rights, the book isn't public property—it's an orphan work, and an archive or a publisher often won't touch it because some heir could surface and sue. Same with abandonware: a company makes a game, goes under in 1998, nobody knows who owns the code anymore, and yet technically its still infringing to host the game or release a patch for it. The owner is gone, but the property keeps bossing everyone around.

So what theory of ownership is the law actually presupposing here? Does it think property is a relation between a person and a thing that lapses when the person disappears, or is it more like a floating claim that keeps binding everyone else even when no identifiable owner exists? Is the system coherent, especially given that a trademark can be cancelled if its owner stops using it and doesn't intend to resume, and a patent lapses if you stop paying maintenance fees, but copyright just keeps going for decades with no use requirement? What would have to change if we switched to a view where ownership requires some live person actually exercising control, or a view where the point of property is just to make resources usable? I'm less interested in the specific statutes than in the implicit philosophy—reconstruct it, tell me where it strictly presupposes a theory versus where rival theories would also license it, and say where its being inconsistent.
