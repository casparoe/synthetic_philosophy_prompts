Something I keep going back and forth on. In the reasons literature people draw a line between the right kind of reasons for an attitude and the wrong kind of reasons. The stock case is a demon who says he'll punish you unless you admire him: the threat gives you a reason to admire him, but it doesn't make him admirable. The supposed difference is between reasons there happen to be for holding an attitude and reasons that make the attitude fitting. Lately I've started to suspect the whole distinction is a philosophers' artifact, not a line ordinary speakers actually draw.

So I want you to take the claim "ordinary people genuinely track a distinction between reasons there are for an attitude and reasons that make the attitude fitting" and design the best test you can of it. Be concrete: what vignettes or cases would you give people, what exactly would you ask them, and which pattern of answers would convince you the distinction is real, which pattern would convice you it's an artifact. Then say what the test couldn't settle even if the results came out clean — for instance, whether ordinary judgment is tracking a real normative difference or just a verbal habit, and whether the philosophers' distinction might be worth keeping even if ordinary speakers don't make it.

Finally, tell me which result or which argument would most likely change your mind about all this, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
