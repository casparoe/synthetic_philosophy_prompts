At work I'm often asked to put a number on the expected payoff of decisions with a long tail of bad outcomes: replacing a legacy codebase, adopting an unproven database, delaying a release to pay down technical debt. My spreadsheets already have optimistic, most-likely, and pessimistic estimates. The policy I actually follow, though, is to distrust the tails of my own estimates. I take my best-guess distribution over outcomes and then weight every outcome worse than my 10th percentile twice as heavily as the distribution says, and every outcome better than my 90th percentile half as heavily, then renormalize. My reasoning is that I am probably reasonably well calibrated for the body of the distribution but systematically overconfident at the tails, because the worst scenarios are precisely the ones I cannot envision in detail ahead of time. Is this a defensible standing rule, or have I just dressed up loss aversion or plain probability distortion in pseudo-quantitative language? What would have to be true about my uncertainty in my own probability estimates for a rule like this to be approximately rational, and under what conditions would it systematically mislead me? Finally, is there a repaired version of the rule that captures what actually worries me, namely unknown unknowns in the left tail, without relying on what look like arbitrary multipliers at arbitrary percentile cutoffs?
