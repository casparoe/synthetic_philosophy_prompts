I have been trying to isolate the distinct problem that an evolutionary account of life's history poses for the doctrine that creation, as it existed before the Fall, was good. My worry is not the general problem of evil, but a narrower chronological and moral difficulty about the pre-human world. The following is the clearest statement of the objection I can give.

(1) If the created order before the Fall was good, then the pervasive and characteristic processes of that order were themselves good, or at least served a good end.
(2) On an evolutionary picture, predation, parasitism, and the competitive replacement of lineages are pervasive and characteristic processes of the pre-human biological world, and are among the principal mechanisms by which the later living order came to be.
(3) These processes routinely involved injury, terror, and death for sentient creatures that were not moral agents, could not have sinned, and could not redeem or transfigure their own suffering.
(4) The lawlike suffering of such creatures is not itself good, and an omnipotent creator could have produced the same living order without making that suffering a pervasive, built-in feature of creation.
(5) Therefore the created order before the Fall was not good.
(6) So either the doctrine of a good creation is false, or the evolutionary account of a long pre-human history of predacious, suffering life is false.

Everyone who hears this argument says something has been smuggled in, but no two people point to the same step. I want a precise diagnosis. Is the argument valid? If it is invalid, identify the exact step at which it fails and say what the corrected step would be. If it is valid but unsound, identify the failing premise and give the strongest version of it that a defender of the doctrine could consistently accept.
