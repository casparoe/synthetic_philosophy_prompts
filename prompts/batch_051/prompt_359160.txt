Rule 4(2) of my wargaming society's tournament code, drafted in 1991 and never amended, provides:

'A dominated strategy shall be struck out. A strategy is dominated if another strategy open to the same player yields an outcome at least as good against every strategy still open to the opponent, and a strictly better outcome against at least one. The test shall be applied repeatedly until no further strategies can be struck out.'

A dispute has reached the adjudicators. One finalist proposes to play a strategy, S. The finalist's only other feasible strategies are T and U. The adjudicators have already struck out one reply open to the opponent — call it R — because R was dominated by another single reply under the same rule. That leaves the opponent with two replies, B and C. Against B and C, S yields 0 and 0 respectively; T yields 4 against B and −3 against C; U yields −3 against B and 4 against C. Neither T nor U dominates S once R is removed: T is worse than S against C, and U is worse than S against B. However, a fifty-fifty lottery over T and U yields an expected outcome of 0.5 against each of B and C, strictly greater than what S yields against either. Against R, S yields 2 while T and U each yield 0, so had R still been in play the lottery would not dominate S; the earlier strike-out of R is what raises the question.

The code is silent about randomised strategies, and the drafters plainly had deliberately selected single strategies in mind; nothing in the rules forbids a player from choosing a strategy by a private coin-flip.

Is S 'dominated' within the meaning of Rule 4(2)? Set out what the text, the purpose of the dominance rule, the likely answer of the drafters if this case had been put to them, and the understanding of a reasonable competitor each imply. Which approach should control, and why?
