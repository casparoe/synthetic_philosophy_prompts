I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I keep running into the same argument in longtermist and AI-risk circles, and I can't settle it. The expected-value framework feels inescapable to me: when you can't know what will happen, you list the possible outcomes, weight each by its probability and its value, and take the option with the highest total. Each of the axioms behind it — dominance, independence, and the rest — looks true seperately, and the rule gives a definite, non-arbitrary answer where other rules waffle. But here's the case I can't shake. A well-dressed stranger offers me two buttons. The first costs a hundred dollars and he walks away. The second costs every asset I own, on the promise that there is a one-in-a-trillion-trillion-trillion chance that he will then bring about a mind-numbingly enormous good — say ten-to-the-thirtieth happy lives, with no upper bound in principle. Expected value says the second button wins by an astronomical margin, and not just here: for any finite cost and any nonzero probability, a big enough payoff will swamp it. So the theory seems to license betting everything on lottery tickets with vanishing odds, and I find that absurd. Is the absurdity just my brain failing to process tiny probabilities and enormous numbers, something I should debunk and override? Or is it evidence that expected-value theory needs fixing — bounding the value function or refusing to take sufficiently small probabilities seriously? Or is there a way to keep the theory for everyday choices while denying it a vote in cases like this? Give me the strongest version of the bite-the-bullet side and the strongest version of the fix-the-theory side, then tell me which should give way and why.
