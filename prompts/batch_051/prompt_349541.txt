A book I read over the summer summarises the gender-equality paradox roughly as follows. Several large cross-national studies find that the more gender-equal a country is on standard international indices of equality, the larger the sex differences in educational and occupational choices become: women in Finland and Norway are more strongly underrepresented in engineering and mathematics than women in some much less gender-equal countries, while remaining heavily overrepresented in health and care work. The book insists that this pattern is robust across different data sets and cannot be dismissed as an artefact of how STEM is defined, because the same pattern appears in measures of personality and preferences. It concludes that when women are free to choose under conditions of economic security, average psychological differences in interests between the sexes are expressed more fully, and that this shows occupational segregation is driven mainly by dispositions rather than by socialisation or discrimination. It draws the further policy lesson that quotas, targets, and campaigns encouraging girls into engineering are working against natural inclinations rather than against injustice, and should therefore be scaled back.

This account is being used in my school debate club to argue that the underrepresentation of women in certain fields is not in itself an injustice. I am inclined to trust the data but suspicious of the jump from the cross-national pattern to the claim that the differences are innate and that policy is misguided. Before I use this in a debate, I would like an accuracy audit. Please separate what the book gets right, what it simplifies or overstates, and what a careful defender of the gender-equality paradox would and would not claim on its basis. Which parts of the summary survive correction, and do the policy conclusions follow? I would rather you commit to an overall verdict on the summary and its use in the debate than lay out the options and leave me to choose.
