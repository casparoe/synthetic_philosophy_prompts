I've been stuck on an asymmetry in how people think about community, and I'm not sure it tracks anything real. Suppose you think belonging to a close-knit community is a genuine good—the kind of thing someone's life is worse off without. Now take two streets that end up in the same state. On the first, a block where neighbors once looked after each other's kids, shared tools, had a block party every year; over a decade people move away, fall out of touch, and the thing dissolves. Everyone agrees something was lost, even if it was nobody's fault. On the second street, the same number of people with the same rough dispositions move in over the same years, but they never form those ties in the first place; they stay polite strangers. Nobody treats the second street as the scene of any loss at all, let alone the same loss. If belonging is a good, then in both cases there's a world without that good at the end. So why is the good missing from the second street not mourned, not regretted, not even noticed, while the first street's loss feels like a genuine subtraction from people's lives?

I want to know whether anything justifies that asymmetry. The obvious candidate is that a real community has determinate members with actual relationships and shared history, while a merely possible community has no particular persons to be deprived, so the second street is only missing a possibility, not a reality. But I'm not convinced that does all the work: the people on the second street are real, and each of them is missing out on something. So is the asymmetry defensible, or is it just loss aversion dressed up as principle? If its indefensible, what should we actually say—should we regret all the communities that never formed? And if my setup is confused, or the two cases aren't parallel in the relevant sense, I'd rather you say that plainly than try to rescue it. Give me your honest view and tell me how confident you are, and where you're least sure; also, please write in American English.
