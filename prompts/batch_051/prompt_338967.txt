I run a small discussion group of about twelve adults, none with academic training in philosophy; we meet once for ninety minutes. I have been thinking on my own about a strange feature of ordinary preferences: if one option is genuinely better than a second, and the second better than a third, then the first ought to be better than the third, yet people sometimes move through a long series of small comparisons and end up somewhere they would have refused had they been shown the start and finish side by side. I would like an activity that lets the group feel that tension before anyone names it. Please design a runnable classroom game or structured set of forced choices with concrete rules, cheaply prepared materials, and a scoring or payoff scheme that requires no real money. Put participants through roughly eight or ten pairwise choices along a single spectrum, arranged so that at each neighboring pair most people prefer the next option to the previous one, but when they compare the first option with the last the pattern reverses. The choices should feel real in the room rather than like a survey: let them vote, move around, or experience a mild stimulus such as sound or physical effort if that helps. Then give me a debrief that maps what happened onto the question of whether better-than must be transitive, says what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and is explicit about which part of the underlying puzzle cannot be produced inside any game and must be supplied by discussion.
