I've been working on a piece about people who outsource big chunks of their memory to their phones, and a philosopher I was talking to pointed me to the extended mind thesis — roughly, the idea that the mind isnt confined to the skull and that a bit of the world can become part of your cognitive system if you use it in the right way. The standard way to defend this is to give criteria something like: the thing has to be reliably there when you need it; its contents have to be directly accessible without much effort; you have to endorse them automatically instead of checking; and you have to have taken the thing on as part of yourself at some point. Thats my rough summary, not a formal definition.

Now for the case that keeps nagging at me. I interviewed a woman — call her Dana — who has worn a little clip-on camera every day for about ten years. It snaps a photo every twenty seconds or so and uploads them to a cloud service. A companion app uses some kind of AI to choose a few photos a day and push them to her phone as 'memories.' Over the years she has come to treat those surfaced photos as her actual recollection of her own life. If you ask her about a trip or a family wedding, what she 'remembers' is mostly what the app has chosen to show her. She knows the feed is curated — she's noticed it skews cheerful — and that makes her a little uneasy, but she still calls the whole set-up how she remembers, and she isnt sure she'd know who she was without it.

So, using the criteria I just listed: is Dana's camera-plus-app system literally part of her mind or memory? Go through each criterion and be honest about where the parallel strains. Where it comes out no, say whether that's because the criteria are too strict or because the case genuinely is different. And tell me whether you think the case exposes a missing condition — something about who does the selecting, or about whether she controls the resource — or whether adding that would just be rigging the answer to stay 'no.'
