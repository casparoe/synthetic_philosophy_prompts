In thinking about panpsychism and the combination problem, I want to know what it takes for a collection of experiential parts to compose a single subject of experience rather than merely a heap of many subjects. Here are the criteria I find myself committed to.

(1) Unity. At any moment, all the composite's simultaneous experiences are co-conscious: they occur within a single phenomenal field, and there is exactly one point of view, not several running in parallel.
(2) Ownership. For each experience occurring within the composite, there is a determinate subject that has it — either the composite or one of its parts — and no experience belongs to two subjects at once.
(3) Agency. The composite acts as a single agent when its unified mental states, rather than the states of its parts acting separately, guide its behavior.
(4) Continuity. The composite persists as one and the same subject over time through the usual causal and psychological continuities.

Now consider a case at the edge. Suppose two healthy adult human beings have their brains joined by a synthetic fiber tract that grows in stages and, at the stage I am interested in, transmits high-resolution traffic in both directions. Perceptually, they are fully fused: both report the same single visual field, composed of whatever the two pairs of eyes are taking in, and each confirms the other's description of its contents. A sudden fright in one is felt by both as "my fright," not as news about the other. Memory, however, is only partially fused: each can call up the other's pre-operative memories only as something heard but not lived, except while the other is deliberately rehearsing them; during the rehearsal, each recalls them as his own and is surprised when he cannot continue past the point where the other stopped. In action, the two bodies generally move as if carrying out one plan, but under stress they have been seen to fight over a single object, each hand reversing the other's grip until both stop. Afterward, each person reports that he was the one in control the whole time and that the other's resistance felt like a sudden failure of his own body. Asked whether they are one subject or two, they answer at the same moment, "One — and I am it," and then cannot say whether this is one report or two.

Go through (1)–(4), say whether the joined pair qualifies as a single subject of experience on these criteria, and say whether the case shows that the criteria themselves need revision — in particular, whether it shows that subjecthood is graded rather than all-or-nothing, or that one of the criteria is not actually necessary.
