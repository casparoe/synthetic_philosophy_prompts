I recently had a long argument with a friend about heterophenomenology as a method for the science of consciousness. My friend argued that Dennett's method is neutral between realists and eliminativists about experience: it treats a subject's first-person reports as data to be interpreted, while officially suspending judgment about whether the reported experiences really exist. I suspect this neutrality is overstated, because the instruction to treat reports as something like narrative or fiction, rather than as windows onto experience, already tilts the method against realism. Can that suspicion be tested, or is the dispute only about what one is allowed to mean by 'neutral'? Design the strongest empirical or methodological test you can of the claim that heterophenomenology is a neutral instrument — for example, comparing how coders with realist versus eliminativist commitments apply the method to the same ambiguous or conflicting first-person reports, or manipulating the bracketing instruction. Say which pattern of results would support the neutrality claim, which would support my suspicion, and be explicit about what even a favourable result would still not settle.
