I am preparing a course on Kant's Critique of Pure Reason, and I have come to think the Antinomies are best taught as a dramatic structure rather than as a set of separate proofs: two opposed conclusions, each apparently demonstrated by reason, followed by a diagnosis about what the whole dispute was really about. I would like a poem to read when we finish the unit—a Petrarchan sonnet (fourteen lines, with a turn from octave to sestet) on the First Antinomy. The octave should present both sides of the mathematical antinomy: the thesis, that the world has a beginning in time and is bounded in space; and the antithesis, that it has neither beginning nor bound and is infinite in both respects. Let each position appear as something reason genuinely proves, rather than as an obvious absurdity, and let the form itself build the collision between them.

The volta should mark Kant's critical turn. His point is not to pick a winner, and not simply to say that reason contradicts itself. It is to show that the contradiction arises because we treat the world-whole as if it were a given object of possible experience, although the regress of conditions is never given as a completed totality. In the sestet, render the resolution proper to a mathematical antinomy: once we distinguish appearances from things in themselves, neither the thesis nor the antithesis holds as a claim about the world as a finished whole; the series is neither finite nor infinite in itself, and the question dissolves instead of being answered. Please do not mention Kant or the Critique by name. Make the turn do the philosophical work, and keep the argument accurate enough that a Kantian would recognize the transcendental idealist resolution rather than a vague gesture at paradox.
