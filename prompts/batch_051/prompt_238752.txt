Two of my flatmates got into it last night about whether God could make something horrific morally obligatory, and they've both decided I'm the referee. They'd been reading around the Mu'tazila–Ash'ari dispute over al-ḥusn wa-l-qubḥ — roughly, whether good and evil are known by reason (ʿaql) or only by revelation (sharʿ) — and it spiralled into a row, with Aisha defending the Mu'tazili line and Dan the Ash'ari line.

(1) Aisha said reason can grasp right and wrong on its own. God commands justice because it is just; there's no possible world where He commands torture, not because something outside Him stops Him, but because that would contradict His justice, which is just what He is. Dan said that makes goodness an independant standard God has to answer to, which undermines tawḥīd. On the Ash'ari picture, acts aren't good or evil in themselves; good is whatever God commands, evil whatever He forbids. There's no 'because' before the command.

(2) Aisha came back that this makes 'God is just' empty. If 'good' just means 'whatever God commands', God could have commanded the opposite and you'd be forced to call it good, so praising Him for justice is like praising a judge for ruling that his own rulings are correct. It also wrecks al-waʿd wa-l-waʿīd (the promise and the threat): if God isn't bound to deliver the reward and punishment He announced, the whole structure of salvation looses its footing. Dan replied that God binding Himself is enough for us to trust Him, and that demanding an independent standard is still measuring the lawmaker against the law.

(3) Aisha then made the nature move: the standard isn't outside God, so there's no second eternal thing. It's internal to who He is. Being unable to command evil isn't a limitation; it's a perfection, like being unable to be foolish. Dan said that just pushes the problem back: if the standard is God's nature, then either that nature is what it is arbitrarily, or it's a fact about God that He didn't create, and the outside standard's come back in through the back door.

(4) Aisha's last point was epistemic: on Dan's view, how do you even recognise a command as genuinely divine? You can't first check that the command is good, because the command is what makes things good — so it looks circular. Dan said she's no better off: if reason is your guide, your 'moral knowledge' is just your own intuitions dressed up as God's nature, which is its own kind of arbitrariness.

They went round in circles after that. Please adjudicate it round by round — for each of the four moves, say who's on stronger ground and where their argument overreaches — and then give an overall verdict: who's more right about what, and what are both of them missing?
