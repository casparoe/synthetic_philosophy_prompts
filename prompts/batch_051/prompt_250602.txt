I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in applied ethics: sixteen students, one 80-minute session. The unit is on the ethics of imposing small risks of serious harm on people who never individually consent. Design a classroom activity that makes the students feel the force of the following idea before it is named for them: actual consent cannot do the work we routinely assign to it in this domain, and each apparent substitute—majority authorization, ex ante compensation, hypothetical consent behind a veil of ignorance, and the conversion of a large risk to one person into a small risk spread over many—changes the moral question rather than answering it. By the end, participants should see that "it was for the best" and "they would have agreed" are not restatements of consent but substantive positions about how to treat separate persons, and they should be able to articulate what is lost when a fixed amount of expected harm is spread across a population.

The activity must be a structured exercise with real (small) stakes, not a lecture and not a discussion of pre-read texts. The rules, roles, and incentives should be arranged so that the students run into the problem themselves: they should feel the paralysis of a strict consent requirement, then the appeal and eventual failure of compensation, then the shift in intuition when risk is spread. Please supply: the setup and materials, including a payoff scheme that does not use real money; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 80 minutes; the sequence of votes, moves, or choices and the rule governing each round; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the distinctions of actual versus hypothetical consent, ex ante versus ex post justification, statistical versus identifiable victims, and the aggregation of small risks. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and what I will need to supply myself.
