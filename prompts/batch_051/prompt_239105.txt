Two colleagues have spent a semester arguing about whether Harry Truman's decision to use atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki was morally justified. One takes an Anscombian position, following the argument Anscombe made when she opposed Oxford's proposed honorary degree for Truman: intentionally killing the innocent as a means to an end is murder, and no expected consequences can make it a legitimate act of war, so Truman was a murderer and the subsequent honors were a scandal. The other takes a consequentialist just-war position: the bombings knowingly killed noncombatants, but they were justified as the least bad available way to end the war quickly, avoid a land invasion, and prevent far greater loss of life on both sides. They have agreed to attempt a good-faith adversarial collaboration, and they want a document they could both sign.

Write the document this collaboration should produce. It should include: (1) a numbered section of claims both parties accept, stated non-vacuously and without slant; (2) a disagreements table giving the precise residual disagreement, with each side's view phrased so that its own proponents would accept the wording; (3) a list of cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling them would genuinely move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, the argument, case, or empirical finding both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I am especially interested in the following candidate cruxes, which you should sort, refine, or reject as appropriate: the absolute-versus-presumptive status of noncombatant immunity; the moral relevance of the intention/foresight distinction and the doctrine of double effect; whether the actual decision-making process among Truman and his advisors shows that the targeting of cities was intended as a means to end the war or foreseen as a side effect; and whether the counterfactual invasion estimates were epistemically available and morally decisive at the time. Please focus on the structure of the moral disagreement rather than on historical scholarship alone, and say whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal.
