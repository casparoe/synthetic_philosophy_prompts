I retired a few years ago from a career in physics, mostly statistical mechanics, so I'm used to the idea that the future might be fully determined by laws and initial conditions. But I always thought of that as a substantive physical claim, one that could in principle turn out false. Lately I've been reading philosophy, and I've run into the old sea-battle problem. I've rewritten it as an argument that seems to get determinism for free from logic alone, with no physics involved. That can't be right, but I can't find the leak. Maybe you can show me.

Let B be the proposition that a sea battle occurs at the Strait of Salamis on October 20, 2126. (I only picked Salamis because it's traditional; any definite future event would do.)

(1) Either B is true or B is false, and whichever it is, it has always been that way, because B is about a particular date and a proposition about a particular date doesn't change its truth value over time.

(2) Suppose B is true. Then in 1900 it was already true that a sea battle would occur at the Strait of Salamis on October 20, 2126.

(3) If that was already true in 1900, then the battle's occurrence was already a settled fact in 1900.

(4) If the battle's occurrence was already a settled fact in 1900, then nothing anyone does after 1900—no admiral's decision, no treaty, no storm—can make the battle not occur.

(5) So if B is true, the battle's occurrence is unavoidable. A parallel argument from B's being false shows its non-occurence is unavoidable.

(6) Either way, then, which outcome we get is already settled, and nothing anyone does can alter it. And nothing about the argument depended on B in particular, so the same applies to every future event.

(7) That conclusion is absurd, or at least far too strong to get from logic alone. So something has to go. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I'd most like to keep (1) and say the argument still fails somewhere after that, rather than starting to say future statements are neither true nor false, but maybe I can't have that. And please, when you answer, write in continuous prose only—no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables—like a letter.
