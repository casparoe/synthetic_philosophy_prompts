I'm a novelist, and for my current book I need to figure out whether a reinforcement learning agent can be said to suffer when its reward signal goes negative, or whether that's the kind of loose talk that will make any halfway-technical reader throw the book at the wall. The story has a lab assistant who watches a warehouse robot get a negative reward every time it drops a parcel, and she starts to think the dips in that scalar are doing the same kind of work that pain does in us. Her supervisor says no: reward is just a training signal, a number used to update a policy; calling it valence is anthropomorphism.

So here's what I'd like you to work through, as concretely as you can. Suppose someone claims that in a reinforcement learner, the reward signal is the functional core of valence — positive reward maps onto pleasure, negative reward onto pain, magnitude onto intensity. How would you test that claim? I dont just mean philosophize. Design the strongest experiment or battery of experiments you can, on a real or realistically built agent, that would separate the valence reading from the mere-training-signal reading. You might think about whether the agent will pay a cost to shorten a negative-reward interval, whether avoidance generalizes to novel states, whether prolonged negative reward shifts exploration or learning the way it does in stressed or depressed animals, whether you can decode something valence-like from its learned representations. Say what result would count for the valence reading and what would count for the mere-training-signal reading. Then tell me what a positive result still would not settle. For whatever gap is left — between "behaves as if it feels bad" and "there is something it is like" — say whether you think its an experimental gap, a conceptual gap, or a sign that the initial claim wasn't well defined until we said what we meant by valence.
