I keep running into what feels like a single pattern in how languages change, but I don't have a clean term for it. Everyone says sound change is regular: a sound shifts everywhere at once, and the old slogan is that sound laws admit no exceptions. But the forms people actually preserve are a pile of exceptions, and they don't look random. They're exactly the high-frequency words. English keeps went as the past of go, and good-better-best, for centuries with no sign of regularizing, while rarer old strong past tenses like holp and clomb turned into helped and climbed within a few centuries because nobody used them often enough to remember they were irregular. Children still overregularize, producing goed before they learn went, but the irregular hangs on at the very top of the frequency scale while the -ed pattern sweeps through everything below it. Something similar seems to be happening now with dreamt turning into dreamed in American English.

So my question is whether this dynamic has an established name — not just the existence of exceptions, but the fact that an exception's survival depends on how frequent it is, and the default rule takes over as soon as the form slips below some vague threshhold. I don't want just a label. I want to know what the standard examples are supposed to show, how this differs from plain irregularity and from suppletion (since go/went is the first example people give for that), and whether it's even right to call the -ed pattern a rule if being crowded out by a frequent form is part of how the whole system normally works.
