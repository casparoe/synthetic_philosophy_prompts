Speaking as a value theorist, I keep going back and forth on whether effort has value beyond what it produces, and I'd like you to force me off the fence. On one side I'm drawn to a clean instrumentalist story: struggle is a cost, and the only reason to care about it is the outcomes or capacities it buys. That view has real virtues — a single ledger of final goods, no extra value that floats free of how the world actually ends up, and it explains why we treat wasted effort as a pity rather than something to admire. But i can't shake the intuition that something is lost there. Take two sculptors: one spends years of failed attempts and finally produces a piece; the other, on a first try and with no training, happens to produce an indistinguishable piece. The external result is identical, and let's stipulate there's no difference in what either learns or becomes. I still want to say the first person's life contains something of value the second's doesn't — not just that we have reason to praise the first, but that the striving itself was good in a way the effortless success wasnt. Same with the runner who grinds for a mediocre time versus the talent who coasts to the same time. Is the intuition tracking something real, or is it my motivational machinery mistaking a cost I chose to pay for a benefit? I don't want a pro/con list. Give me the strongest case for each side and then a negotiated verdict: does the intuition get debunked, does the theory get amended, or is there a reading on which both survive? And tell me where your reasoning is least stable — what argument or example would actually flip your verdict.
