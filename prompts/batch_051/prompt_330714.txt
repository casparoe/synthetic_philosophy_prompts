You are an expert in decision theory and the philosophy of probability. Please think carefully, and start by brainstorming several plausible diagnoses before settling on the most convincing one.

Last night a friend and I spent hours arguing about the Pasadena game: a fair coin is tossed until it comes up heads, and if the first heads is on the nth toss, the payoff is (-1)^(n+1) · 2^n/n dollars. We agree on the mathematical facts: the probability-weighted payoff series is conditionally convergent, its absolute series diverges like the harmonic series, and so the expected value is undefined in the standard sense; by rearranging terms one can make the sum converge to any real number or diverge. We also agree that ordinary expected utility theory assigns no finite price to the game.

But we stalled completely on what follows. My friend thinks the right conclusion is that rational choice is simply indeterminate: since the expectation is undefined, there is no fact of the matter about a fair price. I think that is too quick. The natural-order truncations of the game converge to ln 2, and it seems as though a decision theory should be able to say something informative about a game whose probability-weighted outcomes are so tightly patterned. He replies that finite truncations smuggle in an ordering of states that the underlying probability distribution does not justify, and that any appeal to them is a disguised attempt to manufacture an expectation where none exists. We went in circles like this for hours.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this actually a mathematical disagreement in disguise—for example, about whether conditional convergence is enough for an expectation, or about how to sum over countably many states? Is it a normative disagreement about what a decision theory is supposed to deliver when its canonical numbers are undefined? Is it a verbal disagreement about the phrase "fair price"? Or are we answering different questions under one heading? Given your diagnosis, what would genuinely count as progress—a formal concept, a new principle, a distinction, or a change of question—and what, if anything, would not?
