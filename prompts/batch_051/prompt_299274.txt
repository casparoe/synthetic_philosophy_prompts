I've been volunteering with a language revitalization committee for about six months now. The language has never had a settled writing system—people text in ad hoc spellings, somebody made an alphabet in the 70s that nobody liked, and now the committee has to actually decide. Since I took a couple of linguistics courses once, I got asked to help. The same fights come up every meeting: the younger members want one symbol per sound, no exceptions, so a new reader can sound words out. The elders often want to keep a few distinctions that only they still make (a trilled r versus a tapped r, for example), on the grounds that the writing shouldn't let the old pronunciation disappear. And then there are words that change their final sound depending on what follows—a root ends in the sound b when a suffix vowel comes after it, but surfaces with a p when it stands alone—and the committee splits over whether to write the root the same everywhere or write what you actually hear.

The arrangement is that I produce one written procedure the committee can run every contested letter or spelling decision through, since we meet once a month, have no budget, and most members have zero formal training in phonology. I need a checklist or ordered series of tests, not a lecture: when a sound earns its own symbol, when you deliberately leave a distinction unwritten, when you write the grammar or the root instead of the surface pronunciation, and when tone (high on one syllable per word here) should be marked always, only for minimal pairs, or not at all. The procedure has to be usable by someone who doesn't know terms like phoneme and allophone—actually it should hide that machinery and just ask questions.

Please work through two or three concrete example decisions using the made-up mini-inventory below, and make at least one of the examples come out as an answer the committee wont like, so I can see how the procedure behaves under pressure. Also tell me straight which decisions no procedure can make for us and have to be settled by community judgment, and where the checklist is likely to give a defensible-looking answer that could still be wrong in ten years. Mini-phonology for the examples: consonants p t k b d g s h m n l r y w; vowels i e a o u; high tone falls on one syllable per word and carries some lexical weight; b d g become p t k at word-end; and the trill/tap distinction persists only among the oldest speakers.
