I'm a philosophy PhD student, and next month I have to present a chapter of my dissertation at the med school's ethics grand rounds. I'm dreading the Q&A more than the talk itself. The chapter defends what I call dignity as a status concept: the claim that human dignity is not about feeling respected, not about behaving with decorum, and not just a placeholder for autonomy plus rules against exploitation. Instead I argue it picks out the property of being the kind of thing that may not be treated as fungible -- my dignity is what makes it wrong to kill me and harvest my organs to save five strangers even if that would respect the five, or to enroll me in a high-risk experiment without consent even if the aggregate welfare math comes out positive. I read this as a Kantian status notion that grounds inviolability constraints, and I try to show that nothing made only out of autonomy and welfare can do that same work.

The audience is mixed: a couple of clinicians, a health economist who defaults to QALYs, a bioethicist who has argued that dignity is a useless concept because it adds nothing beyond respect for autonomy and a ban on exploitation, and a nurse-scholar who suspects dignity talk is covert sectarianism. I'm not asking you to write my defense, I want a Q&A prep sheet. Give me the seven or eight objections I'm actually going to get, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way those people would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Spend extra time on what the health economist does with the organ-harvesting case and what the useless-concept person does with inviolability constraints. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and end with a line beginning 'Verdict:' that names that question in a few words.
