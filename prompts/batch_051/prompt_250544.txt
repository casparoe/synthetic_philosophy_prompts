Test this argument about whether every statement must be true or false. It turns on a passage from Aristotle’s *De Interpretatione*, Part 4:

“Every sentence has meaning, not as being the natural means by which a physical faculty is realized, but, as we have said, by convention. Yet every sentence is not a proposition; only such are propositions as have in them either truth or falsity. Thus a prayer is a sentence, but is neither true nor false.”

Here is the argument:

(1) Bivalence is the thesis that every statement is either true or false.  
(2) A prayer is a statement: it is a grammatically complete indicative sentence.  
(3) According to the passage, a prayer is neither true nor false.  
(4) Therefore at least one statement, a prayer, is neither true nor false.  
(5) Therefore bivalence is false.

I suspect the argument is either invalid or equivocates on “statement,” but I cannot make the diagnosis precise. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and under what reading of “statement” would the argument become valid? If it is valid, what should a defender of bivalence say: that the principle needs to be restricted, or that the passage is not about statements in the modern sense?
