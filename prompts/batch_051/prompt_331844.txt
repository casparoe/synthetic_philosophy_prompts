I've been arguing with a study group about Han Feizi's claim that a state should govern through incentives and penalties rather than by trying to make officials or subjects virtuous. To test that idea, suppose a newly chartered administrative region is to be run under what its drafters call a Legalist charter. The charter forbids any organ of the regional government from appealing to character, conscience, or moral suasion: officials may set standards and attach rewards and punishments, but they may not exhort compliance, praise civic virtue, or hire and promote on anything other than quantified outcomes. Officials receive a small fixed stipend plus a large payment tied to their unit's metrics—permits processed, disputes resolved, revenues collected, violations corrected—with automatic deductions whenever an audit attributes a shortfall or irregularity to official decisions. A separate inspectorate monitors the administration: inspectors receive a percentage of every penalty they impose on officials that survives review, and they lose pay for investigations that are overturned and for failures to detect misconduct that is later exposed. Appeals go to a panel of senior inspectors whose own compensation increases as the gap shrinks between the violations the inspectorate confirmed and the violations that later come to light through other channels, and decreases as that gap widens. There is no outside court for administrative discipline, and no provision for pardons, warnings, or leniency grounded in an official's otherwise good record.

I don't want an abstract verdict on whether the system is just or humane. Walk me through how it would actually behave. What gets gamed first, by whom, and by what technique? Does the relationship between the bureaucracy and the inspectorate settle into honest monitoring, collusion, or some oscillation between the two? How does linking inspector pay to penalties shape which misconduct is pursued—and what happens to the misconduct that resists quantification, like slow degradation of services or the corruption of the metrics themselves? What kind of person does this mechanism select for in the civil service, and what do residents experience day to day? Then assess the case in Legalist terms: which of these outcomes would a Han Feizi-style framework have anticipated, given its emphasis on the two handles of reward and punishment and its refusal to treat official good character as a reliable foundation for order? Which outcomes does the framework misdescribe or ignore because it models people as responders to incentives but not as members of coalitions, manipulators of information, or exercisers of discretionary judgment? Finally, say whether the gaps can be closed by more precise targets, steeper penalties, or an additional layer of monitoring—or whether any workable repair would silently reintroduce the virtue-based discretion the charter was designed to exclude.
