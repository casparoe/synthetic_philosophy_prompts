I am a PhD student in the history and philosophy of science, and I serve as the graduate-student representative on my university's Frontiers Fund committee, an internal panel that makes one substantial high-risk research award per cycle. This cycle the panel is deadlocked between two finalists, and the chair has asked me to draft the decision memo. I would like you to write it.

Proposal A is a theoretical project in cell biology. It purports to derive a family of morphogen-gradient phenomena from a single symmetry principle and two free parameters, thereby unifying three lines of experimental work that are currently treated as separate. The panel's mathematicians describe the derivation as unusually elegant. But the model reproduces existing quantitative data only approximately, and one of its two parameters is not yet independently measurable; the apparent fit could be an artifact of latent flexibility.

Proposal B is an explicitly data-driven project in the same area. It would build a large statistical model directly around a newly released single-cell dataset, without commitment to any compact underlying principle. Its fit to the calibration data is excellent, but its equations are cumbersome and its parameter list is long; two external reviewers called the approach "ad hoc" and "ugly."

Supporters of Proposal A argue that elegance has repeatedly been a reliable guide to truth in the history of science, and that funding Proposal B amounts to subsidizing curve-fitting. Supporters of Proposal B reply that the fund's mandate is to support work likely to yield discoveries, that the historical record of elegant theories is flattered by survivor bias, and that an aesthetic preference should not be allowed to masquerade as evidence. The committee is split.

Please write the short decision memo I will circulate before the next meeting, addressed to the panel and the fund's director. It should recommend a concrete course of action: fund Proposal A, fund Proposal B, fund neither, or fund one with conditions (for example, a reduced award, staged milestones, or an independent re-analysis). The memo should be the document on which the panel actually votes. It should state explicitly what epistemic role, if any, elegance may play in the decision, and should distinguish at least among elegance as felt beauty, elegance as parsimony or simplicity, and elegance as unifying power. It should also give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration in favor of the option you do not recommend. Keep the memo to roughly 1,000 words. Because the panel needs a clean bottom line, end the memo with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by one short phrase stating your recommendation.
