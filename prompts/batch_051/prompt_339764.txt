I am a philosopher of mind and serve as the faculty director of a university comparative-cognition laboratory. Our founding charter, adopted in the 1970s to restrain how the group described its experimental animals, contains the following rule of interpretation:

"No member of the laboratory shall attribute to any subject the exercise of a higher psychological faculty — belief, expectation, intention, foresight, or understanding — when the behavior in question can be explained by the exercise of a faculty lower in the scale of psychological development."

The charter's authors had whole animals in mind. We now face a case they could not have foreseen. A collaborating group grows cultures of roughly half a million rat cortical neurons on multi-electrode arrays. Given patterned electrical feedback, a culture reliably improves over thousands of trials at steering a simulated paddle so that it tracks a moving target. One of our doctoral students is preparing a paper and wants to write that the culture "anticipates the target's trajectory" and "learns to avoid persistent errors." Another member objects that the rule of interpretation forbids exactly this: the entire behavior can be explained, she argues, without postulating expectation or understanding at all, by changes in synaptic weights, homeostatic plasticity, and the closed-loop structure of the array. The student replies that an animal's nervous system can likewise be described in those lower-level terms, so if the rule is read that broadly it would forbid nearly every cognitive attribution the field has ever made; and, in any case, the charter was written for animals rather than for a dish of neurons.

We need not reach a practical publication decision so much as a conceptual one. Apply the rule on its plain terms, then by its evident purpose, then as its authors would most plausibly have answered had they been asked in 1975, and finally as a reasonable scientist reading it today would take it to require. Along the way, address the harder challenge the student raises: if a lower-level explanation is always in principle available, can the lower/higher distinction do any work at all in a case like this, or does the rule merely restate an attribution one has already made? If the readings diverge, say which should govern and why, and close by identifying the single feature of the neural-culture case that bears the greatest interpretive weight.
