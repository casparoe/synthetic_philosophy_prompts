In my high school philosophy class we touched on an old puzzle: how can a pattern of sounds, which isn't conscious and isn't about anything, nevertheless be sad? I know the standard moves. Sad music might just carry acoustic features of a sad voice or body — slow tempo, low register, falling contours, soft dynamics — so we hear the resemblance. Or it might be a learned cultural association with minor keys. Or "sad" might be a loose metaphor we shouldn't press.

I think those answers miss something. Here is my own theory, which I haven't seen stated exactly this way. Call it the temporal slope theory. The sadness of music, I claim, is not carried by any single quality of the notes — not by minorness, slowness, or low pitch — but by the relation between notes over time. A passage is sad when it repeatedly builds a point of relative stability (a tonic arrival, a held high note, the end of a phrase) and then falls or slides away downward from it, so that the earlier stability is heard as something given up. Sad music is music that keeps doing this: it opens a small place where you could stay, lets you hear it as a place, and then takes it away. The sadness is not that the sound reminds us of something sad; it is the structure of losing ground in time, heard directly, the way a rising scale is heard as going up. A downward step is not sad by itself, and a minor chord is not sad by itself. On my view, a melody that keeps cadencing and then reopening downward would be sad even in a major key and at a brisk tempo — and I think that is right.

I can see that this is related to expectation theories (sadness as thwarted expectation) and to contour theories, but I think it is narrower than "thwarted expectation": it requires a specific kind of disappointment, a falling away from an already achieved point, not just any surprise.
