I've got a policy I want checked, because I suspect it's half insight and half scar tissue. The policy is about impossibility claims in formal setups—strings under rewrite rules, positions in a game, sentences under axioms. When someone says X can't be reached or proved, I basically don't except the claim unless they can show me an invariant: a property the starting point has, every allowed move preserves, and the target lacks. I picked this up from the MU-puzzle, which I'll spell out in case you don't know it. Alphabet M, I, U; axiom MI; rules: append U to a string ending in I; from Mx derive Mxx; replace III anywhere with U; delete UU anywhere. The question is whether MU is derivable, and the answer is no, because every derivable string has a number of I's that isn't a multiple of 3, while MU has zero I's. That move—jumping out of the system and finding a property all the rules respect—is what I now treat as the only real kind of negative result. If someone tells me they searched exhaustively, or they have a syntactic argument about proof lengths or normalization, I hear them out but I don't actually update. A friend says I've turned one toy puzzle into an epistemology and that plenty of respectable non-derivability results don't go through an invariant. So: is the rule defensible? Where does it fail, and is there a version of it that keeps the insight without being dogmatic about proof style? And how would I tell, from the inside, whether I'm being properly strict about negative claims or just refusing to learn any proof method I can't picture as an invariant?
