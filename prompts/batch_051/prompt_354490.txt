I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I have been following the debate over whether large language models genuinely understand language or merely reproduce statistical regularities. I keep noticing a recurring move in that debate for which I don't have a precise name.

The skeptic's side usually runs like this: there is some ingredient X—phenomenal consciousness, bodily grounding in an environment, communicative intent, or referential contact with the world—and because large language models lack X, they do not understand in any theoretically serious sense; their apparent competence is a byproduct of something else. The optimist's side often concedes the absence of X and argues that X is not necessary for understanding at all: a system that answers novel questions, follows novel instructions, summarizes, translates, and solves problems is doing what understanding does, and any remaining requirement is an artifact of the skeptic's preferred definition of the word. Each side then accuses the other of changing the meaning of the term mid-argument.

What strikes me is that this is not a merely verbal dispute, because the meaning of the term is itself the contested terrain. Often both sides accept the same behavioral evidence, and the fight is over which concept of understanding that evidence should answer to. It is as if the two sides use the same dialectical move from opposite directions: each can say that the other's premise only follows if we accept their definition. Adjudicating the dispute from either side's definition therefore looks question-begging, while adjudicating it without any definition looks impossible.

Is there an established concept for exactly this configuration: a dispute that is partly verbal and partly substantive, in which the boundary between the verbal and the substantive is itself one of the things being fought over? I am aware of the notion of a merely verbal dispute, where disambiguation dissolves the disagreement, and I am aware of the term metalinguistic negotiation, which is usually introduced for disputes over how to use a normative or evaluative term. But merely verbal seems too weak, and metalinguistic negotiation seems aimed at a slightly different shape of disagreement. Does what I am describing have a name of its own, or is it best treated as metalinguistic negotiation with an empirical face? And if nothing established fits, could you coin a term for it and define it precisely? I would not be satisfied with a bare label; what I need is the carving—what the phenomenon is, how it differs from each of those neighboring concepts, and where its edges are.
