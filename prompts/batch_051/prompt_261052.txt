A blogger I respect has been pushing the following argument, and it has been sitting badly with me: the conclusion feels like it outruns the premises, but I can't locate the step that does the work.

(1) Nonhuman animals can suffer, and their suffering matters in the same fundamental way human suffering does.
(2) In most wild habitats the great majority of animals born die very young, by predation, starvation, disease, or exposure, so their lives are most plausibly net-negative in welfare.
(3) Destroying or degrading a habitat lowers the number of animals born into it over time.
(4) So, wherever (2) holds, habitat destruction reduces the total amount of suffering in the world, which is at least a pro tanto moral reason to prefer it to preservation.
(5) By parity, we would have a pro tanto reason to drain wetlands, clear forests, or otherwise replace species-rich ecosystems with species-poor ones wherever doing so would reduce future net-negative lives.
(6) But a conservation ethic that could be pressed into recommending systematic habitat destruction is an absurd conservation ethic.

He concludes that either (2) is false, or ordinary conservation ethics is systematically mistaken about wild-animal welfare. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I'd like a step-by-step diagnosis: which premise or inference you reject, why it initially looks plausible, and what rejecting it costs for the underlying question of whether destroying or degrading habitat reduces wild-animal suffering in the first place.
