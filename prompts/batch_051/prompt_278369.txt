Consider a working definition of societal collapse that combines several common criteria. A society has collapsed when (1) its population falls sharply, especially in the core region; (2) centralized political authority — the state, the ruling dynasty, or the institutions that coordinate the territory — is lost over most of the area rather than transferred intact; (3) major urban or ceremonial centers are abandoned; (4) the society's most distinctive complex cultural production — monumental building, written or inscribed records, specialized craft, long-distance exchange — ceases rather than merely changing style; (5) this happens within at most a few generations; and (6) the affected population does not simply persist elsewhere as a recognizable people with substantial cultural and institutional continuity.

Now apply these criteria to the Classic Maya lowlands, described here in simplified form; treat this description as the stipulated facts of the case. In the mid-to-late first millennium CE, the southern lowlands supported densely populated city-states ruled by divine kings, with inscribed stone monuments bearing calendrical records and with intensive agriculture tying rural populations to their urban centers. Over roughly a century or two, the monuments ceased to be raised, one major center after another was abandoned, core-area population fell very sharply, the institution of the divine king disappeared, and no single conquest or environmental event explains the sequence. Yet the people did not die out: populations relocated toward the coasts, the lakes, and the northern lowlands, some northern centers subsequently flourished, highland communities continued, and Maya-speaking peoples with related ritual and social practices remain populous in the region today.

Does this case qualify as a collapse under the six criteria? Work through them one by one, distinguishing where the stipulated facts clearly satisfy a criterion, where they clearly fail it, and where they cut both ways. Then say whether your overall verdict changes depending on what "a society" is taken to be — the lowland state-system, the southern lowland population, or the Maya as a people. If it does change, is that a defect in the criteria? In particular, should a definition of collapse specify a unit of analysis (a political system, a region, a people), and would doing so force us to redescribe the terminal Classic Maya lowlands as the dissolution of one kind of political order within a society that persisted, rather than the failure of a society as such?
