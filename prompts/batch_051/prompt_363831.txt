You are a philosopher of language who works on the semantics and pragmatics of socially loaded terms, and you have been asked to adjudicate a dispute in a long-running reading group. Think carefully, and before you commit to an answer, run the dilemma through the strongest version of each horn and note where each resolution breaks down; do not include that brainstorm in the final answer.

The group has been arguing about what makes a statement racist. One member has set down the following argument; the conclusion strikes everyone as too strong, but no one can agree where the exit is.

(1) A statement is racist in virtue of one of three things: the speaker’s intent; the meaning the statement has and the uptake it receives; or the statement’s predictable causal effects. These exhaust the plausible criteria.

(2) If intent is the criterion, dog whistles escape. The technique of a dog whistle is precisely to keep the racial aim off the surface so that it can be denied, and the more sincerely deniable the signal is, the better it works. So the concept is disarmed in the very cases it was most needed, and it hands speakers a recipe for evasion: say it obliquely and disclaim the intent.

(3) If meaning-and-uptake is the criterion, the same sentence turns out to be racist or not depending on whether an audience catches the code. That makes the property audience-relative in an uncomfortable way: a speaker can be charged with racism because an audience supplied a code the speaker never sent, while a speaker who did send a code goes uncensured because no one was astute enough to catch it.

(4) If predictable causal effects is the criterion, too much counts. A sober recital of racial disparities in crime or poverty, a historian’s account of racial violence, or a comedian’s set clipped and recontextualized can all foreseeably be used to inflame racial hostility. The concept then no longer distinguishes racist speech from speech with racially unequal or exploitable effects.

(5) So no criterion captures dog whistles while keeping the concept selective enough to do critical work: each either under-generates for the target cases or over-generates for ordinary speech.

(6) Therefore “racist statement” is not a stable kind, and we should either stop treating dog whistles as racist statements or stop using “racist” as a property of statements at all.

The group would most like to keep two claims at once: that dog whistles are genuinely racist statements, and that not everything with bad racial effects counts as racist. But they will settle for whichever cost is smallest. The question is: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it commit you to? If the trichotomy in (1) is false, say precisely where the argument slides and what picture of “racist” the best resolution leaves us with.

Format the answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the step rejected; (2) why that step looked defensible; (3) the positive criterion or diagnosis you favor; (4) what that position gives up or can no longer say; (5) the strongest objection to your answer and your reply.
