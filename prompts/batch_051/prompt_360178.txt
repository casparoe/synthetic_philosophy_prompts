I have been thinking my way, without any formal training, toward a view I have come to call tranquilism: the only things that are ultimately bad are craving and suffering, and the only things that are ultimately good are their absence. Its appeal is that it is simple and, unlike most ethical views I have tried, decisive. When I compare two possible lives or two possible futures, I have a procedure: identify the total craving and suffering in each; everything else—pleasure, achievement, beauty, understanding, love—matters only insofar as it increases or decreases those two quantities. My own experience also seems to vote for it. When I catch myself heavily valuing success, being right, or some projected pleasure, the valuing itself feels like a kind of agitation, and what I would most like is for that agitation to stop.

The problem is that I cannot shake a particular intuition. Consider an hour spent in absorbed attention: someone watching a migration of birds across a marsh, not trying to identify every species, not photographing them, not checking an app, and not restless. The hour contains little craving and little suffering. But I also find myself unwilling to say that its value is exhausted by that absence. It appears to me that the absorption itself is positively good, not merely the absence of something bad. I am not asking to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that yields, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that yields, and whether a third move is available: perhaps a reading of tranquilism on which absorbed attention is not an extra good beyond the absence of craving, but is exactly what the absence of craving looks like from the inside. Would that move preserve the theory, or does it merely protect the theory by quietly changing the meaning of its central term?
