I've been thinking about when resentment is fitting — the standing kind of anger you direct at someone as a person, not just the flash of pain — and my gut verdicts about concrete cases don't seem to add up to any clean rule. Here they are; what I want is either the principle that captures them, or an honest list of which ones I should overturn.

(1) A friend is helping me move a couch and, not seeing my hand under it, drops it and crushes two of my fingers. I yell, but afterward there's no standing anger toward him. I don't resent him.

(2) Same friend, same couch, same fingers, but he drops it on my hand on purpose because he's angry about something I said. I resent him for that.

(3) A guy gets drunk at a bar knowing he'll drive home, runs a light, and totals my parked car while I'm standing next to it; I barely get out of the way. He didn't intend to hurt anyone, but I resent him.

(4) A man with an undiagnosed brain tumor suddenly starts having violent outbursts, and one day he punches me in the face. I don't resent him; I mostly want someone to get him to a neurologist.

(5) A person who's had paranoid delusions for years hits me because a voice told him I was an imposter. I can't resent him, and I don't think I should.

(6) A four-year-old bites me hard on the arm during a tantrum. I'm annoyed, maybe even angry for a second, but I wouldn't say he wronged me, and the feeling isn't the resentment I'd have toward an adult.

(7) A colleague ruins a project we'd worked on for months because he couldn't be bothered to read the instructions. He just didn't care. I resent him.

(8) A friend betrays my trust in a way that really sets me back. He was raised in an abusive environment that plausibly made him cold and manipulative. I know his character has a causal backstory, but I still resent him.

Here's what bugs me. Cases (3), (7), and (8) all involve behavior with clear causes and no particular intent to harm me specifically — recklessness, plain carelessness, a personality shaped by abuse — yet I resent all three people. Cases (4), (5), and (6) also involve behavior with clear causes and no intent to harm me — a tumor, a delusion, being four years old — and I don't resent them. So the principle can't be "resentment is apt only when they intended harm," and it can't be "resentment is apt only when the bad behavior isn't causally explained," because then I'd have to give up either the first three verdicts or the last three. What distinguishes the two trios? Is there one principle that gets all eight, or is the simpler move to overturn one of my verdicts, and if so which one should go first?

Also, tell me what the principle you land on says about two cases I haven't listed: (a) someone who is horribly sleep-deprived and out of character snaps and says something cruel to me, and (b) a corporation that systematically lies about its product and kills people. Does the principle extend to something like resenting a corporation, or does that stretch it too far?
