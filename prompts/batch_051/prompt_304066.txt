I'm a science journalist, and I run a monthly philosophy-of-science reading group for working scientists — physicists and biologists mostly, who never took the subject but like to argue about it after conference talks. Next session is Hempel's ravens, and last time we tried it the discussion stalled because people were using "confirms" to mean five different things at once. So I want to skip the lecture and open with a sorting instrument instead: a "which position do you actually hold?" handout that walks people through a decision tree and drops each person at a named position in the debate.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names. Start everyone at the shared minimal starting point that a black raven does confirm "all ravens are black" to at least some degree, then branch from there. Each node should be a concrete question a person could answer without knowing the labels — e.g., you find a white shoe in the hallway while not looking for shoes: does it make you even slightly more confident that all ravens are black? What about a green apple in a fruit bowl? Should a black raven and a non-black non-raven confirm to the same degree or different degrees? Should logical equivalence preserve confirmation? Does it matter that you know there are vastly more non-black things in the world than ravens, so the shoe is a tinier piece of a much bigger haystack? Does it matter that "non-black" feels gerrymandered in a way "raven" doesn't, even if logic allows it? What happens if you fix the sampling protocol in advance — does the shoe confirm if it was drawn from a random sample of shoes, as opposed to a random sample of non-black things? I especially want at least one branch that separates "the apple confirms, but only infinitesimally, and the intuition is just over-reading a tiny effect" from "the apple confirms in exactly the same sense as a black raven, and we should just bite that bullet." Most quick treatments blur those two, and they're different views.

Leaves should include at least: Hempel's own view, the standard Bayesian line, a view that rejects the equivalence condition, a view that rejects the Nicod condition, and a Goodman-style projectibility line (the one that blames "non-black" as an unprojectible, grue-ish predicate). For any combination of answers nobody occupies, give a one-line diagnosis — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored — and flag any pairs of leaves your questions can't actually separate. Keep it to six or seven leaves and dont go more than five questions deep.
