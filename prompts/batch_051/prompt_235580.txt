For the past few years I have been trying to get straight on something that kept coming up around my uncle's death: the difference between killing someone and letting someone die. The medical team was willing to withhold or withdraw treatments, but they spoke as if there were an enormous gap between that and taking any step to end a life, and no one could tell me what the actual rule was. Since then I have been collecting examples where ordinary speech seems steady, even though I cannot find the principle underneath. If someone fires a gun into a crowd and a person is hit, everyone says that person was killed. If a hiker comes across an injured stranger, decides not to get involved, and the stranger dies, people usually say the hiker let him die rather than killed him. So the distinction is not simply about how bad the conduct was, and it is not only about what a court could prove.

But then I run into cases like these. A family agrees to have a ventilator removed from a relative who is not expected to recover. Doctors and a family decide not to treat pneumonia in a woman with advanced dementia, because the infection will likely end her life peacefully. A dying person in terrible pain receives a high dose of morphine to stop the pain, and everyone knows the dose may also stop her breathing. A parent leaves a toddler in a car on a hot day, forgets, and comes back to find the child dead. I hear people call some of these killings and some of them lettings-die, but not with much consistency, and I cannot tell what is doing the work: the action versus the omission, the intention, the special relationship, or the stopping of something already begun versus the refusal to start it.

Could you build me a working definition of killing, as opposed to letting die? I would like the definition to classify the two straightforward cases correctly and to give a definite answer, even if a difficult one, for each of the four in-between cases. Please state the definition, run every one of my cases through it, be honest about where it seems to give the wrong answer or no answer at all, and then either revise the definition or explain why we should accept the surprising verdict.
