Since retiring from a career in analytical chemistry and taking up philosophy, I have found myself puzzling over three habits of my former laboratory that now look philosophically significant rather than merely conventional.

First, we demanded a degree of material purity that went well beyond what any particular measurement seemed to require: parts-per-billion metal standards for calibrations whose conclusions would have survived parts-per-million contamination, HPLC-grade solvents for routine bulk preparations, and a general preference for the purest grade available. Using anything less felt like carelessness, even when no one could show that the result would change.

Second, we treated established methods as authoritative nearly by default. A pharmacopoeial or international standard method could be followed without further justification, while departing from one required a defense. We likewise cited canonical papers in our introductions as a mark of disciplinary seriousness, whether or not the argument of the paper actually rested on them.

Third, only positive results were written up. Failed runs and null results mostly vanished into laboratory notebooks. A finding was something that came out; the many attempts that came to nothing were not regarded as additions to knowledge.

Taken together, the three habits seem to treat truth-seeking, tradition, and purity as worth honoring for their own sake, yet the third habit appears to discard much of the truth that the first two habits were meant to protect. I would like to understand what theory of value, or what combination of value commitments, this pattern presupposes. Please reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as the practice allows; distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; say whether the three habits presuppose one consistent view, several views that sit uneasily together, or are underdetermined between rival theories; and indicate what would have to change in laboratory practice if truth, tradition, or purity were instead treated as purely instrumental.
