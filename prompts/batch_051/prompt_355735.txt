I have recently been writing short fiction, and I keep running into an obstacle that surprises me. When a story asks readers to accept a morally repugnant state of affairs as right—not merely to understand a character who holds such a view, but to adopt the story’s own moral perspective, even tentatively—readers report that the piece feels broken, impossible to enter, or as if they are being asked to do something they cannot do. A friend mentioned that philosophers call this imaginative resistance, and that the problem goes back at least to David Hume.

Hume addresses a nearby issue in his essay Of the Standard of Taste when he discusses how a writer’s moral views affect the reception of a work. As I understand the passage, he says that a poet may be forgiven many speculative oddities, but a serious moral perversity in the work—where the representation seems to approve what a reader’s settled sentiments condemn—is a blemish that no delicacy of imagination can overlook; the reader cannot enter into the sentiments of the piece, and the work gives displeasure rather than the pleasure proper to fiction. The thought seems to be that the obstacle is not an inability to grasp what is being represented, but a refusal of the feelings the work demands.

What are the most important implications of Hume’s observation for current philosophical accounts of imaginative resistance? In particular, does his way of locating the difficulty in the reader’s settled moral sentiments, rather than in some conceptual impossibility or failure of belief revision, favor accounts that treat imaginative resistance as primarily an affective or evaluative unwillingness rather than a cognitive incapacity? And if Hume is right, what follows about the difference between resisting a moral premise in fiction and resisting a merely factual impossibility, such as a story in which the characters can both be somewhere and not be somewhere? I would especially appreciate an answer that presses beyond the standard taxonomy of imaginative resistance and says what practical conclusions a writer or teacher should draw if the Humean diagnosis is correct.
