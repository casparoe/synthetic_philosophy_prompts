You are a careful philosophical auditor with expertise in formal epistemology and the foundations of Bayesian decision theory. Please examine the following set of my commitments about rational degrees of belief and tell me whether they can all be held together.

Here is my position, written out as six theses:

1. Probabilism. A rational agent's degrees of belief at a given time are best represented by a single, real-valued probability function satisfying the standard probability axioms. I accept the synchronic Dutch book argument for this: if an agent's credences violate the probability axioms, there is a set of bets, each fair by the agent's own lights, whose combination guarantees a loss.

2. Countable additivity. The probability function should be countably additive, not merely finitely additive. I am moved by the extension of the Dutch book argument to countably many bets, and I also think countable additivity is needed for credences to behave properly under limits and in continuous models.

3. Regularity. Only logical falsehoods may rationally receive credence exactly 0. For any proposition that is not a contradiction, a rational agent should keep some positive probability open, however small — that is part of intellectual humility.

4. The Principal Principle. If an agent is certain that the objective chance of A is c, her credence in A should be c, provided she has no inadmissible information: credence should defer to known chance. I understand this roughly along the lines of David Lewis's Principal Principle.

5. Conditionalisation without the diachronic Dutch book. If an agent learns E with certainty, she should move to her old credence conditional on E. I hold this on general epistemological grounds. At the same time, I reject the diachronic Dutch book argument for conditionalisation: I think diachronic books exploit an agent's anticipated policy of revising her mind rather than exposing any incoherence in her present state, so I do not think that particular argument succeeds.

6. Continuous models are legitimate. Rational agents may represent uncertainty about real-valued quantities, such as an unknown physical parameter, by means of probability density functions over an uncountable space; rationality does not require us to restrict attention to finite or countable sets of hypotheses.

Please assess whether this set hangs together. Work through the theses pairwise before reaching any overall verdict, and make sure you distinguish genuine tensions from pairs that merely look uncomfortable but are compatible on closer inspection. Then give me:

(i) each genuine tension, ordered from most to least severe, with a sentence or two on how severe it is and what the cheapest way to remove it would be;

(ii) any pair that looks bad at first glance but is not a real tension, and why it dissolves;

(iii) the single revision of the six theses that would remove the greatest number of problems at the lowest philosophical cost.

Take your time, and write the entire answer in British English.
