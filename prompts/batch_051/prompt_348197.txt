ok i need someone to talk me out of a loop i'm stuck in. i'm a phd student working on moral epistemology, and there's this view about moral testimony i keep defending in seminar and then disowning at 2am. The view is roughly: moral belief is not just another belief you can outsource. If my doctor tells me my thyroid is underactive, that settles it; if my friend tells me the joke I made was cruel, but I can't see the cruelty, then taking her word for it doesn't give me moral knowledge—there's something defective about a moral belief that's just borrowed. It needs to be my own, and for that I need understanding, not just truth handed to me. I like the theory because it explains a lot at once: why we feel cheated when someone says 'i just trust them on that', why moral disagreements can't be settled by polling, why we demand that people give reasons for their moral views, why there's an asymmetry between moral testimony and ordinary factual testimony. It also gives me a nice crisp criterion: in the moral domain, deference is presumptively off the table—you either see it for yourself or you suspend.

But here's the intuition I can't make peace with. Suppose i've had a horrible week, i'm sleep-deprived and furious, and i've drafted an email to my sister that I think is just bluntly honest. My partner—who has a genuinely long track record of being better than me at this exact sort of interpersonal call—reads it and says it's cruel, that sending it would be wrong. I know my own judgment is currently garbage; I know she's usually right. I look again at the email and I honestly can't see the cruelty. It seems to me that the right, humble thing to do is to take her word for it: to believe 'this would be cruel and I shouldn't send it', even though I can't yet see why. The theory seems to tell me I can't do that—that the most I can do is act on her advice, or suspend judgment, and that actually adopting the belief would be epistemically illegitimate or a kind of faking. And that just sounds like epistemic pride dressed up as rigor: I'm refusing to let someone who knows better make me believe the truth when I'm in no position to earn it myself.

So which gives? Should I debunk the intuition—maybe it's really just the intuition that I should *act* on her word and suspend belief, and the feeling that I'm 'believing' is a loose way of talking, in which case the theory survives intact? Or should I amend the theory, say pure moral deference is fine exactly when you have standing reason to think your own moral understanding is compromised? And if I amend it that way, how do I stop deference from being justified all over the place—since am I not always compromised in some way? Walk me through what it costs to give up each side, whether the explanatory goods can survive the amendment, and whether there's a reading of 'take her word for it' on which the theory and the intuition are both right. I don't want a referee's verdict; I want to know what I'd have to give up either way.
