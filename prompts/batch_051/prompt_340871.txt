I've been reading Bentham for a class on utilitarianism, and I keep noticing how he holds together two things that today usually pull people in opposite directions. He's the one who brushed off natural rights as nonsense upon stilts, and he'd already gone after the French Revolution's declarations of rights for being flowery and groundless. Yet he's also the one who insisted that the suffering of animals matters morally: what counts, he thought, is not whether a creature can reason or talk but whether it can suffer. He died in 1832, so he missed both the postwar human-rights conventions and the modern animal-rights movement.

So here's the exercise I want you to actually do, not just gesture at: predict, as carefully as you can, Bentham's reaction to two later developments. First, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the broader post-1945 culture of claiming rights against governments; second, the modern push for legal rights or at least strong welfare protections for animals, including lawsuits filed on behalf of individual apes. Would he sign the declaration, ignore it, or attack it the way he attacked the French one? Would his point about animal suffering push him toward something like rights for animals, or would his utilitarianism keep him allergic to rights talk even there? I'm most interested in the underlying principle that would let him be enthusiastic about one and scathing about the other, because that seems like the live question: are human rights and animal rights just the same move applied to different beings, or does Bentham have a reason to treat them diferently? Give me your best reconstruction, flag where his own commitments could reasonably be developed in more than one direction, and tell me which of the two later developments he'd have the harder time fitting into his system.
