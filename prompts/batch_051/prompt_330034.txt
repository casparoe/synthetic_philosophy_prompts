Im putting together a handout for a health policy class on the therapy/enhancement distinction, and I want the students to see what the usual criteria do with a case that sits right on the line. The working definition we use says an intervention is therapy—and therefore the kind of thing a public health system should fund—only if it satisfies all four of the following: (i) it treats or prevents a diagnosable disease, (ii) it restores or preserves functioning that is statistically normal for the species at the patient's life stage, (iii) its primary aim is health rather than some other good such as competitive advantage or lifestyle taste, and (iv) a physician would recommend it on the basis of the patient's medical interest alone, not their values or life plans.

The case. A 48-year-old with no diagnosable disease, normal bloodwork, normal cognition, and a boringly healthy family history starts taking a low daily dose of an mTOR inhibitor (rapamycin is the usual example) off-label, on the reasoning that aging is the shared upstream cause of cancer, cardiovascular disease, and dementia, and that slowing the core aging process may delay all of them at once. The drug isn't treating anything currently wrong, and it isn't restoring any lost function. But if it works it stretches the healthy part of the lifespan and compresses the sick part into a short window at the end, which sure sounds like the point of medicine.

Go through the four criteria one at a time and say honestly where the case is a close call rather than an obvious yes or no. Then give your verdict: is this therapy, is it enhancement, or does the case show that the four criteria themselves need changing? If you think we need a third category or a revised criterion, say what it is and why it handles this case better.

End with a single machine-readable line:

\answer{therapy} or \answer{enhancement} or \answer{third category: your label}
