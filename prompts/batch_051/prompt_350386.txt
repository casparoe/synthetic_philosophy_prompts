I've been rereading Principia Ethica and I'm trying to turn Moore's account of the highest goods into a checklist so I can stress-test it against a weird case. My checklist for whether some state of consciousness counts as one of the great intrinsic goods he's talking about: (1) the person values it for its own sake, not just as a means; (2) it involves actual awareness of an object — a person, a work, a scene — rather than a free-floating mood; (3) it involves the emotion that fits the object, like affection for a person or admiration for something beautiful; and (4) the object itself has the relevant property — the person is genuinely good, the thing genuinely beautiful — so that the value of the whole isn't just a function of how nice the feeling is.

Now the case. A woman has listened to the same podcast host for eight years, several hours a week. She knows an enormous amount about his life as he's told it, his views, his sense of humor, his griefs. She writes him letters and he's read a couple of them on air. Her affection feels to her exactly like love for a friend: she genuinely wants him to flourish, would grieve if he died, and doesn't get anything tangible out of it. But he doesn't know she exists as an individual, and the relationship is entirely one-way — if she stoped listening tomorrow he'd never notice, and she couldn't be there for him in a crisis. Stipulate that the host really is as decent and interesting as he seems, so criterion (4) isn't the problem.

Does this qualify as the kind of personal affection Moore places among the greatest goods? Go through the checklist, mark where it clearly passes and where it's ambiguous. Then tell me whether the one-way-ness shows my checklist is missing a mutuality condition, or whether Moore would bite the bullet and count it — and what that says about whether the checklist or the case is doing the real work.
