I'm teaching a night class that pairs films with units in philosophy, and this term's unit is population ethics. I want to use Children of Men. The film usually gets read as being about hope and xenophobia, but I'm struck by how completely it treats the end of reproduction as the worst thing imaginable — the whole world is falling apart specifically because children stopped being born. Read the film through the question of whether a larger population is actually better. Does it just assume a pronatalist answer, or does it dramatize something a population ethicist could actually defend? Where does its despair rely on the audience feeling the loss of people who never exist, as if that were the same as the deaths of existing people, and where does the film put pressure on the view that more lives doesn't make the world better? I want the reading to be honest about the places where the movie resists this framing, not just the places where it fits it. And tell me which argument or reading of a scene would make you change your mind about your interpretation.
