I'm a novelist and I'm trying to get a handle on something I noticed in my own retelling of the Orpheus and Eurydice story. The more variants I sit with—the usual ancient ones where he turns around and loses her, a few where the rescue just... fails differently, later retellings where she doesn't want to come back at all—the more I'm aware I have an unspoken theory about which version of a myth is the "real" one, and it's running my choices without my permission. I want to turn my hangup into a little sorting quiz for my writing group (six to eight people, mostly practicing novelists), and I'd like you to build it.

Here's the shape. Eight to ten questions, each one a concrete case where variants clash, with two or three answer options that force a decision about which variant should govern. Use cases like: two tellings of a flood myth that disagree about who survives and why; one folktale collected in three villages with three different endings; an epic with an earlier fragmentary manuscript and a later, much better full one; a myth that survives only as a painting and a prose summary written centuries later; a massively popular modern retelling that contradicts every ancient source; a version reconstructed by scholars that no actual community ever told. No question should just ask "do you think there's an original?" or "is Virgil the real version?"—they have to sort people by what they do with the cases, not by what they call themselves.

The leaves I want are, roughly: the lost original (there was once one correct version, even if it's gone); the majority version (the most widely told variant is canonical); the institutional text (for literary myths, whatever got fixed by the standard edition or anthology counts); the structuralist one (the myth is the invariant skeleton shared across tellings); the functionalist one (the real version is the one doing social work for its community); and the no-canon view (there's no canonical version and the question is malformed). If any of these is empty—no answer pattern leads to it—say so and diagnose whether it's empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. If two leaves can't actually be separated by the question set, flag that and tell me what extra question would do it.

Give me the quiz itself, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the leaves, including what to do when someone is split down the middle. For my purposes the load-bearing distinction is text-with-an-original versus living-practice-where-every-variant-is-a-real-use, so include a couple of questions about retellings that change the ending for a new audience, and about versions nobody sang but scholars reconstructed. And it'd be great if the quiz made the reader feel a little ambushed by their own consistancy—that's the fun of it.
