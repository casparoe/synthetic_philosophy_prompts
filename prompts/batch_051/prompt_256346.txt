I've been kicking around an idea in distributive justice that I've tentatively been calling equality of recovery, and I'd like you to act as a political philosopher who works on the equality debate (welfare, resources, capabilities — you know Sen, Dworkin, Arneson, Anderson) and give me a honest assessment before I take it to a grad seminar. The idea: when we ask what a fair distribution equalizes, we shouldn't look at current welfare, resource bundles, or even capability sets at a fixed moment. We should look at each person's recovery profile: the set of viable life-trajectories open to them within, say, five years of a standardized shock — losing a job, a divorce, a serious illness, a house fire. A just society, on this view, is one where the worst-off person's recovery profile still clears a decent floor, and ideally one where recovery profiles are as equal as liberty allows. The thing that got me excited: two people can have identical incomes and identical current capability sets, but one has family, savings, credentials, and health while the other doesn't, so their recovery profiles diverge sharply — and that divergence, not the snapshot inequality, is what people actually resent. It also seems to sidestep the expensive-tastes problem (preference satisfaction drops out) and the disability worry for welfarism (what matters is keeping a disabled person's trajectory-set viable, not equalizing her utility numbers).

But I can't tell whether I've built something new or just re-described the capability approach with a temporal index. It might also collapse into social-mobility talk, or into something like Dworkin's hypothetical insurance run under a different thought experiment. So please think carefully and be candid rather than encouraging. Reconstruct the view in its strongest form, name the nearest existing positions (the "old wine" if that's what it is), and then lay out the strongest three or four objections — especially around how to specify the standardized shock without rigging the results, whether this ends up being equal opportunity with an extra step, and whether it ignores people whose suffering is bad right now even if their five-year trajectory looks fine. Is any objection fatal, or can the view be patched by treating it as a necessary condition rather than a complete metric of justice? Where does it genuinely break?
