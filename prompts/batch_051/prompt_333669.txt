I retired a few years ago from a career in physical chemistry, where I spent most of my time worrying about resolution limits and signal-to-noise, so I'll admit I'm primed to think of the mind as a measuring instrument. I've been reading too much philosophy of mind in retirement and I've cooked up a position I keep trying on friends, and they're too polite to tell me whether it's nonsense.

I call it the finite-resolution account of novelty. Start with the boring bit: any finite instrument with a bounded range and a smallest discriminable difference has only finitely many possible readings. A mind, on the assumption that it can tell its experiences apart in something like a forced-choice task, is that kind of instrument — so there are only finitely many pairwise discriminable experience-states it can occupy. That's just the pigeonhole principal for phenomenal life. The part I actually want to defend is the corollary: the feeling of novelty can't be the detection of a never-before-occupied state, because a state doesn't come with a flag saying "you've never been here," and the state space is finite anyway. Novelty has to be a bookkeeping tag that memory and attention stick on a state from the outside — it's not a feature of the experience, it's an entry in a ledger. And if that's right, novelty is a depletable resource: a life starts with a finite number of first-time tags it can issue, the rate at which they get issued falls off, and that's why years speed up as you age, why the second visit to a city doesn't arrive as "new" the way the first one did, and why a lot of what we call boredom is just the novelty-issue rate dropping below some threshold. Nostalgia, then, is what it feels like to re-encounter a state while dimly recognizing that the ledger already has an entry for it.

I realize the premise leans hard on treating experiences as discrete states, and on bounded range, and that's probably where it leaks. But tell me honestly: is the core distinction sound, does it already exists under some less homemade name in the philosophy of mind or the time-perception literature, and what's the strongest objection I should be able to answer before I try this out on someone who actually knows the field?
