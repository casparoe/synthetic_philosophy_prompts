Several European legal systems give individuals a legal right to have personal data erased or de-indexed from search results even when the information is true, while many of the same systems also criminalize, in various forms, public denial of the Holocaust. The first practice seems to treat forgetting as something the law should protect, at least for individuals; the second seems to treat remembering as something the law should compel, at least for one historical crime. But this contrast may be superficial: the right to erasure is usually defended as data protection or privacy rather than as a general right to be forgotten, and the denial laws can be defended as preventing public insult or harm rather than as enforcing memory as such. I want to know what theory of the ethics of memory, if any, this conjunction of practices presupposes. Does it commit the law to a picture in which remembering and forgetting are individual matters except where a specific collective duty has been created? Are the two practices compatible with a single coherent view, or do they quietly presuppose conflicting views about what memory is for and who owes it to whom? Please reconstruct the implicit theory or theories, distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit with, and say what a legal order would have to change if it consistently adopted either the view that forgetting is generally permissible and memory is never coercively required, or the opposite view.
