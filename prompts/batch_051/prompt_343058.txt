A friend of mine works on visual prosthetics and keeps insisting that, within a decade or two, a good enough cortical stimulator will be able to give people genuinely novel color experiences — not just reddish-greens or yellowish-blues of the sort that are supposed to be ruled out by opponent processing, not just new shades inside the familiar color solid, but hues that sit outside the space of possible human color qualia altogether. I find the claim weirdly hard to nail down. On the one hand it sounds like an empirical prediction about a gadget and some neurons. On the other hand, all we would ever have is a subject saying "okay, that one is different from any color I've ever seen," and there are a hundred boring explanations of that report: they've learned a new label, they're describing the same old red with more confidence, they're confabulating, or the stimulation is adding some non-color visual texture — flicker, shape, brightness — and they're calling it a new hue.

So suppose we actually had a precise, personalized interface that could stimulate the visual areas that encode color, and suppose one subject repeatedly picks out a single stimulation pattern as a stable, distinct, nameable color they insist is neither red nor green nor blue nor yellow nor any mixture of them. Design the single strongest test you can of the claim that this is a genuinely new color experience rather than a renamed or confabulated ordinary one. Tell me what results would push each way, and be explicit about what even a clean positive result would leave open. At the end, would we have evidence of novel qualia, or just a report that has passed every check we knew how to build? If "qualia" is doing no work in the original claim and we should just be talking about new stimulus categories, I want you to say that plainly rather than humoring me.
