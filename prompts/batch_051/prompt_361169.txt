Since retiring from a career in experimental science, I have lately been reading the philosophical literature on causation, and one thing has struck me: the dispute about causation by omission seems to have made almost no progress, in print or in my reading group. The same standoff recurs. One side maintains that omissions are plainly causes: the technician's failure to replace a safety valve caused the explosion, and the gardener's not watering the plant caused its death. The other side replies that this is loose talk: an omission is the non-occurrence of an event, not an event in its own right, and if causation is a relation between events, a non-occurrence cannot literally do anything. What killed the plant was the dry soil; the gardener's inaction is a matter for moral or legal judgement, not natural fact. A third party in the group insists that counterfactual dependence settles the whole matter—had the gardener watered the plant, it would not have died, so the omission caused its death—and that the resistance is only a hangover from an outdated ontology. Each week the argument runs through the same cases and ends exactly where it began.

I am not asking who is right. I am asking what kind of disagreement this is. Is it verbal—a dispute over how to use the word "cause"—or is it substantive, a dispute over what causation is? Are the parties disagreeing about which cases must be accommodated, about what would count as evidence, or about bedrock intuitions that no argument can reach? My own suspicion, formed in the laboratory, is that the real fault line lies between those who tie causal claims to the possibility of intervention and control and those who regard a counterfactual test as sufficient; but I offer this only as a hypothesis, not as a position I want defended. Given that diagnosis, is there any distinction, example, or experiment that would constitute progress, or should the impasse be expected to be permanent?
