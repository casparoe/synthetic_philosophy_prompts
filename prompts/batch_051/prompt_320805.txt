I was a physical chemist for almost forty years, mostly doing statistical mechanics of liquids, and now that I'm retired I've been poking around in philosophy of mind. I came across Hofstadter's careenium thought experiment—a box with little spinning magnetic balls he calls simmballs, where at the micro level they just bounce and spin according to local forces, but at a coarser level you see patterns, like a kind of vortex or a pseudo-particle, that seems to travel around and react to other patterns. I might have some of the mechanical details off, but the setup is enough for what's bothering me.

Here's the argument that's been stuck in my head. (1) The simmballs obey Newtonian mechanics in a closed box, so their full trajectory is fixed once you specify initial positions and velocities. (2) The coarse patterns, the 'simmball symbols' if you like, are nothing but arrangements of simmballs; there is no extra ingredient. (3) If you could compute the micro trajectories, you would never need to mention the coarse patterns to get every simmball exactly right. (4) So the coarse patterns exert no causal push on the simmballs; the patterns are epiphenomenal. (5) By the same reasoning, the symbols, thoughts, and reasons in a human brain are nothing but patterns of particles, so they too exert no causal push on the particles. (6) Therefore, my reason for typing this sentence did not push my fingers to type it. That conclusion can't be right—or if it is, it is a very expensive right.

I have enough physics to know each step sounds exactly like what I used to teach, but the conclusion makes the exercise feel rigged. Walk me through the argument step by step and tell me if it is valid, and if not, exactly which step slips and how. I especially want to know whether 'push' is being used univocally, or whether the conclusion only sounds bad because it conflates physical closure with the explanatory force of patterns. If some version of the argument is actually valid, say so and tell me what I have to accept.
