I'm a PhD candidate in moral psychology working on the politics of the negative emotions, and I keep running into a decades-long debate that seems to have stalled in a philosophically revealing way. The question is whether envy and resentment are best understood as vices to be overcome or as signals of injustice that a decent political order should heed. One camp—call it the vice-first view—treats envy as a vice in the strict sense: a hostile sensitivity to another person's superiority, aimed at bringing that person down rather than at getting what one is owed. On this view, the Rawlsian contrast between envy and resentment does the central work: resentment is a moral response to being wronged and aims at restoring just relations, whereas envy aims at lowering the envied even at cost to oneself. The task is therefore to train envy away and to keep it out of political judgment. The other camp—call it the signal-first view—answers that the clean split between envy and resentment is part of the problem. Its defenders, who include social critics and some writers in feminist and anti-oppression thought, argue that calling the anger of the worse-off mere envy has been a reliable way of discrediting refusal and grievance without answering their content. On this view, the felt desire to see a dominant person humbled is often a demand for standing or recognition that has no available social form, and the sharp vice/moral-emotion distinction presupposes exactly the account of justice that is in dispute.

Both camps agree about the endpoints: sheer spite is bad, and anger at outright exploitation is warranted. The disagreement is concentrated in the murky middle. Consider a colleague who resents another's inherited advantages inside a formally meritocratic order; a worker who wants the boss taken down a peg rather than merely compensated; a person more disturbed by another's unearned recognition than by the substance of what was lost. In these cases the two camps cannot even seem to agree about which emotion the agent is feeling. Making it worse, the canonical vice-first position contains a complication: Rawls himself allows that envy may be excusable when arrangements are so unequal or so unjust that self-respect is difficult to sustain. The signal-first camp reads this as admitting that what is called envy can be a legitimate response to injustice; the vice-first camp reads it as a limiting case that leaves the verdict against envy standing.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because it is really three disagreements fused together: a semantic disagreement about whether envy and resentment name exclusive categories; a substantive disagreement about when relative disadvantage is itself an injustice; and a methodological disagreement about whether agents' own felt grievance should count as evidence of injustice, or only as a datum to be explained. If the first dominates, the impasse might dissolve with better categories; if the second, the argument has to be fought inside a theory of justice; if the third, it may be permanently open because the sides trust different evidence.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and not a survey of the literature. Act as an expert in political philosophy and moral psychology. Take your time; brainstorm at least three candidate diagnoses before settling on one. Then give me: (1) a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, locating the real fault line or fault lines; (2) what each side would have to accept, or what hidden question would have to be answered, for progress to become possible; and (3) what would count as genuine movement given your diagnosis—a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding, a formal result, a change of question—or, if you conclude no progress should be expected, an argument for that conclusion. Conclude by telling me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different diagnosis, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
