I've been rereading Umberto Eco's The Name of the Rose and it struck me that the whole murder plot is really a version of the Reformation-era problem of who decides what a text means. Jorge treats the lost second book of Aristotle's Poetics as if its meaning were a live grenade that only he knows how to handle, and he kills to keep anyone else from reading it; meanwhile the Franciscans and the papal legates quote the very same scriptures at each other about apostolic poverty and come out with opposite results. I want to think about the novel in terms of sola scriptura and what's sometimes called the regress of interpretation: if scripture alone is authoritative and clear, you still need someone to say what it means when readings conflict, and at that point you seem stuck between every reader being their own pope or rebuilding an institutional judge that you officially threw out.

Could you read the novel through that lens? I'd particularly like attention to the argument over whether Christ laughed, and to William's method of reading signs in the world rather than decoding the book's secrets the way Jorge does. Does the novel actually take a side — does it think the regress is fatal to sola scriptura, or does it show that the alternative (one office fixing the meaning, violently) is worse, or does it point to some third option like a community of fallible readers who never arrive at a final reading? Wether the novel is fair here matters to me: it's set before Luther and written by a semiotician with his own hobbyhorses, so I could imagine it making both the institutional and the "text alone" positions look dumber than their best defenders would allow. I'm drafting a lecture and don't want the novel to just stand in for my own thesis.
