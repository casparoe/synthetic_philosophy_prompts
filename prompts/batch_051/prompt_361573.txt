I keep noticing a move in moral thought and argument that I would like to identify and understand precisely. A person does something harmful in one context and treats a good deed in an unrelated context as making up for it, even though the people helped and the people harmed are not the same. For example, a supervisor who treats graduate students poorly may excuse the behavior by pointing to large charitable donations elsewhere; a company that underpays its factory workers may answer criticism by pointing to the school it built in the same town. The underlying thought seems to be that good and bad deeds sit in a single moral ledger, so credits can cancel debits across different acts, contexts, and affected persons. I can see that this is near the logic of carbon offsets and of the popular image of buying indulgences, and near the psychological idea that doing good can make a person feel permitted to do bad, but none of those names exactly the general structure I am after. Is there an established concept or literature for it? More important than any label, I want to know how to carve the phenomenon: which cases genuinely fit it, which nearby cases do not, and which features would make the move legitimate rather than a piece of self-serving bookkeeping.
