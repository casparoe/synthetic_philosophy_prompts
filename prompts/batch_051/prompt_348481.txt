I find myself torn between a theory I keep being pulled toward and an intuition I cannot get rid of, and I want to think through which one should give way.

The theory is a two-component account of pain. On this view, a painful experience divides into a sensory-discriminative component and an affective-motivational component. The first component is what tells you that bodily tissue is being disturbed: it carries information about where the disturbance is, how intense it is, and what quality it has. The second component is what makes the experience hurt in the ordinary sense: the felt badness, the urge to escape, the sense that the state ought to stop. These two components are said to be separable, and the separation does real explanatory work. Patients with a syndrome called pain asymbolia, and in a more transient way some patients given morphine, report that a noxious stimulus feels the same in its sensory dimensions but no longer bothers them: they can say it is sharp, burning, and intense while remaining indifferent to it, neither suffering nor wanting particularly to end it. The two-component view also promises to explain why a burn that would be unbearable at night can be almost ignored during a fight, why the same injury hurts more when one is afraid than when one is absorbed, and why nociception is not uniformly awful across contexts. If the badness of pain were an intrinsic, self-announcing feature of the raw sensation itself, these dissociations should not occur. So the badness begins to look like something the organism does with, or makes of, the signal: a felt evaluation, or a motivational push, added to a sensation that is in itself merely informative. That is a tidy and powerful picture, and it keeps convincing me.

But then I actually injure myself, and the theory seems to describe something that is not my experience. When I put my hand on something hot, I do not encounter a neutral sensory report and then notice that I dislike it. The pain presents itself as bad through and through; the burning and the hurting are inseparable in what it is like, and the badness appears to be a feature of the experience itself rather than an attitude I bring to it. The strongest way I can put the intuition is this: a pain that no longer bothered me at all would not be a pain with one part subtracted, but a pain replaced by something else — a bare damage signal, an alarm that keeps ringing after it has been silenced. If the theory says the badness was never in the pain itself, I feel it has stopped talking about pain and started talking about mere nociception, the neural registration of injury.

I do not need someone to hand me a winner. I want to know which moves are genuinely available and what each costs. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that gives — a real debunking of the sense that the badness is given within the experience, not merely an instruction to distrust it? What would it take for the theory to give — a principled reason to say the clinical dissociations are not separations of components but something else, such as changes in the injury signal itself or in the wider state of the person? And is there a third reading on which both survive, for example one on which the badness is indeed part of the phenomenal content of pain while still being an evaluative or presentational feature rather than an intrinsic sensory quality? I am less interested in labels than in knowing how one would tell, from the inside or from the clinical evidence, which of these reconciliations is earned and which is only a redescription with the hard joints smoothed over.
