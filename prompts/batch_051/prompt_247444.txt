For years a close friend and I have been going in circles about a single family case, and I have begun to suspect that the argument we keep having is concealing a different argument underneath it. The case is this: a relative who has been serially unfaithful is also the most vocal person in the family about condemning other people's infidelity. My friend thinks the denunciations are defective — the things said may be accurate, but the speaker has forfeited the right to say them, and the person being denounced is under no obligation to take them seriously. I think this is a dressed-up version of shooting the messenger: a claim about someone's conduct stands or falls on its accuracy, and refusing to hear an accurate accusation because the accuser is a hypocrite is itself an evasion. Whenever I say we should separate the message from the messenger, my friend replies that this separation is precisely what is at stake, so the argument loops. What unsettles me is that we agree on every fact, we agree the conduct in question is wrong, and we agree the speaker is a hypocrite; we have still made no progress in years. I would like a diagnosis of what this disagreement actually is, rather than a verdict on it. My working hypothesis is that one of us is treating moral criticism as a claim whose truth can be assessed apart from who utters it, while the other is treating it as an action that a person may be disqualified from performing. Is that the real fault line, or is the dispute secretly about something else — about what blame is for, about whether someone who violates a norm can still hold others to it, about whether the target's resentment is justified — and, on the diagnosis you find most honest, is there anything that would count as progress rather than just another restatement of the same two positions?
