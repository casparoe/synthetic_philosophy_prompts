At our reading group last week two friends got into it and asked me to referee, but I've read just enough epistemology since I retired to see why it's hard, not enough to settle it. The question was whether you're justified in believing something simply because someone told it to you.

Elena said no — not unless you have positive reason to think the person is reliable. Otherwise you're just deferring to a stranger. A scientist wouldn't accept a result from an unknown lab without some evidence the lab is competent, and believing on bare say-so is the same mistake.

Marcus said that picture can't be right, because it makes a child's learning irrational: a toddler trusts her parents long before she has evidence of their reliability, and that trust is exactly how she later gets the evidence. So testimony has to be a basic source of justification, like perception — you're entitled to accept it by default, and only defeaters (signs of lying, confusion) undercut it.

Elena shot back that the default isn't doing any work: the child does have evidence, namely a long passive background record that assertions usually pan out, so learning from parents is just one more ordinary inference from that background. Calling testimony a 'basic source,' she said, just relabels the phenomenon.

Marcus replied that this background record is itself inherited mostly through other people's word, so using it to justify trust in testimony is circular. And the perception analogy shows the structure: vision is a basic source even though it's fallible, and we don't need a track record of our own visual reliability to be justified in trusting our eyes.

Elena's last point was that if testimony is really a 'basic source,' then the whole machinery of checking credentials, citing track records, and peer review becomes epistemically pointless, just sprinkling a 'no defeaters' condition on top of everything.

They want a verdict. Could you give me exactly five numbered points, each one a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion, adjudicating the dispute: who is right about which specific claim, and who's more right over all?
