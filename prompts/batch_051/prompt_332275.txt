I manage the grants programme of the Voss Foundation, a private trust whose articles of incorporation confine us to a hedonistic criterion: a project is fundable only if, on the best available evidence, it can be expected to increase the net pleasure of the people it serves, with every form of pleasure counting equally — sensory, intellectual, social, aesthetic, and otherwise. The founder was explicit that no pleasure is to be preferred merely because it is refined, improving, or productive.

The difficulty we face is the heterogeneity of the pleasures applicants claim. The drowsy comfort of a warm, dry room for an elderly person in a damp flat shares no obvious felt quality with the absorbed concentration of a teenager working through a chess problem; the hilarity of a street festival shares little with the quiet satisfaction of being clear of debt after years of dread. This is the heterogeneity problem for hedonism in its practical form: if the things we call pleasures have no common quality, there may be no single quantity for the foundation to maximize, and any procedure that pretends to compare them directly may be doing so with hidden weights of its own.

I would like you to write a decision procedure my two grant officers — neither with philosophical training — can run on each application before it reaches the trustees. We receive roughly twenty applications a quarter; grants range from $20,000 to $400,000 a year; and the trustees vote on our recommendation and almost always follow it, so the procedure in practice decides the allocation. The deliverable should be a checklist of questions asked in order, with, for each question, a statement of what counts as an adequate answer. It should say where the procedure returns a no and the application is rejected on the evidence, where it must halt and hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact, and where judgment cannot be eliminated however carefully the procedure is followed. It must be usable from start to finish in about 45 minutes per application by someone who cannot give an account of what pleasure is. Most applications arrive with enthusiastic testimony but no controlled evidence, so part of the procedure's job is to say what should be done with unsupported claims. It must remain faithful to the equal-consideration clause — so it should include a check that would catch a grant officer quietly preferring a pleasure they personally share over one they do not — without smuggling in any preference for refined or productive pleasures. In writing it, be explicit about which distinctions in the underlying principle you have preserved, which you have deliberately flattened to keep the checklist usable, and what is lost by flattening them.

Then run the procedure on the three applications below, and include at least one outcome my trustees will not like, stating plainly when the procedure returns it. End by saying what the procedure cannot do: in particular, whether it resolves the worry that heterogeneous pleasures are incommensurable, or only makes that worry manageable at decision time.

The applications:
1. Warm Rooms — heating subsidies and supervised common rooms for elderly residents in poorly insulated flats, expected to produce modest, steady sensory comfort and fewer daily aches, with little novelty or development.
2. Chess in Schools — an after-school chess and problem-solving club for secondary pupils, expected to produce intellectual absorption and the pleasure of mastery for many participants, but with a real risk of frustration and discouragement for weaker students.
3. Street Festival — a monthly open-air festival expected to produce intense but short-lived collective joy for a large number of residents, with no credible evidence of any lasting effect on mood or satisfaction beyond the evening itself.
