I'm putting together a handout for a small reading group on the sorites paradox, and I want it to start with a "which theory of vagueness do you actually hold?" quiz, the kind people fill out before the discussion so they can then argue from their own answers. These are mostly non-philosophers but they're game to think carefully. I'd like eight questions, each one a concrete case with two or three answer options, and the quiz should sort people by what they actually say about borderline cases rather than by which label they like. No question should just ask "do you think vague predicates have sharp boundaries?" or "do you like supervaluationism?"; nobody can answer those cold. Every question should be answerable by someone who has never heard any of the terminology. Please make sure the quiz uses at least these cases: a row of color chips going from clearly blue to clearly green, a heap where you remove grains one at a time, an age that's borderline for "young," and one weirder one involving a predicate that is vague but not obviously gradable (like "is a religion" or "is a game"). You can add other cases if they help separate positions.

For the scoring key, I'd like the leaves to be epistemicism, supervaluationism, many-valued/degree theories, and contextualism; if the answer patterns leave room for a nihilist/incoherentist position (someone who says the original questions are somehow defective and there's no fact of the matter at all), include that as a leaf too. After the key, add a section on empty or rarely occupied cells: which combinations of answers are logically available but no one actually lands in, and for each one say whether it looks incoherent, unattractive-but-coherent, or just unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of positions your quiz genuinly can't separate, and say exactly why. I'm fine if the answer patterns are messy and don't map one-to-one onto the positions, so long as the mess is described rather than papered over.
