Most American parents I know work surprisingly hard to keep their kids believing in Santa, the tooth fairy, and the Easter bunny -- they eat the cookies, leave the coins, answer endless questions, and invent whole backstories -- and then around seven or eight they come clean, and the kid is usually fine with it. These same parents tend to say that lying to your children is a serious wrong, and they'd be horrified by a parent who lied about something like weather a grandparent was sick. I'm trying to figure out what this practice actually commits someone to. What does it take for granted about trust, about children's epistemic dependance, and about why this kind of deception is supposed to be harmless? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the Santa routine. Then tell me which parts the practice strictly presupposes and which parts it merely fits with, whether the whole package is coherent, and what a family that took an absolute no-lying-to-kids line would have to give up or change. End with a JSON object with these fields: "implicit_theory" (the reconstructed view in a few sentences), "strictly_presupposed" (the commitments the practice can't do without), "merely_compatible" (views it fits with but does not require), "coherence_verdict" (whether the parts hang together), "rival_views_that_license_it" (other theories that could support the same practice), and "what_changes_under_no_lying" (what a strict anti-lying-to-children view would alter).
