I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of language and I've ended up down a rabbit hole about whether ordinary words actually have meanings at all, or just semantic ranges that lexicographers tidy up into numbered senses. A visiting speaker gave a talk on the semantics/pragmatics interface, and afterwards a few of us reconstructed what we thought their master argument was. I've tidied it into steps and I half-believe it, but I suspect one step is doing more work then it should, and I can't quite say which. It goes like this:

(1) If a word has a meaning, there is something common to all and only its correct uses.
(2) A word like 'line' has correct uses that cover at least: a line of people, a drawn line, a line of argument, a phone line, a railway line, a line of text.
(3) Any candidate content that covers all of those uses either includes something at least one use lacks (say, spatial extension), or is so thin that it fails to rule out lots of non-uses (say, 'a connected series').
(4) A candidate content that fails in either of those ways is not a meaning; at best it is a family-resemblance cluster or a disjunction, and a disjunction of meanings is not itself a single meaning.
(5) So 'line' has no meaning, and the same goes for ordinary words generally.

I find the conclusion both appealing and absurd. When I try to resist it I end up either retreating to a very deflated sense of 'meaning' or leaning on context-sensitivity in a way that feels like changing the subject. Is the argument valid as stated? If not, which step fails, and under what reading? Is (4) wrong to say a disjunction of meanings cannot count as a meaning, or is (1) already smuggling in a criterion that lexical semanticists are right to abandon? I'd rather be told the exact step that breaks than get a general lecture about definitions.
