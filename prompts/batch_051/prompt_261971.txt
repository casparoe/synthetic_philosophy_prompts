I recently sat through a policy discussion in which a minister, defending the government's decision to close borders and impose lockdown restrictions without waiting for full parliamentary approval, reached for the familiar comparison: the country was like a ship that had sprung a leak in a storm, and no reasonable person would expect the captain to poll the passengers before altering course, sealing the hatches, or throwing cargo overboard. The measures may have bent or quietly bypassed ordinary legal requirements, the argument ran, but that is exactly what legitimacy demands; a state that insisted on ordinary legality while the hull was taking on water would be failing its most basic duty to everyone aboard. The same idea appeared again in wartime dress, as though the virus were an invading army and peacetime procedural niceties a luxury, but the ship in the storm was the image doing most of the work.

I want an audit of that analogy as an argument about the relation between legality and legitimacy in a public-health emergency. Set out the correspondence table first: what maps onto the captain, the crew, the passengers, the storm, the leak, the cargo being jettisoned, the safe harbor, and the length and purpose of the voyage. Then work through where the mapping breaks, and sort the disanalogies into those that are merely decorative and those that damage the intended inference. In particular, address what the image quietly imports — a clear chain of command, a single shared destination, a danger that threatens everyone equally, and an endpoint beyond which normal order resumes — and whether a political community under lockdown actually has those features. Finally, say whether the minister's conclusion, that legitimacy may override ordinary legality in such a moment, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there is a better analogy that could carry whatever is left of it.
