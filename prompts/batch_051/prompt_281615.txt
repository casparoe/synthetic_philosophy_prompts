Im taking a philosophy of information course and we've been going around in circles about whether information about you should be treated as your property. The seminar kept making the standard moves: information is non-rivalrous, so it doesn't work like owning a bike; you didn't mix your labour into the fact that you walked past a coffee shop, even though you did the walking; and turning data into property would just let people sell away their privacy and call it freedom. But I feel like these arguments quietly lump together pretty different things. The fact that I was in the campus library from 2 to 4 is mine in a different sense than a company's record of that fact sitting on their server, and both feel different from a prediction they built by comparing my clicks to thousands of other students to guess that I'm likely to drop out. One is a raw fact about me, another is someone else's copy of a fact about me, and the third is an inference someone computed about me. Maybe those differences should change the answer instead of being smoothed over.

So: write an essay on whether information about a person should be understood as their property. Try not to answer the whole question at a single level. Distinguish at least between raw facts about someone, records or copies of those facts held by others, and inferred or derived facts about them. Weigh the strongest arguments for and against treating each of those as property, and then take a stand and justify it. If you think the property frame is just wrong for some or all of them, defend that, but still say what a person is owed over information about them. And dont end on a flat "it depends on the kind of information" unless you've actually given a principled account of when it depends and why.
