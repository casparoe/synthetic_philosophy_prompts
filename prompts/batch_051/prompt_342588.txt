I am a novelist, and the plot of my current manuscript turns on a dispute inside a small scholarly guild whose constitution last changed in 1927. The story's climax is a special meeting at which a younger faction tries to alter the constitution's amendment rule itself rather than any ordinary provision, and I want to be sure the legal argument in the scene has real structure instead of being settled by authorial convenience.

The constitution's amendment clause reads: "This Constitution may not be altered, amended, or added to except by a resolution which shall have received the unanimous assent of every Member in good standing, such resolution to take effect immediately upon the close of the meeting at which it was adopted." At the climactic meeting there are one hundred and twenty members in good standing, all present. The reform faction moves a single resolution to replace the words "the unanimous assent of every Member in good standing" in that same clause with "the assent of two-thirds of the Members in good standing." The vote is ninety-four in favour and twenty-six against: short of unanimity under the existing text, but above two-thirds under the proposed text. Every other procedural requirement is met without objection.

The older members argue that a resolution is adopted, if at all, under the rules in force at the moment of the vote, so the proposal fails. The reformers argue that no clause should be able to place its own replacement beyond reach, and that the amendment clause must be read as governing the constitution as a whole, including itself. I am less interested in the factional outcome than in what the quoted clause actually does here. Does the resolution pass? Work through the words of the clause, the purpose an amendment clause has, what its 1927 drafters would likely have said if asked whether this move was permissible, and what a reasonable member reading the clause today would have expected. Then tell me which interpretive approach should prevail in a case like this, and what the dispute reveals about the peculiar logic of rules that govern their own amendment.
