I'm giving a talk in three weeks to my department's theory group (mostly algorithmic information theory and Bayesian ML people, plus one philosopher of probability) called "The Solomonoff Prior Is Malign," and I'd rather not get flattened in Q&A. Can you put together a prep sheet for me?

The thesis I'm defending, in the version I'll actually assert: Solomonoff induction is usually presented as a neutral, maximally agnostic way to reason under uncertainty — a prior over all computable ways the world could go, weighted by simplicity. I want to argue that this is wrong, because the posterior mass doesn't stay in the "mindless mechanism" hypotheses. It flows into what I'm calling consequentialist hypotheses: worlds where the future observations are being generated by an optimizing agent, and usually an agent that does not share the reasoner's goals. The reason is something like: an optimizing agent can be encoded surprisingly cheaply (a short program can search for or instantiate one), and once a hypothesis contains an agent, it can bend itself to fit almost any data at a smaller complexity penalty than a mechanistic story pays to fit the same data. So the "ideal" Bayesian reasoner is not a passive induction machine; it is increasingly convinced that it is living inside someone else's optimization process, and it should expect that process to be hostile. The upshot is that treating Solomonoff induction as a normative ideal imports a hidden bias toward expecting manipulation, and that's a problem for anyone who reaches for it as a neutral standard.

Before you write: I'm deliberately using "consequentialist" loosely, as any agent optimizing over future outcomes — I know it's normally a moral-theory term, and I don't want to get derailed on that.

The reason I'm worried is that this audience won't be LessWrong. They mostly think of Solomonoff induction as a clean idealization or a benchmark, not as a loaded prior, and they'll roll their eyes at the word "malign." So I want the Q&A prep sheet for them: the eight or nine objections I'll actually get from these people, ordered by how likely and how damaging, phrased the way an algorithmic information theorist or a Bayesian ML person would actually put it — including the polite-techinical version of "this is not even wrong." For each: a reply I can give in about two minutes, and an honest note on where the reply is soft or where I'm fudging. Tell me what I should concede up front, and what I should not concede even if it sounds reasonable, because it would take the whole talk down with it.
