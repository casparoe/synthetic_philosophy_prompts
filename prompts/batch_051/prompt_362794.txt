I am a philosophy PhD student working on practical rationality, and I have been turning over an argument from our reading group about a version of Parfit's hitchhiker that I cannot settle. The case was set up as follows: you are stranded in the desert and a driver offers to take you to safety only if you sincerely promise to pay him a thousand dollars once you reach home; he is an excellent judge of character and will leave you behind unless he is convinced at the moment of the promise that you really will pay. It is stipulated that after he drops you off the two of you will never meet again and no one else will ever learn whether you kept the promise. For the purposes of the argument, the question is only about self-interested rationality, not about any moral obligation to keep a promise. Elena argued that in this case it would be rational to pay: the act inherits its rationality from the fact that it issues from the only kind of character under which the driver would have picked you up, and someone prepared to abandon that character once rescued could not have formed the commitment the case requires. Marcus replied that this conflates a disposition with its manifestation. Once you are home and safe, he said, the rescue lies in the past, and the fact that your trustworthiness got you rescued adds nothing to the balance of self-interested reasons for handing over the money; paying is therefore irrational, even though it was rational to have the disposition that produces the payment. Elena answered that Marcus's position makes the rational agent self-defeating: an agent who accepted his advice would foresee that his later self will renege, could not then be the kind of person the driver will pick up, and would die in the desert while the allegedly irrational agent drives home; a standard of rationality that systematically disables those who follow it cannot be the right one. Marcus answered that this shows only that rationality and self-interest can come apart. The rational agent should seek some binding device, and if none is available, he should accept that he cannot credibly promise, rather than retrospectively redescribe the later payment as rational. There the argument stalled.

Could you adjudicate their exchange? I would like to know, for each move in the back-and-forth, who is right about that specific point, what the strongest version of each position is, and whether one side is more defensible overall—or whether the dispute depends on an equivocation somewhere.
