Consider the St. Petersburg game: a fair coin is tossed until it first comes up heads, and if the first heads occurs on the nth toss the player receives 2^n dollars. Each outcome contributes one dollar to the expected value, so the game has an infinite expected monetary value; yet almost no one would pay more than a modest fee to play it. Below are seven candidate responses to this discrepancy.

Please sort these seven responses into tiers according to how much each would force a decision theorist to overhaul the ordinary expected-value reasoning they apply to finite, everyday gambles. The top tier should contain the responses that require the least overhaul, and the bottom tier the responses that require the most. Responses may be tied. After the tier list, give a one-sentence justification for where each response lands.

1. Money has diminishing marginal utility — for instance, utility in money might grow only logarithmically — so the expected utility of the game is finite even though its expected monetary value is infinite.
2. Utility is bounded above, which keeps expected utility finite for every possible payoff distribution.
3. The game cannot actually be offered as described, because any real backer has finite assets; the payoff schedule is therefore truncated in practice, and the true expected value is finite.
4. A rational agent may disregard or heavily discount sufficiently tiny probabilities, so the astronomically improbable large payoffs should do little or no work in deliberation.
5. The relevant quantity for evaluating a gamble is its time-average growth rate under repeated play rather than its ensemble expectation, and the St. Petersburg gamble has only a modest growth rate.
6. Expected-value maximization is the wrong norm for a one-shot, never-to-be-repeated gamble: a rational agent may exhibit some other risk attitude even when the expectation is infinite.
7. The paradox exposes a domain-restriction problem in expected-utility theory: an unbounded utility function does not assign a well-defined expectation to every random variable, so the principle of maximizing expected utility should be restricted to gambles with finite expectation.
