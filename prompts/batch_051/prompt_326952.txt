I'm a novelist, and I keep tripping over a lopsidedness in my own moral reactions, and I want to know whether it's defensible. When I write a character who suffers badly, it honestly bothers me — I feel like I owe them something, like I made a choice that somehow implicated me. But I have never once felt the reverse about the swarms of happy characters I simply didn't write. Their absence doesnt strike me as a loss to anyone, and I doubt any reader would call it one. So: bringing a miserable person into (fictional) existence feels like it calls for justification, while not bringing a happy person into existence feels like it calls for nothing at all. I gather that's basically the procreation asymmetry, just moved one step into make-believe. But here's the puzzle: if the obvious principle is 'moral reasons only concern actual, existing people,' then why does a fictional character's suffering bother me in the first place? I want the strongest single principle that could make sense of both halves, an honest verdict on whether it actually works, and a clear statement of whether the asymmetry should be kept or dropped. Please write your answer in American English.
