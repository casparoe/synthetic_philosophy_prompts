I've been trying to draw a clean line between inflection and derivation, and I keep coming back to a test I invented that I can't tell is just obvious or circular. The test: a piece of morphology is inflectional iff a speaker can have the form corrected and still take themselves to have produced the same word, only fixed up. If a kid says I runned to school and I say ran, they accept ran as the word they meant, said properly. If someone says I'm a bad cooker meaning a person who cooks, and I say you mean cook, they treat that as me switching the word, not repairing it. So the claim is that inflectional morphology is exactly the form variation that can survive correction under the assumption of same-word, and derivation is the morphology that can't. I like that this gets the easy cases: plurals and past tenses come out inflectional (mice, went), while agentive -er comes out derivational. It also predicts that comparative -er is inflectional because more fast to faster feels like fixing the same adjective, even though its not fully productive, which I know people fight about.

My worry is that it's circular. I'm using same word to define the inflection/derivation boundary, but the standard way of drawing that boundary already says inflection gives you forms of one lexeme and derivation gives you new lexemes, so my test looks like it presupposes the very thing it's for. Unless the correction data shows speakers independently track word-identity, in which case maybe it's a psycholinguistic version of the lexeme criterion rather than a genuinely new test.

Where does it break badly? Suppletion (go/went) actually seems fine. Pluralia tantum like scissors are weird, and English possessive 's (clitic or inflection?) doesn't sit cleanly in it. And then there are participles: is a broken window the same word as break or a different one? I genuinely don't know. Be candid with me: is my test circular, is it just the lexeme criterion restated, is it salvageable, and which case is fatal rather than just awkward?
