Over the past year I've found myself replacing the standard importance–tractability–neglectedness framework with something I call the tractability schedule. The idea is that tractability should never have been a single number. What matters for deciding where to put effort is a curve: the expected marginal value per unit of effort as a function of cumulative effort already applied to a cause. Two causes can receive similar ITN scores—similar judged importance, similar current tractability, similar crowdedness—and yet be very different prospects, because one is a quick-win cause whose marginal returns collapse as soon as the existing gap is patched, while the other has a long, nearly flat plateau and can absorb many times more effort before returns diminish. The scalar framework cannot see that difference; the schedule can.

I've gone further and started treating neglectedness as not an independent factor at all. On the schedule picture, to say a cause is neglected is just to say we are near the left end of its curve, before cumulative investment has pushed us into diminishing returns. Importance, likewise, starts looking like the total remaining area under the curve rather than a separate magnitude. So my working view is that importance, tractability, and neglectedness are three partial descriptions of one underlying curve, and that treating them as independent inputs into a cause evaluation risks double-counting.

I want an honest assessment, not vindication. Is the tractability schedule a real analytic tool, or am I just redrawing well-known ideas—diminishing marginal returns, room for more funding, funding gaps—under a new name? If it is distinct, does it actually matter in practice, given that we rarely know the shape of the curve beyond the current margin? And where does it break: what does the scalar framework get right that my schedule loses, and which kinds of causes would my reconstruction systematically misrank? Please take a definite position on whether the schedule should replace, supplement, or be dropped, and defend that position; don't just lay out considerations on each side. Write in American English.
