I'm a philosophy undergrad doing a module on formal epistemology, and we just did a unit on imprecise probabilities — the view that your evidence often only determines a range of rationally permissible credences (like 0.4 to 0.7) instead of one precise number. Since then I've adopted a policy: whenever someone asks me how likely I think something is, I give an interval and refuse to name a point estimate. This comes up a lot, because my friends keep asking me for odds before they make decisions — whether the landlord will actually fix the boiler, whether my brother's startup will survive the year, whether a weird symptom is worth seeing a doctor about. My reasoning is that any single number is false precision: my evidence basically never pins down exactly one value, so the interval is the only honest answer. But my flatmate says I'm just dodging commitment, and honestly he's got a point, because after I give the range I usualy won't say what I'd actually do if I had to choose. Last week he asked whether he should go in on a lease with some people, and I said the chance the landlord ends up screwing him over is 25–60%. He said that's useless for a yes/no decision.

We also touched, in class, on the worry that imprecise credences don't pick out an action until you add some extra rule on top (maximin, optimism-pessimism, that sort of thing), and I left that part fuzzy. So my question: is my policy defensible as stated? Or does it run together the job of representing my uncertainty with the job of recommending an action? Is there a defensible version of the rule, if this one isn't? And how would I tell from the inside whether I actually believe in imprecise credences, as opposed to just being risk-averse and using the theory as cover for never committing?
