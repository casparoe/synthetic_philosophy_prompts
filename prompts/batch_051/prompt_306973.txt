I recently moved into a group that reviews proposals for unconventional computing hardware, and I have been asked to referee a submission whose central promise is a physical device that decides the halting problem — that is, a device that computes a function no Turing machine can compute. My colleagues dismiss the proposal as impossible in principle, because they accept the physical Church–Turing thesis: the claim that no physically realizable process can compute a function beyond the Turing-computable ones. But the more I look at it, the less I understand what kind of claim that is — a mathematical clarification of effective computability, or a sweeping empirical hypothesis about what nature allows. Please design the strongest feasible experimental or observational strategy for testing it: specify the setup, state what result would count as evidence that some physical process outruns Turing computability, state what result would count as support for the thesis, and then say exactly what a positive result would and would not show. Be candid about the residue no finite test can reach — in particular, whether any finite evidence could ever establish that a real device computes the full halting function over an infinite domain, and whether a finite observation could conclusively falsify the thesis or only make one physical theory more reasonable than another.
