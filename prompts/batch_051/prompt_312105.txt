I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a feature about a deceptively simple question: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? The piece will turn on the fact that a letter is not its outline. In a serif text face, the lowercase a is a double-story form; in italics and in most handwriting, it is a single-story loop that shares almost no outline with the first, yet readers, type designers, and optical character recognition systems treat the two as the same letter. At the same time, the bowl-and-stem configurations that make b, d, p, and q are kept rigidly apart; a Latin lowercase a and a Cyrillic lowercase a can be visually indistinguishable in one font while belonging to different alphabets; and the vertical strokes used for lowercase l, uppercase I, and the digit 1 can be nearly identical while belonging to different categories altogether. Handwriting, historical scripts, and stylized logos push all of these boundaries continuously.

I want to structure the article around the standard a theory would have to meet, not around a survey of existing work, so I'd like you to write the desiderata — the full list of requirements — that a theory of letterform identity must meet to count as adequate. By a theory I mean any account of what makes scattered physical marks count as instances of the same letter: a psychologist's model of category learning, a type designer's working doctrine of what survives restyling, a formal account of the relation between glyphs and characters. I am not asking you for the theory itself; I am asking for the criteria against which candidate theories should be judged.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it — a shape, a reader behavior, a writing-system fact, or a boundary case that would embarrass a theory that ignored it. Then mark it as non-negotiable, or as a preference of a particular camp (algorithmic implementability, psychological realism, fidelity to a designer's intent, coverage of decorative lettering, and so on), and say briefly why. Then tell me whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable or in genuine tension, since that is the part I most want for the piece, and identify which requirement does the most to rule out otherwise plausible theories.

Fix the purpose as: a theory adequate for explaining how a writing system can maintain a finite, stable alphabet while its physical realizations vary continuously across typefaces, hands, media, and centuries of drift — and adequate in the sense that it could, in principle, underwrite both human reading and automatic character recognition. Leave out a scoring of actual theories, and leave out citations and named research programs; I will layer those in during reporting. Begin with the list of requirements itself, with no preface or commentary, and do not restate what I asked.
