Here is an argument that has been bothering me, and I cannot tell whether it goes through or whether one of the steps is subtly defective. It seems to show that the Principal Principle cannot survive the discovery that the world is deterministic. By the Principal Principle I mean the constraint that a rational agent who knows the objective chance of a future event is x should set her credence in that event to x, at least in the absence of inadmissible information.

(1) If the world is deterministic, then, on the standard view, the objective chance of any future event E is either 0 or 1.
(2) The Principal Principle says that a rational agent who knows the objective chance of E is x should set her credence in E to x, barring inadmissible information.
(3) So a rational agent who knows she lives in a deterministic world should set her credence in every future event to either 0 or 1: for each event, the chance is either 0 or 1, the agent knows this, and the Principal Principle then directs her to adopt the value that is in fact the chance.
(4) But an agent can know she lives in a deterministic world and still rationally be uncertain about, say, tomorrow's weather.
(5) Therefore the Principal Principle is either false or misstated.

I suspect the conclusion is wrong, but I cannot locate the misstep in a way that satisfies me. Please treat this as a diagnostic exercise. Say whether the argument is valid; if it is not valid, identify exactly which step fails and under which reading it fails; then state the corrected formulation of the Principal Principle that avoids the problem. If you think the argument is valid, explain why the conclusion should be accepted despite sounding odd.
