I'm the senior editor for a high-school and intro-college biology imprint, and I own the style guide for how we talk about functions. Twice a year our copy editors flag every sentence where a writer wants to say 'the function of X is to Y'—feathers, the appendix, fever, junk DNA, flagella, tailbones, that sort of thing—and I have to decide whether that language survives. If we get it wrong, the sentence gets taught to a few hundred thousand students for the next eight years, so the stakes feel high and the precedent sticks. I've read enough philosophy of biology to know there are two camps: etiological accounts, where a function is what a trait was selected for, and causal-role accounts, where a function is what a trait currently does in the system. They give different answers for exaptations, vestiges, and traits that arose by drift but are now useful, so I cant just pick one and be done. Build me a reusable procedure for our editors, who aren't philosophers and have about twenty minutes per flagged sentence. It should tell them when to publish 'the function of X is to Y,' when to soften to 'X contributes to Y' or 'X does Y,' and when to kick the sentence to a biologist. Include the hard cases it has to distinguish, at least two worked examples (one where the procedure forces us to change wording we've used for years), and be explicit about the places where the procedure will misfire if we pretend it's mechanical and an editor just has to use judgment.
