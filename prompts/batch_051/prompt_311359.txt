I'm a retired geologist — forty years in the field, mostly mapping and dam safety — and a couple of years ago I stumbled into a philosophy reading group at the public library. We've been chewing on collective memory versus history, and for the last several months we've been stuck on a case I can't get out of my head. I'd like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement we're actually having.

The case is fictional but compresses things I've seen: a small mining town where a tailings dam failed in 1963, killing 19 people. The community's memory is vivid and unanimous: the company knew the dam was unsafe, ignored warnings from the workers, and used its lawyers to bury the negligence afterward. There's a memorial every year, the story is taught in the local school, and asking whether it might not be true is treated as an insult to the dead.

The archival record — state inspection reports, a post-failure geological survey, court transcripts — tells a different story. The dam met the standards of the day; the failure ran along an undetected clay seam that no standard practice of the era would have found; the negligence charges were dismissed because the evidence couldn't support them, not because of some legal trick. And, apperently, the town's memory traces back mainly to the newspaper coverage in the first weeks after the collapse, which blamed the company before the geology was in.

Whats interesting is that everyone in the group agrees on these facts. The split is about what to do with them. One side says that once the record conflicts with memory, memory has to give way: the town's identity is built on a false accusation, and commemorating false blame is an injustice not just to the truth but to the company's engineers and their families. The other side says the memory is doing a different job than the record — it's not a claim about the clay seam, it's a claim about vulnerability, about whose word counts, about a company that would have walked away either way. Correcting the memory with the archive, they say, would just be silencing the town a second time. A third faction keeps saying we should stop asking which is true and instead ask which question each is answering, which sounds right to me some days and like a dodge on others.

We've been at this for months and nobody has moved. My working theory is that this isn't a factual disagreement at all — it's a disagreement about what a community's memory is for, and about who gets authority over the past. Sort of an authority dispute wearing an epistemic hat. But I've been wrong enough in this group to want someone to check me.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it substantive or verbal? A difference about facts, about standards of evidence, about values, or two groups answering different questions under one heading? Diagnose it using what each side actually says. Then, given that diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a change of question, an argument that we shouldn't expect any, and why. And be explicit about what would change your mind about the diagnosis itself: which argument or kind of case would push you to a different one, or where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating the question.
