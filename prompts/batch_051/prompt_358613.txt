I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, which in practice means I spend a lot of time on status, credit, and inequality. I've been following a mental rule for years and finally want it stress-tested. Whenever I feel a spike of envy or resentment while reporting on how some good is distributed—who gets the fellowships, who gets quoted as the expert, who gets credit on a paper, who gets payed for identical work—I treat that feeling as weak evidence that the distribution is unjust, not merely as a character flaw to suppress. Part of this comes from John Rawls's A Theory of Justice. He's careful to say envy is not a moral feeling (it doesn't require a claim that the distribution is unfair), but he also allows for a category he calls excusable envy, where the feeling is an understandable response to a loss of self-respect or to inequalities that make self-respect hard to hold onto. I read that as permission to treat my own envy and resentment as possibly tracking something real in the world. My editor says no: it's sour grapes with a Rawls citation, and resentment tracks my sense of my own standing, not justice. Who's right? Under what conditions are a person's own envy or resentment reliable evidence of injustice, and should the two get different weight? How do I tell from the inside whether this episode is the excusable, signal-like kind or the ordinary self-serving kind? Is there a principled discount I should apply, instead of either zeroing the feelings out or letting them drive the story? Give me a verdict, and end with a last line that begins 'Verdict:' and summarizes it in one sentence.
