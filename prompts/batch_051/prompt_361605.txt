I'm writing my new novel around a character who spends much of the story defending what I thought was an exotic position—one I first picked up from Ibn Rushd's Decisive Treatise, the part where he distinguishes three classes of people: those who reach the truth by demonstration, those who can only be reached by dialectical argument, and those who need rhetoric and images, especially the law's images, to live in the truth they can't inspect. So there's a division of labor: demonstrative philosophers grasp things strictly, the theologians argue over them, and ordinary believers are not supposed to have allegorical interpretations preached to them because it would corrupt them and maybe even slide into unbelief. My character defends this as a kind of harmony: everyone is closer to the truth than they could be if one class's method were forced on the others, and the image-loving class isn't being lied to; they're holding the same truth in the only form they can.

I need to test this because my whole wager is that you can build a real, non-condescending psychology from it. Here is my worry. It feels to me like Ibn Rushd thinks of these as three kinds of people, grand categories. But when I actually write scenes—say, a village audience where a grandmother swears and a young clerk and a hermit are all reacting to the same sermon—people are always sliding from one register to the other. A person is demonstrative about the price of wool and image-bound about marriage; one character moves dialects in the space of an afternoon. So is the three-class scheme just a baked-in military rank of minds, or can it be read as three postures that any one soul passes through? And if it is postures, doesn't the argument start to look circular—like, here it is fine to give someone a demonstrative proof because they happen to have entered the demonstration-capable posture in the next minute, except the scheme gives you no real way to know that? I want you to make the strongest case that the distinction is tracking something true rather than just a cleric's control of the conversation, and then give me the hardest objection to that case. Where does the distinction snap first in a real human community, and what would a novelist have to add to Ibn Rushd to keep his picture honest?
