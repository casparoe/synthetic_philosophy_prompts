Every time I watch people argue about politics there's a moment where somebody says "the people have spoken" or "that would betray the will of the people," as if the phrase named a fact like the temperature. Recently it came up in an argument about whether a president could ignore a legislature because a referendum had delievered a clear verdict. I understand the rhetorical move — it's meant as a trump card. But I honestly can't tell what claim is being made, because depending on how I read the words, the sentence slides between something trivially true and something fairly scary. I want a menu of the different precise claims "the will of the people" could be expressing, not a defense of any single one. In particular: does the phrase pick out a thing that exists (one will out there in the population), a construction, or just a performative way of declaring that one side should count? What distinct theses about aggregation could it stand for — majority, supermajority, something about intensity, something about what people would want if they were better informed? And how much silent work is the phrase borrowing from Rousseau's idea of a general will without actually committing to it?

Structure the answer as four sections, in this order: (1) Readings — lay out the distinct precise claims as a list, one or two sentences each; (2) Verdicts — for each reading, say in a sentence whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false; (3) In my debate — which readings would actually do the work in the referendum-versus-legislature argument, and why; (4) Bottom line — the reading I should get someone to commit to before the argument goes further. Aim for roughly 350, 250, 200, and 100 words.
