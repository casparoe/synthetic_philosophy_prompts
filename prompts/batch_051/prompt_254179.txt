I'm in an epistemology class right now, and someone in my discussion section laid out an argument that's been bugging me for a week. It seems to show that you can know your own color perception is reliable just by using your color perception, which sounds like letting the witness testify to his own honesty. But when I try to point at the exact step where it cheats, my finger won't land. Here's the argument:

(1) Right now I know the wall is red, just by looking at it.

(2) If I know the wall is red by looking at it, then I'm also in a position to know that my color perception got this particular case right—that the wall really is the color it looked to be.

(3) If I can know that my color perception got this case right, then I can do the same thing across many cases (the grass looks green and is green, the tomato looks red and is red) and pile up a track record from which I can conclude that my color perception is generally reliable.

(4) If my color perception is generally reliable, then I can trust it as a way of forming beliefs about colors.

(5) So, by using my color perception to gather the very evidence that it is reliable, I can come to know that my color perception is reliable.

It's not that I think circularity is always and everywhere bad. But this seems like the bad kind: if it works, any faculty that happens to be reliable gets to certify itself, and you could never discover that one of your own faculties was unreliable. That can't be right. Step (1) seems fine, step (3) is just induction, and step (4) is almost a definition. So either the argument is valid and I have to accept the conclusion, or there's a specific step I haven't caught. What I want is the precise diagnosis, not just "circularity is suspicious." If it fails, tell me exactly which step fails and under what reading—is it a false premise, an equivocation, a bad inference, or a hidden principle being smuggled in? And if a reliabilist would say the argument is fine and I should just accept the conclusion, tell me what they have to give up or swallow to say that.
