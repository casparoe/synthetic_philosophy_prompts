Two people in my decision-theory reading group have been arguing for a year about whether expected utility theory is a coherence standard, and we finally talked them into an adversarial collaboration rather then just talking past each other. One of them, call them the Axiomatist, holds that the standard expected-utility representation theorem — especially independence / the sure-thing principle — pins down what it is to have a single consistent evaluation of uncertain prospects: if you show an Allais-style common-consequence pattern or Ellsberg-style ambiguity aversion, you aren't merely risk-averse, you're incoherent in a way that leaves you open to a money pump or a sure-loss series of trades. The other, call them the Risk Attitudinist, thinks this reads the theorem backwards: independence is a substantive separability assumption, not a logical requirement, and preferences that deviate from it can be fully coherent — their holders just care about how outcomes are correlated across events, or about ambiguity itself. Both are competent and well-read; neither is a strawman.

Draft the joint statement they should have produced after that collaboration. It should include (1) a numbered list of claims both can sign — not vacuous ones, including what the representation theorem does and does not establish, which constraints on preference are genuine common ground (e.g. some form of transitivity, respect for stochastic dominance, and plan-level consistency), and exactly which preference patterns are in dispute; (2) a disagreements table with the residual points, each formulated in wording the respective side would accept for its own view; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions the disagreement actually turns on; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written in advance. Don't adjudicate; if you conclude part of the dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why.
