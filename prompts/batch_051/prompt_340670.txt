A friend in medical ethics keeps insisting dignity is a useless concept. His line, which he says is basically Macklin's, is that when someone says a practice "violates patient dignity," the heavy lifting is always being done by some other idea—mostly respect for autonomy—and "dignity" is either a decorative synonym or a vague noise that lets people moralize without saying why. I've always been more sympathetic to the Kantian thought that dignity names a kind of worth that can't be priced or traded away, even with the person's own consent. Our argument got us wondering whether this is partly an empirical question hiding inside a conceptual one. So here's what I want. How could we test the claim that dignity does normative work over and above autonomy—that there's a seperate complaint in play even when autonomy is fully respected? I'm imagining vignettes where a person gives genuine, informed, uncoerced consent to something degrading: agreeing to be spat on for pay, signing a contract to be treated as property for a weekend, paying someone to publicly humiliate them. What would a Macklin-style deflationist predict about people's wrongness judgments in these cases, what would a dignity-first theorist predict, and what pattern of results would count for one side or the other? Be concrete about the design—manipulations, controls, dependent measures—and then be just as honest about what even the cleanest result would not settle, and why it leaves that residue.
