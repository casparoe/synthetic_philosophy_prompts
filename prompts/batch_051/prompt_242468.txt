I teach an undergrad seminar called something like "narrative, truth, and oral tradition" — 16 students, 75-minute sessions. I want an activity where they feel, not just hear, why the idea of a single canonical version of a myth is a bit strange. I'll hand out three short variants of a made-up folk story (pasted below) and tell them the local historical society has room for only one version on a commemorative plaque, so they have to produce a single official text. I want them to get into real arguments about which details belong and which get cut, and only later notice that the pressure to pick one version did as much to shape the story as the story itself did. No lecture up front, and dont name "canon" until the debrief.

The variants:

A. Long ago the river rose while the village slept. A girl named Senn heard a bell ringing from the reeds and ran through the lanes shouting until everyone reached the high ground. No one else heard it. The elders said it was the river-spirit, built a shrine at the reed bed, and made Senn its keeper.

B. In another telling, Senn was a servant who had been forbidden to ring the warning bell except on the elders' order. She saw the water coming, rang it anyway, and saved the village. The elders declared the bell defiled because it had been rung without authority, so they melted it down. Senn left before winter.

C. In the oldest-sounding version there is no girl at all. The bell hung in a tower by the ford, and when the river rose the water pulled a chain that ran under the bank, so the bell rang by itself. The village crossed to the far side, watched the flood take the houses, and never rebuilt in the valley. The bell is still under the water.

Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the philosophical issue — whether a canonical version is discovered or made, what is lost when variants get flattened, whether later variants are corruptions or just different uses of the story.
