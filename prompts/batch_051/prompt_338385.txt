I keep coming back to this argument that prior choice is irrelevant in the long run, and I can't tell whether it proves too much. Suppose two Bayesians, R and S, share the same finite set of candidate hypotheses H1,...,Hk and the same likelihood functions for those hypotheses. The truth is among the Hj; no two of the Hj have likelihood functions that coincide on every observable data sequence; and both agents assign strictly positive prior probability to every Hj. Then:

(1) For any Hj and Hi, the ratio of posterior probabilities equals the ratio of prior probabilities multiplied by the Bayes factor P(D | Hj)/P(D | Hi).
(2) If Hi is the true hypothesis and Hj is false, then as the data accumulate, the Bayes factor P(D | Hj)/P(D | Hi) tends to 0.
(3) A fixed finite prior ratio, multiplied by a quantity tending to 0, tends to 0.
(4) Therefore each agent's posterior probability for the true hypothesis tends to 1 regardless of the prior they started from, so the two priors wash out.
(5) Therefore a Bayesian never has much reason to worry about choosing a prior: with enough data, any prior that assigns positive probability to the truth converges to it.

I suspect (5) overstates what has been shown, but I can't say exactly why. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and what is the strongest version of the conclusion that actually follows? Answer in at most 350 words.
