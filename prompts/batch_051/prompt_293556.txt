Exam question: Compare Peirce and James on truth. In your answer: (a) state each thinker's account of what it is for a belief to be true; (b) explain one objection each thinker would raise against the other's account; (c) say which account you find more defensible and why.

Student's answer:

Peirce and James are both pragmatists, so they agree that the significance of an idea lies in the practical difference it makes, but they give different accounts of truth. Peirce holds that truth is the opinion which an indefinitely extended community of inquirers would eventually settle on if they kept investigating as long as necessary and followed the right methods. This makes truth independent of any particular individual's beliefs, because it is the community's final consensus rather than private judgment. James holds that truth is a matter of how a belief works in experience: an idea is true when believing it fits coherently with the rest of what we already hold and leaves us satisfied. For James, truth is a species of good, and a true belief is one that it is good for us to believe, for reasons we can identify in experience.

The objections follow directly. Peirce would object that James makes truth too subjective: if truth is whatever satisfies a person, then two people with conflicting satisfactions could both be right, and the distinction between truth and comfortable illusion collapses. James would object that Peirce makes truth transcend every actual inquiry: a final consensus at the limit of investigation is something we never actually possess, so it can do no practical work when we have to decide what to accept here and now. I find James more defensible. James is more honest about how inquiry actually ends, and he gives truth a concrete role in our actual lives, whereas Peirce trades this in for an idealization that cannot guide us.
