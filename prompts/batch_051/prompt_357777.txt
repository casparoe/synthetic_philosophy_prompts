I've spent the last few years covering finance and, as a side thing, formal epistemology, and I keep tripping over what looks like the same sentance wearing two hats. Van Fraassen's Reflection principle says: conditional on your future self assigning probability r to some claim, you should assign r to it now. So your current credence in a proposition is just the expected value of your future credence in it. Meanwhile the efficient market hypothesis, in its martingale form, says the current price of an asset is the market's best forecast of its future price: today's price equals the expected price tomorrow, conditional on everything currently known. Both say a system should treat its current estimate as the expectation of its own future estimate. The thing is, they also seem to have the same engine under the hood: the no-arbitrage argument for a martingale price looks an awful lot like the Dutch book argument for Reflection. So I can't tell if this is a real identity of structure or just me pattern-matching on the word "expectation." If it's real, what transfers? Finance has a whole catalog of ways markets fail to be efficient—noise traders, limits to arbitrage, transaction costs. Do those predict corresponding ways a rational agent could fail to satisfy Reflection? And if the parallel is only skin-deep, what exactly is the first point where the two come apart?
