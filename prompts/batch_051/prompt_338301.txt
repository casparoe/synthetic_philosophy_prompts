In my seminar we keep returning to the argument from illusion, and I want to force a precise diagnosis of where its force really lies. Consider the familiar case: a straight stick half-submerged in water looks bent. Here is the argument as I have been reconstructing it.

(1) In the illusory experience, you are directly aware of something that is bent.
(2) What you are directly aware of in that experience is not the stick itself, since the stick is straight.
(3) The only mind-independent candidate for what you are directly aware of in that experience is the stick.
(4) Therefore, in the illusory experience, what you are directly aware of is not a mind-independent object; it is a mental particular, which I will call a sense-datum.
(5) An illusory experience of this kind could be subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical perception of a genuinely bent stick.
(6) If two experiences are subjectively indistinguishable, then the subject is directly aware of the same kind of thing in each experience.
(7) Therefore, in the veridical perception of a bent stick, you are directly aware of a sense-datum rather than the mind-independent stick.
(8) Since any veridical perception has, or could have, an illusory counterpart subjectively indistinguishable from it, we are never directly aware of mind-independent objects; the direct objects of perception are always sense-data.

I want your diagnosis rather than a survey of the literature. First, tell me whether the argument is valid as it stands. If it is not, say exactly which step fails and under what reading of the terms. If it is valid, give the strongest reading of the contested terms on which it goes through. Second, identify where a disjunctivist about perceptual experience should dig in. In particular, I want to know whether her quarrel is with step (6), the move from subjective indistinguishability to sameness of the kind of thing one is directly aware of, or whether she must already resist step (1) by denying that in the illusion you are directly aware of a bent thing at all. Explain what the denial you identify commits her to, and whether she can honestly concede the indistinguishability in step (5) while avoiding the sense-data conclusion. Finally, decide whether there is a third way to stop the argument that is neither the sense-data theory nor disjunctivism, and say whether that third way is genuinely distinct or is just one of those two positions under another description.

One request about the form of your answer: please write it in continuous prose, as you would write a letter or an email, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic text, and no tables.
