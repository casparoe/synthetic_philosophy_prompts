I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and I've been trying to wrap my head around something for a piece on conservation and animal ethics that I keep almost grasping and then losing. The usual conservation framing says habitat loss harms wild animals because it destroys the places they live. But a freind who works on wild animal welfare told me that's surprisingly hard to defend once you separate animal welfare from biodiversity: if a typical wild animal lives a short, hungry, parasite-ridden life, then clearing a forest or draining a wetland might lower the total amount of suffering in the world rather than raise it. I can see that this is partly an empirical question about how bad wild animal lives actually are. But I suspect there's a conceptual tangle underneath it that I can't quite articulate. When someone says habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering, what exactly is the relevant comparison? Destroyed habitat compared to intact habitat holding what fixed — the same individual animals, the same number of animals, the same species mix, total welfare, average welfare? And how do I think about the fact that cleared or drained land usually gets recolonized by something? If new animals flood into a disturbed site, is the destruction better described as preventing future suffering lives, or just as replacing one set of suffering lives with another? The friend also kept insisting that this is really about comparing populations of different sizes, and that I shouldn't assume that having more animals alive counts in the habitat's favor if the extra lives are net negative — but I'm not sure whether that framing helps or quietly imports a bigger, contested population-ethics stance.

I'd love a careful walk-through of the conceptual options here — the different things someone might mean by the net welfare of an ecosystem, the different units you could compare the outcomes in, and where the argument genuinely goes through or doesn't. I don't want a reading list or citations; I want the structure of the issue in a form I could use to interview both conservation biologists and people in the wild animal welfare world without having them talk past one another. Please be explicit about which parts of this are broadly settled — or at least widely accepted by people who actually work on it — and which parts are genuinely contested, so I don't report a controversial view as if it were the consensus.
