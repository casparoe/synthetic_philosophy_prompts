I am a high school student, and I recently read an essay that tried to explain the main argument of Douglas Hofstadter’s Gödel, Escher, Bach to a general audience. I would like to know how much of the essay’s summary to trust.

As I understood it, the essay’s version of the argument runs like this. Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem shows that any consistent formal system strong enough to represent arithmetic can be made to contain a sentence that, under the system’s own coding, says of itself that it is not provable. If the system proved that sentence, it would be proving a falsehood and would therefore be inconsistent; if the system proved the sentence’s negation, it would also be inconsistent. So the sentence is true but unprovable from within the system, even though we can see from the outside that it is true. The essay then says that Hofstadter’s central move is to treat this self-referential structure as the key to consciousness: a mind is a symbol system that has become able to refer to itself as a whole, forming what Hofstadter calls a strange loop, and the self is the pattern that results, not a separate substance added to the machinery.

So far the summary matched the vague impression I already had of the book. But the essay went on to draw two conclusions that struck me as suspicious. First, it said Hofstadter uses Gödel’s theorem to show that no formal system, including any possible computer program, could ever replicate or instantiate a human mind, because every formal system has a blind spot of this kind. Second, it said that since a machine could never recognize the truth of certain sentences from inside its own system, a machine could never reason reliably about its own behavior in the way a human being can.

Is the essay’s summary fair to what Hofstadter actually argues? Please separate the parts that someone familiar with the book would accept, the parts that are common misreadings or conflations of distinct claims, and the parts that are simply wrong. Do the final two conclusions follow from the accurate parts of the summary, or do they depend on a shift in the argument? And does a commitment to strange loops commit Hofstadter to the view that minds cannot be formal systems, to the opposite view, or to neither?
