You are an experienced teacher of decision theory and the philosophy of economics who has run many in-class experiments. I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on risk, choice, and rationality, with 16 students and a 75-minute session. The topic is the St. Petersburg paradox, but I do not want it explained up front; I want the students to feel its force through their own choices and only then see the mathematics that produced their discomfort.

Design a complete, runnable classroom activity. Include:

- Learning goals, stated in terms of what students should come away seeing rather than facts they should recite.
- Materials, all cheap or free: coins, bidding slips, a visible banker's ledger, whatever you need, with no real money.
- A timeline for the 75 minutes, with room for a debrief.
- A payoff scheme implementable without real stakes, plus a sealed-bid auction or equivalent mechanism so students reveal their genuine maximum willingness to pay rather than performing for one another.
- At least two variants beyond the first auction: (1) a finite-bankroll version in which the banker has a stated, visible, very large bankroll, so the expected value is finite and grows only logarithmically with the bankroll; and (2) a debrief step in which payoffs are re-imagined in units of utility rather than money, to test whether students' reluctance is really just diminishing marginal utility of money. For each variant, say exactly what I should do and say, and what the students should notice before anything is explained.

The debrief should map specific moments in the activity — the hesitation during bidding, the repricing under a finite bankroll, the utility-unit thought experiment — onto the conceptual structure of the paradox. It should carefully distinguish the mathematical claim, that the idealized gamble has infinite expected value, from the normative claim, that one should therefore pay any finite price for it, and it should show why common resolutions — finite bankrolls, diminishing marginal utility, risk aversion, the unlikelihood of a long run — either fail for the idealized game or change the subject. End by being frank about which feature of the paradox the classroom cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must supply by discussion alone.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: students who already know the paradox gaming the auction, the class actually seeing a long run of tails and overlearning from it, students concluding that the paradox is only about small probabilities, and social anchoring in the bids. Give a concrete remedy for each.

Before settling on a final design, brainstorm at least two or three possible mechanics and briefly explain why you rejected the alternatives. Think carefully about timing, and make sure the whole thing can actually be run in 75 minutes by one instructor.
