I write legal thrillers—two or three a year, plus occasional short fiction—and my plots frequently depend on whether a criminal statute I have invented would survive a void-for-vagueness challenge under American federal or state law. I am a novelist, not a lawyer. My research lawyer, who has to sign off on the legal scaffolding of each manuscript before my editor sees it, keeps marking fictional statutes as either unconstitutionally vague in ways I did not intend, or not vague in a way that would actually produce a challenge; the rewrites are expensive and delay submission. I want a decision procedure I can run myself, in about thirty minutes per statute, before she ever sees a draft: an ordered set of questions, a clear standard for what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a point where the procedure can tell me the statute is likely safe or likely vulnerable as drafted, a point where it stops and tells me to call the lawyer, and an honest label for the steps that are judgment rather than law. The setting is contemporary American law, so the procedure should track the real doctrine rather than my characters' courtroom rhetoric. Please preserve the two strands my lawyer keeps pressing on me: vagueness is about fair notice to ordinary people and about preventing arbitrary or discriminatory enforcement, which means a law made of ordinary words can still be vague in its core, and a law made of technical words can be precise yet still invite arbitrary policing.

Then run the procedure on two statutes I am considering. The first is a municipal ordinance making it an offense to behave in a suspicious manner in a public place after dark and to fail to give a peace officer a satisfactory explanation for one's presence. The second is a state felony statute punishing the operation of a drone or other automated system in a way that causes substantial and unjustified interference with a critical service. I included the second because it sounds precise and technological, and my instinct is that it would survive; if the procedure disagrees, I need to hear that plainly, and if the procedure would tell me something I do not want to hear about either statute, do not soften it. Tell me which step of the procedure drives each conclusion, where the two statutes part company, and what the procedure cannot do—where it would give me false confidence or miss a challenge that a court would take seriously.
