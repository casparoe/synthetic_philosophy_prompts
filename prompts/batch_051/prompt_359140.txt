I've been in a reading group doing constitutional theory, and last week we got stuck on whether it's legitimate for a constitution to contain provisions that are deliberately very hard to amend — a rights charter that needs a supermajority twice plus a referendum, or a clause marked unamendable, that kind of thing. Every time I pushed on whether that doesn't just let dead people rule the living, the moderator shrugged and said it's just tying yourself to the mast, like Ulysses. But the more I think about it, the more I think that one-liner is doing several different jobs at once, and I can't tell which one is suppose to be doing the actual argumentative work. Sometimes it sounds like a banal point about self-control, sometimes like a heavy claim that a nation is a single agent that persists over centuries, sometimes like a point about courts being the crew with the ropes.

So here's what I want. Take the one-liner "the constitution is just tying yourself to the mast" and pull apart the precise claims it could be making, from the harmless to the radical. Don't just explain Ulysses and don't give me a reading list. I want a disambiguation with verdicts.

Lay it out as a table with these columns:

- Reading: a short label for the distinct claim (like "intrapersonal self-binding", "dead-hand constraint", "external enforcement", whatever you find).
- Claim stated precisely: one or two sentences, an actual proposition — something that could be true or false, not a vibe.
- Verdict: trivial, substantive-and-plausibly-true, substantive-and-contested, false, or category mistake — with a phrase saying why.
- Work it does: does this reading actually support treating entrenched clauses as legitimate? Or does it only do that if it secretly leans on one of the other readings? Name which one.

Order the rows from most harmless to most radical. After the table, give me one short paragraph on which reading our moderator needed for the constitutional argument, and whether that reading survives the obvious objection that the people alive today never tied themselves to anything.
