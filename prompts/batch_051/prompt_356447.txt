I'm putting together a short interactive feature for a workshop on decision-making and self-knowledge, and I'd like it to take the form of a "which position on predicting your own choices do you actually hold?" quiz. The audience is mixed: a few participants have done graduate work in decision theory, but most are simply thoughtful people who enjoy arguing about Newcomb's problem or about whether you can predict what you'll order before you've decided. I want the quiz to sort readers by how they answer concrete cases, not by which label they like, so no question should directly ask anything like "do you think you can assign probabilities to your own choices?" Each of the eight to ten questions should present a concrete scenario and two or three answer options, and the options should be phrased as actions or reactions, not as theoretical commitments.

Please include at least these seven cases, in any order, and add one to three of your own: (1) a standard Newcomb problem; (2) a transparent-box variant in which the player can see whether the predictor has already put the large sum in the box before choosing; (3) a case where a reliable psychologist announces a 90% prediction of your choice from a brain scan before you choose; (4) a mundane case in which a friend offers you odds on what you'll order at a restaurant while you are still deciding; (5) a job-offer case where you are asked to announce a probability that you will accept before you have deliberated; (6) a commitment-device case in which someone tries to pre-commit against a predicted future lapse; and (7) a prediction-market case in which you may trade on a contract that pays out if you finish a self-chosen goal by a deadline. The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least these five positions, described so readers can recognize themselves without prior jargon: the view that you can and should assign sharp probabilities to your own choices and let those predictions feed back into the decision; the view that you can predict yourself but such predictions should be kept out of the deliberation; the view that deliberation crowds out prediction, so that assigning probabilities to your own options while deciding is either impossible or subtly corrupts the choice; the view that self-predictions can be made but only as vague or interval-valued judgments; and the view that there is a first-person/third-person asymmetry, so that predicting your own future choices is systematically less reliable than predicting someone else's. For each leaf position, add one sentence on the most awkward case for that position. Flag any pair of positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate and say in a sentence why the cases fail to distinguish them; if some answer pattern has no leaf, diagnose briefly whether the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Keep the entire quiz, including the scoring key, to at most 1,200 words.
