Last year I hosted a reading group through my university's law faculty, and the discussion leader, an evidence professor, left us with a sharp record of verdicts on the canonical hypotheticals. In the blue bus case, where the only evidence is that 60 percent of the buses in town are operated by the Blue Company, she says the injured plaintiff loses. In the gatecrasher case, where 1,000 people attended the rodeo, only 499 paid, and the organizer sues a randomly selected attendee under a preponderance standard, she says the organizer loses. In the prison-yard case, where 99 of 100 prisoners took part in the attack and the prosecution cannot tell which one did not, she says no one may be convicted. Yet she convicts on a cold-hit DNA match with a random-match probability of one in a hundred million and on a bare latent fingerprint match, in each case with no other evidence tying the defendant to the crime. I now have a personal reason to know what theory she is running. My cousin was struck by a delivery van in a hit-and-run, and the only evidence we have is that 99.9 percent of vans of that make, model, and livery in the city are operated by a single courier company; there is no witness who saw the company's name, and no paint transfer, vehicle part, or other trace evidence. Predict what the professor would say about liability in my cousin's case. More important than the prediction: set out the candidate rules that fit her past verdicts, say what each rule predicts for the van case, give your best guess about her prediction with the reasons for preferring that rule over the others, and specify the further case, ideally one I could put to her directly, that would best discriminate between the remaining candidate rules.
