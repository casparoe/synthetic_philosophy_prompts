I'm a philosophy professor, and my colleague across the hall in psychology studies cultural tightness and looseness. Over lunches she's built up a little track record of verdicts on the small rule-enforcement disputes I keep bringing to her. She thought the city was right to fine a man for watering his yard during a drought even though he used rainwater from barrels, because a drought rule is a commons rule and exeptions nibble it to death. She thought the hospital was right to require flu vaccination even for staff who never see patients, because one exemption here and one there is how the norm erodes. She defended the town's fine for backyard fireworks on a dry, fire-prone hillside even though the person had soaked the ground around the launch site, again on the grounds that a blanket rule cannot be administered case by case. But she thought the condo board was wrong to fine an owner for replanting a water-hungry lawn with drought-tolerant wildflowers just because it spoiled the look; and she thought suspending a kid over an untucked shirt at a Zoom assembly was absurd.

Now I want to predict her on a new case. The town is considering fining someone who put his trash bins out an hour before the 7 a.m. set-out, even though he blocked no one and the automated truck still picked them up on schedule. Nobody claims the early bins were unsafe or unsanitary; the only justification offered is that the route runs smoothly only if residents don't improvise individual exceptions. Should she support the fine for consistency with her earlier verdicts? Which of her earlier cases is the closest analogue, what principle would she be adopting with either answer, and what further case would best settle which principle she is actually running?
