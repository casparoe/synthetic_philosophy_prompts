I'm a staff software engineer at a company whose production estate has become a zoo: a fifteen-year-old monolith nobody fully understands, dozens of microservices, several machine-learned models, and a newer LLM-based support agent. A colleague recently mentioned Dennett's distinction between the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance — roughly, predicting a system from its physical constitution, from what it was designed to do, or by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and goals. The toy examples make sense, but I need something my team can actually use during an incident, often within the first half hour before we start touching code or retraining a model. Please write a reusable decision procedure — a checklist or an ordered set of questions — for deciding which stance to adopt first when a system behaves surprisingly, what should count as evidence that the current stance has stopped earning its keep, and when we should switch. I gather the deeper claim is that the intentional stance is warranted only when it locks onto a real pattern in the system's behavior rather than a projection of ours, so please make that operational: what would make a pattern real enough to license treating a system as an agent, and what would show we were merely anthropomorphizing. The procedure has to be usable by engineers who have never encountered this vocabulary and do not want a philosophy lecture, it should say where it stops with a no, and it should flag the steps that are judgment calls or values questions rather than factual ones.

Then run the procedure on three cases, including at least one where it returns an answer my team will not like: (1) an LLM support agent that sometimes invents a policy and asserts it with total confidence; (2) a legacy pricing service that has begun producing wrong outputs after an undocumented change elsewhere, under conditions no one can reproduce; (3) a recommendation model that abruptly started boosting one category of products after a silent upstream data change. I am not asking for an exegesis of Dennett; I am asking whether the distinction can be ground down into a tool that does real work.
