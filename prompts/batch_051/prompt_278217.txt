I spent my working life as an experimental physical chemist, and in retirement I have been reading moral philosophy with the habits of someone who once designed calibration studies. I keep encountering a claim about the structure of comparative evaluation that strikes me as only partly conceptual: that relations like better than, worse than, and at least as good as are transitive.

The arguments that trouble me are the ones usually called spectrum arguments. One constructs a long sequence of alternatives S0, S1, ..., Sn such that each adjacent pair differs by an amount too small to count as a genuine worsening, yet Sn is plainly far worse than S0. If the relevant comparative relation is transitive, the absence of any genuine worsening between adjacent alternatives forces the conclusion that Sn is not worse than S0, which almost everyone rejects. The main escape routes appear to be these: one may hold that some adjacent pair is worse after all, however small the difference; one may deny that the relevant comparative relation is transitive; one may hold that the adjacent judgments concern a nontransitive relation such as roughly equal or not worse while strict better than remains transitive; or one may hold that the alternatives themselves, or the dimensions on which they are compared, shift along the series.

I would like you to treat this as a problem in measurement rather than as an exercise in terminological defense. Please give me a concrete experimental design that could separate these four structures from one another, with particular attention to the fact that individual pairwise choices are noisy. Use a spectrum whose endpoints differ in something plainly evaluable — duration of discomfort, intrusiveness of a policy, number of people affected — and have the task elicit comparative value judgments rather than discrimination responses. Specify the stimuli, the task, the response format, the sampling plan, and the analysis, and explain which quantitative pattern would count as evidence for each structure. Then give an honest account of what the results would and would not settle: which of the four structures remain underidentified by choice data alone, and what part of the philosophical dispute would survive even a decisive experimental result. I am less interested in a survey of authors than in the logic of the test and the limits of the inference.
