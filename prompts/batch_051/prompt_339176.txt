I have a standing rule for deciding which criticisms of my political views to take seriously. When I encounter a criticism of a view I hold, I first ask whether the critic ever held that view. If they never did, I largely discount what they say, on the grounds that they are probably attacking a flattened or outdated version of it and cannot know from the inside why it seemed right to reasonable people. If the critic did once hold the view and later abandoned it, I give the criticism special weight: they know what the view actually commits one to and can say precisely what they found untenable. I realize this means that the only outside criticisms that reach me come from defectors, and that I treat the same argument differently depending on who delivers it. Recently I set aside a carefully reported article criticizing a policy I support because the author had never supported the policy, while I took very seriously a former supporter's loosely argued essay making essentially the same complaint. Is this rule a defensible response to living in an echo chamber, or is it a mechanism for staying in one? Under what conditions does a defector's criticism actually deserve more weight than an outsider's? What are the rule's main failure modes, and is there a restated version that keeps its benefits without sealing me off?
