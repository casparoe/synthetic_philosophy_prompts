Many contemporary health practices treat eating as a morally charged activity: meal-tracking apps reward consecutive days of hitting macronutrient targets, wellness discourse divides foods into clean and dirty or pure and toxic, and workplace programs sometimes frame dietary discipline as evidence of professional reliability. At the same time, a strand of thought holds that diet is not incidental to intellectual life: that what one eats shapes one's energy, mood, and even the character of one's thinking. Nietzsche, in his late autobiographical writings, says something of this second sort, suggesting that a heavy national diet is connected to a heavy style of thought, that his own thinking improved after he changed what he ate, and that the question of how one should nourish oneself matters more for human flourishing than many properly theological questions. Yet the same Nietzsche also attacks Christian asceticism and the moralization of bodily denial.

What philosophical commitments does the first practice—the moralized, quantified, disciplinary attitude toward eating that pervades contemporary wellness culture—presuppose? Does it presuppose that health is a form of virtue, that the body is something to be optimized rather than simply lived, that appetites are untrustworthy and must be overridden by will, or that physiological condition and cognitive or moral condition are closely linked? Which of these commitments would Nietzsche share, and which would he reject? Is the practice philosophically underdetermined—could one accept it for purely instrumental reasons, or reject it while still believing that diet shapes thought? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify the places where its implicit theory is inconsistent or where several rival theories would license the same behavior.
