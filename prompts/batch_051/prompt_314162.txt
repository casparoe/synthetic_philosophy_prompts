I'm putting together a list of distinct interpretive positions on how Mill's defense of despotic colonial rule in India relates to his liberal principles, and I need one more that is not already on the list. The positions I have collected so far are: (1) the developmental tutelage reading, on which despotic rule is legitimate only for peoples at an earlier stage of human progress and only as a temporary means of preparing them for liberal self-government; (2) the institutional-capacity reading, on which representative government requires psychological and social preconditions that Mill believed Indian society did not yet meet, so that introducing it prematurely would fail even by its own standards; (3) the East India Company reading, which emphasizes Mill's professional career and his claim that the Company's expert, insulated administration better served Indian subjects than direct rule by the British public or Parliament would; (4) the utilitarian-civic reading, on which the criterion shifts from liberty as such to the improvement of the governed, and paternal despotism is defended as the arrangement that best promotes that improvement under current conditions; (5) the stadial-history reading, on which forms of government fit stages of civilization, so that what would count as tyranny in a developed European society is not a violation of the same principle when applied to a society Mill classified as not yet capable of improvement through free discussion; and (6) the internal-critique reading, on which Mill's colonial exception is simply a betrayal of his own arguments against paternalism, because his grounds for valuing liberty and his rejection of coercive interference for a person's own good should extend to all sane adults.
