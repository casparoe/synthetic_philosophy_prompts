I have recently started reviewing consent forms for a clinical study at my institution, and I keep returning to the same worry. The framework I was taught says that consent is valid when it is sufficiently informed, voluntary, and given by someone with the relevant competence, with deception, coercion, and incapacity as the three kinds of defects. But the study I am reviewing offers participants something they badly need, and I cannot decide whether that offer is a legitimate inducement or a form of pressure. That has sent me back to a historical case I want you to analyze through the framework.

Take the hepatitis experiments at the Willowbrook State School on Staten Island in the 1950s and 1960s. Researchers, led by Saul Krugman, studied viral hepatitis by deliberately infecting children in an institution for children with intellectual disabilities, on the rationale that the children would likely contract hepatitis anyway in the crowded wards. Parents were given some information and signed consent forms, but the institution was severely overcrowded, and the usual accounts say that a place in the research unit was sometimes what made admission possible. I do not need more of the history; I need the case used as a test of the deception–coercion–competence framework.

Say what the framework says about each stage: what it treats as deception, where it draws the line between an offer and coercion when the alternative is dire, and how it handles competence when the subjects themselves cannot consent and the parents' circumstances are themselves constrained. Be disciplined about the mapping: who is the consenting party at each step, what were their actual options, and which defeater is doing the work or failing to do it. Then say where the framework is silent or has to be stretched, and what the episode in turn shows about it — whether the case confirms the framework, exposes an ambiguity in one of its concepts, or reveals something it misses. Reach and defend one overall verdict on that question. Finally, tell me which argument or consideration would most plausibly change your mind about the verdict, and where your reasoning is least secure.
