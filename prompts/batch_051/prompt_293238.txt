My decision theory reading group has built an argument that seems to show that two standard puzzles jointly force a contradiction in the idea of rational choice. I want a diagnosis. Here is the argument, written out with numbered premises.

In Counterfactual Mugging, Omega, an extremely good predictor, flips a fair coin. If the coin lands tails, Omega asks you for $100. If it lands heads, Omega pays you $10,000, but only if Omega's prediction system would have predicted that, had it landed tails, you would have paid the $100. You know all of this. At the time you are asked for the $100, the coin has already landed tails.

The argument proceeds as follows.

(1) At any choice point, a rational agent selects the available act with the best expected causal consequences for their own utility function, taking all already-settled facts as fixed.  
(2) In the tails branch of Counterfactual Mugging, your decision whether to pay cannot causally affect the coin, nor can it causally affect Omega's earlier prediction. The only causal consequence of paying is that you lose $100.  
(3) Therefore, by (1), you should not pay in the tails branch.  
(4) But ex ante, before the coin is flipped, the best policy to adopt is to pay if tails. That policy yields an expected payoff of $4,950, while the policy of not paying if tails yields an expected payoff of $0.  
(5) A rational agent's ex post choices must implement the policy that was rational ex ante, since otherwise the agent's future self would reliably abandon the policy that gives the best expected outcome.  
(6) Therefore, by (4) and (5), you should pay in the tails branch.  
(7) But (3) and (6) cannot both be true.

Now add the second puzzle. In XOR blackmail, a blackmailer makes two perfect, non-interacting copies of you, A and B, and credibly tells each copy: I will impose a large loss on one copy if and only if the other copy refuses to pay a small fee. Your own payment causally affects only the other copy's welfare, not your own. Each copy cares only about their own welfare, and each knows the other is in an identical situation running the identical decision procedure.

The same split arises. Premise (1) seems to force the conclusion that each copy should refuse, since refusing has no negative causal consequence for the refusing copy. But because each copy reasons this way, both refuse, and each suffers the large loss that the other's refusal triggers. Meanwhile, if each accepted premise (5) and followed the ex ante best policy, each would pay the small fee and avoid the large loss. So the same tension between causal dominance and policy rationality reappears in a case where the ex post causal recommendation looks like mutual self-sabotage.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which numbered premise do you reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost in one or the other puzzle? I would most like to keep premise (1), but I am not sure whether it can survive premise (5) once the two cases are set side by side.
