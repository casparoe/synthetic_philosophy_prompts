I have been brooding over a self-amending rule and have reconstructed an argument that seems to prove something absurd. I half-suspect the argument is cheating somewhere, but I cannot say exactly where, and I want a precise diagnosis rather than an impression.

Here is the setup. Consider a tiny normative system — a game, or a micro-legal order — whose entire rulebook is one rule, Rule R:

“Any rule of this system, including this rule itself, may be repealed by a two-thirds vote of the assembly.”

No other rule exists. The assembly, following the only procedure the system contains, votes by a two-thirds majority to repeal Rule R.

Now consider this argument for the conclusion that the repeal cannot succeed:

(1) Before the vote, Rule R is in force.
(2) So at the moment the vote is held, a two-thirds vote to repeal Rule R is an act authorized by Rule R.
(3) An act authorized by a rule that is in force at the moment the act is performed is a valid act.
(4) Therefore the vote to repeal Rule R is valid, and Rule R is repealed the moment the vote’s result is announced.
(5) But from that moment on, Rule R is no longer in force.
(6) If Rule R is no longer in force, then no rule of the system authorizes the repeal.
(7) A rule-repeal is valid only if some rule in force authorizes it; hence, once Rule R is gone, the repeal no longer has any authorizing rule and ceases to be valid.
(8) A single act cannot be both valid and invalid. So the repeal must never have occurred, and Rule R remains in force after all.

That makes the power to repeal “any rule, including this rule” strangely empty precisely in the one case that matters. But the argument seems to win by quietly treating “valid” as if it were a state that must keep being maintained.

In particular: is Rule R’s self-repeal genuinely possible, genuinely impossible, or ill-posed?
