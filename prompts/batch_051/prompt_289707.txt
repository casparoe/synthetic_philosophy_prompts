Act as a professional philosopher of historiography who has also worked as a software engineer. I want a serious judgement about whether an analogy I've been turning over is exact enough to matter, so please don't just riff: first brainstorm the mapping, steelman the strongest version of the parallel, then try to break it, and answer as precisely as you can.

Here's the parallel. Whig history, as I understand it, is the tendency to write the past as an inevitable climb toward us: the Reformation becomes a "step toward tolerance", the Glorious Revolution becomes a "step toward constitutionalism", and everything in the archive gets sorted into "helped get us here" or "detour". The endpoint is smuggled in as the goal of the process from the beginning; dead alternatives are erased or redescribed as losing paths that never had a chance.

Now the software side. When a team inherits a legacy codebase and tries to understand its architecture, they almost inevitably reconstruct the history backwards from the current state of the code. Every surviving commit message, PR comment, old design doc, and migration note gets rationalized as a step toward the present architecture. But read the commits forward, chronologically, and you see the real texture: workarounds for infrastructure that was decommissioned years ago, features built for customers who churned before launch, a monolith-splitting attempt abandonned twice, a dependency chosen because one engineer left. The architecture we now describe as "the system" usually was not designed as a target at all. It's just the state that happened to survive, with the survivors' story written after the fact.

Proposed mapping:

- current codebase ↔ the present
- the total commit/PR/doc record ↔ the historical archive
- architecture docs and "how the system evolved" narratives ↔ Whig historiography
- dead branches, reverted refactors, abandoned migrations ↔ erased alternatives and defeated parties

Two things I already suspect might collapse the analogy, and I'd like you to treat them seriously rather than wave them away. First, the git record is (mostly) total and machine-recorded, whereas the historical record is fragmentary and always already interpreted. Second, and maybe more deeply: in git, the current state really is the literal product of past commits, so saying "each commit led to here" is just causal bookkeeping; the Whig error in historiography is partly the normative/evaluative overlay — that the past was "on our side", that we are the point of it. Is that overlay present in the software case, or does the analogy lose the very thing that makes Whig history a vice?

So: is this a genuine structural identity, a loose analogy, or me pattern-matching on the word "history"? Could anything transfer from the historiography literature to codebase archaeology or post-incident reviews? Give me at least one prediction the parallel makes about software teams that is clearly false, and one that is probably true, if you think any are.
