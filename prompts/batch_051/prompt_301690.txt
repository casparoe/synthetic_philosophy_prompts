I am a novelist at work on a book about a woman who signed an advance directive in middle age and later, after a dementia diagnosis, seems to have become someone else: she is warm, content, and full of small pleasures, though she no longer recognizes the document she signed or the person who signed it. The novel turns on a conflict between her adult daughter, who insists that the earlier self's written wishes must be honored, and her sister, who insists that the woman in front of them is the only one whose present preferences should count. While researching this, I have been asked to moderate a podcast interview with a philosopher who defends the strong authority of the earlier self. He argues that a competent person's advance directive is an act of self-governance that does not lapse when competence does, and that the later self's apparent contentment, even genuine enjoyment, does not by itself unseat that authority. The audience will not be specialists, but I want to question him the way a careful philosopher would, through the hardest cases rather than around them. Please write a sequence of interview questions that presses his position from several directions at once: whether the earlier self is really in a position to bind the later self; whether it matters that the later self may no longer share the values behind the directive; how he distinguishes authority over one's future from a failure to update in light of new evidence about what dementia is like; and how far the authority of the earlier self extends before it becomes something more like moral rigorism. For each question, include the reply his position would most naturally invite and the follow-up that would keep that reply from ending the matter too comfortably, and shape the whole sequence so one question builds on what the previous one exposes.
