I'm a journalist working on a piece about computational irreducibility — the idea that for a lot of systems, the only way to find out what they'll do is to run them; there's no fast-forward. One of my sources, a physicist, kept reaching for the same analogy, and its doing a lot of argumentative work for him: he says the fundamental laws are the source code of the universe, and that explaining a system just is having the code. Once you've got the equations, you've got the program, and predicting the behavior is only a matter of running it forward with enough compute. His punchline is that higher-level explanations (chemistry, biology, economics) are conveniences for beings with slow brains, not extra facts — so reductionism wins by default.

I want to audit that analogy for the article rather than just repeat it. Lay out the mapping explicitly: what corresponds to the code, the programmer, the runtime, the compiler or interpreter, the initial conditions or input, the output, the debugging? Then work through where the source-code picture breaks. What do you actually get when you have a program's source, and does computational irreducibility break the step from "I have the code" to "I can predict, understand, or explain the behavior"? What does the analogy quietly import from software — that code is readable at a glance, that it was written with a function in mind, that the runtime is well-behaved — that the world might not supply? Does his reductionist conclusion survive once the disanalogies are named, and is there a better analogy for what fundamental laws buy you?

And for my own editing: where is your audit most likely to fail, and what argument would it take to make you come out on the physicist's side instead?
