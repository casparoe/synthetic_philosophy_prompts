I am a novelist, and I have ended up far outside my depth. The protagonist of my current book is a logician haunted by self-referential sentences, and in trying to make her intellectual life feel lived-in rather than merely decorative, I began working through the Liar paradox myself. The reply I first latched onto — that the liar sentence is neither true nor false — felt like a clean escape, and I planned to give it to my character as the position she tries to hold before eventually abandoning it. But a philosopher friend has pressed me on it, and I can no longer tell whether he has shown the clean escape to be unstable or merely shown that I was stating it too loosely. I need to know exactly where, if anywhere, his argument breaks.

He asks me to consider the sentence L, which says of itself: L is not true. The neither-true-nor-false solution is committed, at a minimum, to saying that L is neither true nor false. Against that solution he gives the following argument:

(0) Assume for the sake of argument that L is neither true nor false.
(1) If L is neither true nor false, then L is not true.
(2) So L is not true.
(3) L says exactly that L is not true.
(4) Whenever a sentence says that things stand a certain way, and things do stand that way, the sentence is true.
(5) Therefore, since L says that L is not true and L is not true, it follows by (4) that L is true.
(6) So L is true and L is not true, which is a contradiction.
(7) Since the contradiction follows from the neither-true-nor-false solution through innocent-looking steps, that solution cannot be the stable resting point it appeared to be.

My question is no longer really about the novel; it is about the argument. Is it valid? If it is invalid, at which numbered step does the inference break down, and why? If it is valid, which premise is false or too strong, and what would the corrected premise have to be? My friend also claims that the only alternatives are to locate the failure precisely or to accept that L really is both true and not true; I would like to know whether he is right about that as well.
