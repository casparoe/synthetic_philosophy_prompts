I've been reading T. H. Huxley's On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata (the 1874 essay, not Aldous), and there's this part where he says consciousness is like a steam whistle on a locomotive: the engine produces the whistle, but the whistle doesn't actually drive the wheels or push the train forward. It's just a side-effect of the working parts. That image got me stuck on something, and I want to see how the standard thought experiment changes if we take Huxley literally and then drop Mary into it.

Here's the variation. Suppose Mary is the usual brilliant color scientist who has lived her whole life in a black-and-white room, except she's also a convinced epiphenomenalist. She has read Huxley and she believes that conscious experiences are caused by brain processes but have no causal powers at all. Before she leaves the room, she knows all the physical facts about color vision, and she also expects that when she sees red, the redness experience will be caused by her brain but won't cause anything—not even her own judgment that she is having it. Then she walks out, sees a red tomato, and says or thinks "so this is what red looks like."

Now here's what I keep going around in circles about. If the redness experience is truly epiphenomenal, then it couldn't have caused her to make that judgment. Her brain state must have caused both the experience and the judgment, probably with the judgment already locked in before she ever saw the tomato. So was her little "aha" moment evidence that she learned something new, or is it just the output of a physical process that would have happened anyway? Does the knowledge argument still have its force in this setup, or does having Mary be an epiphenomenalist from the start undermine the argument by making her judgment causally disconnected from the very thing she supposedly learned? And what would a non-epiphenomenalist physicalist say about this version—would they just say the physical brain state causes both the experience and the judgment, and that's all there is to it?

Work through how the knowledge argument and the epiphenomenalist reply each handle this modified Mary case, and pinpoint the exact premise where they part ways.
