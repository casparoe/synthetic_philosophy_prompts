Suppose an isolated island community of roughly two thousand people has, for the past four hundred years, spoken a complete human language that children acquire natively and adults use for trade, dispute, storytelling, and instruction. The language has one systematic gap: it contains no indexicals and no pronouns. There are no first- or second-person forms (nothing corresponding to I, you, me, or we), no demonstratives (this, that), no spatial or temporal indexicals (here, there, now, then, today, yesterday), and no tense inflection, since tense locates an event relative to the moment of utterance. The language is otherwise adequate: it has proper names, titles and kinship terms, verbs, numerals, a calendar of named days, and a system of spatial reference based on landmarks, the sun's position, and body parts rather than on the speaker's own location.

Give me your reasoned prediction of how such a language would actually be used and how it would change over the next few generations, working through the following four situations. Assume for purposes of the exercise that the speakers cannot resolve the situations by pointing or other gesture, so the burden falls on the language itself. In each case, say whether the language succeeds by circumlocution, fails outright, or forces its speakers to innovate the very thing the language supposedly lacks—and explain the mechanism involved.

1. A speaker wants a particular coconut from a heap of a dozen nearly identical coconuts, in full view of both speaker and hearer, while both of them are carrying firewood.
2. A speaker wants the hearer to know that the fire they had planned to light is being lit at the moment of the utterance, rather than at the later time they had agreed on.
3. A speaker wants to convey that they themselves, not one of several nearby people, caught a certain fish—and the hearer does not know the speaker's name and cannot see who is currently speaking.
4. Two speakers need to arrange to meet again at the same place, at the same time the following day, when neither knows a fixed name for either the place or the time.

Then state your overall expectation: would the language remain free of indexicals, or would some of its descriptive resources be conventionalized and bleached into pronouns, demonstratives, or tense markers over the next few generations? Be explicit about which links in your reasoning are robust structural pressures on a language used by ordinary speakers and which are more speculative, and say what would have to be true for your overall prediction to be wrong. Finally, answer the underlying question directly: does this counterfactual community show that a human language could genuinely do without indexicals, or does it show that context-dependent reference tends to be reinvented under normal use? Defend whichever conclusion you reach.
