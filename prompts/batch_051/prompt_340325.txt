After a seminar on cost–benefit analysis of flood protection, two people from my department got into it over dinner and I ended up as the unofficial referee. (1) Dana said the whole method is built on sand: it adds up benefits and costs across different people, but utility is private to each person, so there is no common ruler and the resulting sum doesn't mean anything. (2) Marco said that's a ridiculous standard; if you really couldn't compare across people, ordinary claims like "the flood walls help the residents more than they cost taxpayers" would be empty, and so would basically every decision about taxes or triage. (3) Dana said triage uses a partial ordering over obvious harms, not degrees of utility; the real problem is that a utility function is only unique up to any increasing transformation, so two people's functions don't share units, and a claim that one person's gain "outweighs" another's loss isn't pinned down by their preferences alone. (4) Marco said there's a principled common unit: imagine a judge who could wake up as either person, with that person's tastes and situation, and ask which life the judge would rather have. That's an interpersonal comparison made on a single scale. (5) Dana said that's a third party's preference over two lives, not a measurement of the two people's own utility; switch the judge and you switch the comparison, so it's just arbitrary. (6) Marco said the same is true of any measuring instument, and the answer is a better-informed, more sympathetic judge — not pretending the comparison is impossible.

I couldn't decide who was right, and they've both asked me to settle it. Can you go through the moves one by one and say who is right about what, whether either framing is missing an option, and who has the better overall position?
