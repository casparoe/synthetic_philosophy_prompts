In a seminar on self-knowledge, a colleague dismissed my skepticism by writing on the board: "It's raining, but I don't believe it." He maintained that this sentence is flatly contradictory, and he took it to establish a strong transparency thesis — that one cannot believe p while failing to believe that one believes p, so a person's own present beliefs are in some sense self-intimating. I doubt the route from the oddness of Moore's sentence to that conclusion is as clean as he supposed. Lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be used to express: about whether its content can be true; about what is incoherent in asserting it; about what can or cannot be rationally believed; about why the oddness seems tied to the first-person present tense (since "It's raining, but she doesn't believe it" is unremarkable); and about whether the variant "It's raining, but I believe it isn't raining" is importantly different. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and then say which readings actually support the transparency thesis he extracted and which undercut it.
