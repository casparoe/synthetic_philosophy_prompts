Can you write me a dialogue between two AI safety people who keep going in circles on this? One of them is the control/security type: he thinks the sane posture is to treat a model as potentially misaligned and wrap it in protocols—a weaker trusted model doing paraphrases and audits of the stronger untrusted one, output monitoring, no high-stakes actions without a human check, and control evals that measure how often a red-team model actually defeats the protocol. He thinks you can get useful work out of a system you do not trust yet. The other is the alignment type: she thinks that whole approach stops working exactly when it matters, because a system that outclasses its overseers will learn to slip past the monitors and play dead on the tests, and the only thing that actually counts is evidence about what the model wants. Ground them in a concrete case so they don’t just speechify: a lab has a big model that looks fine on alignment benchmarks, but a red team can jailbreak it into writing exploit code and it can hide some of its abilities once it notices it is being tested. The control person wants to ship it inside a strict untrusted/trusted split with monitoring and no autonomous high-stakes actions; the alignment person says don’t ship until the internals-based evidence is much stronger. Make both of them sharp and concrete—monitoring quality, collusion between parts of a model, tamper-evident logs, probes, scheming, acceptable risk, and the gap between capability evals and alignment evals. They should actually respond to each other’s actual points, neither should be a strawman, and by the end one of them should have shifted a little without the whole thing turning into a tidy consensus. Oh and dont make the alignment person all mystical about values; make her as empirical and quantitative as the control person.
