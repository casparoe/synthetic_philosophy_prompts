I am a novelist, and over the past year I have been using generative language models less as ghostwriters than as revision partners: I feed them a paragraph of my own and ask how it would read with different habits of syntax and imagery, or how the same scene would sound if its verbal surface were retooled. What unsettles me is how readily such a tool detaches style from what is being said. Style becomes a transferable surface — a bundle of sentence rhythms, word choices, and mannerisms that can be lifted off one work's concerns and draped over arbitrary material, while the material's underlying structure stays largely fixed.

This struck me as a live, contemporary version of something Adorno and Horkheimer describe in their account of the culture industry. On my understanding of their view, the culture industry offers consumers what they call pseudo-individuality: products are differentiated on the surface — different plots, voices, faces, twists — but this differentiation is manufactured on top of an underlying schema that remains standardized, so that apparent novelty never disturbs the administered sameness of the whole. Style, in their picture, ceases to be the way a particular experience takes form and becomes an interchangeable stylization that can be applied to any content while concealing the absence of real aesthetic autonomy. The parallel I keep seeing is structural: in both cases, local variation is real but is generated within a fixed global invariance — the standardized commodity schema on one side, the learned distribution of the model on the other. The consumer or user encounters the surface difference; the invariant level stays hidden. The obvious disanalogy is that culture-industry standardization is administered by concentrated economic power from outside the consumer, whereas a language model is tool-like, and an individual author can reject or override its suggestions. I cannot tell whether that disanalogy breaks the parallel or merely relocates the pressure.
