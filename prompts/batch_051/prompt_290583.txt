In Plato's Euthyphro, 12a (Perseus edition), Socrates asks: "Or is all which is holy right, and not all which is right holy, but part of it holy and part something else?"

Consider an asymmetry that seems to descend from this partition. In ordinary moral judgment, many people treat the violation of a distinctively holy, sacred, or religious obligation as a special kind of wrong—worse, or marked by a kind of pollution or sacrilege—even when the secular harm is small, while they do not give any parallel extra weight to the positive performance of a distinctively holy act: fulfilling a religious vow is good, but not praised the way the broken vow is condemned. So the "holy" sub-region of the right appears to bear an added weight only on its negative side. Is anything in the Socratic claim that the holy is one part of the right able to justify this asymmetry, or should we treat failures of that part like any other failure of justice and drop the special stigma? I want the strongest candidate justification you can find, tested against nearby cases (oath-breaking versus lying, sacrilege versus vandalism, ritual neglect versus broken promise), and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. Answer in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
