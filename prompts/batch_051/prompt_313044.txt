I've been going round in circles on something I've started calling the ripple problem, and I want a thought experiment built to order rather than another explanation of it. The worry is this: whenever we judge an action by its consequences, the consequences we can actually see are only a tiny slice of the whole, because everything sets off longer chains (who meets who, what gets built or not built later, what tiny social shift starts from it) that are genuinely unknowable and could be huge in either direction. If you take that seriously you seem to end up unable to choose anything; if you dont, you seem to be ignoring half of what judging-by-consequences says matters.

I want a single decision problem, invented by you, where two rules I've been toying with give opposite verdicts.

Rule 1, the sight-line rule: when the far-downstream effects are unknowable, decide by the best estimate of the consequences you can actually see, and dont let the unknowable stuff change your act.

Rule 2, the ripple rule: when the far-downstream effects of an act are genuinely unknowable and could be large in either direction, the visible consequences don't settle the choice. You then have to fall back on a tie-breaker, which I'll set as: prefer the option whose visible harms are easier to undo if it turns out badly.

The case must obey these constraints: a perfectly ordinary setting (no perfect predictors, no exotic science, no demons or oracles), payoffs a normal person could face, and neither option should be grossly harmful on its face. The visible balance should favour Rule 1's choice, but not by so much that Rule 2's worry looks mad. And the long-ripple worry should come from the structure of the two options—one having branchy, irreversible downstream effects while the other mostly stays local—not just from you asserting that one option might have unknown good effects. Also please don't rig the set-up by saying "Rule 2 picks option B"; the facts of the scenario should make the rules land where they land.
