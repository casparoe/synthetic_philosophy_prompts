a mate in my behavioural ecology group sent this round last week and none of us can pin down the dodgy step. His line is that the handicap principle isn't one mechanism among several for keeping signals honest – it's the only one, and so-called "index" signals are just handicaps where we haven't measured the costs yet. The conclusion feels absurdly strong, but I can't say why.

(1) A signal is honest only if it pays for signallers who actually possess the advertised quality and doesn't pay (or pays less) for signallers who lack it.

(2) Whether a signal pays for a sender is just the benefit to the sender of the receiver's response, minus the cost to the sender of producing the signal.

(3) But the receiver responds to the signal, not to the sender: the same signal, the same response. If the response is the same, the benefit that response confers is the same too.

(4) Therefore, whenever honest and dishonest senders differ in whether the signal pays, the difference must come entirely from the cost side – the signal has to be more expensive for low-quality senders to produce. And that's the handicap principle.

(5) Now take a signal people call an "index": honest because it physically can't be faked, e.g. a frog whose call pitch tracks body size, so a small frog simply cannot produce the low-pitched call of a big frog. Since the low-quality sender never produces the signal, (1) is trivially met, and there is no differential cost of production.

(6) If the signal is just a by-product of having the quality, producing it doesn't cost the honest signaller anything over and above having the quality. So the cost side isn't doing the separating work either.

(7) So either index signals are honest in virtue of something other than cost – which contradicts (4) – or they aren't honest in the relevant sense at all.

(8) Hence every honest signal is a handicap, and "index" is just a label for cases where we've failed to add up the costs.

I want to reject (8), and I'd rather keep the thought that constraint and cost are genuinely different honesty mechanisms. But I can't see whether the trouble is in (3), in (6), or somewhere else, and I don't know what I'd be giving up elsewhere. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it do to how we should think about indices and the handicap principle? Please keep the answer under 600 words.
