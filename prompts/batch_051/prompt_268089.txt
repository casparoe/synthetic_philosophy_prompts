I'm a novelist, and I'm working on a book about how a society remembers—or doesn't remember—its own violence. While researching, I've been digging into the charts and datasets people cite when they claim the world is becoming less violent, and there's something about how those numbers get counted that keeps bugging me.

As I understand the practice, the big datasets behind those charts count deaths from direct violence: soldiers killed in combat, civilians killed in bombings or massacres, that kind of thing. They don't count the people who die a year later because a war destroyed the hospitals, or because cholera spread through a refugee camp, or because fields were burned and a famine followed. Even when everyone could see those deaths coming a long way off. The datasets also have a floor: below some minimum number of direct deaths in a year, a conflict simply doesn't enter the tally. And the headline charts are almost always per capita—deaths per hundred thousand—rather than raw numbers of people killed.

What picture of violence does that practice presuppose? It seems to treat violence as something that has to be direct and intentional, or at least something close to it, and it seems to treat the rate at which people die as the thing that matters. But I can't tell whether the practice is genuinely committed to those views, or whether it's just a pragmatic choice about what's easy to count. Do its parts even presuppose consistant things—excluding indirect deaths but then switching to per-capita rates? And what would have to change if someone thought violence could include a foreseeable harm you set in motion without aiming at it, like the famine that follows a scorched-earth campaign? I'm not asking for a moral verdict so much as for the implicit theory the counting practice rests on, and where that practice is philosophically underdetermined.
