I recently encountered the following argument about generics — sentences like "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria." The argument is meant to show that generics cannot both describe kinds and guide reasoning about particular members of those kinds. Each step seems plausible to me, but the conclusion is stronger than I am willing to accept, so I want a careful diagnosis: does the argument go through, and if not, exactly which step fails and why?

(1) A generic of the form "Ks are F" is acceptable only if the Ks that are not F form a principled, independently identifiable subclass of K, the way male ducks and ducklings form the principled exceptions to "ducks lay eggs." (2) To accept a generic is to accept a default inference rule: if all one knows about x is that it is a K, one may proceed as if x is F, retracting only upon learning that x falls into the excepted subclass. (3) So, from "ducks lay eggs," one may proceed as if an arbitrary duck lays eggs, since the exceptions can be separated off. (4) But the exceptions to "mosquitoes carry malaria" cannot be separated off in the same way: at any given moment, only a small fraction of mosquitoes in an endemic area actually carry the parasite, and the non-carriers include males, non-vector species, and uninfected females of vector species — the exceptions are most of the kind, not a neat subclass. (5) By parity of reasoning, one should then either reject "mosquitoes carry malaria" or refuse to treat an arbitrary mosquito as a carrier. (6) Yet "mosquitoes carry malaria" is a standard example of a true generic, and public-health reasoning appears to rely on it. (7) Therefore the principled-exception account cannot handle both paradigm cases without splitting generics into two unrelated kinds of truth; generics either lack stable truth conditions or cannot guide inference to particular individuals.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step fails, and is the failure a falsity in that step or a shift in the sense of one of its key terms? If the argument is sound, defend the conclusion that generics have no single kind of truth condition.
