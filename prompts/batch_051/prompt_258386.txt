I spent 35 years as a physical chemist, and since I retired I've been reading philosophy of biology. I keep tripping over heritability. A friend of mine — he's an engineer, not a biologist, but he reads a lot of popular science — summarized a behavioral genetics review for me like this:

'Heritability is the proportion of variation in a trait that's caused by genes. Twin studies put the heritability of intelligence around .5 to .8, so within a population, genes are the main reason people differ in IQ. Because identical twins control for shared environment, those numbers are about real genetic effects, not confounds. That gives us two practical conclusions. First, environmental interventions can only close the non-genetic share of the variation — somewhere between twenty and fifty percent is the ceiling. Second, if the causes of within-group differences are mostly genetic, then average differences between groups are probably at least partly genetic too, since its the same trait and the same causes.'

I know enough statistics to be suspicious — it sounds like he's sliding from variance decomposition to causes, and maybe from causes to ceilings — but I cant tell exactly which moves are the bad ones, or whether they're all fine. Which parts of this summary would a careful behavioral geneticist accept? Which parts conflate claims the field keeps separate? And do the two practical conclusions survive once the corrections are made? Is the summary fair?
