I'm on the board of a small history museum in a town that grew up around a mining operation. The museum is redoing its permanent exhibit, and the draft panel on the 1890s says the company "committed genocide" against the immigrant workers it brought over under contract. The men were housed in a company camp, worked in dangerous conditions, and after the cave-in and the fever outbreak the company buried the dead in unmarked graves, and then the town got built over part of that ground. We vote on the exhibit wording next month, and the board is split. One group says genocide is the only honest word and anything softer is scrubbing the record. Another group says the word has a specific legal meaning — roughly, intent to destroy a group as such — and the surviving records show neglect and greed but not that kind of intent, so using it would be factually wrong, would get us sued, and would loose our funding from the mining company's foundation. A third group thinks the legal definition is itself part of the problem, since it lets the institutions in power set the bar for what can be named. None of us are lawyers or historians, so we need something we can actually act on. Please draft the short decision memo I would circulate before the vote: recommend a concrete label for the panel (or a concrete way of handling the contested word in the exhibit), give reasons the rest of the board can use, and take the strongest argument on the losing side seriously rather than brushing it off. Keep it under two pages, and end with the exact paragraph or label you think we should put on the panel if we follow your recommendation.
