In the community around a persistent building game I play, two long-time players have been arguing since a large settlement was destroyed, and we have been asked to break the tie. For context: the game runs on a single shared world made of millions of individually tracked blocks; players can build and terraform together, and settlements often function only as public gathering places, with no mechanical bonus. Last month a griefer found an exploit that let them destroy another player's settlement in a way the server could not undo without rolling back everyone's progress by six weeks. The settlement had been built over eight months by a player named Vela, who has since left the game. Mara and Senn, the two disputants, agreed to frame the question as whether the destruction constituted a real harm to Vela.

The exchange has gone roughly as follows. Mara: "Vela gave up eight months of scarce time and attention to build something, and someone deliberately annihilated it. If a neighbor had burned down a wooden garden she had spent eight months on, no one would hesitate to call that a real harm; the fact that this was made of server data rather than lumber is beside the point." Senn: "You are counting the felt loss as the loss itself. People are genuinely distraught when a beloved character dies in a novel, but we do not say something real was taken from them. A harm has to be an event in the world, and a pattern of bits is a description of a settlement, not a settlement." Mara: "The bits are in the world. The settlement was a stable, publicly visible pattern that persisted on a server, the game's rules marked those blocks as Vela's property, and the exploit destroyed a state of a real machine, setting back an interest she was entitled to have protected. A real change with real causes and effects is what most people mean by a real harm." Senn: "Now you have slipped from 'the object is real' to 'she had an interest.' I grant that her interest was real, but a representation is not the thing it represents. Deleting the last copy of a novel one wrote with someone destroys a text, not a house, however upsetting that is; the only non-representational thing changed here is Vela's emotional state, and she chose to attach it to a fiction." Mara: "Then account for the market. The settlement could have been sold for real money through the game's deed and trading system. An entity that can be irreversibly lost, that others will pay real currency for, and that a community treats as transferable property is real in the only sense that matters for harm; a destroyed bank account is also just an arrangement of records, but a depositor has plainly lost something real." Senn: "A market shows that the community has built a valuable practice around the settlement, not that the settlement is an object in any deeper sense. A person can be genuinely wronged and even owed compensation by losing standing in a club whose rules are pure convention, or by a broken promise, without any object being taken from them. So Vela may well have been wronged, but we should not say a real object was destroyed."

I keep going back and forth, and the principals have asked for an outside adjudication rather than another slogan. Please work through the disagreement move by move: for each turn, say who is right about which specific sub-claim and where that person's reasoning overreaches or fails; then say what both of them are missing; and finally evaluate whether their implicit framing — treating "was a real object destroyed?" as the test for "was Vela really harmed?" — is the right test, or whether the question should have been set up differently.
