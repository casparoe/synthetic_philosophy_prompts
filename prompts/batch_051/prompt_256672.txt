Write the joint statement that a committed Agency Purist and a committed Content Integrationist in the aesthetics of games could both sign after a year-long adversarial collaboration. The question dividing them is this: when a game is evaluated as art, should the primary object of appreciation be the designed structure of practical agency — the way the game shapes what the player tries, attends to, risks, and values — or the representational content the game puts the player in contact with — its fiction, characters, themes, and the meanings of its mechanics as symbols?

Define the positions as follows. The Agency Purist holds that games are, at bottom, best appreciated as designed structures of agency: the distinctive aesthetic achievement of a game is the quality of the practical stance it lets a player inhabit, and the aesthetic value of a decision lies in the felt texture of deciding, striving, and failing rather than in what the decision depicts or communicates. The Purist points to abstract and near-abstract works — a tightly designed roguelike, a fighting game, a minimalist puzzle game, a speedrun of a familiar title — where the aesthetic payoff seems to survive the removal of almost all fiction. The Content Integrationist holds that agency is a vehicle rather than the point: interactivity is a representational device, comparable to prosody in poetry or editing in film, and a game's value as art lies in what its mechanics and choices represent, express, or argue. The Integrationist points to walking simulators, visual novels, and games whose mechanics function as metaphors or arguments, where removing the content would leave something engaging but not a work of art in the full sense.

Produce the document such a collaboration would yield, with these sections in this order and roughly these lengths:

1. Agreed Claims (about 350 words). A numbered list of substantive claims both camps can sign as worded. They should be neither vacuous nor slanted, and should include claims about what each side grants about the other's central examples, about the difference between a game and a mere toy or puzzle, about the role of failure and repetition, and about what would count as a failed game on either view.

2. The Disagreement, Precisely Stated (about 400 words). A table stating the residual disagreement on three or four sub-questions. Give the Agency Purist's and the Content Integrationist's positions on each, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own view, and add a short note on the formulations each side rejects as a caricature.

3. Cruxes (about 300 words). The sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. For each crux, state which camp it would move and why the other camp currently believes the evidence favors it.

4. Cases Both Sides Accept as Bearing on the Cruxes (about 400 words). For each crux, specify a concrete case or design contrast — real or constructed — that both camps agree would bear on it, and record in advance each side's prediction about what the case shows and what result would cause that side to revise.

5. What Is Merely Verbal (about 150 words). Identify any part of the dispute that, after the collaboration, both sides agree was about terminology rather than substance, and state the terminological fix each side accepts.

Write in American English, as a formal document the two collaborators could put their names to. Do not appeal to authority or citations; the arguments must stand on their own.
