One of my roommates took a game theory class this semester and came home convinced that our co-op's treasurer election next week is basically going to be an exercise in strategic lying. We vote by ranked ballots, and there are three candidates, so he says the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem applies directly. Last night he wrote the argument on the kitchen whiteboard:

(1) Gibbard–Satterthwaite says: take any ranked voting method with at least three candidates that allows every possible strict ranking, that can elect any candidate under some pattern of votes, and that is not a dictatorship (no single person's top choice wins no matter how everyone else votes). Then the method is manipulable — meaning there is some pattern of sincere rankings at which a voter gets a better result by submitting a dishonest ranking than by submitting an honest one. (2) Our ranked-ballot method with these three candidates satisfies all the conditions: every strict ranking is allowed, each candidate could win under some pattern of votes, and no one's ballot dictates the result in that sense. (3) So, by the theorem, for our method there is some pattern of rankings where somebody has a profitable lie. (4) Whatever rankings people actually hand in next week are just one of the patterns the theorem ranges over. (5) Therefore, in next week's actual election, at least one voter will have a profitable lie — a ballot that differs from their sincere ranking and produces an outcome they strictly prefer.

He says step-by-step it's airtight, and I don't know enough to push back. But it seems weird that a theorem could guarantee a particular voter in our specific election has a strategic move, without knowing anything about how our members actually rank the candidates. Is the argument valid? If not, point to the exact place the inference breaks down, and say what we'd need to know to figure out whether someone actually has a profitable misreport next week.
