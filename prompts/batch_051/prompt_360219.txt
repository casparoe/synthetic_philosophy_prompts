I have been arguing with a colleague in natural language processing about whether a statistical machine translation system can be said to understand the sentences it translates. He holds that modern systems must grasp language at some level, since they cope with word order, idiom, and context in ways that would be extremely improbable if they were only matching surface forms. I pushed back with the skeptical line associated with Hofstadter, that statistical translation is shallow and that fluency is not comprehension; his reply was that I was confusing the training objective with the competence the trained system actually acquires. That forced me to reconstruct the skeptical argument more carefully. The version I ended up with runs as follows.

(1) A standard neural machine translation system is trained, to a first approximation, on a single kind of signal: the probability of a target-language string conditional on a source-language string. Its parameters are updated only to make observed translation pairs more likely and competing strings less likely. (2) So anything the trained system internally represents, it represents only because, and only insofar as, that representation improved its string-probability estimates. (3) A representation whose existence and content are explained solely by its usefulness for improving string-probability estimates can carry at most statistical information about the distribution of strings. (4) What a sentence says is not exhausted by statistical information about the distribution of strings: two sentences with no shared surface structure can say the same thing, and the same sentence can say different things in different contexts. (5) Understanding a sentence requires representing what it says, not merely statistical information about its form. (6) Therefore a statistical machine translation system does not understand the sentences it translates.

This argument strikes me as too strong, and my NLP colleague would certainly reject it, but I cannot pin down exactly where it derails. I suspect the move from (2) to (3), or perhaps (3) itself, is smuggling in a principle I have not yet stated. Is the argument valid? If it is not, say precisely which step fails and state the hidden premise or distinction that would be needed to repair that step; if it is valid, identify the premise a defender of statistical translation should deny, and give the strongest version of their denial.
