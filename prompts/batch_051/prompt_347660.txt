A recurring move in debates over frontier AI is to use the atomic bomb as the master analogy and to conclude that secrecy about dangerous capabilities is hopeless because the underlying science is public. I would like you to argue for a less common position: that the Franck Report's answer to the secrecy proposal actually points somewhere more precise—namely, that the decisive layer for AI security is not the public fundamentals but the hard-to-replicate engineering and operational procedures, such as training recipes, data pipelines, evaluation methods, and the organizational know-how that makes evaluations meaningful—and that the report therefore supports strict protection at that layer rather than resignation to inevitable capability diffusion. Build the argument around this passage, from the Report of the Committee on Political and Social Problems, Manhattan Project "Metallurgical Laboratory," University of Chicago, June 11, 1945, usually called the Franck Report. The report has just considered the suggestion that America could avoid an arms race by keeping its discoveries secret:

"It could be suggested that the danger of destruction by nuclear weapons can be prevented - at least as far as this country is concerned - by keeping our discoveries secret for an indefinite time, or by developing our nucleonic armaments at such a pace that no other nations would think of attacking us from fear of overwhelming retaliation.

The answer to the first suggestion is that although we undoubtedly are at present ahead of the rest of the world in this field, the fundamental facts of nuclear power are a subject of common knowledge. British scientists know as much as we do about the basic wartime progress of nucleonics - with the exception of specific processes used in our engineering developments - and the background of French nuclear physicists plus their occasional contact with our Projects, will enable them to catch up rapidly, at least as far as basic scientific facts are concerned."

Take seriously the strongest objection: modern AI may differ from 1945 nuclear physics precisely because its fundamentals are even more thoroughly public, and the worldwide diffusion of machine-learning talent, compute access, and open-weight models might threaten to make even the engineering layer leak faster than the report predicts. When you respond, please keep separate what looks settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study these issues, and do not present a contested reading of the Franck Report, or a contested empirical claim about AI diffusion, as if it were the consensus.
