I review several dozen glyph submissions a month at a digital type foundry that licenses fonts for text and display use. A recurring dispute is whether a stylized glyph should be accepted as an instance of the letter it ships under — an a, say — or has drifted so far from that category that labeling it a misleads readers and breaks search, sorting, and accessibility tools; rejecting too much, however, flattens expressive type design. Please write a reusable decision procedure my reviewers can follow to decide whether a submitted glyph is still an instance of its target letter, using a as the running example. The reviewer has no graduate training in type design and about fifteen minutes per glyph; a senior type designer gives the final sign-off, but the procedure should settle what it can before any hand-off. Ground the procedure in the actual structure of the problem — what seems to preserve letter identity across styles, the difference between recovering a letter in reading and matching a remembered shape, and which distortions are stylistic and which destroy category membership — not in type-design folklore. Then run the procedure on three submissions: a geometric sans a drawn as a circle with a short vertical stem on the right; an italic a shaped as a single closed bowl with no exit stroke; and a stencil a whose bowl is reduced to two disconnected horizontal bars joined by a diagonal. Make at least one case come out in a way the foundry would not like, flag every step that is a judgment call dressed up as a test, and say where the procedure will predictably misfire.
