I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of rational choice, with twelve to fifteen students and a single 90-minute session. Design an in-class activity that makes participants feel the force of Quinn's self-torturer and the associated idea that rational preferences can fail to be transitive: a chain of individually reasonable one-step choices can land a person in a state they would refuse if it were offered as a single package. The point should emerge from what the students do, not from a lecture, and they should not hear the name self-torturer or any theoretical framing until the debrief.

The activity must preserve the structural core of the puzzle. There is an ordered sequence of states, running from a comfortable starting point to an endpoint that students would regard as clearly worse in some respect, even after accounting for all the rewards accumulated along the way. For each adjacent pair of states, the step upward must be framed so that, considered on its own, most students find it worth accepting: the added burden is small and the reward is attractive. The burden must be mild, safe, reversible, and suited to a classroom — tedious transcription, an awkward sustained posture, a collectively imposed rule, or something of that kind rather than pain, shock, temperature, or social humiliation. No real money; use points exchangeable for modest prizes. Allow opt-outs, but design the game so that the interesting thing to watch is how many students who do opt in keep stepping upward.

A crucial element is a package check. At some stage, students must be confronted with the bundle they have effectively accepted — their current state plus the rewards they have accumulated — offered as a one-time choice from the starting point. Arrange the timing so that the contrast between the stepwise path and the package verdict becomes vivid and recordable, ideally within the same students rather than across different people.

Give the facilitator a complete design: materials, rules, a payoff schedule, a timeline for 90 minutes, a script for the debrief, and clear instructions about what must not be said until the debrief. The debrief should map the moments of the activity onto the parts of the idea — the local attractiveness of each step, the non-transitivity of the relation of being only slightly worse than, the rejection of the package, and the question of where someone who wanted to resist the conclusion would have to stop and at what cost. It should also distinguish the self-torturer structure from an ordinary cycle of preferences that a money pump could exploit, if that distinction helps, but without turning into a lecture.

Commit to one specific design rather than surveying options. Defend the main parameter choices: the particular burden, the step size, the reward schedule, and when the package check happens. Where a choice is genuinely a judgement call, say so briefly and make it anyway. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, and finish with an honest note about which features of Quinn's puzzle the activity cannot reproduce — for example, features tied to the private phenomenology of pain or to the claim that the stepwise choices are genuinely rational rather than merely tempting.

Please write the answer in British English.
