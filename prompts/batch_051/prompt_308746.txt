I spent thirty years in a lab where the rule was: if you can't say what measurement would settle a question, you don't have a question yet. Now that I'm retired and reading philosophy of mind, I keep running into a claim I cant figure out how to operationalize. Some philosophers call themselves illusionists and say that phenomenal consciousness — the felt redness of a tomato, the ache of a stubbed toe, all of it — is an introspective illusion. There are no private qualitative properties, on their view; there are just cognitive states that represent us as having them, and we systematically mistake that representation for a real something. I'm not asking whether this is true. I'm asking whether anything could test it.

Here's the best I can do. If phenomenal properties aren't real but introspective reports of them are produced by some mechanism, then a system could in principle generate exactly the reports I generate without any phenomenal properties. So one test would be to find or build a system that grounds its introspective reports in the same sort of behavioral and neural processes we do, but that we have independent reason to think lacks phenomenality, and then see whether we're still tempted to attribute qualia to it. But I can immediately see the hole: any independent reason would itself be behavioral or functional, and the illusionist and the realist will just disagree about what could count as independent evidence. I also know about change blindness, blindsight, and split-brain confabulations, where people seem plain wrong about their own mental lives, and I've seen these cited as evidence that introspection is a lot less transparent than it feels. But a realist can just say introspection is occasionally wrong, not that the whole stream is fabricated.

So here's my question. Design the best concrete test you can of the claim that phenomenal properties are an introspective illusion rather than something real that introspection sometimes misreports. Be specific about the setup, the measurement, and what pattern of results would push you toward illusionism or toward realism. Then say what a result in either direction would and would not settle: which part of the illusionist claim a test can actually reach, and which part has to remain a matter of interpretation no matter how the data come out.
