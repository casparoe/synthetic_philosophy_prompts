A friend laid this out and I can't tell whether its sound or where it slips.

(1) To forgive someone for a wrong is to give up your resentment toward them for that wrong.
(2) To give up your resentment toward someone for a wrong, you have to judge that the wrong no longer gives you any reason to resent them — resentment is just the attitude of taking a wrong to be a live reason to hold something against the person.
(3) So forgiving someone requires judging that the wrong no longer gives you any reason to resent them.
(4) If a wrong does give you reason to resent someone, then no obligation can require you to judge that it doesn't; that would be a requirement to get the matter wrong.
(5) If a wrong gives you no reason to resent someone, then in giving up your resentment you're not forgiving anything, you're just correcting a mistake.
(6) So an obligation to forgive no matter what the offender does would require either judging something false (in cases where the wrong gives reason for resentment), or doing something that isn't forgiveness (in cases where it doesn't).
(7) Therefore unconditional forgiveness can't be obligatory.

I keep feeling the problem is in (2), but I can't make the objection precise — isn't ceasing to resent someone exactly ceasing to see what they did as a reason to resent them? Tell me which step, if any, fails, and give the corrected version of that step.
