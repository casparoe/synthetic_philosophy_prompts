I'm a PhD student in decision theory and I've been TAing a probability-for-philosophers course, which has mostly been fine, but last week we hit the St. Petersburg game and a student asked why we don't just alternate the signs of the payoffs to tame the infinite expectation. I ended up sketching the Pasadena game on the board and now I can't shake the feeling that I've proved to much. Here's the argument I keep running over, cleaned up:

(1) A gamble's fair price is its expected monetary value. (2) Expected value is the probability-weighted sum of the individual outcomes. (3) In the Pasadena game, a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads; if the first heads is on toss n, the payoff is (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n / n dollars, where a negative payoff means you pay out. So the contribution of outcome n is (1/2^n) × [(−1)^(n−1) · 2^n / n] = (−1)^(n−1) / n. (4) In the natural order, these contributions sum to 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ... , which converges to ln 2, so the game seems worth ln 2 dollars. (5) But that series is only conditionally convergent, and any conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to sum to any real number at all, or to diverge. (6) There's no privileged order in which the outcomes 'should' be summed: writing them as n = 1, 2, 3, ... is just a convention. (7) Therefore the game has no determinate expected value and hence no determinate fair price, even though some finite price will look defensible relative to some ordering or other.

I want to know whether this argument is valid, and if not, exactly which step fails and on what reading. I half-suspect the trick is in (2) or (6), but when I try to fix those steps I end up either just accepting the conclusion or inventing ad hoc patches I don't actually believe. Tell me what the clean resolution is, and also say what would change your mind: which consideration would push you to the opposite verdict about which step goes wrong, or where in your own reasoning you're least confident.
