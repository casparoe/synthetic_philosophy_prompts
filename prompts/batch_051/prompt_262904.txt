I would like you to act as an expert on classical Daoist philosophy and on the rhetoric of performance, and to think carefully before you write. I am preparing the opening remarks for the annual banquet of a chamber music institute where I teach. The audience will be roughly half musicians and teachers—several of whom know the idea of wu-wei, the Daoist ideal of effortless action that is nevertheless highly skilled, and the paradox that deliberately trying to be effortless tends to make one more self-conscious and less fluid—and half parents, donors, and spouses who have never encountered the term. Last night I had a long after-dinner argument with a friend about exactly this. Her view is that a speech on wu-wei is self-defeating from the outset: composing a polished address about not forcing is already a form of forcing, and the only honest options are to tell one story and sit down, or not to give the speech at all. My view is that the speech can still work, but only if the idea does structural work rather than being preached: it should make the audience feel and use the distinction between effort and effortfulness, without once instructing them to relax, stop trying, or trust the process.

The speech should run about seven minutes, welcome the new students and their families at the start of the season, and give the performers something they can actually carry into their practice rooms. It must land for the faculty members who will recognize the philosophical issue, including the problem that any teaching of effortlessness borders on paradox, while remaining fully intelligible and warm for the families. It should avoid the standard moves—the set-piece ox-carving parable trotted out as if it settles the matter, or a string of short, faux-profound sentences about letting go. I would also like the prose to model some of what it describes: at ease without performing ease, precise without stiffness. Please start by brainstorming two or three rhetorical strategies for satisfying these constraints (for instance, making the paradox itself the subject, shaping the speech around a single anecdote, or working by analogy from tuning and breathing), then say briefly which strategy you chose and why, and then write the full speech for me.
