I'm working through Augustine's *On Lying*, and there's a place where he seems to contradict himself. Early in the work he sets jokes aside, saying they have never been accounted lies because the joker's tone and manner make evident that he does not mean to deceive, even if the words are false. But a little later he defines lying this way: "Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind." By that definition, a joke seems to be a lie: the joker has one thing in his mind and utters another. How is Augustine entitled to exclude jokes? Is he tacitly packing an intent-to-deceive condition into the phrase "has one thing in his mind and utters another," even though that is not what the words say? Or is his considered view that jokes are lies too, and the earlier exclusion was only a provisional concession he later abandons? I'm trying to determine whether Augustine's definition of lying is best read as purely about insincerity—saying something other than what one believes—or as about the intention to deceive.
