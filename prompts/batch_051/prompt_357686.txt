I've ended up defending a view I'm not entirely comfortable with, and I want to know what would actually move me off it. The view: elegance—roughly simplicity, symmetry, unifying power, the sense that a theory is "beautiful"—is at least weak evidence for the truth of a scientific or mathematical claim. Not that it's decisive, and not that ugly theories are always wrong; just that, all else equal, if two theories fit the data about equally well, the more elegant one is more likely to be true, and maybe elegance even licenses continuing to work on a theory long after the data has stopped cooperating. I keep arguing about this with a biologist friend who points out that evolution produces kludgy, historically contingent systems that no sane engineer would design, and she thinks aesthetic criteria are epistemically worthless in biology. I don't buy her argument as stated, but it's made me realize I can't cleanly say what would count against my own position.

So treat the view as the object of inquiry. What would have to be true for elegance to not count as evidence, or to be systematically misleading? I want defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward: historical cases, underdetermination arguments, results in model selection or machine learning, evolutionary debunking of the aesthetic sense itself—whatever you think actually bites—including what a committed defender of the view would themselves concede counts against it rather than only what critics say. I also want an honest verdict on whether the view risks anything at all or has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that's a problem for a claim of this kind. I'm most interested in empirical science, but if you think the mathematical case changes the picture, say a bit about that too. If I'm honest about myself, the thing most likely to move me is a concrete case where the elegant option was preferred for aesthetic reasons and turned out wrong in an instructive way; but say whether even that really cuts against the weak version of the view.
