A student in my metaphysics seminar stopped me after class with the following argument, and I couldn't find the flaw on the spot. I've been chewing on it since.

(1) If the gardener's failure to water the orchid caused it to die, then omissions are sometimes causes.
(2) An omission is just the absence of an event. The gardener's not-watering and a stranger's not-watering are both mere absences; as absences, they have no intrinsic features by which to tell one from the other.
(3) The only thing that could mark the gardener's absence as causally special is something normative — he was being paid, it was his job, we expected it. But normative facts about blame or obligation can't make a non-event into a cause.
(4) So if his omission caused the death, every failure to do any of the indefinitely many things that would have saved the orchid caused it on exactly the same footing.
(5) Then the orchid's death has indefinitely many causes, none of them more a cause than the others.
(6) That can't be right: causal talk is useful only if it filters, and if every non-event in creation counted as a cause, the notion would loose whatever edge it has.
(7) Therefore the gardener's omission didn't cause the death; and since nothing in the argument hangs on gardening, no omission ever causes anything.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? Tell me which step you reject, why it looked so natural, and what each exit costs. For what it's worth, I'd rather keep (1) and (6) than (2), but I can't yet say why that isn't just wishful thinking.
