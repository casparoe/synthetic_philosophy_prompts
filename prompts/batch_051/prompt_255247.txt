Take the Bayesian reconstruction often used to steelman Hume's argument about believing miracle reports. In the canonical version, a single witness W reports that a miracle M occurred. The agent has prior credence p = 10^-6 in M, justified by the uniform-experience reasoning Hume invokes. Suppose W's reliability is fixed by a true-positive rate of 0.8 and a false-positive rate of 0.01: P(W reports M | M) = 0.8 and P(W reports M | ¬M) = 0.01. Run the posterior calculation and verify that the report leaves the agent's credence very low.

Now change one feature: n witnesses each report M, all with the same two error rates, and the reports are conditionally independent given M and given ¬M. Write down the posterior odds after n reports, and compute the smallest n at which the posterior first exceeds 0.5 and the smallest n at which it first exceeds 0.99. Then explain how the analysis changes under three further modifications. (a) The reports are not independent because every witness is repeating one original rumor whose false-positive rate is still 0.01, so the reports count as one effective independent report; how does the posterior behave as n grows? (b) The prior over M is exactly zero rather than merely very small; what happens for any finite n, and which reading of Hume does this formalize? (c) The reported fact is not a law-of-nature violation but an unprecedented natural event, such as ice reported to someone who has never encountered freezing; with a prior of 0.05, compute the posterior after a single report and contrast it with the original case. Conclude by saying whether Hume's conclusion survives the addition of genuinely independent witnesses, and which feature of the reconstruction is doing the real work.
