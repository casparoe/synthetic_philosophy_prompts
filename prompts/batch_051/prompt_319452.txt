I am an undergraduate taking a seminar on responsibility and distributive justice, and I am trying to work out what set of commitments a familiar practice actually rests on.

Where I live, private health insurers are permitted to charge tobacco users a recurring premium surcharge, and they offer discounts for enrolling in wellness programs and completing health screenings. But those same insurers are barred from charging higher premiums to people with a genetic condition, a congenital disability, or another medical risk that arose through no choice of the customer. At the same time, once a policyholder becomes sick, the cost-sharing rules do not distinguish among causes: a smoker's lung cancer and a never-smoker's lung cancer of unknown origin are covered on exactly the same terms, and no one is billed later for a disease they could have avoided.

On its face, the first part of this practice seems to presuppose a distinction between disadvantages a person chose and disadvantages that merely happened to them — between option luck and brute luck, to use our seminar's vocabulary — and to say: you may be made to pay for the first but not the second, while health care itself is kept cause-blind. I want to know whether that really is the theory the practice commits us to, and whether the commitment is even coherent.

Here is what worries me most. Smoking is a strange vehicle for the chosen/unchosen distinction. Whether someone starts smoking as a teenager, and how excruciating they find quitting, is itself predicted by unchosen facts: the neighborhood they grew up in, whether their parents smoked, and genetic variants that affect nicotine metabolism. If the practice is going to penalize a chosen risk, it needs some principled way to isolate the voluntary part of the behavior; it is not obvious that there is one. But the practice also seems underdetermined in the other direction: a purely welfarist rationale would support tobacco surcharges simply because they deter smoking, and a purely anti-discrimination rationale could explain the ban on genetic rating without any view about who deserves what.

So, concretely: reconstruct the theory of responsibility and fairness that best makes sense of this set of policies. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, and note where it is philosophically underdetermined. Is the apparent commitment to the chosen/unchosen distinction coherent, given how badly smoking blurs that line? And what would have to change — in insurance rating, in public health, or in our broader practice of holding people financially responsible for their health — if we took the distinction seriously all the way down, or if we gave it up entirely?
