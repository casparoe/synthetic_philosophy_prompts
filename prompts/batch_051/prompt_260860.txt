I recently read a long-form magazine piece about moral philosophy and the hidden arithmetics of everyday harms, and it used a thought experiment I hadn't met before that I can't stop turning over. The author summarized it roughly like this. You must choose between two outcomes, with no third option. In the first, an unimaginably large number of people — the author wrote the number as 3^^^3; if you haven't met the notation, the important point is that it is larger than any number you could write out, and "astronomical" is a wild understatement — each gets a single dust speck in the eye, a small, momentary irritation, over in seconds and forgotten by evening. In the second, one person is tortured, severely and continuously, for fifty years. Nearly everyone, the author said, would judge that we ought to bring about the first outcome: torturing a person is something we refuse to do even to spare an enormous crowd from a trivial annoyance. But, the author continued, a dust speck is a small harm, not zero harm. When you sum that tiny harm across 3^^^3 people, the total suffering in the first outcome is vastly greater than the suffering of the torture in the second. Therefore, the argument runs, any view that tells us to minimize total suffering — any form of utilitarianism, and really any view that takes consequences seriously at all — is forced to say that the torture is the better outcome and that a conscientious agent ought to bring it about. Since that verdict is morally intolerable, the thought experiment is supposed to expose a deep flaw in aggregating tiny harms across many people; and the author drew the further conclusion that the whole consequentialist approach to ethics rests on this flawed sort of aggregation.

I keep coming back to this because the arithmetic seems hard to dispute, and yet the conclusion feels too fast. I'm inclined to think the article is being unfair to consequentialism somewhere, but I don't know enough to say whether the problem lies in how the case is set up, in the claim about what utilitarianism is committed to, or in the leap from a striking intuition to the rejection of aggregation — and I'm not even sure the intuitive verdict the author leans on is as nearly universal as the piece suggests. Could you give me an accuracy audit of this summary? Please go through it piece by piece and separate what a careful consequentialist would accept, what is a simplification or a conflation of distinct claims, and what is overclaimed or just wrong. In particular, does the final inference — from the tension between summation and intuition, to the rejection of aggregating small harms, and from there to the rejection of consequentialism as a whole — actually go through? And what are the main lines of response open to someone who wants to keep an aggregative approach to ethics without shrugging off torture?
