Over lunch a colleague from the law school was arguing that the whole debate about legal personhood for AI is overwrought. His line was: we already did this with corporations. A corporation is a legal person — it can own property, enter contracts, sue and be sued, and even claim some constitutional protections — despite not being a human being, and nobody thinks that's spooky anymore; its just a useful legal fiction for organizing liability and economic activity. So, he said, treating an AI as a legal person is the same move: another non-human "person" in the eyes of the law, created because it's useful, not because we've gone all metaphysical. I'd like an audit of that analogy. What actually maps from the corporate case to the AI case, where does the mapping break down, and which features of corporate personhood does the analogy quietly import that an AI might lack — like the fact that corporations have human beings behind them, with shares, residual claims, and fiduciary duties running through the whole structure? Are the disanalogies the sort that actually matter for his conclusion, or just noise? And does his bottom line — roughly, that AI legal personhood is a modest extension of existing law — survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If the analogy is bad, what would be a better one? Spare me the case law and citations; I want the conceptual mapping. And tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and which point you're least sure of.
