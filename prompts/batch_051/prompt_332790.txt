Recently I took over a safety model at work, and the central dispute has turned out to be philosophical rather than technical. On one side I find myself drawn to a principle — call it regularity — that a rational probability assignment should give positive probability to every possibility. The principle is compact and appealing: if probabilities are supposed to represent the space of possibilities, then a zero looks like a judgment that something cannot happen, and assigning zero to something that could happen looks like a mistake rather than a harmless rounding. It also acts as a decision procedure, since it rules out whole classes of assignments instead of leaving every disputed zero to case-by-case intuition.

On the other side I have a conviction I cannot dislodge: a continuous measurement such as the exact load on a structural component has a continuum of possible values, and a well-built continuous model assigns each exact value probability zero. I would not call an engineer irrational for using such a model; I would call the alternative unusable in practice. But I also do not want to accept the easy reply that the zero-probability outcomes are therefore impossible, because that seems to rewrite "possible" just to rescue the principle.

I am not asking which side is standard practice. I am asking what it would cost to give up either side, and whether there is a reading on which both survive. In particular: should I debunk the intuition that each of a continuum of exact values is genuinely possible, or amend the regularity principle, and what would each move require? Is the right resolution to distinguish senses of "possible" — logical, physical, epistemic — and hold that the principle governs only one of them? Would moving to infinitesimal probabilities, or relaxing countable additivity (the rule that the probability of a countable union of disjoint events is the sum of their probabilities), preserve the principle at an acceptable price? And if the principle has to be weakened or abandoned, what remains of the idea that probability is a measure of possibility?
