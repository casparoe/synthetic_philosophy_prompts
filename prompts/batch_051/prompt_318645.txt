I'm taking an epistemology seminar on testimony, and we've been working through the debate between reductionists and anti-reductionists. The reductionist position rests on a familiar argument: a hearer is justified in believing what a speaker says only if she already has enough independent evidence, from her own perception, memory, and induction, that the speaker's reports tend to track the facts; testimonial justification is therefore not a basic source of warrant but reduces to other sources. That argument was built for a world where testimony mostly came from identifiable people whom you could observe, question, and build up a track record about. Now suppose a large share of what an ordinary hearer encounters is anonymous, algorithmically curated text, some of it produced by systems that have no beliefs, no sincerity, and no individual track record the hearer could ever check. Does the reductionist argument survive this change? Which specific premise does the change put pressure on, and can the argument be repaired in a weaker form that still does the work in the new setting? If it cannot be repaired, say what the anti-reductionist default-trust view would need to assert about machine-generated or anonymous text in order to stay plausible, or whether some third option is forced. I'm interested in the fate of the argument itself, not in platform or technology policy.
