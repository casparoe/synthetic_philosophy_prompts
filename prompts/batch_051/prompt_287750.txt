A student in my critical thinking course gave an argument that seems to prove too much, and I've been unable to locate the exact step to push back on. The student wrote it out like this:

(1) You choose a metric M because it is correlated with the true target T you actually care about; if it weren't correlated, you'd have no reason to use it.
(2) Correlation is a claim about the range of cases you've seen so far, not about the whole space of possible behaviors.
(3) Once people are rewarded or punished on M, they start searching the space of possible behaviors for things that score high on M.
(4) As the search gets more thorough, it eventually reaches a behavior that scores very high on M but scores terribly or perversely on T. That's what I was taught Goodhart's law amounts to: a number that gets optimized stops acting as a faithful stand-in for the thing it was supposed to stand in for.
(5) So for any M that is not literally identical to T, sustained optimization of M will drive T down in the long run, no matter how good the correlation looked at the start.
(6) Therefore the only metric that is safe to optimize is one that is the target itself, but if you could write down the target as a measurable quantity you wouldn't have needed a proxy in the first place.
(7) Therefore any use of a metric — for grading, hiring, hospital quality, online content moderation, or giving a language model a reward signal — is eventually going to make the underlying thing worse.

I don't accept (7). Students who study for exams mostly learn more than they would with no exams at all, speed limits do seem to cut fatalities even though "safe driving" is much richer than "not exceeding the limit," and the fact that people game a metric doesn't prove the metric is a net negative compared to no metric. So something in (1)–(6) has to go, or one of the inferences doesn't go through. I'd like to keep Goodhart's law — I've watched too many metrics get gamed to just drop it — but I'd also like to keep the plain claim that some metrics are net useful. Tell me which step or inference I should give up, why it looked airtight even though is isnt, and what giving it up would cost someone who wanted to hang onto the student's conclusion.
