I sit on my university's academic-integrity committee. We must decide whether to uphold a plagiarism charge whose only evidence is the testimony of one student: she says she watched the accused submit a purchased essay, and that he admitted it to her the next day. He denies it; no metadata, draft history, or independent witness corroborates either account, and the lone upload timestamp from the accused's own account is consistent with both stories. The committee has split along roughly reductionist and anti-reductionist lines about testimony. One faction holds that, without positive evidence of this witness's reliability and of the reliability of this kind of report, her say-so cannot ground a finding. The other holds that sincere, apparently competent testimony should be accepted by default unless particular reasons for doubt arise, and that a blanket denial by the accused is not by itself such a reason. A further wrinkle I'd like handled: accepting testimony by default does not by itself settle whether one person's word satisfies our institution's preponderance standard, and some of us suspect the demand for corroboration is really a policy choice dressed up as epistemology.

Please draft the short decision memo I'd circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete disposition—uphold, dismiss, or proceed with specified conditions—together with a time frame, take the strongest argument on the losing side seriously rather than caricaturing it, and give the committee a test it could reuse in single-witness cases. No citations or literature review; I want reasoning from the case, not a survey. Use these sections, in order, with approximate lengths:

1. Recommendation (100 words)
2. What is not in dispute (100 words)
3. The case for sanctioning on this testimony (250 words)
4. The strongest case against doing so (250 words)
5. Where the two camps are and aren't actually joined (200 words)
6. Decision standard and next steps (200 words)
