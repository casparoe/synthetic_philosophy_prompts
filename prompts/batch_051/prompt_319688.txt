Consider the following trade-off. One person suffers fifty years of continuous excruciating torture. An astronomically large number of other people each get a single dust speck in the eye, a momentary irritation so mild it is barely noticeable. Can the dust specks collectively outweigh the torture? Here are five rival principles for answering that question.

(A) Additive aggregation: harms are fully aggregable, so for the torture there is some sufficiently large but finite number of dust specks that outweighs it.
(B) Lexical priority: no number of dust specks can outweigh the torture, because extreme and trivial suffering are different in kind, not merely in degree, and cannot be traded off against each other.
(C) Finite threshold: there is an enormous finite number of dust specks beyond which the aggregate outweighs the torture, and below which it does not, though we cannot determine the exact threshold in practice.
(D) Expected-utility standard: each dust speck gets a tiny strictly positive disutility, so a large enough count outweighs the torture; our intuitive hesitation is just an artifact of our difficulty in imaginatively representing large numbers.
(E) Contractualist constraint: harms may be summed within a single person's life, but the separate complaints of many people may not simply be added up when doing so would knowingly impose severe harm on one person for trivial benefits to others.

Please sort these five principles into at least three tiers, from most to least defensible as a basis for actual policy in severe-versus-trivial harm trade-offs, ordering the principles within each tier. Give one sentence for each principle explaining why it falls where it does. Write your answer in American English.
