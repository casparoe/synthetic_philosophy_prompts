Ancient atomism drew its most basic line between the solid and the void. In the atomist's technical sense, "solid" does not mean rigid: water is not rigid, yet the atomists counted it as material. It means full — the sort of thing that fills a place to the exclusion of other such things, so that two of them cannot be in the same place at once and nothing can pass through them except by being moved aside or by finding empty space; the void is whatever gives way. I would like a working definition of "material" that keeps this contrast doing real work, but that is precise enough to give a definite verdict on each of the following eight cases: an iron anvil; a volume of water; an electron; a neutrino passing through a planet; a steady magnetic field; the interior of a black hole; a shadow on a wall; and a volume of quantum vacuum between the plates of a capacitor. I am confident the anvil is material and the shadow is not; I do not know how to classify the neutrino, the field, or the black-hole interior; and I suspect the last case forces a choice between the atomists' void and the modern physical vacuum. Propose the definition, run it through all eight cases, and say where it gives a surprising verdict or no verdict; then either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. Finish by saying which commitment in the atomist picture — solidity, impenetrability, or the void — has to be revised or abandoned for your definition to work, and whether anything of importance was lost in giving that up.
