I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a moral philosopher whose recent book defends the category of the suberogatory: acts that are morally permissible yet still morally bad, such as refusing a colleague a trivial, costless favor out of spite. Her central thesis is that suberogation is the mirror image of supererogation, and that acknowledging it allows a deontic theory to capture negative moral assessments of conduct that no one is entitled to forbid. Draft eight to ten interview questions that push past having her restate the view. Include at least one question each on: the charge that her category collapses because a genuinely bad act must be wrong at least pro tanto; why suberogatory acts warrant moral criticism without any requirement of apology, repair, or guilt; whether the mirror-image relation to supererogation really holds, given that supererogatory acts earn positive moral credit while suberogatory acts seem to merit something short of blame; and the objection that talk of suberogation is just virtue theory's vocabulary of vices, such as callousness, dressed up as a deontic category. For each, add a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give.
