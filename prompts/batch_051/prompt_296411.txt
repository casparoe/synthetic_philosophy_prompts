As a retired natural scientist—much of my career involved checking whether an instrument or model was actually saying what its creators claimed—I have recently been reading moral philosophy, and one problem keeps pulling me back: whether there is a real moral difference between lying and deliberately misleading through true statements. Kant’s essay on the murderer at the door has become my test case. As I understand it, he denies that one may lie even to a murderer who asks where his intended victim is hiding, and part of his argument connects the wrong of lying to a formal duty of truthfulness rather than to the harm a particular false statement causes a hearer. But the essay assumes a fairly simple case: a false assertion offered to someone who asks a direct question about a location.

I want to ask an anachronistic question. Suppose Kant could study modern practices that are built so that each individual assertion is true, but the whole communication predictably leaves a false belief: corporate disclosures in which the danger is buried in true clauses and placed where investors will stop reading, public-health statements that are true sentence by sentence yet arrange a causal inference they do not assert, legal testimony that answers the exact words while defeating the evident point of the question, or graphs whose axes and baselines are chosen to create a false impression without stating a falsehood. Would Kant’s framework classify these as lies, as another kind of wrong, or as cases in which his doctrine of truthfulness gives less guidance than commentators assume? I am asking for a prediction of how his commitments would extend, not a summary of what he explicitly says. In particular, I would like to know whether Kant could locate the wrong, if any, in the misleading practice itself rather than in the hearer’s inference, and whether his notion of a maxim would treat the plan to exploit a hearer’s uptake as the relevant object of moral assessment. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. As you go, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
