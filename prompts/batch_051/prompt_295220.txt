Speakers of English quietly tolerate several words with two opposed senses: "sanction" can mean permit or penalize, "oversight" can mean supervision or a failure to notice, and "dust" can mean remove fine particles or apply them. These cases draw little complaint. Many of the same speakers, however, treat the newer two-sense use of "literally" — literal and non-literal intensifier — as a real defect of the language rather than another manageable ambiguity, even though context disambiguates "literally" about as reliably as it disambiguates "sanction." Is there a principled difference between the ambiguities we tolerate and the ones we police, or is the asymmetry best explained by the ambiguity's age, the perceived social standing of its introducers, or something similarly non-linguistic? Give the strongest principle you can for drawing the line, then test it against a case it handles poorly.
