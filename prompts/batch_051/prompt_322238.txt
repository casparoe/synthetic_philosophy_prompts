I retired from a career in experimental biology a few years ago and have since been reading my way through the philosophy of science, which is how I came to sit on the review board of a small private foundation that supports work on the conceptual foundations of the life sciences. At Friday's meeting we must decide on a proposal from two computational biologists, working title: A Reference Ontology of Vertebrate Cell Types. They propose to assemble single-cell expression atlases — coarse maps of which genes are active in individual cells sampled across several vertebrate species — partition the data with an unsupervised clustering algorithm, and publish the resulting clusters as a standardized ontology of cell types, complete with a standing nomenclature committee. Their central claim is that certain settings of the clustering algorithm yield groupings that are stable across laboratories, individual organisms, and species, and that this stability is evidence that the groupings mark real divisions in nature rather than artifacts of their computational pipeline.

The board is divided. One faction, led by the board's most statistically sophisticated member, argues that every clustering procedure has a resolution parameter, so the number of kinds it returns is partly a choice made by the analyst. On that view, the proposal has no independent criterion for a cluster's being natural beyond intersubjective stability, and many conventional or administrative categories are stable in the same way; the project would therefore reify a computational partition as an ontology. The other faction replies that rejecting the proposal on those grounds assumes in advance that nature's divisions can only be established from some already-privileged theory. Robust cross-species conservation and across-laboratory reproducibility, they argue, are exactly what one would expect if the clusters tracked something real, and a foundation dedicated to conceptual foundations would be derelict to defer the question merely because the method is imperfect. I have been asked to prepare the brief written opinion for circulation before Friday's meeting.

Please draft that opinion. It should recommend a concrete course of action — fund, reject, or fund with conditions, with a time frame where relevant — and it should give the board a rationale it can act on, including an honest and searching treatment of the strongest consideration on the side you recommend against.
