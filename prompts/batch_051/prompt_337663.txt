I have been rereading P. F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment," and I want to push on one of its central claims. In the essay, Strawson distinguishes between the participant stance, in which we hold one another to ordinary interpersonal expectations and feel resentment, gratitude, indignation, and so on, and the objective stance, in which we view another person as something to be managed, handled, cured, trained, or made the object of social policy. He grants that the objective stance is sometimes appropriate in local cases, as with severe mental illness, immaturity, or extreme strain, but he argues that it could not become the normal human stance across the board. The reactive attitudes are not, on his view, a mere policy we could choose to suspend; they are built into the fabric of ordinary human involvement with one another.

My question is whether that argument survives a specific development. Suppose public institutions increasingly rely on machine-learning risk models that assign each person an actuarial score based on school records, family history, neighborhood data, social contacts, and other proxies, and suppose those scores are used to justify monitoring, supervision, or preventive intervention well before any particular offense has occurred. This looks like a systematic extension of the objective stance: people are being treated as objects of social policy not because they are insane, immature, or under overwhelming strain, but because a model predicts that they are statistically dangerous. Does Strawson's claim that the objective stance is inherently limited still hold in such a society, or does the predictive system show that the objective stance can be sustained much more widely than he thought, at the cost of displacing the participant stance across ordinary domains? I want to know exactly which premise of Strawson's argument this development bears on, and whether the argument can be repaired once that premise is exposed.
