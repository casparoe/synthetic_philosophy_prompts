I chair a public bioethics review committe that sees a steady stream of proposals out of gene-editing labs and neurotech startups, all of them selling some version of the idea that we should use biotechnology to get rid of suffering — some of them literal followers of the hedonistic imperative, some of them just VC-backed startups that found the word "suffering" tested well. My reviewers are mostly scientists and clinicians, not philosophers, and they have maybe two hours per proposal. The stakes are high: approving the wrong thing can't easily be undone, and rejecting the wrong thing means millions of animals stay in pain for another decade. I keep trying to get them to stop arguing case-by-case from their gut and instead run a single procedure every time.

I'd like you to write me that procedure: a decision tree or checklist, grounded in what the hedonistic imperative actually commits you to (as opposed to the mushy "reducing suffering is good" version), that a panel with no philosophy background can run. It should start with the questions that in your view do most of the filtering, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, say where the procedure stops with a no, and say where it has to punt upstairs to us because the question is one of values rather than facts. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about where the procedure will misfire — I'd rather know that than have a false sense of rigor.

Then run it on these three, and don't soften the verdicts:

(a) a proposal to gene-edit dairy cattle so they have congenital insensitivity to pain (but not to touch or pressure), on the argument that mastitis, lameness, and calving are large sources of intense suffering in the industry, and most factory farms won't pay for better housing;
(b) a proposal to trial a neuroimplant in humans that suppresses, on demand and reversibly, the affective component of pain and also grief, shame, and heartbreak — marketed not just to patients but to healthy adults who want it;
(c) a proposal to release a gene drive into wild mouse populations that would knock out their pain system, on the grounds that wild animals suffer enormous amounts from predation, disease, and starvation and this is our best shot at doing something about it.

At least one of these should come out differently than a naive reading of the hedonistic imperative would predict, and I want you to flag where that happens and why the principle, run properly, gives the unexpected verdict.
