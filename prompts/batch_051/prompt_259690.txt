In my metaphysics course we have been discussing cross-world identity and the conditions under which a particular person can be said to exist, and our instructor keeps compressing the discussion into the line: "You couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg." The same sentence gets made to do several different jobs. It is used to show that if my parents had conceived a month later than they actually did, I would never have existed. It is used to show that there is a determinate fact about when my life began, fixed by which gametes produced me. And in a session on reproductive ethics, it was used to argue that selecting a different embryo in IVF cannot wrong the child who would otherwise have been born, because that child would not have existed to be wronged. I doubt that one slogan can bear all this weight, but I cannot see exactly where the readings come apart. Please tease apart the distinct precise claims that "You couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg" might be expressing — for example, claims about the necessity of origins, about whether the relevant unit is the sperm and egg or the fertilized zygote, and about what the word "you" picks out in these conditionals. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive but plausible, or false, and say which readings would actually support each of the three uses I described.
