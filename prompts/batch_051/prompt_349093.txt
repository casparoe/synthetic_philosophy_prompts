I keep going back and forth on something from a class I'm taking on the history of work, and I'd like the argument actually pulled apart rather than just repeated. The old claim (I think it was Keynes) was that rising productivity would solve the economic problem: people would only need to work around fifteen hours a week, and the real future problem would be how to fill all the free time. The last few decades look like a different experiment, though. Full-time hours in rich countries never really collapsed, even as computing and automation erased entire kinds of task. Instead work seems to have rearranged itself into jobs that would have sounded ridiculous to the person making the prediction — corporate wellness coordinators, compliance layers, influencer marketing, people writing reports nobody reads, whole jobs who's point seems to be being visibly busy. And now AI looks like it's tightening deadlines and raising expectations more than it's shortening the week.

What I want is an actual verdict on whether the old "automation shortens work" argument survives the later "work didn't disappear, it became bullshit" picture. Which premise is doing the real work — that people eventually prefer free time over more income, that productivity gains get spread around, that work is just a cost to be minimized — and which one broke? Can the argument be repaired into something weaker but still important about what happens to meaning and identity when work is automated away? Or is it just dead?
