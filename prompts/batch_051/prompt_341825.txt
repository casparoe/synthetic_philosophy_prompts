The author summarizes the problem like this. Expressivists say moral sentences like "lying is wrong" are not in the business of describing moral facts; they're expressions of disapproval, more like "boo lying" than like "the cat is on the mat." Then he says the problem is that in an embedded context like "if lying is wrong, then encouraging your little brother to lie is wrong," the speaker isn't expressing disapproval of lying. So either the phrase "lying is wrong" changes meaning from standalone to embedded use, or the word "if" has to combine an attitude with a proposition. The first wrecks compositionality, and the second makes no sense, so expressivism is stuck. He then brings in a passage from Frege's "The Thought" which he renders roughly as: the same thought can be present in a sentence without being put forward as true, as in the antecedent of a conditional; assertoric force is added on top of the thought, and truth or falsity belongs to the thought itself, not to the assertion. He says Frege already identified exactly the distinction expressivists need and can't have, because if the attitude is the meaning, there is no thought left to embed. I'm suspicious: I've seen it said that expressivists have answers to Frege-Geach, and that the argument needs extra premises, but I can't tell where the author is cutting corners. Which parts are fair, which parts are common simplifications or conflations, and does the Frege passage do the work the author says it does?
