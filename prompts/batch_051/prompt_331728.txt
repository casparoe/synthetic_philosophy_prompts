A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about one find: a deliberately broken clay vessel, its sherds set around a fist-sized stone in the corner of a house floor at a prehistoric settlement. She does commercial archaeology and holds that once you go beyond technology, diet, and economy you are writing fiction about people who left no words: at most we can say that a pot was broken and moved, never that this is ritual. I hold that some objects and contexts — repeated, patterned, costly, non-utilitarian — really do warrant cautious inference to past belief and institutions, and that archaeology can do that less badly than she thinks. We were really arguing about how high the bar should be and which evidence would meet it, and neither of us had a procedure; we just had positions.

I am the curator of collections at a regional museum. We are re-cataloguing a prehistoric farming settlement with no written records, and my two junior staff must run a classification on dozens of objects and deposits a week for the rest of the season; their tags feed our public labels. The procedure should return, for each object or deposit, one of: probable ritual, probable mundane, dual (a mundane use and a ritual role), or unknown — and must record what evidence would overturn that classification.

Write me the decision procedure: an ordered set of steps and tests, the sort of thing that could be implemented as a flowchart and taught in an afternoon to staff who have not read archaeological theory. It must be grounded in the real machinery of archaeological inference, not a generic checklist. Keep the old ladder-of-inference point that the farther you move from subsistence and technology toward social institutions and belief, the weaker the inference becomes and the higher the bar must be. Keep the middle-range point that every step from a material trace to a behavior or belief needs an explicit linking argument — an observed causal regularity, a controlled analogy, or independent convergence — rather than a bare resemblance or what the object looks like. Preserve the distinction between an object's function and its ritual meaning or role, so that a mundane use does not automatically exclude a ritual role and vice versa. Do not let the "probable ritual" tag attach unless independent lines of evidence — manufacture, use-wear, depositional context, and repetition across the site — pull in the same direction. Make the procedure state which rung of inference a given classification actually commits us to — repeated practice, for example, before specified belief content — and default to the lower rung rather than letting a label smuggle in a deity or a calendar when all that is licensed is patterned deposition. Block the two standard failures: using ritual as the dustbin for anything unexplained, and treating a practical explanation as automatically more parsimonious than a ritual one. Wherever a step is a judgment call rather than the elimination of a possibility by evidence, the procedure must say so, and it must say when a classification should be escalated to me and, where heritage is implicated, to a community liaison or outside specialist.

Then work the procedure through three cases and show what it returns for each, and why: (1) the broken pot with sherds arranged around a stone from my argument; (2) a set of small, schematically human figurines found mixed into a midden with no other special treatment; (3) a pit filled with ordinary household debris and ash that could be either a domestic rubbish pit or a deliberate closing deposit. At least one of the three should return an answer I will not like; tell me which one and why. End with what the procedure cannot do — the kinds of inference, and the kinds of case, in which no flowchart should be permitted to produce a public label.
