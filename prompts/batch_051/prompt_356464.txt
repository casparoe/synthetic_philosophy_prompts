So I had a long argument with a friend last night about deepfakes, and I came out of it unsure whether my actual practice is rational or just a habit im protecting. Her line is that video used to be the epistemic backstop — the thing you could fall back on when documents, memories, and testimony conflicted — and now it isnt, because any clip can in principle be generated cheaply, so a contested video just becomes one more contested claim. I took the other side, but when she pressed me I realized my rule isnt the one I was defending. My real rule is: video stays a backstop unless there is something specifically wrong with that video. Weird artifacts, no source, nobody else caught the scene, the clip being just a little too convenient — those count against it. The bare fact that deepfakes exist, or that the technology keeps getting better, does not. I accept a normal-looking clip in a dispute without asking for independent evidence of its authenticity, but I demand specific evidence of inauthenticity before I will drop it. She said this is the whole problem: I am treating an old default as privileged, and my checks for "something wrong" are exactly the features deepfakes can now target. That stuck with me. Is my rule defensible, or is it a bias protecting the old backstop? What would have to be true about how many fakes are out there, what they look like, and which sorts of mistakes cost me most, for a policy like mine to beat both naive trust and doubt-by-default? And if its not defensible as-is, is there a nearby version that keeps video useful without pretending deepfakes dont exist?
