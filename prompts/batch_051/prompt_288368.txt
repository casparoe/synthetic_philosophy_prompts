Two research analysts on my team, Neha and Carlos, spent the better part of a planning meeting arguing about the scoring model we use to compare grant recommendations, and I was asked to produce a neutral account of the dispute. The narrow question is how a single intervention score should combine two quantities: a beneficiary's welfare capacity — roughly, how conscious or sentient the being is — and the expected change in that being's happiness or suffering. The exchange, reconstructed from my notes, went like this.

(1) Neha argued that the score should be the product of the two quantities. A hedonic gain for a being with almost no conscious experience should count for almost nothing, while the same gain for a highly conscious being should count for a great deal, and multiplication builds that in automatically. If the model adds the quantities instead, a sufficiently large pile of insect welfare points will outrank a modest gain for a human, and few of us would actually endorse that verdict.

(2) Carlos replied that the product only looks principled because it treats our capacity estimates as though they were ratio-scale measurements. We do not have a unit of consciousness in which a score of 0.4 is honestly four times a score of 0.1; those numbers are judgment calls, at best rough cardinal estimates. Multiplying two guesses together makes the output look more precise than either input is. If something must go into the model, a weighted sum is easier to audit: welfare points plus an explicitly stated sentience weight, with the weight printed beside the output.

(3) Neha responded that a weighted sum lets volume swamp significance. With additive weights, no matter how small you set the sentience weight, a large enough number of low-sentience beneficiaries will eventually outrank a fixed number of high-sentience beneficiaries — that is just arithmetic. Multiplication at least lets a near-zero capacity suppress the happiness term. If we must err, she said, we should err on the side that does not treat a trillion barely sentient organisms as interchangeable with a person.

(4) Carlos came back that multiplication does not stop the swamping either; it only hides the threshold. Multiply any positive capacity by a large enough population and the product overtakes the human case too. If Neha really believes there is a line below which beings should not be traded off against persons, she should say so directly: use a lexical rule — sort beneficiaries into capacity tiers, never allow trade-offs across tiers, and aggregate additively, or however we like, within a tier. Her product, he said, is an unstable compromise that neither prevents swamping nor owns up to the hard threshold she is actually leaning on.

Please adjudicate. Go through the four moves in order and assess each one: what it gets right, what it gets wrong, and how strong the argument is as actually stated. Then give your overall ruling on the dispute — which specific claims from each side you accept or reject, what the two of them are not seeing, and what that implies for how the scoring model ought to combine welfare capacity with expected happiness or suffering.
