I am drawn to the principle of indifference in something close to its classical form: when my evidence gives me no more reason to expect any one member of a partition than another, I should distribute my credence evenly across the partition. I am attracted to it for the right reasons, or so they seem to me. It is simple, it is symmetric, and it supplies a determinate answer in cases of complete ignorance where rival norms would leave me silent. It also looks like the hidden backbone of a great deal of ordinary probabilistic reasoning: if nothing distinguishes the six faces of a die, assigning each face a probability of one-sixth feels less like a convention than like the only honest answer a finite mind can give.

But Bertrand's paradox will not leave me alone. A chord of a circle is selected at random, and the question is the probability that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Depending on how I parameterize the selection, by two endpoints on the circumference, by a radius and a point along that radius, or by a point chosen uniformly in the interior of the circle, I arrive at one-third, one-half, or one-fourth. I cannot shake the conviction that this must be a question about one thing, the random chord, and that it therefore has one answer, and that a principle that generates three different answers, each apparently by its own lights a correct application, has not merely been misapplied but has been shown to be internally defective. Yet I cannot find, inside the principle, the rule that tells me which parameterization is preferred, and I suspect that any rule I add will be a substantive new premise rather than an innocent clarification.

I do not want to be told ahead of time which side wins. I want to understand what would justify each possible surrender. Would it be right to say that Bertrand's paradox refutes the principle of indifference, and that the intuition standing firm is the good one? Or would it be right to say that the request that a chord be chosen at random is underspecified, so that the intuition that there must be a single determinate probability is the thing to be given up, and the principle is exonerated? Or should the principle be amended into something that survives, for example, a version that permits equal credence only across partitions that are invariant under a specified symmetry group, or only after a generating procedure has been fixed, and would that amendment preserve the virtues that attracted me in the first place? Or is the honest resolution a negotiated one, on which my original principle is demoted to a heuristic inside a background model while the intuition survives only for questions asked relative to a fully specified chance setup? Present the strongest case for each move, and tell me which of my two starting convictions, the principle and the intuition that the paradox reveals a real incoherence, should be abandoned, amended, or read as compatible, and exactly what would justify that verdict. My only request about the form of your answer is that you write in continuous prose, as you would in an email or a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
