I am getting ready to work through the main theories of the atonement — penal substitution, satisfaction, and moral influence — and I want to fix the standard of evaluation before I compare them. Write out the desiderata that any adequate theory of the atonement must meet, with the theological or moral pressure behind each. For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable and would count against any theory that failed it, or whether it is a demand peculiar to one camp that other camps may reasonably reject. Keep the weight on the criteria themselves, not on scoring the three theories against them; I will do the scoring later.

To start, sort through the following candidate requirements, which I have collected but have not yet endorsed. (i) It must explain why Christ's death in particular is the vehicle of atonement, rather than his teaching, example, or resurrection alone. (ii) It must show how divine justice and divine mercy are both genuinely at work, not how one is a rhetorical cover for the other. (iii) It must not make God the punisher of an innocent person, on the assumption that punishing the innocent is unjust — or, if it does, it must explain why punishment of the innocent is not unjust in this case. (iv) It must capture substitution: something borne in the sinner's place, not merely a moral influence exerted on the sinner. (v) It must explain how the atonement is applied to individual persons — why it does not automatically save everyone — without making the application a second, independent work. (vi) It must make moral transformation a real consequence of, or at least coherent with, the transaction the theory posits rather than an unrelated add-on. Tell me which of these are genuine requirements, which are dispensable, and what else belongs on the list.

Then do two further things. First, say which single requirement does the most work in excluding the standard candidates. Second, flag any subset of requirements that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; I have a suspicion that there is a tension somewhere among the demands that divine justice be satisfied by penalty, that punishing an innocent person is unjust, and that Christ's death was a just substitutionary penalty, but I want to be told whether that tension is real or rests on stating one of the requirements too strongly.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the sorting as a whole, and identify the single point in your answer you are least sure about.
