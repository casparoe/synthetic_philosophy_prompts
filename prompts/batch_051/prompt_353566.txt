You are a philosopher of mathematics with a strong interest in mathematical practice and the epistemology of proof. Think carefully, and before you settle on an answer, work through the strongest formulations of both positions and the concrete costs of each possible resolution. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for citations; I want your own reconstruction and your considered verdict.

I find myself pulled in two directions about what it means to understand a proof.

On the one hand, I am attracted to a deflationary, verificationist account. A proof, on this view, is simply a finite sequence of statements each of which is an axiom or follows from earlier statements by an accepted rule of inference, and to understand a proof is to be able to verify each step and reconstruct the derivation. Nothing further — no private act of "grasping the idea," no "seeing why" — needs to be added to explain mathematical acceptance. I like this account for systematic reasons: it makes understanding publicly checkable rather than introspectively mysterious, it gives a crisp criterion for adjudicating disagreement about whether someone understands, and it fits the fact that formal verification systems certify proofs without anything resembling insight.

On the other hand, I cannot shake the intuition that verification and understanding come apart. I can imagine checking every line of a computer-certified proof that proceeds by exhaustive case analysis — the kind used for a classification theorem — and still wanting to insist that neither I nor the machine has thereby understood why the theorem is true. More plainly: a student can reproduce the classic proof that the square root of 2 is irrational, correctly justify each step when prompted, and still be unable to adapt the underlying idea to prove that the square root of 3 is irrational or to say what the proof does to the assumption of rationality. Conversely, a working mathematician may grasp the central idea of a proof while botching or forgetting a technical inequality, and I am strongly inclined to say she understands the proof better than the flawless memorizer does. These feel like facts about understanding itself, not merely about psychology or efficiency.

So the question is which should yield. Present the strongest version of the deflationary account — including its best explanation of my contrary cases — and the strongest version of the intuition — including its best account of what is missing from mere verification. Then tell me what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes (what sort of debunking would show it is tracking nothing beyond a feeling of fluency), and what it would take for the deflationary account to be amended or abandoned (what must be added, and at what cost to its systematic virtues). Finally, say whether there is a reading of "understanding" on which both survive, and which of the three moves you would ultimately make. Answer in American English.
