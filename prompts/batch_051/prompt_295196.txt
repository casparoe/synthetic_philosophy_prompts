I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been reading about physicalism on my own. I built the following argument, which I now suspect proves too much, because it seems to show that a serious metaphysical thesis is either false or contentless before any evidence is even consulted. Please treat this as a consultation with a professional philosopher working in philosophy of mind and metaphysics. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the distinct ways the key term could be defined; then diagnose the argument step by step, without taking a position merely for the sake of having one.

The argument runs as follows:

(1) Physicalism is the substantive thesis that every real thing is physical, or, more carefully, that all facts obtain in virtue of the physical facts.

(2) The term "physical" must mean either (a) the kinds of entities and properties posited by current physics, or (b) the kinds of entities and properties posited by some future, completed, ideally adequate physics.

(3) If it means (a), physicalism is very likely false. Current physics is, by its own practitioners' lights, incomplete, and there are phenomena — conscious experience being the usual example — that current physics plainly does not yet describe.

(4) If it means (b), physicalism surrenders its content. A definition that defers to an unknown future theory is like validating software against a specification that has not yet been written and might be written to cover anything; since we cannot say in advance what the completed physics will include, the claim that everything is physical now excludes nothing.

(5) Therefore physicalism is either probably false, on reading (a), or empty, on reading (b).

(6) Therefore physicalism, as a meaningful substantive thesis, cannot get off the ground — not because some things demonstrably are nonphysical, but because its central term cannot do the work asked of it.

I find the conclusion in (6) too strong. Many careful people treat physicalism as meaningful and worth arguing about, and I would rather preserve the idea that something true and substantive is being claimed. So my question is: where does this argument go wrong? If the flaw is in a premise, say which one and explain why it looked obvious to me; if the flaw is a slide or an equivocation between steps, show me exactly where a term is doing double duty. For whatever I give up, tell me what the standard alternatives pay for their fix, and whether my software analogy in step (4) is misleading or actually helpful. I want a diagnosis, not a survey.
