I am the policy chair of my high school's student government, and I need a reusable procedure for deciding which student proposals to test, how to test them, and when to recommend making them permanent. I review two or three proposals a month concerning school rules, new programs, and recurring events. The principal and faculty advisor will approve a permanent change only if a pilot produced observable results, but student government meets for forty minutes a week, I usually have about a week to vet each proposal before it goes on the agenda, we have almost no budget for data collection, and none of us are trained in social science. The stakes are moderate but real: a bad policy can make daily school life worse for hundreds of students for a semester, and a good policy that is presented badly can die in the principal's office.

I have been reading John Dewey's experimentalism, and I want it converted into a procedure I can actually run rather than left as a set of slogans. As I understand Dewey, he treats democracy as more than voting: it is a way of living together in which shared problems are met through inquiry, where inquiry means forming a plan, trying it out in a limited way, observing what actually happens, and revising the plan — including the goal itself — in light of the consequences. He also holds that inquiry is not the same as collecting opinions or measuring popularity; a proposal has to be made specific enough that acting on it can produce evidence that counts against it, and that evidence has to be allowed to override what we wanted to be true. I do not need a lengthy exposition of Dewey; I need the translation into a monthly procedure.

The procedure should cover: how to restate a proposal as a testable hypothesis; what kind of evidence we can realistically collect in a school; when to recommend making a pilot permanent; when to kill it even though it is popular; and where the procedure must stop and admit that judgment cannot be eliminated. It has to handle at least these hard cases:

(1) a pilot that is popular but whose measured results are ambiguous or negative;
(2) a proposal about student rights or dignity, such as a gender-neutral dress code, that seems wrong to reduce to an outcome metric;
(3) a program that visibly helps a small struggling group but shows no effect on the average student;
(4) an improvement whose main benefits would appear only after high school, so they cannot be observed during a pilot;
(5) a metric that students can game once they know it is being tracked.

Please run the procedure through two or three worked examples, and make at least one of them a case in which the procedure tells me to do something I will probably dislike. Also state what the procedure cannot do, and identify which parts of Dewey's experimentalism you are simplifying or stretching to fit a school setting.

Because I have to present this to the rest of student government, format the main deliverable as a table with these columns: Stage; Guiding question; Action to take; Stop, revise, or continue test; Rule or judgment call (state which applies and why); Most likely failure in a real school. Then present the worked examples as a second table with these columns: Case; How the procedure handles it; What it cannot settle; Confidence. Finally, outside the tables, state explicitly how confident you are in the overall procedure and identify the single point in it you are least sure about.
