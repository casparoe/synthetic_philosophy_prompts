At a seminar I attended, two people got into an argument about whether there is an asymmetry in our reasons to create new people. Priya defended the claim that the fact a possible child would be happy is not itself a reason to bring that child into existence, while the fact a possible child would suffer is a reason not to bring that child into existence. Jonas maintained that this intuition could not survive scrutiny. Their exchange, reconstructed as closely as I remember, went as follows.

Priya: If a couple decides not to have a child, no actual person is left worse off. They fail to create a beneficiary, but that failure is not a loss to anyone. If they have a child whose life will be painful enough, however, a real person is created whose suffering was avoidable. The two cases are therefore not symmetrical.

Jonas: Both choices are being compared with the child's never having existed. If nonexistence is not a state in which a person can be better or worse off, then existence with a bad life cannot be worse for the child either—worse compared with what? You are using nonexistence as a baseline when it lets you condemn one choice, but refusing to use it when it would let you praise the other.

Priya: In the harmful case there is a future actual child who will be worse off than if she had never been created. In the beneficial case there is no person in the uncreated alternative who could have been made better off. The asymmetry tracks the need for a subject who can be harmed.

Jonas: That does not explain why a future actual beneficiary is not enough in the beneficial case. If the fact that a possible child will become actual is enough to make its future suffering count at the time of decision, then the same structure should make its future happiness count. Otherwise you are stipulating that only negative facts count, not deriving it.

Priya: I do not think it is a stipulation. Creating a happy child is like giving an unrequested gift: it may be good, but one is not required to give it. Creating a child whose life will be miserable is imposing an unconsented burden, and burdens require justification in a way gifts do not.

Jonas: But every creation is an unconsented imposition. If consent is the issue, the happy child is imposed upon too. If the size of the burden is the issue, then with sufficiently great predicted happiness you would be willing to impose the same burden, which means you do not really object to imposition as such. You are now defending a general priority of avoiding harm over conferring benefit, not anything peculiar to procreation.

Priya: Perhaps I am. In ordinary life we recognize that one may have no duty to confer a benefit one could confer, while having a strict duty not to cause the corresponding harm. Creation simply applies that general priority to people who do not yet exist.

Jonas: That general priority is itself controversial, because it suggests that no amount of benefit to one person can make up for a small harm to another. And applied to procreation it is exactly the asymmetry we set out to test: a surplus of happy lives never counts in favor of creating them, while one sufficiently bad life counts decisively against. So far we have only restated the intuition at a higher level of abstraction.

Go through this exchange and say who is right about which specific point—not just who wins overall. Where an argument shifts ground, identify the shift. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing.
