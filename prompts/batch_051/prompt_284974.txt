Last weekend, after a long argument that ended nowhere, a friend pressed on me what he considers a decisive case against human freedom. He described the digger wasp Sphex, which paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of its burrow, leaves it there, goes inside to inspect the burrow, comes back out, and only then drags the cricket in. If the cricket is nudged a few inches away while the wasp is down inspecting, she does not simply drag it the rest of the way inside when she re-emerges; she drags it back to the mouth, goes down to re-inspect the already-inspected burrow, and then completes the sequence, apparently because the subroutine demands it. He called this mechanical rigidity sphexishness and offered the following argument:

(1) The wasp’s behavior is sphexish in this sense: a sequence that looks goal-directed is actually a fixed routine triggered by a narrow perceptual cue, and the routine runs even when it has become pointless.
(2) To be sphexish in that sense just is to be a system whose behavior is a fixed function of its current trigger and its current internal state.
(3) Any deterministic system, human beings included, is a fixed function of its present state and circumstances; on a deterministic picture, the human merely stores far more routines and conditional branches than the wasp.
(4) Therefore the difference between the wasp and us can be only a difference in the complexity and number of programs, not a difference in kind.
(5) Free will requires a difference in kind, namely a capacity to step back from one’s routines and act for reasons that are not already written into one’s programming.
(6) So if determinism is true, free will is an illusion, and the wasp is simply an honest version of what we are.

I have the persistent feeling that something illicit happens around step (2) or step (3), around the phrase fixed function, but whenever I try to say exactly what, I find myself sounding as though I am merely denying step (5), which I would rather not make carry the whole weight. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what is the strongest reformulation of that step that would let the argument go through? If it is valid, is the remaining dispute purely empirical, or is there still a conceptual mistake hiding in step (5)?
