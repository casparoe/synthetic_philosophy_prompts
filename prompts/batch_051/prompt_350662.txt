I work as a software engineer on an internal platform team, and one of the tools I maintain scores proposed projects by estimating, for each possible outcome, its probability and a monetary value, and then summing those products into an overall expected value that management uses to rank initiatives. Recently a colleague directed me to a puzzle I have since learned is called the Pasadena paradox. As I understand it, the puzzle constructs a gamble with payoffs that alternate in sign and whose probabilities are arranged so that the expected value is the alternating harmonic series, 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ... . That series converges to ln 2 when summed in its natural order, but because it converges only conditionally, its terms can be rearranged to converge to any real number or to diverge. The apparent lesson is that expected value is not well defined for such prospects unless one imposes an absolute-convergence condition or some privileged order of summation.

This has made me uneasy about our scoring tool. Many of the proposals we evaluate have long tails of small-probability gains and losses, and our code currently truncates the outcome list and sums it in a fixed, conventional order before reporting a point estimate. I would like to understand the most important implications of this under-definedness for a practical decision-support system of this kind. Should we treat some projects as unrankable rather than assigning them a number, or is there a principled way to regularize the calculation that does not simply smuggle in an arbitrary ordering? More generally, what conceptual lessons should a technically literate non-specialist take from the Pasadena paradox about when expected-value reasoning can be trusted?

I am not trained in philosophy and would prefer an explanation grounded in the structure of the puzzle itself rather than in a body of literature I would need to look up.
