I'm writing a paper for my history class on Fernand Braudel's distinction among three temporal scales: the short time of events, the intermediate time of conjunctures, and the longue durée of slow structures such as geography and climate. My problem is that, although Braudel contrasts events with structures, he never gives a crisp account of what makes something an event in the first place, and his examples seem to pull in different directions.

Give me a working definition of a historical event in Braudel's sense—the kind of thing he treats as belonging to the shortest time scale—that correctly classifies all of the following:

- the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in 1914;
- the slow cooling of the Northern Hemisphere during the Little Ice Age;
- the Black Death of 1347–1351;
- the signing of Magna Carta in 1215;
- the Industrial Revolution;
- the spread of double-entry bookkeeping across Europe;
- the Neolithic transition from hunting and gathering to agriculture;
- the 1883 eruption of Krakatoa.

I think the first is clearly an event and the second clearly is not, but I am genuinely unsure about the middle cases, especially the Black Death and Magna Carta, which each look like a single datable occurrence that only matters because of slow processes around it.

Propose the definition, run it through all eight cases, and tell me which of your classifications you are least confident about. If the definition gives a verdict you think is wrong, revise it rather than defend it. Also say whether the definition captures why Braudel thought events were less important than the longue durée, or whether that evaluative judgment needs a separate argument. Please write your answer in American English.
