I have been trying to understand why the St. Petersburg paradox is still an open argument after three centuries rather than a settled puzzle. The game is easy to state: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads, and the payoff starts at two units and doubles for each toss that came up tails before that first heads; the monetary expectation is therefore infinite, yet almost everyone would pay only a modest amount to play. The standard responses are also easy to state. Bernoulli argued that people do not value money linearly, because marginal utility declines, so the relevant expectation is finite. Later writers replied that the modest price is rational because no actual banker could cover the enormous tail payoffs. Others observed that any bounded utility function removes the problem, only to be met with the objection that bounded utility is an ad hoc patch and the real issue is whether value can be unbounded. More recently, some economists have treated the paradox as an artifact of confusing an ensemble average with the time average that governs an actual long sequence of bets.

Yet none of these has ended the argument, and each camp tends to say the others changed the subject rather than answered the question. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, not another proposed resolution. Is it a substantive dispute within decision theory about whether rationality consists in maximizing some expectation? Is it a dispute about which idealizations are legitimate when modeling a gamble no one can actually offer? Is it a verbal dispute about what the value of the game is supposed to mean? My working hypothesis is that the debate has persisted because it compresses at least three separate questions into one: how an individual should price a single play, how an indefinite sequence of plays should be priced or evaluated by an actual long-run bettor, and what a theory of rational choice must say about gambles with unbounded support. I want a diagnosis that engages the actual positions, locates the impasse, and says what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a change of question — or, if no progress should be expected, why the disagreement is permanent.
