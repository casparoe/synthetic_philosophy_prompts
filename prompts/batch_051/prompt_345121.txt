Take the Robert Moses Long Island parkway overpasses as a case study for the claim that artifacts can have politics — the thesis, associated with Langdon Winner, that some technologies settle social questions through their physical form rather than merely being neutral tools held by political actors. Use Winner's distinction between two kinds of political technology: technologies that are political in the strong sense because they require or strongly favor a particular form of political organization, and particular technical arrangements that embed a pattern of order, authority, or exclusion in their structure. The low overpass is the canonical illustration of the second kind. Reconstruct both senses in your own words; I am not asking for bibliography or citations, just a usable analytical reconstruction.

Here is the working background. Starting in the 1920s and 1930s, Robert Moses — then the dominant figure in New York-area park, parkway, and bridge construction — built the parkway system leading to Jones Beach and other Long Island parks. The standard account holds that the overpasses along those parkways were deliberately built with clearances too low for standard buses, so the parks were effectively limited to people who could arrive by car. Since car ownership then was disproportionately white and middle class, the bridge heights are read as a race and class filter written into concrete. The revisionist account contests this: the clearances, it says, were ordinary for parkway design of the period, and the exclusionary outcome is better explained by a bundle of other decisions — land-use, fare policy, housing, and which transit proposals Moses did or did not support — than by the height of any bridge. I am not asking you to settle the historical dispute; I am asking what the theory does when its canonical example turns out to have a contested causal center. Do not spend words re-narrating the story beyond what the argument requires.

Now do the following. First, fix the unit of analysis. What exactly is the artifact here — a single overpass, the integrated parkway system, the bus that is rendered unusable, or the design standard together with the institutions that maintained it? Say why this choice changes what it could mean for the artifact to have politics.

Second, run the episode through each of the two senses of the theory. For each, say whether the Moses case counts as political, what would confirm or refute that classification, and what the theory predicts about who could later change the outcome and at what cost.

Third, make the revisionist reading earn its keep. If the low clearances were not the product of an explicit intention to exclude, can the embedded-order sense of the theory still hold? Does the politics of an artifact require design intent, or can it reside in the arrangement's downstream effects and in the durable way it allocates access? Answer as the theory's best defender would, and state what commitment the theory needs to make in order to keep the Moses case as an illustration. Use the distinction between intent and effect explicitly, but do not reduce the question to current discrimination law.

Fourth, say where the framework illuminates the episode and where it misdescribes it. In particular: what does the bridge story hide about the upstream institutions, budgets, agency, and housing policy that produced the exclusion? Does treating the overpass as the bearer of politics smuggle in a reverse technological determinism? And what would the counterfactual of full-height bridges with everything else held fixed show about how much work the artifact itself was doing?

Finally, reach a verdict about what this episode, with its contested factual center, shows about the theory: is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory can absorb only by becoming so flexible that it is unfalsifiable? Defend that verdict and identify which feature of the case is doing the argumentative work.

Throughout, mark your confidence: which claims are well established, which are your own construction, and which are fragile. End by naming the single point you are least sure of.
