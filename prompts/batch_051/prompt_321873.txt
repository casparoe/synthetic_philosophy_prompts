I have been trying to pin down a friend's implicit theory of voting, because her verdicts on individual cases have been much more consistent than any single principle I can articulate. She is a committed anti-spoiler thinker: in a statewide general election that a forecasting model she trusts treated as a pure toss-up between a genuinely bad major-party candidate and a merely disappointing one, she said the only responsible vote was for the disappointing one, and she was hard on a Green-leaning friend who voted Green — in a dead heat, she argued, a third-party vote is a de facto subsidy to the candidate you least want. She said much the same to a different friend in the same state who stayed home out of disgust with both major candidates: abstention is not a way to keep one's hands clean, but simply cedes the race to the worse outcome. Yet in a state the model gave the worse major candidate virtually no chance of winning, she thought voting Green was not merely permissible but the best use of the ballot, because it could not affect who won between the major parties and it registered a public demand that neither major party was taking seriously. The one thing she rejected even in that safe state was a write-in of "no confidence," which she called a private sulk dressed up as speech: a ballot is not a diary, and a write-in whose count communicates nothing to anyone expresses nothing.

The case we cannot agree on is this. The same model now gives the candidate she considers worse a 4 percent chance of winning the upcoming statewide race, and gives the merely disappointing major-party candidate a 96 percent chance; a Green candidate is also on the ballot. Our Green-leaning friend wants to know whether he should vote Green, as he would in the safe state, or hold his nose and vote for the disappointing major-party candidate, as he would in the toss-up. If Elena wants to be consistent with her own record, what should she say? Lay out the rules that fit her earlier verdicts, say whether they converge or diverge on this 4 percent case, and identify which earlier commitment — the anti-spoiler duty, the safe-state permission for expressive voting, or the anti-write-in rule — each answer would force her to sharpen or revise. If her record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say what principle she would be adopting with each possible verdict, and specify the further case that would most cleanly tell the principles apart.
