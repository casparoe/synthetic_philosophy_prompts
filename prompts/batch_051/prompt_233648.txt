After my philosophy of psychiatry seminar last week — the reading was Hacking on looping effects — two of the grad students got into it in the hallway and it spilled into the pub. I mostly just listened. They were arguing about a proposed label I'll call fragile autonomy disorder, FAD for short: a pattern where someone who has spent years being treated as incapable of managing their own life (by family, a partner, an institution) comes to act helpless, avoid decisions, and describe themselves as unable to cope. The label is meant to pick out that downstream pattern, not the original mistreatment. The argument went roughly like this.

Dana: That category is going to be self-fulfilling. You hand people a label for "you can't manage your own life," they fold it into their self-understanding, and the self-understanding produces more of the same behavior. So you can't validate FAD the way you'd validate a disease. Any study that "finds" the category will partly be measuring the effects of having applied it.

Marco: Diagnosis changes the diagnosed all over medicine. Tell someone they have hypertension, and they change their diet and take the pills. If feedback alone made a category suspect, half of medicine would be suspect.

Dana: That's the difference, not the rebuttal. In the hypertension case the loop runs through salt and vessels — the classification changes behavior, which changes blood pressure. In FAD the loop runs through self-conception, and self-conception is part of the syndrome itself. That's exactly the kind of interactive kind Hacking says loops.

Marco: Looping makes the object unstable; it doesn't make it unreal. There were people acting like this before anyone named it — that's what motivates the label in the first place. Hacking is careful here: he doesn't say multiple personality was invented from nothing; he says the classification made new ways of being a person available. That complicates validation but doesn't make it impossible.

Dana: The problem is worse than a complication. Once the label circulates, it enters the clinical encounter. The clinician hears "I can't cope, I have FAD" and takes that as evidence for FAD — but the patient only says that because the label exists. Now measuring the syndrome and producing the syndrome are the same process. Validation becomes circular.

Marco: That's a real problem for validating the category from self-report alone, but not a blanket defeat. You can anchor FAD in things that don't go through the label: observer-rated behavior over time, longitudinal course, response to treatment. If FAD tracks a real pattern it should predict something beyond the patient having learned the word. If it doesn't, you'll see the epidemiology wander — which is exactly the kind of loop Hacking was telling us to watch for.

Dana: Suppose the epidemiology is stable. That stability could just be patients and clinicians having learned the same script and reinforcing it. A stable cultural attractor looks, from the inside, exactly like a validated category. So "stable and predictive" doesn't seperate discovering a fact from successfully spreading a convention. Which is why I think the right question about FAD was never "is it valid?" in the DSM's sense; the right question is what happens to people when you introduce the label and who benefits.

Marco: You've just changed the subject. We started with whether FAD has a determinate object, and now you're saying the only real question is moral and political. That doesn't follow: a category can be ethically dangerous and still have a fact of the matter. And your convention story has a gap. Scripts don't take hold equally — some people accept the label, some reject it, some try it and drop it. If those differences have systematic causes, then something independent of the label is doing causal work.
