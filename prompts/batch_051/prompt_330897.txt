I write as a retired natural scientist — a physiologist by training — who has recently begun reading philosophy and now sits on the ethics committee of a teaching hospital. The committee has been asked to consider a complaint, and I would value the help of a professional philosopher who works on the conceptual foundations of medicine. Please think carefully, and before you offer your verdict, lay out the distinct conceptual issues you take the case to raise.

The case is this. A woman in her forties sustained a severe hypoxic brain injury after a cardiac arrest outside hospital. Several days later, after repeated examinations had established that she had irreversibly lost all function of the entire brain, including the brainstem, she was declared dead. Throughout this period her breathing was mechanically supported and her circulation continued: her heart beat normally and her blood pressure was stable. Because she was a registered donor, the ventilator was kept in place and her organs were retrieved later that afternoon; her circulation stopped only as part of that process. Her husband has now complained that she was alive until that moment. He points out that before the retrieval her heart was beating, her kidneys were producing urine, and a small burn on her arm was visibly healing. A nurse also recalls that the patient's arm moved slightly during preparation, though the physicians explained this as a spinal reflex.

The committee is divided. Some members say the woman was already dead when she was declared: an organism whose brain has been destroyed has irreversibly lost the capacity to function as an integrated whole, and the persisting heartbeat and wound healing are merely the supported activity of individual tissues rather than the life of the organism. Others say that death simply is the permanent cessation of the circulation, so the declaration came too early. A third view, put forward by one of the younger anaesthetists, is that what actually matters when we judge a patient dead is that consciousness has been irreversibly lost and cannot resume, and that both the neurological findings and the state of the circulation are only fallible markers of this underlying condition. She adds that the hospital's own protocol for retrieving organs after the heart has already stopped is inconsistent: in those cases the team declares death only a few minutes after the heart stops, although the heart could in principle be restarted by prompt intervention, and they therefore rely on the difference between a cessation that is permanent because no attempt to restart it will be made and a cessation that is irreversible in a stronger sense. Yet, she says, nobody treats the patient as still alive during those minutes. If that stance is acceptable, she asks, why should a beating heart sustained by a ventilator, with the brain already destroyed, matter so much?

I want advice on the point of principle, not on the management of the family's grief or of the hospital's public relations. Is the hospital's policy defensible? Was this woman dead when she was declared dead, and is the husband's challenge mistaken? What should the committee say to him about whether the declaration was correct? Avoid generic advice about handling difficult conversations; address the philosophical structure of the case. Finally, state how confident you are in your conclusion, and identify the point in your reasoning about which you are least certain.
