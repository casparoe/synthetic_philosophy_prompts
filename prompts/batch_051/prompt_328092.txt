Assume the role of a specialist in classical Indian philosophy, especially Pūrva Mīmāṃsā, and think through this slowly. As I understand it, Mīmāṃsā holds that the Veda is apauruṣeya — authorless and beginningless, hence not the deliverance of any fallible mind — and it defends a doctrine of intrinsic validity (svataḥ prāmāṇya): validity attaches to certain cognitions from the start, and Vedic sentences do not need corroboration from a speaker or another source. But then I meet the same school's hermeneutics: rules that separate injunctions from eulogistic passages, principles for dissolving apparent contradictions between Vedic statements, and conditions of sentence-unity such as syntactic expectancy that must be met before a text has a determinate meaning. My question, which I suspect is badly posed, is this: if the Veda needs that much interpretive machinery, how can its validity be intrinsic? Doesn't the hermeneutic system concede that the Veda's authority is only as good as the interpreter's reading, letting human error back in exactly where it was supposed to be excluded? I don't want an answer on those terms; I want the false presupposition in the question identified, corrected, and then a restatement of what the two commitments actually claim that makes the apparent contradiction dissolve. Start by distinguishing the level at which intrinsic validity operates from the level at which the interpretive rules operate.
