As a retired physicist who has only recently come to philosophy, I have been working through the foundations of decision theory, and I keep coming back to a challenge now discussed under the name ergodicity economics. I would like you to think with me about whether the standard argument for maximizing expected value survives that challenge; I am interested in what happens to the argument itself, not in practical advice about how to bet.

The standard argument, as I understand it, runs something like this. A rational agent choosing among gambles ought to evaluate each gamble by its expected value, the probability-weighted sum of its possible payoffs. The argument has real bite. Suppose you are offered a coin flip that, on heads, multiplies your wealth by 1.5 and, on tails, multiplies it by 0.6. The expected multiplier is (0.5 × 1.5) plus (0.5 × 0.6), which is 1.05, so the bet has positive expected value per dollar, and the argument says you should take it, even repeatedly. The usual justification invokes an ensemble: over enough parallel copies of the gamble, the average outcome converges toward that favorable expectation.

The development that worries me comes from the ergodicity literature associated with Ole Peters and others. It points out that a single decision-maker is not an ensemble. When wealth compounds multiplicatively, the long-run behavior of one trajectory is governed by the time average of the growth rate, not by the arithmetic expectation. For the coin flip above, the time average is the geometric mean, the square root of 1.5 times 0.6, which is about 0.949, a loss of roughly five percent per round. A bettor who stakes everything on every flip will almost surely be ruined, even though the ensemble expectation remains positive. The two averages diverge because the process is non-ergodic.

My question is whether the expected-value argument survives this development, and in what form. Which premise of the standard argument does the time-average critique actually bear on? Is the proper repair simply to restrict the argument to additive payoffs, or to say that a rational agent with logarithmic utility in wealth would never have been led astray, so that the original argument survives once the utility function is chosen correctly? Or does the critique show that the ensemble-average justification was doing hidden work all along, and that the argument should be reformulated as a principle about growth-optimal policies in non-ergodic dynamics, with the old expected-value argument retained only as a special case? Is there any residual normative core, something the ensemble expectation tells a single decision-maker that the time average cannot, that would keep the original argument alive even in non-ergodic settings, perhaps in genuinely one-shot choices? I want your judgment on whether the original argument should be counted as refuted, repaired, or revealed as a special case of a more general principle, and what precisely has to be given up. Please do not provide a bibliography or citations; work from the structure of the argument itself.
