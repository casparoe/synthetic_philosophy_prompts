I am chair of the historical accuracy review panel for a mid-sized history and antiquities museum, the only such museum for several hundred miles, and roughly once a month a special-exhibition proposal, a donor-backed show, or an educational leaflet lands on my desk proposing that we present some claim about the deep past, with Atlantis, ancient-astronaut, and phantom-chronology material showing up most often. School groups build their units around our galleries, and our decisions are reviewed by a board that includes nonspecialists and at least one trustee who is currently pressing us to host a touring Atlantis exhibition, so I can no longer decide these one at a time by gut. Please write me a reusable decision procedure my panel can actually run, grounded in the best thinking about what separates historical revision from pseudohistory, but written for people without that theory. It should be a sequence of questions to ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of institutional values or professional judgment rather than evidence. It must handle the hard case cleanly: a genuine revision resting on new evidence must not be treated as pseudohistory merely because it cuts against the textbooks or our own gallery labels. It must also say what to do when a proposal is too vague to test, or when its proponents will not name anything that would change their minds. The procedure has to be workable in about half a day by three curators, a marketing officer, and a trustee, with no academic historian on call, and it has to yield a decision I can defend in front of the board, with the judgment calls flagged as judgment calls rather than dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three proposals and show the panel's reasoning at each step: a donor-backed touring show arguing that a lost Atlantic civilization seeded pyramid-building in both Egypt and Mexico because the monuments encode the same hidden astronomical knowledge; a Fomenko-style proposal that the accepted chronology of antiquity is off by roughly a millennium because most ancient sources are later fabrications and the professional consensus is sustained by institutional inertia and embarrassment; and a nearby university team's request to display new radiocarbon and stratigraphic evidence showing a local hill-fort is three centuries older than the textbooks say, contradicting two prominent scholars and one of our own labels. Make sure at least one of the three comes out as a decision I would rather not have to defend in public. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot settle. Write all of this in continuous prose, as if in a letter or a memo: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
