Early in A Mathematical Theory of Saving, Ramsey says that discounting later enjoyments in favour of earlier ones is ethically indefensible and comes from a weakness of the imagination rather than from any real argument. Yet ordinary policy does exactly that: at a 3 percent discount rate a prevented death in 2100 counts for about a tenth of a prevented death today, and at 5 percent for a few percent. But we don't treat a single person's future that way—if you're told you'll have a year of chronic pain in 2050, nobody says it barely matters because it's far off. So there's an asymmetry between how we treat future people and how we treat our own future selves. Is there a defensible reason for that, or is it just present bias dressed up as technical method?
