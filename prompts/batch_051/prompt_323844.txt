I've been teaching a course on the fall of the Roman Republic this semester, and the last seminar kept drifting into comparisons with present-day democracies. Between my students and I, we came up with six analogies that we now use as a running list: (1) the breakdown of unwritten norms, like the killings of the Gracchi and the street violence of Clodius's and Milo's gangs making force a normal political tool; (2) commanders with personal followings and effectively private armies, as with Marius and Sulla, whose soldiers came to be more loyal to the general than to the republic; (3) severe wealth and land concentration, with populares pushing redistribution and the optimates treating any reform as an attack on property; (4) emergency decrees like the senatus consultum ultimum that let magistrates claim the safety of the state to bypass normal legal protections, the standart example being Cicero executing the Catilinarian conspirators without trial; (5) erosion of norms about office-holding, from Marius's repeated consulships to Caesar's dictatorship for life; and (6) abuse of veto points, with the tribunate becoming a tool of factional obstruction rather than a shield for ordinary people.

I keep having the feeling that there's a seventh analogy out there that isn't on this list, and isn't just a version of one of the six—'strongmen use angry mobs' is really just number 2, and 'both eras were polarized' is too vague to be useful. Can you give me one more specific analogy between the stresses on the late Republic and those on a modern democracy? Say exactly what it captures that the six above don't, and be honest about whether it holds up when you look at it closely.
