Two people in my philosophy of mind study group got into it after a lecture on whether you can argue from what's conceivable to what's possible. The first one said the zombie case is the cleanest version of the point: if we can conceive of a being that is physically identical to a person, down to every neuron, but with no conscious experience at all, then such a being is at least possible, and that would mean the physical facts don't by themselves guarantee consciousness. The second one said this is exactly the kind of move that looks great until you test it against other cases: people once found it perfectly conceivable that water could exist without being H2O, but that's impossible, so conceivability by itself is a fact about our information, not about what's really possible. The first one answered that there's a difference between sloppy, pre-theoretic conceivability and what remains conceivable after you fix all the relevant facts and reason carefully: water stops being a counterexample once the chemistry is on the table, but no pile of physical detail about a brain seems to rule out the zombie, and that's why the case has force. The second one said that's still the same mistake one level up, because it moves from "I can't see the contradiction, even after thinking hard" to "there is no contradiction," and that inference is exactly what needs to be justified. The first one then said that if you refuse that inference across the board, you end up unable to say even mundane things like that you could have had eggs instead of toast this morning, or that this table could have been two feet to the left. The second one replied that the mundane cases are different because they're constrained by physics we already accept, whereas in the zombie case the whole question is wether physics is the whole story, so using conceivability there just assumes the thing at issue. It ended with the first one saying the physicalist still owes us an account of why the zombie is only conceivably, not possibly, real, and the second one saying the so-called gap is a gap in our concepts, not in the world. I genuinely couldn't tell who had the better of it.
