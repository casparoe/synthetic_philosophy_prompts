At lunch two people in my program got into it over the part of Two Dogmas of Empiricism where Quine argues that semantical rules for an artificial language don't help us understand analyticity unless we already understand analyticity (or some kindred notion). They had four distinct moves and I honestly couldn't refute either of them on the spot, becuase I kept seeing both sides.

Noah defended Quine: if you define analytic-in-L by stipulating semantical rules, someone can always ask why those rules count as semantical rather than arbitrary. The only non-arbitrary answer seems to be that they reflect meanings, and that presupposes the very notion Quine is challenging.

Mia said that only shows absolute, language-independent analyticity is circular. Carnap can define analyticity relative to a formal language, and that definition is not circular; it is just a syntactic/formal construct. You don't need to justify calling the rules semantical for the formal purpose.

Noah came back that then analyticity is a misnomer: it is just theoremhood in a chosen system. If you can make anything analytic by fiat, the notion has lost the epistemological content that made it interesting, so it can't explain the a priori.

Mia replied that this confuses the formal notion with its practical adoption. We do adopt languages, and within an adopted language some statements are knowable just by knowing the rules. The relative notion is all Carnap needs. Quine's demand for a non-relative notion is too strong.

Please adjudicate move by move: which moves are right, which are rhetoric, and who is more right overall? I especially want to know whether Noah's circularity charge applies to relative analyticity or only to absolute analyticity, and whether Mia's adoption point actually answers the arbitrariness worry.
