For the final exercise in my jurisprudence seminar, I want you to think about the absurdity doctrine — the principle that a court may decline to apply a statute's clear words when doing so would produce an outcome the enacting legislature could not plausibly have intended — and, more searchingly, about whether that doctrine has any principled boundary.

Consider this provision, from a state anti-theft statute enacted in 1969, before keyless ignition systems existed:

"No person shall leave a motor vehicle unattended upon any public way unless the person has first stopped the engine, locked the ignition, and removed the key from the ignition."

Now the case. A driver parks a late-model vehicle on a public street on a hot afternoon and leaves it unattended by any person (her dog remains inside) for several minutes while she goes into a pharmacy. The vehicle has no ignition key and no ignition slot: it is started, stopped, and locked by an electronic fob, and its immobilizer prevents the car from being put into gear unless the fob is inside it. The driver leaves the engine running so the air conditioning continues to run, and she locks the doors and takes the fob with her. She has not stopped the engine; there is no key to remove; and the vehicle is, in the sense the 1969 legislature cared about, as secure against theft as a conventional car left with the engine off and the key removed.

Your task is to say how a court should decide whether she has violated the provision, and why. Work through the main interpretive approaches in turn: the ordinary meaning of the words to a reader in 1969; the ordinary meaning of those same words to a reasonable reader today; the evident purpose of the provision; what the enacting legislature would have said in 1969 if asked about keyless vehicles; and what a reasonable reader, then and now, would take the law to require of this driver. Then state which approach should govern, and defend it.

Finally, press on the deeper question. Is this a case for the absurdity doctrine at all, or is it better handled as ordinary interpretation of a statute overtaken by technological change? Notice the asymmetry in the three requirements: two of them are impossible for this driver to perform as written, while the first — stop the engine — is perfectly possible, only dangerous to the dog and arguably unnecessary to the anti-theft aim. For comparison, consider the driver of the same vehicle who stops the engine, locks the doors, and takes the fob, but is prosecuted for failing to remove a key that does not exist; is the doctrine's application there different in kind, or only in degree? Your analysis should force you to state a principled test for when the absurdity doctrine applies. You should also face, candidly, the possibility that no such test survives these cases — and if it does not, say what that reveals about whether the doctrine is a principled part of interpretation or a device for judicial correction dressed in the language of restraint.
