I got put on my church's doctrine committee about a month ago, and we have spent the last three meetings stuck on a dispute about our founding document. The basis clause, adopted in 1884, reads: "The only rule of faith and practice for this congregation is the Bible. In every controversy over what to believe or how to live, the text read in its whole context is the highest court of appeal. No confession, council, officer, or vote of the congregation may bind a member's conscience to a reading the text itself will not support."

The case that set this off is weirdly modern. The elders hired a programmer to fine-tune a language model on the senior pastor's sermons and the church's old newsletters, and then published the model's verse-by-verse notes as the official study notes in our church app. Hardly any of the glosses were checked by a human before publication; the pastor keeps saying the thing just reports what the text says, without a human interpreter. A woman who has taught the adult Genesis class for years has now been barred from teaching because the app's note on Genesis 6 says the sons of God were angelic beings and calls that the plain reading. She says the phrase could just as well refer to the line of Seth, and in any case the passage is famously unsettled, so calling it the plain reading is doing a lot of work.

I'm not asking you to settle what Genesis 6 means. I want to know what the basis clause permits. Do the elders' actions fall inside it, or are they binding people's consciences in exactly the way the clause forbids? Work through what the quoted words require, what the clause is for, what its 1884 writers would have said if they'd been asked about unreviewed machine-generated official notes, and what a reasonable member would understand. Then commit: which approach should govern here, and why?
