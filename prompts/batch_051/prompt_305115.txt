For next term's graduate seminar on the philosophy and cognitive science of delusion, I want to give my students a single handout before they read anything: a specification of what a theory of delusion is actually required to get right. Last year we spent weeks pitting the two-factor theory against doxastic and anti-doxastic accounts, and the argument kept slipping because nobody had written down the adequacy conditions we were all implicitly enforcing. I would like that list written down properly. An adequate theory must handle the textbook monothematic cases — Capgras, Cotard, mirrored-self misidentification — without treating them as the whole job; it should also take a position on the messier boundary phenomena such as anosognosia for paralysis and confabulation, even if the correct verdict is that some of these are not delusions proper. It must respect rather than collapse a series of distinctions, and for each distinction the case that forces it should be made explicit: formation versus maintenance, delusion versus ordinary motivated false belief, delusion versus overvalued idea, and belief versus mere avowal or acceptance. And it must respect the empirical fixed points: the role of an anomalous experience as the first factor, the resistance of the conviction to counter-evidence, the circumscribed character of monothematic delusions, and the partial dissociation between what patients assert and what they do — the phenomenon often called double bookkeeping.

For every requirement, say what consideration makes it a requirement rather than a preference, and identify which cases would be mishandled if it were dropped. Then classify the requirements as negotiable or non-negotiable, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude the standard contenders — in particular, where that leaves one-factor accounts and simple doxasticism. Finally, say whether any two requirements are jointly unsatisfiable: I am especially interested in whether the demand to explain the sincere assertion of the delusional content and the demand to explain the behavioral and inferential insensitivity to that very content can be met by one account, or whether their tension is precisely what pushes the field toward two-factor and anti-doxastic views. Keep the scorecard of the contenders brief; the criteria and their internal tensions are the deliverable.
