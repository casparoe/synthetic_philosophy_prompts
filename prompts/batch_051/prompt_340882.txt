I am a novelist, and a character I am drafting has broken a piece of my working vocabulary. After a small brain injury, he reports pain the way a person might report the weather: he says a needle or a burn "hurts," he can tell you it is sharp, hot, and in his left palm, and he can even rate its intensity—but he does not wince, does not pull away, and when someone asks whether it feels bad he says no, it simply is. I gather this is close to what clinicians call pain asymbolia. I had always assumed that the badness of pain was part of pain itself, and now I no longer know what I mean by "suffering."

Give me a working definition of suffering—or, if you think "suffering" is the wrong target, of pain's badness, understood as the property in virtue of which a pain is bad for the one in it—that gets these cases right:

- A child with a broken arm, sobbing, cradling it, and desperate for the hurting to stop.
- My character above, who says a burn is painful and can specify that it is sharp and strong, but leaves his hand where it is and is not in the least distressed by it.
- The leaden ache in someone's chest during grief, with no injured tissue, but something she feels as awful and wants gone.
- The burning muscles of a marathoner in the final mile, intense and, in a sense, wanted—welcomed as the feel of effort paying off.
- A person who has arranged to be hurt in a scene he chose, and who flinches and wants the sting to stop for the seconds it lasts, while nevertheless counting the whole episode as good and wanting to repeat it.

I am fairly confident the first and third are suffering, and that the second is not, though I would still call it pain. I have no settled view about the fourth and fifth. Propose the definition, run it through all five cases, and say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer; then revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. I am not asking for clinical references, just for a distinction I can write from.

Please answer in exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. A structure that fits the task is: (1) the proposed definition; (2) how it handles the clear cases; (3) how it handles the asymbolia case; (4) how it handles the two borderline cases, including any failure; (5) the revised definition or the defense.
