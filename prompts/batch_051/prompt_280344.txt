Suppose a medium-sized democracy (say 40 million people, common-law-ish courts, a written bill of rights) amends its constitution tomorrow so that legal personhood is no longer binary or automatic for humans. Instead it is a graded, relational status inspired by ubuntu—the idea that one becomes a person through other people. A newborn has a thin floor of protections (against being killed, tortured, or grossly neglected) but not full standing; full standing—suing, voting, owning, contracting, being heard as a victim in criminal proceedings—is accrued through durable relationships of care, obligation, and reciprocity. A civil registry assigns and updates each person's score, with points for recognized caring relationships and deductions for sustained isolation or pure one-way dependence.

Predict how this actually runs fifty years in. I want the mechanism-level story, not a verdict on whether the reform is good: which relationships get counted and which get gamed or manufactured, how courts and registry officials cope with a scalar property where there used to be a bright line, what happens to the elderly, severely disabled people, prisoners, migrants, hermits, and people whose entire relational life is online, and whether it drifts back into a de facto binary with extra paperwork or turns into something its designers didn't intend. Mark which parts of your prediction are robust to implementation details, which are fragile, and what unstated assumption would most change the answer.
