Act as a moral and political philosopher who specialises in fairness, reciprocity, and the ethics of public goods. Please think slowly and carefully: before you commit to any principle, brainstorm two or three candidate principles and test each one against the cases below, including at least one case where it fails.

I am trying to work out what I actually think about free riding — when, if ever, it is wrong to take the benefits of a cooperative practice without contributing to it. I have gone on my gut about a series of cases, and they feel mostly coherent but not entirely. Here they are.

1. Someone has listened to a listener-funded classical radio station almost daily for a decade, can comfortably afford the annual subscription, and has never given a penny. I think this is wrong, though only mildly.
2. Someone takes a job whose salary, holidays, and conditions are set by a union-negotiated collective agreement, and then declines to join the union or pay any equivalent contribution, though membership is optional. I think this is wrong, and more clearly wrong than the radio case, but I cannot immediately say why.
3. Someone slips onto a bus without paying, on a route that would run anyway and has empty seats. I think this is wrong.
4. On a university group project, one member contributes nothing all term but accepts the shared mark. I think this is wrong, and worse than the bus and radio cases.
5. A friend has eaten dinner at my house, and at our other friends' houses, for years, but has never once hosted — not because of poverty, a cramped flat, or caring duties, but simply from disinclination. I think this is wrong in a small way, though I hesitate over whether it is really a moral failing or merely bad manners.
6. A recluse lives alone in a remote cabin and contributes nothing to any public scheme, but benefits in a diffuse way from roads, national defence, and the institutions that keep everyday life stable. I do not think he does anything wrong, and I do not think he should be forced to contribute.
7. A citizen never votes, never attends local meetings, and never writes to an elected representative, but enjoys the benefits of a stable democracy. I do not think this is wrong — or only trivially so. Yet I notice that this verdict sits uneasily beside case 1, since both involve voluntarily receiving the fruits of a large collective practice while contributing nothing.

Is there a single principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts, or most of them? If so, state it precisely; show how it handles each case; and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which I should overturn. If there is no clean fit, tell me which verdicts are doing the damage and what the cheapest repairs would be.

I keep reaching for this rough principle: one may not knowingly and voluntarily accept the benefits of a cooperative practice while refusing to bear one's fair share of its burdens, where a benefit counts as accepted only if one could cheaply have avoided it. But I suspect that 'cooperative practice' and 'accept' are doing too much hidden work, and that the principle misfires somewhere. Test it properly, and repair or replace it as needed.

Finally, apply whatever principle you settle on to a case I have not listed: a resident of a block of flats refuses to pay into the building's voluntary lift-repair fund but uses the lift every day; the lift is repaired anyway because the other residents have paid.

In your answer, please separate what is genuinely settled among philosophers who work on this topic from what is contested, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus. Please write in British English.
