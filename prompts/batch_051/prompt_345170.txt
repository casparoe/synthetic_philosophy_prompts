I'm a journalist on the science and ideas desk, working on a piece about the weird gap between the tidy rules in style guides and the actual, patchy mess of English verb morphology. At a press I sometimes write for there's a fight brewing over this. Their style guide has a rule, exact wording: "Where a word has both a regular and an irregular form in current use, prefer the regular form. An irregular form may stand only when no regular alternative is in current use." Everyone knows it was written for pairs like burned/burnt or dived/dove, where two forms are both circulating. Now a children's author has sent in a story where a five-year-old character says "I goed to the store." The copyeditor changed it to "went." The author is pointing at the style rule: "goed" is the regular past tense, "went" is the irregular, suppletive form — historically from a different verb entirely, not just an odd ending — and the rule says prefer the regular whenever there's a choice. The editors are stuck becuase "goed" isn't in adult English; it's an overregularization kids produce and mostly grow out of. Walk me through what the rule, read strictly, tells the editor to do; what the people who drafted the rule would have said if someone had put this exact case to them; and which reading a reasonable editor should follow. Then tell me what this dispute shows about whether the style guide's rule is really a rule of grammar, or just a policy for choosing among established variants. Keep it anchored in go/went rather than general editorial philosophy.
