Adverse possession is the legal rule that someone who openly occupies and uses another person's land for a long enough period, without permission, can eventually acquire legal title, and the original owner's title is simply extinguished. Defenders usually justify this by appeal to certainty and stale claims. But the rule also seem to take a stand on whose attachment to the land matters. If people tend to value things they already possess more than identical things they do not yet own (the endowment effect, loss aversion), then the title-holder's loss is exactly the sort of thing those theories say people feel most keenly; yet the law says that attachment has lapsed, while the occupant's attachment, built up through actual use, wins. What view of legitimate preference does this practice presuppose? Does it treat the owner's valuation as a mere bias and the occupant's as a real interest, and is there a stable way to draw that line? Or is adverse possession better read as a bookkeeping rule that has nothing deep to say about value?
