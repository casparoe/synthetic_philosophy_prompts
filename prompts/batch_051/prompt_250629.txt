Title: Aumann's theorem is not a conversational norm

People in rationalist circles often reach for Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree theorem as if it says two rational people with common priors can't end up disagreeing. That's not quite the result. What Aumann actually showed, if I have this right, is narrower: if two Bayesian agents have a common prior, and their posterior probabilities for a given event are common knowledge, then those posterior probabilities must be equal. The phrase "common knowledge" is doing alot of work. In the theorem it doesn't mean "we both expressed our views and heard each other." It means the posterior itself is public in the formal sense: each agent knows the other's posterior, knows the other knows it, and so on up the hierarchy.

That condition is almost never met in real arguments. When I put the probability of some AI catastrophe this century at around a third and you put it at a tenth, your posterior is not common knowledge to me in the formal sense, and mine is not common knowledge to you. I observe your stated number, but I don't observe the information partition that produced it, and I can't distinguish your posterior from the posterior you would have if you were conditioning on a different model of the world. So the theorem does not say that we are both irrational if we fail to converge after an hour of arguing. It says that if our actual posteriors really were common knowledge in the technical sense, the common-prior assumption would force them to be identical, which means one of the premises has to give.

This is not a live dispute about how rational people should update when they hear a disagreement; that's the part I want checked. The theorem looks like an equilibrium condition, not a conversational norm. It characterizes a state of affairs that cannot obtain under certain assumptions, but it doesn't tell either agent how much weight to put on the other person's stated belief in a non-common-knowledge setting. The standard rationalist inference — "if we had the same priors and were both rational, we would already agree, so the fact that we disagree is evidence that one of us is irrational" — seems like a use/mention confusion between the theorem's conditions and everyday argument. But maybe I'm attacking a strawman and nobody who understands the proof ever actually makes that move.

End draft.

Is the equilibrium-condition-versus-conversational-norm contrast a real distinction or just vibes? And is the last paragraph too confident?
