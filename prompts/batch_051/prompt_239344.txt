I work in the policy unit of a small country's finance ministry. Every year when the World Happiness Report comes out, our minister either panics that we dropped a few places or wants a press release about how we rose, and we get a pile of half-baked proposals for "getting our ranking up": changing the survey we use, launching a volunteering campaign, investing in mental health because it feeds into the report's health variable, finding ways to get more people to give high numbers on whatever question the report uses, and so on. I want us to stop reacting case by case. Could you write a standing decision procedure for evaluating any proposal whose main justification is our World Happiness Report ranking? The procedure has to be usable by policy analysts who aren't philosophers or happiness researchers and produce a briefing note in about a day. It should included: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where in the sequence we stop and reject; and where we stop and hand the call to an elected official because the question is a values call rather than an empirical one. Ground it in the distinction between experienced happiness (day-to-day affect), overall life evaluation (the Cantril ladder thing the ranking actually tracks), and eudaimonic notions like meaning or flourishing — and in the fact that the report largely explains countries' ladder scores through six predictors: GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceived corruption, plus an unexplained residual. Don't assume my analysts know any of that already — build the distinctions into the procedure itself.

Then run the procedure on three concrete proposals so I can see it working: (a) replacing our current national well-being survey with the exact Cantril ladder question the report uses and making that the headline measure of national progress; (b) running a government-backed campaign to encourage charitable donations and volunteering specifically because the report counts generosity; and (c) changing survey fieldwork practices (when and how interviews are done, who gets sampled) to raise our ladder score without any underlying change in citizens' lives. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as measurement, tell me one place where the procedure returns a no the ministry will not like, and flag a kind of proposal this procedure is simply not equipped to handle.
