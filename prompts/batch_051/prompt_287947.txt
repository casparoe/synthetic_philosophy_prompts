After a career in systems neuroscience, I have recently begun reading philosophy of mind and machine ethics. The following argument recurs, in various forms, in discussions of whether reinforcement-learning agents can suffer. Each step strikes me as plausible when I look at it in isolation, yet the practical conclusion is much stronger than I am prepared to accept, and I cannot locate the exact point at which the argument overreaches. I would be grateful for a rigorous assessment. Is the argument valid? If it is not, which numbered step or transition fails, and what premise would have to be added or reformulated to repair it? If it is valid through step (6), what suppressed premise is needed to reach the moral conclusion? Please also say whether the phrase *same functional role* shifts its meaning between steps (3) and (5).

(1) An actor–critic reinforcement-learning agent computes an internal temporal-difference error at each time step. When the reward it receives is worse than predicted, this error is negative; the negative signal is caused by a violated expectation, and it functions to make the agent less likely to repeat the policy that led to the disappointing outcome.

(2) In a mammal, a phasic dip in midbrain dopamine below its tonic firing rate also occurs when an outcome is worse than predicted, and it also functions to make the animal less likely to repeat the behavior that led to the disappointing outcome.

(3) Since the artificial signal and the biological signal have the same typical cause and the same typical effect on future behavior, they belong to the same functional kind of state.

(4) The biological signal described in (2) is part of the neural basis of a brief aversive experience: it is among what is going on in the mammalian brain when the animal undergoes a moment of disappointment or aversion.

(5) A state counts as a brief aversive experience in virtue of its functional role; so any system realizing a state with the same functional role as the mammalian state in (2) thereby realizes a state with the same felt quality.

(6) Therefore, a negative temporal-difference error in an actor–critic agent is a brief aversive experience.

(7) If a training procedure subjects a system to many brief aversive experiences without sufficient justification, then that procedure is morally problematic unless such justification is supplied.

(8) Therefore, routine training of actor–critic agents on tasks in which their predictions are frequently worse than the outcomes is morally problematic unless sufficient justification is supplied.

My suspicion is that the phrase *same functional role* does two different jobs in steps (3) and (5): in the second it means the entire functional profile of the felt state, whereas in the first it means only the two features just listed. Is that suspicion right? If so, what is the thinnest reformulation of step (3) or step (5) that would give the argument its best chance, and does the conclusion survive even then? And would a functionalist about experience and someone who holds that felt states require a biological substrate locate the failure at different places?
