A friend and I spent an evening arguing about whether the alignment problem — the problem of building advanced AI systems that robustly do what their operators intend across the situations they will actually meet, rather than optimizing a misspecified proxy — is solvable in principle. He insisted that "in principle" carries real weight: a problem can be solvable in principle without being solvable in practice, just as a theorem can be provable without anyone yet possessing a proof. I replied that for alignment the qualifier often does no work, because it slides among at least three claims: that a solution is logically or mathematically possible; that it is physically realizable in this universe; and that it is reachable by the kind of institutions, compute budgets, and training procedures that could actually exist. On the first reading the claim may be too weak to matter; on the last it looks like an empirical bet about future engineering dressed as a philosophical one. We ended without agreeing on what the phrase means, or on whether anything could settle the question.

Treat me as the skeptic. Take the claim that alignment is solvable in principle but perhaps not in practice. First, separate the readings of "in principle" on which the claim is neither vacuous nor a disguised empirical prediction. Then, for the most defensible such reading, design the strongest test or programme of evidence one could carry out now or in the near future. Be concrete about what would be demonstrated or measured, what result would count in favour, what result would count against, and what a positive result would actually license. In particular: would a positive result establish solvability-in-principle, or only some weaker local claim about a particular model, task, or training setup? And is there any observation that could even in theory refute the in-principle claim, or can a negative result always be deflected as a failure of the attempt rather than the insufficiency of the idea? Say plainly what part of the claim no test can reach.
