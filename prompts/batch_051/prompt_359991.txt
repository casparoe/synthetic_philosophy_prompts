Two people in the reading group I joined after I retired — I was a field ecologist, so this shouldve been my turf — got into it about whether an ant colony is one organism or a lot of them. Marta and Dave went back and forth for a while, and neither one budged.

Dave said an ant colony is literally an organism. The queen is the germline and the workers are the body; the colony grows, sends out reproductives, keeps the nest temperature steady, and defends itself as a unit. Marta said that's a good analogy but it's still an analogy. Each worker is its own organism: it has a full genome, it feeds itself, and in some species workers can lay viable eggs. Calling workers somatic cells is a metaphor, not something you discover under a microscope.

Dave came back with: if being physically separate is what bothers you, then a big aspen clone shouldn't count as one organism either, but biologists do talk about those clones as single organisms, with all the stems joined by one root system. Marta said no, an aspen clone is still physically connected and it's genetically identical; an ant colony is neither — the workers are sisters, not clones, and there's no shared body. She added that this is why you have to distinguish a genetic individual from a physiological one.

Dave said fine, but the superorganism view was never about genetic identity. It's not shared DNA that makes my body one organism; it's that the parts share a reproductive fate and are tightly integrated. By that test, a colony is more unified than a lot of clonal plants.

Marta said only if you ignore the cracks: workers can sometimes reproduce, colonies can replace queens, and workers police each other's eggs, so the "shared reproductive fate" is only partial. And anyway, if functional integration is your criterion, then a coral holobiont — the coral animal plus its zooxanthellae and its whole microbial community — is one organism, even though it's several lineages, several species, and a symbiosis. That just stretches the word until it covers any tightly coupled ecosystem.

Dave said maybe it should stretch that far; there are researchers who say exactly that about corals, and the real mistake is thinking "organism" has one sharp definition instead of a few criteria that pull apart.

They asked me to settle it. I never could. Please go through this move by move and say who is right about which specific claims, and what both of them are missing. I'd like the answer in American English.
