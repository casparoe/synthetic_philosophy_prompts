I'm a grad student teaching one fifty-minute intro lecture on the regress problem. The students have read a short bit on knowledge as justified true belief, but they have no prior exposure to foundationalism, coherentism, or infinitism. I'd like to frame the whole thing with Sextus Empiricus's Outlines of Pyrrhonism, specifically the Five Modes. In that text, Sextus says that when a dispute arises, if someone keeps demanding a reason for the thing offered as a reason, you either go on forever, or stop by making an arbitrary assumption, or circle back and use something already in dispute. He also includes disagreement and relativity as ways in which the conflict looks undecidable. I don't plan to quote the passage at length; I want to use that sequence as the engine.

Plan the lecture. What is the order the ideas must go in so the regress feels like a trap the students have fallen into themselves? What single example should carry the whole explanation, and what must it have and lack? I'm less interested in a script than in the dependancy structure: which idea can't land until which earlier idea is in place, and which misconception is most likely at each step. Start by brainstorming the misconceptions, then build the plan around them.
