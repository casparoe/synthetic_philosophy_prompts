I cover courts and constitutions, and I've been carrying around a little theory I can't shake. I call it the clerk theory of constitutional gaps. The claim: when the rules run out — a hung election, a disputed succession, a coalition that can't be formed — effective power doesn't go to the most legitimate actor, or the most popular one, or even the strongest. It drains down to whoever still has a clear, boring, mechanical rulebook and controls some small lever in the machine: the official who can certify a result, convene a body, or keep signing the checks. Big constitutional silences get resolved by tiny administrative certainties. A legislature can argue for weeks about who should govern, but in the meantime the gap is actually governed by the clerk who publishes the agenda and the comptroller who won't release a payment without the right form. It explains a lot of what I see, and it also worries me, because it makes legitimacy during a gap kind of accidental. Its probably been said before, and its probably wrong in some basic way. Give me the strongest version of this idea, tell me if it already exists under another name, and say where it actually breaks — specifically, can you predict which boring person wins, or does that prediction just collapse back into whoever has the guns or the money?
