I'm taking an applied ethics module and we've been chewing on the lying vs misleading distinction — the idea that lying involves asserting something you believe false, whereas you can mislead someone using only individually true statements, through omission or by letting them draw the inference you've set up. Last week I got into a late-night argument about whether professional codes that only ban false statements are therefore blind to half the wrongs they exist to stop.

The code were looking at is from a fictional body, the Society of Certified Data Advisors, and its Rule 4.2 reads:

"A member shall not, in the course of professional practice, knowingly make a false statement of material fact to a person who is entitled to rely on the member's judgment."

Here's the case that came up. A member builds a customer-facing chatbot for her practice. She configures it so it will only ever output statements she has personally checked are true, but she curates the source material so that, for the questions clients actually ask, the bot gives true but systematically incomplete answers. For instance, when a client asks whether a data-sharing arrangement is compliant, the bot says "Our review found no violation of the consent requirements." That sentence is true, but the bot was deliberately built to surface the one favorable review and never mention the two reviews that flag unresolved issues. The member didn't author a single false sentence; in fact she forbade the bot from ever asserting anything false. She just selected which true sentences would be said and which true sentences would never be said. The drafters wrote Rule 4.2 in the mid-1990s and clearly didn't forsee a member using a software intermediary they'd configured themselves.

Could you act as an expert on professional ethics and the philosophy of language and work through Rule 4.2 as applied to this case? I want to know what the plain wording gives, what the purpose of the rule gives, how a reasonable client reading the handbook would take it, and what the drafters would have said if asked about this case — and which of those approaches should win here and why. Then give an all-things-considered verdict on whether the member violated the rule, and whether this case shows that the lying/misleading distinction is doing real moral work or just exposing a badly drafted rule. Please brainstorm the strongest arguments on each side before settling.
