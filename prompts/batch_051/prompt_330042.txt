I’m a novelist, not a philosopher, and I’ve been roped into giving the toast at the retirement dinner of the editor who ran a small literary press for the last thirty-odd years and has been my editor for most of my books. The room will be a mix of publishing people who know the books and the balance sheets, and her family, who mostly know that she never turned the heating on and kept every scrap of wrapping paper. I want the toast to be built around a distinction that’s been nagging at me whenever I think about her, and I’m not even sure I’ve got the terms right: the difference between being frugal and promoting frugality. On the first, deontological-ish reading, frugality is just a requirement on you — waste is something you personally don’t do, full stop, whatever the consequences. On the second, consequentialist-ish reading, what actually matters is that resources get used well in the round, so the question is whether you’re making the world as a whole less wasteful, which might mean spending money to save money, or nagging other people and institutions to be careful, or honestly even being a bit lavish at the right moment if that somehow led to less waste later. She’s obviously been a bit of a martyr to the first reading all her life, and half the people in the room have stories about it. The trickier bit is that on the second reading she was sometimes a disaster — some of her economies cost the press far more than they saved, authors drifted away to other houses over tiny acts of penny-pinching, and a couple of the judgement calls she made look less like promoting frugality than like refusing to spend now and paying twice over later. I don’t want the toast to be a lecture or an airing of grievances; it has to land as real affection, and it can be funny. But I do want the philosophical distinction to do the structural work — it should shape what I can and cannot honestly say about her, and I’d rather the speech earn it's ending than smooth it all over. Could you write it? Something around five minutes when read aloud, and please just give me the speech itself as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no stage directions in brackets; just the words I’d say, like a letter read out loud.
