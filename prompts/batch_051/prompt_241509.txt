Give me a working definition of "random" as applied to a finite binary string — a definition that returns a verdict, random or not, for any given string. I want it tested against the cases below, and I want you to tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer and either revise it or defend the surprising verdict. I suspect the ordinary word does two jobs: one about how the string was produced (a chance process), and one about the string itself (patternlessness, incompressibility). If you agree, give me two definitions and say which cases move between them.

The cases:

1. 00000000000000000000 (twenty zeros). I say not random, even though a fair coin could output it; fair production shouldn't make every output random.

2. 01010101010101010101. Not random; it has a two-word description, "print 01 ten times."

3. 10011000111011101001, which I got by flipping a coin just now. I want to say random, but I can't name a feature of the string itself that the first two lack except that I don't know a short description of it.

4. The first 100 bits of the binary expansion of pi. They are fixed by a short deterministic rule, yet they look statistically patternless. I lean not random; but that seems to make randomness a fact about origins rather than about the string.

5. A 100-bit output of a standard pseudorandom generator seeded with 64 bits. It passes the usual statistical tests, but is fully determined by 64 bits of input. I lean not random, or "practically random but not really." Your definition should say which.

6. A 100-bit string with no description shorter than itself — that is, no program shorter than the string prints it. I lean random: if no shorter description exists, what would make it nonrandom? But on this definition most strings turn out random, and no particular string's status can be verified in practice.

7. The one-bit string "1". Give a definite answer either way, and say why the concept feels degenerate at this limit.

8. A 100-bit string from a quantum random-number generator that uses photon arrival times. This is my paradigm case of really random. Now consider the identical string produced, unbeknownst to you, by a deterministic simulation of quantum noise. Does your verdict change? I think it shouldn't, but case 4 suggests it might.

9. The first 100 bits of the pattern 1, 10, 100, 1000, and so on (each block is a 1 followed by a growing run of zeros). Nonperiodic but trivial to generate, and surely not random. I include it to force your definition to catch nonperiodic patterns, not just periodic ones.

State the definition or definitions, run them through all nine cases, then say which of your verdicts is least secure and whether you would revise the definition or bite the bullet on that verdict.
