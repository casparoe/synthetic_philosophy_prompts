I retired from a career in physical chemistry about four years ago and have been slowly working through the existentialists and some of the religious writers who precede them. Lately I've been stuck on Pascal's Pensées, especially the fragments about diversion. As I understand him (reading an old translation and probably mangling it), he says something like: people cannot bear to sit alone in a room because then there is nothing to distract them from their own condition, so they chase hunting, gambling, conversation, war, and the rest of the noise of life in order not to feel the void that is already there. That description feels true to me, but I keep noticing cases where it seems to coarse.

My verdicts, for what they're worth, are these. After I finished the last big project of my career—a decade of work that had given shape to my days—there was a party in my honor, and in the middle of it I felt something hollow open up, almost like the project had been a lid I'd been standing on. A quiet Sunday with no tasks, no company, and no screens often brings a restless kind of dread in my chest, and I will reach for a newspaper or a puzzle at once. But during my recovery from a back surgery last year, when I couldn't read much and just sat watching light move across the wall, the blankness did not feel like dread; it felt wide and almost pleasant. And I've had moments at a telescope eyepiece, waiting for a transit, where the emptiness of the sky and the emptiness of my own concerns seemed to be the same absence, but not a threatening one. Yet Pascal would presumably say the telescope and the puzzle are just more diversion, since they keep me from the bare fact of myself.

So I have a collection of sensations that all involve a kind of felt void or emptiness: the party after the achievement, the Sunday restlessness, the post-surgery blankness, the sky at the eyepiece. I want to know whether there is a principled way to sort them. Is there one underlying phenomenon with different tones, or are there two kinds of emptiness—the one that drives you to distraction and the one that seems available only after distraction drops away? What distinguishes them, and does Pascal's account of diversion capture the difference, or does it force me to call the peaceful cases self-deception? If you can articulate the principle that fits my verdicts, tell me which of my verdicts it preserves and which it overturns, and what it says about a case I haven't settled: sitting through two hours of mild chronic pain with nothing to do, which felt empty but also sharply present, neither restful nor anxious.
