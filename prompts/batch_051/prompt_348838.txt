I got stuck last night mediating a two-hour argument between my roommate and my brother, and I still can't tell who's right about what. The topic was whether a life with lots of striving and struggle but also lots of pain could be better than a life where you've basically rid yourself of craving and suffering but dont accomplish much. My brother has been reading about tranquilism — the view that only the absence of craving and suffering ultimately matters — and my roommate thinks it's absurd. Here's roughly how it went:

1. My brother: Imagine two people. A has pain, desire, failure, but also writes a book, falls in love, travels, builds things. B has no unfulfilled cravings, no pain, but spends years sitting on a porch, occasionally eating rice. If B is genuinely untroubled and not bored, B's life is better, because suffering and craving are what make lives bad. Everything else is neutral filler.
2. My roommate: That's not neutral filler. If all you do is sit on a porch, your life is missing positive goods — achievement, love, beauty, understanding. You can't just ignore them and call them "neutral."
3. My brother: You're smuggling in the idea that those positive goods have value even when they come with suffering. If you remove the suffering, those goods are fine too — they're just not necessary for a good life. The point is you don't need them.
4. My roommate: But that argument proves too much. A rock has no suffering and no craving. On your view a rock's existence is better than any human life that contains a stubbed toe. You need at least consciousness or something for welfare.
5. My brother: Fine — B is conscious, just contented. A rock isn't conscious, so it doesn't have welfare at all. B does. The comparison is between conscious lives, not between life and nonlife.
6. My roommate: Then what about a person in a permanent opiate dream, with no craving and no pain but also no contact with reality? Is that the ideal life?
7. My brother: If it's genuinely free of suffering and craving, and not mixed with hidden cravings or withdrawal, then yes — or at least it's not worse than the striving life.
8. My roommate: That's absurd. You're treating lives like containers where only negative states count, and the positive states are just decorations. Why call the absence of something good better than its presence?

Please go through this move by move and tell me who's right at each step, where each of them is overreaching or conflating things, and whether tranquilism can survive step 8 as my brother states it. Also, say what argument or consideration would change your mind about the overall verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
