Write the desiderata for a theory of causation, with causation by omission as the load-bearing case. Give me the specification before any scorecard: an explicit list of what any adequate theory has to deliver, the case or consideration behind each requirement, and a mark against each of whether it is non-negotiable or only a demand from one camp. Fix the target as a theory of ordinary causal attribution, one that covers how causal claims are generated, explained, criticised, and relied on in everyday, legal, and moral contexts as well as in physics.

At minimum, make the theory pass these pressure tests, and add any further requirements you judge load-bearing.

1. The neglected plant. A gardener does not water a plant, and it dies. The theory must return a clear verdict on whether the gardener's failure to water caused the death, and must say what in the world makes that verdict true.

2. The irrelevant omission. A stranger on the other side of town also did not water the plant. The theory must explain why we cite the gardener's omission and not the stranger's, and it cannot settle the matter merely by calling the stranger's omission a cause that is conversationally out of place. It must give the principled difference or justify treating both as causes.

3. Joint omissions as components. Two engineers each fail to close one of two floodgates, and the flood requires both gates open. The theory must handle absences acting as components of a jointly sufficient condition, and must say whether the standard counterfactual test of cause-in-fact needs to be replaced or only supplemented here.

4. The ineffective withheld treatment. A doctor withholds a drug that, as things stand, would not have saved the patient anyway. The theory must say whether the but-for test is working correctly when it denies causation here, or whether something like a lost-chance or NESS-style criterion does better, and it must do so without quietly giving omissions a special exemption.

5. Duty and the passer-by. Two people each fail to water a plant; one had promised to water it and the other is a passer-by with no connection to it. The theory must say whether the promisor's omission is causally different or only normatively different, and whether a norm-free theory can still find a relevant omission. If the theory appeals to normality, default behaviour, or expected events, it must explain what fixes the default without circular appeal to what caused the outcome.

6. The doing/allowing boundary. The theory must either identify a purely causal difference between commissions and omissions or locate the felt asymmetry, if any, in the normative domain rather than the metaphysical one. It must not let a moral distinction do causal work without saying so.

7. The relata. The theory must state what its causal relata are — events, facts, processes, absences, contrast classes — and take responsibility for the choice. If causes can be absences, it must say how an absence enters the causal structure of the world; if they cannot, it must say what makes the true omission claims true.

Then flag any subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied. I especially want a verdict on this package: omissions are genuine causes; causal relata must be something more substantial than non-occurrences; and there is a principled, non-normative cutoff between relevant and irrelevant omissions. Say whether all three can be had at once, and if not, which one must give.

Finish with a short scorecard: how a simple counterfactual account, a physical process view, a norm- or default-relative account, and a structural equations account each do against the list, and which single requirement does the most work eliminating candidates.

Throughout, separate what is settled among people working on causation from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as consensus. No bibliography, citations, or reading list; I want the reasoning, not the sources.
