I was reading the fourth book of Origen's De Principiis and he has this argument I keep turning over. He notices that in Genesis you get morning and evening, the first day and the second day, before God even makes the sun and moon and stars. And he says look, this is supposed to be a signal. The text is divinely inspired and can't just be making a mistake, so the little absurdities are planted on purpose to push you past the literal sense into an allegorical one. If you stop at the literal meaning you get a cosmology that doesn't work, and Origen thinks that's the point.

But here's whats bugging me. Since then biblical scholarship has shown that Genesis is part of a whole ancient Near Eastern literary world, written from within a particular cultural horizon. So the very weirdness Origen pointed to now looks less like a special divine clue and more like the author just sharing the cosmological assumptions of his time and place. That seems to weaken the argument: if the absurdity is just culture, then it isn't a deliberate wink, its just the writer's horizon.

Does Origen's argument actually survive this development? Is it dead, or can you repair it into something weaker but still true — maybe something like: the places where the text is most culturally conditioned are exactly where we should be most careful about literal readings, not because the author planted a clue but because interpreters always need to ask what genre a passage is in. And if the argument is dead, what should replace it for someone who still wants to take scripture seriously but isn't a fundamentalist?
