I am a retired research scientist — my career was in organic chemistry — who has come to philosophy only recently, and I have spent the past year reading Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments with a discussion group. The philosophy department at my former university has asked me to help set this year's upper-level undergraduate paper in moral philosophy, and I would like to use that opportunity to draft one substantial essay question on Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator. My scientific training did not prepare me for writing philosophy examinations, so I would be grateful for expert assistance.

Please act as a professional philosopher who specialises in Smith's moral psychology. Before writing anything final, think carefully and brainstorm two or three candidate questions, then settle on the one that best forces students to do more than reproduce the machinery of Smith's system. I want a question that cannot be answered by reciting the definitions of sympathy, propriety, and merit, but instead requires students to assess whether Smith's account has the resources to handle a genuine objection.

The students will have read Theory of Moral Sentiments, mainly Parts I–III. They will know the distinction between actual spectators and the impartial spectator, the idea of the man within the breast, the difference between propriety and merit, and the notion of a desire for praiseworthiness rather than mere praise. The difficulty should therefore lie in testing the theory, not in recalling its terminology. Please do not require any bibliography, citations, or knowledge of secondary literature, since the students are not expected to have that.

Here is the crux I want the question built around: Can Smith's impartial spectator explain how a person comes to condemn a practice that most of the actual spectators around them accept? In other words, does Smith's account leave room for genuine moral criticism of one's own society, or does it reduce conscience to an internalised voice of the surrounding community and thus to a form of conformism? If you find it useful, you may anchor the discussion in a concrete case, for instance a researcher who refuses to participate in data-fabrication that her colleagues tacitly tolerate.

Please provide the following three components:

1. The examination question itself, framed precisely in your own words, intended for a 90-minute essay. It should be a single well-focused question, not a list.

2. A model answer of approximately 700 words in clear expository prose. A strong answer should distinguish the relevant dimensions of Smith's view — for example, propriety and merit, actual as against impartial spectators, the role of the man within the breast, the desire for praiseworthiness, and the general rules of morality — and should acknowledge both the strengths and the limitations of the account.

3. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, total 20 marks, stating the marks each point is worth and adding for each a one-line note on how candidates typically mishandle that point.

Please write everything, including the model answer, in British English (for example, 'behaviour', 'judgement', 'recognise').
