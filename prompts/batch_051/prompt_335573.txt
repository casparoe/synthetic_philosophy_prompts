At my magazine we have a stylebook clause, written by editors in the 1960s who'd read a lot of Sartre and were sick of profiles that explained people away, that says: "No feature shall present a subject's actions as the inevitable result of his or her nature, background, or unconscious drives; every subject is to be treated as a being who chooses and is responsible for what he or she becomes." I'm working on a piece about a woman with severe, treatment-resistant OCD whose compulsions are so automatic that several neurologists describe them as a basal ganglia loop rather than a decision. The clause long predates any serious neuropsychiatric account of OCD. Does the clause, read the way its authors intended, require me to treat her compulsions as choices? Does it bar me from saying "she couldn't help it"? I'm less interested in the practical editing answer than in what this shows about the coherence of existence precedes essence once you push it into cases the existentialists never really engaged. Is there a coherent reading of that doctrine that handles this without collapsing into compatibilism or just using the word "choice" to mean "whatever a person does"? And if there isn't, is that a problem with the doctrine or with the old editors' attempt to turn it into a stylebook rule?
