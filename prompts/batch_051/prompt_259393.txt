Before we can resolve disputes about the legitimacy of moral testimony, we need a specification of what a theory in this area must get right. Provide that specification. List the requirements an adequate theory must meet, each backed by the case or consideration that motivates it: the central cases it has to handle (for example, a child being told that hitting is wrong, an adult deferring to a moral philosopher about the permissibility of a complex policy, a friend telling you that a joke you find funny is cruel, expert testimony drawing your attention to a morally relevant fact you would otherwise have missed), the distinctions it may not collapse (such as reliability versus understanding, permissibility versus epistemic value, and evidence for a claim's truth versus transmission of knowledge), and the constraints it inherits from the broader epistemology of testimony about how secondhand cognition works. For each requirement, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or a desideratum pressed mainly by one side of the debate, and flag any subsets of requirements that threaten to be jointly unsatisfiable. Then identify the single requirement that most sharply sorts the existing theories, and briefly score the optimistic and pessimistic positions on moral testimony against your list. A table is fine.
