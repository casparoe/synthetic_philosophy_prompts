I teach a senior seminar in the philosophy of play and leisure, and for several years I have kept notes on my colleague's classifications of particular activities. She is resolutely case-by-case: she refuses to announce a general criterion, but she will say of any activity whether it counts as genuine play, as genuine leisure, as both, or as neither. By 'leisure' she means something normative and demanding — an activity or state worthwhile as an end in itself, not mere time off, recreation, or idleness. By 'play' she means something analogous in its own register — a genuinely self-contained form of activity, not simply anything that happens to be called a game.

Her recorded verdicts, so far as they bear on the present question, are these:

- A small child wholly absorbed in an elaborate make-believe game with other children on a playground: genuine play, but she will not call it leisure.
- A professional footballer during a competitive league fixture, playing under contract: neither genuine play nor genuine leisure.
- A commuter playing a colourful puzzle game on a phone solely to make a tedious journey pass: neither genuine play nor genuine leisure.
- An adult who works through a cryptic crossword each morning for the intellectual pleasure of it: both genuine play and genuine leisure.
- A member of a monastic community in silent, contemplative prayer: genuine leisure, but not genuine play.
- Four friends who rehearse string quartets together every week for the joy of the music: both genuine play and genuine leisure.
- A teenager who practises violin scales solely because a parent insists and an examination is approaching: neither genuine play nor genuine leisure.
- A painter who executes a commissioned portrait to a client's detailed brief: neither genuine play nor genuine leisure.

Next week we are to discuss a new case, and I want to go in with a clear sense of what follows from her record. The case is this: a retired chemistry teacher sits alone by her window most evenings and simply looks out over the water for an hour. She is attentive but not introspective; she reports that she is neither meditating nor working through problems, and she does nothing with the experience afterwards. She describes the hour as the most worthwhile part of her day.

I would like you to predict, as a matter of consistency with her recorded verdicts, whether my colleague would count this as genuine leisure, as genuine play, as both, or as neither. More important than the bare prediction, set out the two or three principles that would rationalise her record about equally well — in particular the implicit distinction between play and leisure, and between leisure and idleness — and state what each principle predicts for the new case. Then offer your best-guess prediction, identify which of her earlier verdicts does the most work in anchoring it, and propose a further case that, if needed, would most cleanly separate the leading principles she might be running.

Please write your answer in British English.
