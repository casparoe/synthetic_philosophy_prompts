I've been rereading Marx's The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte (1852) for a seminar on revolutionary politics, and I keep oscillating between two reactions to the way it interprets the French Revolution. The systematic virtues of the Marxist reading feel hard to give up: it makes the chaos of 1789–94 — the borrowed Roman costumes, the Terror, the eventual settling into bourgeois order — intelligible as one mechanism, with material class content doing the real causal work while ideals serve as decoration and self-deception. But I also have a firm, almost visceral intuition that this cannot be the whole story. It seems impossible to write off the deputies and Jacobins who were moved by principle as merely self-deceived about the class-bounded content of what they were doing, and it seems to me that the way the Revolution devoured its own leaders had an irreducibly ideological and contingent dynamic — factions, speeches, mistimed decisions — that a class-struggle schema flattens. I don't want a one-sided answer; I want to know which of these must yield and what each surrender costs.

The passage I keep returning to is this one (Karl Marx, The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte, ch. 1, trans. Saul K. Padover):

"But unheroic though bourgeois society is, it nevertheless needed heroism, sacrifice, terror, civil war, and national wars to bring it into being. And in the austere classical traditions of the Roman Republic the bourgeois gladiators found the ideals and the art forms, the self-deceptions, that they needed to conceal from themselves the bourgeois-limited content of their struggles and to keep their passion on the high plane of great historic tragedy. Similarly, at another stage of development a century earlier, Cromwell and the English people had borrowed from the Old Testament the speech, emotions, and illusions for their bourgeois revolution. When the real goal had been achieved and the bourgeois transformation of English society had been accomplished, Locke supplanted Habakkuk."

Please negotiate between the theory enacted in this passage and my intuition. Give the strongest case for reading the Revolution as Marx does here, including what systematic work the Cromwell comparison does, and the strongest case for my intuition that revolutionary ideas had independent causal power and that contingency explains part of the Revolution's self-destructive course. Then, for each of the four moves available — debunk the intuition, amend the theory, abandon the theory, or reconcile them — say what would justify that move and what it would cost.
