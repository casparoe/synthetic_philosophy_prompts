For my philosophy class I have been reading Plato’s Meno, and I am stuck on a passage near 97e–98a. Here is my paraphrase of the passage: Socrates compares true beliefs to the statues of Daedalus, which were said to be so lifelike that they would run away unless they were tied down. In the same way, he says, a true belief is a fine thing and produces good results as long as it stays with us, but it will not stay put for long, and so it is not worth much until one fastens it by working out the reason why it is true. Once it is fastened in this way, it becomes knowledge and remains in the soul.

I have two questions about this. First, what exactly is the difference between a true belief that has been fastened by reasoning and a true belief that has not? If both have the same content and both are true, then the only obvious difference seems to be that the fastened one is more stable. But then Socrates’ contrast between knowledge and mere true belief would reduce to a difference in how long each tends to stay in the soul, which seems too thin to justify treating knowledge as something higher. Second, how is this passage consistent with Socrates’ earlier claim in the same dialogue that true belief guides action no worse than knowledge? If a true belief is already just as good while it is present, then in what sense is it not worth much until it is tied down?

I am not looking for a summary of the dialogue or background about Plato. I want to understand what the argument in this passage is supposed to show, and whether the apparent tension I have identified is real or comes from my misreading.
