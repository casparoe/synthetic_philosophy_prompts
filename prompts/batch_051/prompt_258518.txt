I edit the science-and-ideas section of a magazine, and several times a month I read essays about artificial intelligence, radical life extension, space settlement, and markets that slide from analysis into a tone of veneration — describing a technology as the project that gives an age its meaning, a calling, an answer to death, or a source of salvation. I want a consistent standard for judging when such writing becomes misplaced worship: valuing something whose worth is instrumental or subordinate as though it were an ultimate source of value. Design a reusable editorial procedure I can hand to line editors: an ordered set of questions, each indicating what textual evidence would settle it, that can be applied in about twenty minutes by someone without philosophical training. The procedure should specify where it returns a clear revise-or-reject, where it clears a manuscript, and where it must stop because the remaining question is one of values and the call belongs to me or another senior editor. Then run the procedure on two submissions: (a) an essay arguing that building aligned artificial general intelligence is the central moral undertaking of our time and that talented people have an obligation to commit to it as to a vocation; and (b) an essay on radical life extension that presents the abolition of aging as the definitive solution to the human predicament and technological progress as the source that confers meaning on ordinary aims. I am inclined to pass (a) and reject (b); apply the procedure honestly even where it conflicts with that inclination. Conclude with a short account of what the procedure cannot detect.
