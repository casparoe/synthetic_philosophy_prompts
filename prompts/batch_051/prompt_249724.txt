Lately I have been reading popular accounts of the outbreak of World War I, and a phrase keeps appearing: historians say the war was overdetermined. My rough understanding is that this means several large forces, such as the alliance system, imperial and economic rivalries, mobilization timetables, and domestic political pressures, were each so powerful that any one of them, or something near it, might have been enough to bring about a general European war. But if that is what overdetermination means, I do not know what to do with the counterfactual questions that otherwise seem central to thinking about history. For instance, would the war still have happened without the assassination at Sarajevo? Would it still have happened if one great power had decided to stay out? Are those questions simply unanswerable when an event is overdetermined, or is it a kind of category mistake even to ask them?

I would like to know, first, whether I have understood overdetermination correctly, and second, what accepting that an event was overdetermined would and would not imply for the ordinary what-if reasoning people use when they try to understand why something happened. Please write your answer in American English.
