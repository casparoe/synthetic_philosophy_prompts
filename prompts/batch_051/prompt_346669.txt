Two colleagues of mine, Elena and Marcus, argued at lunch yesterday about a news story from that morning. After a public inquiry into the collapse of a refurbished footbridge in a provincial city, it emerged that no single employee or contractor had breached any rule. The collapse had been traced to a long chain of individually defensible decisions about budgets, inspections, and deferred maintenance, spread across several departments over more than a decade. The company that owned and operated the bridge was fined, and the inquiry concluded that the underlying cause was the company's cost-cutting culture.

(1) Elena said the inquiry had got it exactly right: the company itself is morally to blame, and this is not shorthand for blaming individuals. The wrongdoing, she argued, lay in the pattern of decisions rather than in any one act; and since a pattern cannot be located inside any single head, the only thing that did the wrong was the company.

(2) Marcus called this a category error. A company has no mind, no intentions, and no conscience; it cannot grasp the wrongness of what it does or feel remorse; so it cannot be the sort of thing to which moral blame attaches. Blaming a company, he said, is a metaphor we reach for when we have not yet worked out which people to blame.

(3) Elena replied that if it is a metaphor, it is a metaphor with no literal equivalent. Each person in the chain could say, truthfully, that their particular decision was defensible on what they knew. So individual blame either lets everyone off or manufactures a scapegoat. The company, she said, acts through standing procedures, delegated authority, and committee minutes; those are genuinely its decisions, not merely the decisions of the people who happen to staff them.

(4) Marcus answered that procedures and committees are just further individual acts arranged in boxes on an organisational chart; an arrangement of human acts is no more a moral agent than a storm or a market is. A company can be held liable, fined, dissolved, or compelled to change its practices, he conceded, but liability is not blame.

(5) Elena said he was ignoring ordinary moral talk: we say a regiment committed an atrocity, a board was recklessly negligent, a club made the wrong decision. Those claims, she maintained, are not merely figurative; they pick out the only plausible location of responsibility for organised wrongdoing.

(6) Marcus accepted that some groups may be structured enough to have group-level beliefs and intentions, but he said that makes them agents only in a thin sense. Blame proper, he argued, is a reactive attitude such as resentment or indignation, directed at something with a quality of will that can answer for itself as a single self. A company has no inner life and cannot answer as a self, so at best it is a useful fiction we punish for the sake of social regulation.

They asked me to referee, and I could not. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, in the order the moves were made: for each of (1) to (6), say whether it is mostly right, mostly wrong, or mixed, and briefly say why. Then go further and give a committed overall verdict. I want you to take a definite position on whether, and in what sense, a company can genuinely be blamed, and to defend it with reasons, rather than surveying the positions and leaving me to choose. Also say what, if anything, both of them are missing. Please write your answer in continuous prose, as you would in an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or other formatting.
