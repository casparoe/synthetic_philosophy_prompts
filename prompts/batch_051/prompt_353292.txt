I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on whether its narrator's reconstruction of her childhood home, decades after the fact, can amount to knowledge rather than vivid confabulation. For years I have been collecting a philosopher friend's verdicts on cases about when someone comes to know that one of their own sources of information is reliable, and I need to know what he should say, if he is to remain consistent with himself, about the case I now face.

His precedents are these. He granted that a woman with normal color vision who looks at a red wall in good light knows that the wall is red, even though she has never first checked that her vision works; he has defended that verdict by saying ordinary perception yields knowledge too easily for a prior reliability check to be required. He denied that a driver whose fuel gauge reads Full, and who has never checked the gauge against anything else, can come to know that the gauge is reliable by inferring this from a series of the gauge's own readings; he called that move bootstrapping. He granted that a pilot who cross-checks a new altimeter against an independent radar system over many flights can come to know that the altimeter is reliable. And he denied that a self-described clairvoyant whose correct guesses are recorded only in the clairvoyant's own notebook thereby comes to know that the clairvoyance is reliable.

My own situation is this. I am trying to establish both that the layout of my childhood home is as I remember it and that my episodic memory for that period is reliable. My evidence is supplied entirely by that same memory: the stable spatial images, the cross-references among remembered details, and my recollection of many past occasions on which remembered details later proved accurate. Pressed to justify the reliability claim, I can only rehearse that remembered track record; memory is the only instrument I have for inspecting memory. Would my friend class this with the red-wall case or with the fuel-gauge case? Set out the candidate rules that would each make sense of his four precedents and say what each predicts in my case; then give your best prediction of his verdict and your reasons, identify the closest precedent, and name the principle he would be adopting either way. End by naming one further case that would discriminate between any rules that remain live, and finish with a line of the form \answer{...} in which the content is one of: knows-reliable, does-not-know-reliable, or underdetermined.
