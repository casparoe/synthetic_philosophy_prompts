So I drafted this short piece for a philosophy of science seminar, and Im worried it's either unfair to Hofstadter or smuggling in a position on genetic information without arguing for it. Could you read it and give me comments? Here's the draft:

Typogenetics is Hofstadter's toy formal system from Gödel, Escher, Bach: strands made of the four bases A, C, G, and T, plus enzymes that operate on strands. A fixed table, the Typogenetic Code, maps each pair of bases to an amino acid, and an amino acid is itself a command—cut the strand, delete a base, insert a base, turn on copy mode, move along the strand, and so on. An enzyme also has a binding preference, and it attaches wherever it first finds the matching short sequence, then does its command. The important thing is that a strand can code for an enzyme that acts on strands, including the very strand that coded for it. With the right strand, the enzyme's operations can build a copy of the original strand by the complementary base-pairing trick, so the system contains a toy version of self-replication. Hofstadter treats this as something like the biological analogue of Gödel's self-referential sentence: Gödel used a numbering scheme to let arithmetic formulas encode and talk about formulas, including themselves; Typogenetics uses the Typogenetic Code to let a strand specify an enzyme that operates on the same kind of object the strand is.

My problem is that this is more an analogy than an isomorphism. In the Gödel case, the self-reference has semantic content: the encoded formula is used inside a sentence to say something about a property (provability) of that very sentence. The loop closes through arithmetic's ability to represent the syntax of its own language. In the Typogenetics case, the loop closes causally: a strand makes an enzyme, and the enzyme makes more of the strand. Nothing in the strand says anything about itself; it does not ascribe a property to its own code. Unless you already hold a strong, literal reading of genetic information—that DNA means something the way a formula means something—the strand is just a cause, not an assertion. So the DNA–Gödel analogy either stays at the level of a shared abstract pattern (a thing acting on itself indirectly through a code) or quietly presupposes the controversial thesis it is supposed to be illustrating.

But maybe the analogy is still doing real work, because it forces the question: what would you have to add to a causal self-action loop to turn it into a semantic self-reference loop? Answering that is close to answering what people mean when they say DNA is a code or carries information, and whether that is literal or metaphorical. On that reading, the DNA–Gödel "isomorphism" is best understood not as a result but as a precise way of posing the question.

Specific comments I'd like: (1) does the semantic-loop/causal-loop contrast do real work, or does it beg the question against someone who thinks DNA is literally semantic? (2) where is the weakest step in the argument, and what would strengthen it? (3) is this worth submitting, or is it just making an obvious point? And if you can think of a way to make the Typogenetics machinery do more in the last two paragraphs, I'd like to hear it. Please write your comments in American English.
