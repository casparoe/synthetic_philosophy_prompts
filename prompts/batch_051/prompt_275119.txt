I have been reading about population and resource limits on my own, and I recently came across an account of Malthus in a popular podcast that I would like to have checked before I repeat it. The account, as I understood it, went like this. Malthus claimed that population tends to grow geometrically, while the food supply can grow only arithmetically, and he therefore predicted that large-scale famine was inevitable by the early nineteenth century. On this telling, he held that helping the poor was either futile or actively harmful, because any temporary improvement in their condition would simply produce more births and bring the population back down to subsistence; the only real checks on population were war, disease, and starvation. The podcast concluded that the Industrial Revolution and, later, the Green Revolution decisively refuted him, since food production increased far faster than he thought possible, so the whole Malthusian framework has been falsified and is now mainly of historical interest. I found this plausible, but I also felt it was pulling in two directions: dismissing Malthus as refuted while also suggesting he had identified something durable about pressure between population and resources. Could you go through this summary and tell me which parts a careful reader of Malthus would accept, which parts are subtly wrong, and what a defender of Malthus would say in response to the claim that he was simply refuted? In particular, I would like to understand what it would mean for a long-run prediction like his to be right but early, and what would have to be true for the view to be rescued rather than falsified when its timetable fails.
