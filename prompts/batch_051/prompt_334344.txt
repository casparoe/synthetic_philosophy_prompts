I was rereading Galileo’s Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (1615) and found myself stopped by a sentence near the beginning that sounds to me like a point about module boundaries, not only a point about biblical interpretation:

“To this end they hurled various charges and published numerous writings filled with vain arguments, and they made the grave mistake of sprinkling these with passages taken from places in the Bible which they had failed to understand properly, and which were ill-suited to their purposes.”

The parallel I cannot get out of my head is with software systems built around distinct authoritative components, each with its own contract. Galileo seems to be saying that Scripture has a domain of competence and that his opponents are querying it outside that domain: they want a text whose authority covers salvation and morals to settle claims about the arrangement of the heavens, and then they treat the misfit as evidence against astronomy. In software terms, that is like sending a physical query to the soteriological module and declaring the universe wrong when the response is inapposite. The correct move would be to recognize that the query was addressed to the wrong interface, not to revise the physics.

Is this a genuine structural parallel, or am I just rewriting an early-modern hermeneutic in programmer vocabulary? I want a candid assessment: reconstruct Galileo’s actual principle as strongly as possible, then state exactly where the software analogy matches and where it breaks. In particular, who fixes the contract for a sacred text, and what should Galileo’s view say when Scripture appears to assert something physical in ordinary language and with full authority, rather than only being misapplied by his opponents?
