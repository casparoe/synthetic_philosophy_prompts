For the past year I have been evaluating claims in my research group about what an AI system is really trying to do and which internal search procedure it runs. I have adopted a working policy: I treat an attributed objective function or search space as a factual claim only when it makes a behavioral prediction that distinguishes it from rival attributions; if it is behaviorally indistinguishable from its alternatives, I treat it as a compact redescription of the system's behavior rather than a hypothesis about hidden machinery. On this basis I have dismissed a claim that a system is pursuing a deceptive goal when its behavior was equally consistent with several benign objectives, and claims that it uses a particular search algorithm when many different algorithms would produce the same outputs. I know this policy has the shape of revealed-preference arguments in economics, and I also know that legitimate scientific posits are often underdetermined by data yet earn their keep over time. So I am unsure whether I am practicing good epistemic hygiene or smuggling back a verificationism that would rule out latent variables and hidden causes on principle. Is the policy defensible as a general approach to inferring an optimizer's objective and search space from its behavior? When does observed behavior actually justify one attribution over another, and how should the policy be revised so that it keeps genuine identifiability constraints without rejecting useful underdetermined explanations?
