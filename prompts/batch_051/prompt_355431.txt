I am a novelist finishing a book whose protagonist, a radio astronomer, is slowly undone by the absence of any signal, and in researching it I have read widely in the Fermi-paradox literature — Rare Earth, the Great Filter, the zoo hypothesis, the claim that the silence is an artifact of selection effects or of how briefly we have listened. What strikes me is how little the debate has moved in decades even as exoplanet data have improved. The same people, looking at the same numbers, draw opposite conclusions about whether the silence is ominous at all. My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement is not empirical but inferential: it is a dispute about what a single data point — a null result of unspecified size — can teach us when the priors along the chain from nonliving chemistry to communicating intelligence are set more by temperament than by evidence and are almost never made explicit. Some treat the silence as information; others treat it as noise; and the argument proceeds as if it were about facts. I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a verdict on who is right: is it a disagreement about priors, about which terms in the Drake equation should be treated as knowable, about how much weight a nondetection may carry, or about the legitimacy of anthropic reasoning itself? And given that diagnosis, what would count as progress — a null result of a certain size, a formal result about how far data can move such priors, a distinction drawn cleanly, or a change of question?
