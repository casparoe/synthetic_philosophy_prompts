I'm writing a final paper for a course on Chinese philosophy, and I keep going back and forth on the fasting of the mind episode in the Zhuangzi, chapter four, the long bit where Confucius is supposedly instructing Yan Hui. Quick background as I understand it: Yan Hui wants to go reform a dangerous ruler, Confucius keeps rejecting his plans as still too self-conscious and manipulative, and eventually Yan Hui asks what to do. Confucius tells him, roughly, that he must fast—not the fasting of the body, but a different kind, where he stops listening with his ears, then stops listening with his mind, and lets his qi or spirit listen instead, so that the self becomes empty and the ten thousand things are encountered as they are.

Here’s the thing I’m stuck on. The passage is often used to illustrate wu-wei, especially the paradox of trying not to try. Confucius is telling Yan Hui to empty himself, but Yan Hui’s entire project—going to reform the ruler—seems to be a deliberate, goal-directed action. If he successfully empties his own agenda, wouldn’t he also lose the motive that sent him to the ruler in the first place? And the instructions themselves sound like a technique: to stop using your mind, you have to somehow use your mind to stop using your mind, which feels circular. Some people I’ve talked to say the point is that you can’t turn emptiness into another achievement, and the text is trying to trap you into seeing that. Others say no, the text really does recommend a kind of mental discipline, just one that doesn’t aim at any particular outcome.

I’d like you to write an essay that takes a definite position on how this passage handles the paradox of trying not to try. Your essay should weigh the two readings I just sketched (and maybe a third if you think neither works), and it should reach and justify a conclusion rather than just laying out options. Keep it focused on the logic of the situation in the passage—I don’t need historical philology or a general account of all of Zhuangzi. Oh, and if you can, keep it accessible to someone who hasn’t read the Chinese. I’m not grading you on how scholarly it sounds.
