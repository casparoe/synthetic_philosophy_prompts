I'm a software engineer, and a few months ago I built an emotion-journaling app as a side project — people log how they feel, what happened, and what they were thinking at the time. After staring at enough of the data, I came around to a view I now have to defend in public: emotions are a kind of perception, a fast readout of changes in my body and my situation, rather than judgments or conclusions I draw. I got invited to give a short talk on this at a philosophy of mind reading group (mostly grad students, a couple of retired professors, and they've been working through the "what are emotions?" debate — judgments, perceptions, or feelings — all semester). My rough argument is: (1) emotions usually show up before people can articulate a clear reason or thought behind them; (2) they present themselves as revealing something about how things are going for me, not as conclusions I've reached; and (3) they can misfire like a perceptual illusion — I can know full well the situation isn't dangerous and still feel afraid, the same way I know the lines are parallel and still see them as angled.

The room I'm presenting to is a bit scary for this view. Two of the grad students are hardcore cognitivists — they think emotions are, or at least depend on, evaluative judgments, and they love cases of slow, deliberative emotions like resentment that builds over years or grief that deepens the more you understand the loss. There's a retired psychologist in the feelings camp who thinks my story still makes emotions too smart and too outward-focused, and who will push on whether I'm just smuggling judgments into what I call "perception." The rest are mostly agnostic and will pounce on any place where my engineering analogies break down.

I want a prep sheet for the Q&A: the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way these particular people would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own position that I should definately practice defending instead of hoping nobody brings it up.
