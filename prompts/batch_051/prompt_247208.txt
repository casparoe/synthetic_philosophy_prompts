I'm a PhD student in logic, and I keep coming back to a version of Skolem's paradox that I first met in a model theory course. At the time I filed it under "weird facts about first-order logic" and moved on, but lately it's started to look like an argument that proves too much, and I cant find the exit. Here is the argument as I've reconstructed it.

(1) By the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem, if first-order ZFC has a model at all, it has a countable model M.
(2) In M there is an object R that satisfies the formal definition of the set of real numbers, and M satisfies the sentence "R is uncountable," since ZFC proves there is no bijection between the natural numbers and the reals.
(3) But M itself is countable, so the elements of R can be paired off with natural numbers from outside the model.
(4) So the very same collection is countable from the outside and uncountable from the inside.
(5) Therefore uncountability is not a property a set has outright; it is relative to which bijections happen to exist in the model.
(6) And by the same token, no first-order theory can pin down the intended real numbers, or any intended notion of cardinality, since there is nothing to choose between the countable model and the uncountable ones.

The conclusion (6) is what bothers me: it makes ordinary set-theoretic practice look like a game with symbols, and it seems to show that talk of "the real numbers" is fatally underdetermined. I'd much rather reject (5) or (6) than give up the theorem, but I can't see how to do it without hand-waving about intended interpretations. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please write your answer in British English.
