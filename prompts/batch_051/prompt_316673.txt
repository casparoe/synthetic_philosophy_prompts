So I'm a novelist, not a philsopher, and I've been drafting an essay called "The Thought Experiment as Flash Fiction," and I'd like comments before I embarrass myself in public with it. The claim: thought experiments in philosophy do their real work as tiny pieces of fiction. The setup tells you which details are furniture and which are load-bearing, the same way genre tells you whether a gun on the wall is a clue or decor, and the resulting "intuition" is mostly your sense of how a story of that type should end once the scene has been wound up. I know philosophers already distinguish evidence / illustration / intuition pump, and my worry is that I'm just saying "intuition pump" with extra steps. The essay runs in three parts: (1) thought experiments are read the way flash fiction is read — one counterfactual change, everything else held by unstated convention; (2) the punch of a thought experiment is a narrative effect, the scene refusing the ending your genre expectations predicted, which then gets reported as a philosophical intuition; (3) that's why smart people deadlock over them, becuase they're not disagreeing about the argument but about what genre the story is. Then I claim this is fine and maybe even useful, but it means thought experiments are closer to argumentative fiction than to evidence. What I'd like from you: is the central idea actually distinct from the standard intuition-pump account, or have I just re-dressed it? Where am I being unfair to how philosophers actually use these cases? And give me the two sharpest objections you'd raise if this landed on your desk. Be candid; I write fiction, I can take a reader's report.
