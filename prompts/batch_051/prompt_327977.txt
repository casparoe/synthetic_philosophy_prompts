I'm trying to sort out which of these moves about the hard problem of consciousness are actually talking about the hard problem, and which ones quietly change the subject. Here are the moves, more or less in the order people reach for them. (i) Once we've explained all the functions, behaviors, and neural mechanisms—wakefulness, reportability, attention, access to information—there is no leftover problem; that was the whole thing. (ii) The problem is real, but it's a problem about our conceptual scheme: experience seems to resist physical description because physical description is built from relational and structural terms, not because experience has extra nonphysical ingredients. (iii) Consciousness is fundamental, so there is nothing left to explain about how it arises from nonconscious matter; the real job is to map the laws that connect phenomenal properties to physics. (iv) We may be cognitively closed to the solution—the gap between physical description and experience could be a limit of our faculties, the way a dog can't do set theory. (v) The demand itself is unfair: every explanation we know how to give is relational or structural, and the hard problem asks for an explanation of experience in exactly those terms, so of course no answer seems satisfying. (vi) The conceivability of zombies—a molecule-for-molecule physical duplicate of you with no inner experience at all—shows the physical facts don't guarantee phenomenal facts, so the problem is real and no amount of extra physical detail will close it.

For each, give a score from 0 to 1 for how much it genuinely takes the hard problem on board rather than dissolving it, relocating it, or explaining it away. 1 means it stares the original problem in the face; 0 means it only looks like an answer because the subject has been changed. Then rank the six moves from best to worst by that score.
