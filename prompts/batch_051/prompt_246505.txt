heres one for the dataset. I'm grading a philosophy of science midterm and want a second opinion on one answer before I finalize the grade. The question was:

"Explain what inference to the best explanation (IBE) is and how it differs from enumerative induction. Then discuss whether the fact that a hypothesis is the best available explanation of the evidence gives us a reason to think it is true, paying attention to the relationship between explanatory goodness and probability, and to at least one objection to IBE."

The student's answer (typed, so any typos are theirs) was:

"Inference to the best explanation is the principle that when a body of evidence can be explained by several competing hypotheses, we should accept the one that explains it best. It differs from enumerative induction because enumerative induction just generalizes from observed instances, whereas IBE appeals to theoretical virtues like simplicity, scope, and consilience (unifying previously unrelated phenomena). For example, we think the mail carrier delivered the letters because that hypothesis explains the letters being in the mailbox better than the alternatives (a neighbor breaking in to leave mail, a prank, etc.). The best explanation is the hypothesis that scores highest on the explanatory virtues. Since these virtues are what make a hypothesis probable given the evidence, the best explanation is by definition the hypothesis most likely to be true, so ranking explanations by how good they are is the same as ranking them by posterior probability. IBE therefore gives us a reason to believe the best explanation is true. The main objection is van Fraassen's bad lot argument, which says we might be choosing the best of a bad lot of hypotheses and the true one might not be among those we considered. But this just shows that we should include more hypotheses before we infer; it doesn't undermine IBE itself."
