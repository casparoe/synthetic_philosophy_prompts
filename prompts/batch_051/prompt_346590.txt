For a paper I'm writing on the School of Names, I've been trying to reconstruct Gongsun Long's white horse argument as something better than a piece of stage sophistry, and the best I can get is below. I don't fully trust step (6), but the more I stare at it the less sure I am what exactly goes wrong, and on some days I think the argument is valid but proves much less than it sounds.

(1) 'Horse' picks out the kind horse; 'white horse' picks out the kind white horse.
(2) The kind white horse is the kind horse with a further condition added, namely being white.
(3) A kind plus an extra condition is a different condition from the kind alone: being a horse and being white is not the same condition as being a horse, since a black horse has the latter condition but not the former.
(4) So being a white horse is a different condition from being a horse.
(5) If two conditions are different, then a thing's satisfying the one is not the same fact as its satisfying the other.
(6) If a thing's satisfying F is not the same fact as its satisfying G, then what is F is not G.
(7) Therefore a white horse is not a horse.

What I'd actually like: (i) is the argument valid as written, and if not, where is the first bad step; (ii) if (6) is the culprit, what is the strongest interpretation under which (6) comes out true, and does (7) still follow once (6) is read that way; and (iii) is premise (1) already cheating by treating 'white horse' as a kind term on the same level as 'horse', and if so can the argument survive without it? I'm also unsure whether the conclusion, on the reading where the argument works, is the strong claim that no white horse is a horse, or the much weaker claim that the kind white-horse isn't identical to the kind horse.
