ok so ive been going in circles on this and keep hitting the same wall. i've gotten really taken with an idea about how confident a person should be in their beliefs. the setup is: you can score a set of confidences by how close they end up to the truth, and there's a neat argument that whenever someones confidences break the usual probability rules - say being 0.6 sure the flight is on time and 0.6 sure its not - there's another set of confidences, one that follows those rules, that will be closer to the truth no matter which way the world turns out. not just on average, in every single case. so the theory says the probability rules are not bookkeeping: a mind that violates them is failing in a way that no outcome could excuse. and i find that almost beautiful, because it makes rationality look like not being beaten by a better opinion you couldve held.

but i also have a very firm sense that an actual person can be fully reasonable and still have confidences that don't add up. my uncle would tell you hes 70 percent sure the flight is on time and 70 percent sure its not, and hes not confused about airports, he just hasnt sat down and audited his own head. and its not like that tidier set of confidences is something he couldve settled on from where he stands. is he irrational in any sense that matters? it seems to me the theory is treating him like a weather app with an internal contradiction, when a person hedging at the gate is making an altogether different kind of move, not a math error.

so ive got a theory with a neat argument behind it saying one thing, and a firm intuition saying the theory has overreached. i want someone to tell me which one should give, and what it would take. is the intuition just me refusing to accept what the argument shows? is the theory mistaking a tidy scoring rule for an account of what real people ought to do? or is there a reading where both survive, and the argument is true but says something narrower than it looks?
