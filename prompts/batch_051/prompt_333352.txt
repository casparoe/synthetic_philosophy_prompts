For my American literature class I am writing a paper on John Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath. In history we have been reading Fernand Braudel, who argues that the deepest explanations of historical change lie in slow-moving structures—geography, climate, and economic systems—and that dramatic events and individual decisions are mostly surface effects, brief and secondary by comparison with those deeper currents. Steinbeck's novel seems almost deliberately Braudelian: the Joad family's story is interrupted again and again by panoramic chapters about drought, tractors, banks, and highways, as though the family is being carried along by forces it cannot see, much less control. And yet the novel is also intensely concerned with individual dignity and choice, and it ends with a single intimate act rather than with a structural explanation. Read the novel against Braudel. Does it ultimately side with the longue durée against the individual, or does it resist that reading—and which scenes matter most for deciding? Please also say where your own reading is most likely to break down: which parts of the novel, or which objections from the other side, would most plausibly change your mind.
