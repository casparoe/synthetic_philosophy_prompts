I have been trying to repair the method of reflective equilibrium rather than abandon it, and I want an honest assessment of a test I have been relying on. The standard method, as I understand it, lets principles and considered judgments negotiate — each may be revised in light of the other — and treats any sufficiently coherent package as justified. My worry is that all judgments are given the same vote, when in practice some judgments do the anchoring: they stay fixed while principles are bent around them, and others get quietly revised away. I distinguish anchor judgments from provisional judgments and propose a one-sided rule. A considered judgment may anchor an equilibrium only if we can tell, in good faith, a debunking story about it — an account of why people like us would hold that judgment even if it were false — and we still keep the judgment once the story is on the table. If no such story can be told, the judgment is merely provisional and may not veto a principle. In moral cases this tends to certify strong aversions, since we can give evolutionary stories about them and keep them anyway, and to decertify judgments that look like self-serving bias, such as some desert-based political judgments. In formal cases the test is awkward, because a debunking story about modus ponens would itself have to use inference. The obvious objection is that the test assumes, without testing, that debunkability matters to whether a judgment may anchor — and that assumption may fail its own test. Give me a candid assessment of what I am calling the anchor test: steelman it, separate the regress problem from the other difficulties, say whether it genuinely fixes the method or quietly stops being reflective equilibrium, and tell me where it breaks first.

Structure your answer exactly as follows: (1) Steelman, about 150 words, restating the proposal in its strongest and clearest form; (2) Objections, about 350 words, opening with the self-application regress sharpened and then giving the two strongest objections I have not noticed; (3) What survives, about 250 words, including whether the test improves reflective equilibrium, quietly abandons it, or collapses into a recognizable existing position; (4) Verdict, about 150 words, on where the proposal breaks first and whether that break is fatal.
