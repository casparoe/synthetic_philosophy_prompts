I have recently been made chair of the screening committee at a donor collaborative that funds economic-behavior programs—household saving, school effort, small-business formation—and we are receiving more and more proposals whose central claim is that the target behavior will improve once the target population's "culture" or "values" improve: a work ethic, a savings culture, a norm of school effort. The committee meets monthly, must score each proposal in one sitting, and moves enough money that a wrong causal model gets locked into programs for years; the board we report to signs off on our shortlist and is tired of both vague moralizing and the assumption that material constraints are the whole story. I am not trained in social theory, and neither are most reviewers. I want a reusable decision procedure, based on what the Weber Protestant-ethic controversy and its afterlife actually teaches about whether culture can be a cause, for deciding when a values-first explanation in a proposal is doing genuine causal work and when it is a just-so story—especially the danger that the value is simply inferred from the very behavior it is meant to explain. Please give the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than evidence. Then run the procedure on three proposals: (a) a micro-saving program justified by the claim that low savings are caused by a local "present-oriented culture"; (b) a vocational-training program justified by the goal of instilling a sense of "calling" in trainees; and (c) a school-enrollment program that attributes remaining absences to "parental attitudes" after transport, fees, and school quality have been addressed. I expect at least one result the program officers will dislike, and I want an honest statement of what the procedure cannot do.
