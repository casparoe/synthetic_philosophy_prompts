I've been turning over an argument my friend made in a historiography seminar, and I can't decide if it's a neat refutation or a trick with the word caused. It's about explaining the outbreak of the First World War, but it is meant to be general.

(1) A standard counterfactual test says X caused Y just in case Y would not have happened if X had not happened (ignore the fiddly details). (2) The coming of the war in 1914 was massively overdetermined: the alliance system, the German assurance to Austria, the Austrian ultimatum, Russian general mobilisation, and the rigid war plans form a web in which any one factor could be removed and the others would still have produced some war. (3) So for each individual factor, say Russian mobilisation, it is false that without it the war would not have happened. (4) Therefore, by (1), Russian mobilisation did not cause the war, and likewise for every other factor. (5) But historians plainly say, and should say, that Russian mobilisation and the German assurance to Austria were among the causes of the war breaking out when it did. (6) So either the counterfactual theory of causation is false, or most ordinary historical causal claims are false. My friend thinks (6) is a knockdown argument against counterfactual theories, and honestly I can't find the exit.

Which step do you reject? If I had to choose I'd rather keep (5) than (1), but I want to know what that costs. Or is there a way to keep both by reinterpreting one of the steps? Tell me which premise to give up and why it looked true.
