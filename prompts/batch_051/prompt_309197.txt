I'm on the three-person committee that awards my department's annual essay prize in the history of ideas, and I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether we can take a donor's money on the condition that this year's prize goes to a specific essay. The essay is his old college roomate's: it's competent and readable, but nobody thinks it's in the top handful, and at least two submissions are clearly better. The donor, an alumnus who did well in logistics software, will endow the prize permanently and fund a yearly public lecture if we pick the essay.

My friend's line is that we're overthinking it: the prize is a resource we control, giving it costs us one year of preferring a weaker essay, and the endowment keeps a mostly broke department alive. He kept pushing on whether the honor of one essayist is worth more than a lecture series and a permanent prize fund, and I didn't have a great comeback. But I can't shake the feeling that a prize is the department publicly standing behind an evaluative judgment about which essay is best, and the donor's money is a reason to want to give the prize, not a reason to think the essay is the best — it's the wrong kind of reason. My friend says that's a philosopher's hangup and we'd just be allocating a resource, not asserting anything.

Draft the decision memo I'll circulate before our Thursday meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action — accept the condition, decline, or something in between — with a timeline, and it has to engage the strongest version of my friend's position rather than wave it away. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence: the actual recommendation. No preamble, no restating the setup. After the memo, add a short note to me, not for circulation, saying which consideration from the losing side would most likely flip the recommendation, or where the memo's reasoning is most likely to fail.
