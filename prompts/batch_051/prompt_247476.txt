Courts ordinarily describe the civil standard of proof as the balance of probabilities (preponderance of the evidence), yet they are widely thought to resist deciding cases on naked statistical evidence alone. In the blue bus hypothetical, a pedestrian is injured by a bus and can prove only that one company operates 80% of the buses on the route; there is no witness to the color of the bus, no paint trace, and no other evidence linking this particular accident to that company's bus. In the gatecrasher hypothetical, 501 of 1,000 spectators gatecrashed an event while 499 paid, and a randomly selected spectator is slightly more likely than not to have gatecrashed, but a court would not find her liable on that statistic alone. The reluctance is even stronger in criminal cases, where many hold that no matter how high the statistical probability, a conviction based purely on base rates would not satisfy proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

Or do those cases reveal that the law's notion of proof was never purely probabilistic in the first place?
