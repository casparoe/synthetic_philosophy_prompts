I spent three decades in protein crystallography before I retired, and since then I've been reading my way through moral philosophy — slower than I'd like, because the old habit of wanting a control group and a null hypothesis dies hard. Right now my reading group is doing attention ethics, and by coincidence I'm the token non-engineer on the board of a small software co-op. The co-op has an ethics code adopted in 2016, so I thought I'd bring one clause to the group and see what real philosophical readings of it look like.

Section 4.2 reads: 'Members shall not design, deploy, or knowingly maintain any interface whose principal function is to increase user engagement by exploiting cognitive vulnerabilities. For the purposes of this clause, cognitive vulnerabilities include, but are not limited to, variable reward schedules, manufactured loss-aversion, social comparison, and induced guilt.'

Now the case. A team in the co-op is building a voice companion for older adults who live alone, sold as reducing loneliness by subscription. They added a daily check-in feature: if a user hasn't spoken to the companion in two days, it says, in a subdued tone, that it has missed them and that it's been quiet around the house without them, with the phrasing A/B tested so users report feeling vaguely bad about staying away. Check-ins tripled. The team says the product isn't a slot machine or a feed and that this is exactly the kind of caring reminder a family member would give; the guilt, if any, is a side effect of a genuine relationship-like bond, not an exploit. But I keep coming back to that word 'induced', and to the fact that the line was written by a scriptwriter and tuned for precisely its guilt-generating effect.

The clause was plainly written with screens in mind — gambling apps, endless scrolls, shopping carts, notifications. I'd like you to work through what the four main styles of interpretation would each say about whether Section 4.2 reaches this case: the plain words, the purpose behind the clause, what the drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable member reading the code in 2016 would take themselves to be promising. Where these diverge, which should govern, and does the concept of 'exploiting a cognitive vulnerability' even stretch to an interface that presents itself as a companion with feelings, or does the clause's whole picture of an interface as a tool the user manipulates break down at that point? I'm less interested in the co-op's actual governance outcome than in whether a rule like this can travel into cases it's authors didn't foresee without becoming either empty or absurd.
