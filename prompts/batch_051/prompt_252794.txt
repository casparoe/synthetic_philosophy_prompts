Last week I sat in on an argument between two graduate students that I have not been able to stop turning over. I will reconstruct their exchange as faithfully as I can, because I would like a referee's verdict on it.

One of them—call him M—opened with the standard cases: Newton and Leibniz both produced the calculus in the late seventeenth century, and Darwin and Wallace both arrived at natural selection in the mid-nineteenth. M argued that when a discovery is made twice, independently, within a short window, that is evidence the discovery was "in the air"—that the background knowledge, techniques, and problems had matured to the point where someone was going to find it. If so, he continued, the particular discoverer matters far less than the standard story suggests. He compared the discoverer to a seed crystal dropped into a supersaturated solution: any suitable impurity would have set off crystallization, so singling out the actual crystal afterward is arbitrary. His conclusion was that priority disputes and eponymous theorems are largely irrational, because they turn a contingent accident into a deep individual achievement.

The other—call her N—pushed back on several fronts. She said "in the air" is doing more work than the evidence permits. A double discovery shows that a result was possible in that milieu, not that it was inevitable. She also said the seed-crystal analogy leaves out most of what a discoverer contributes: not a bare idea but a specific formulation—a notation, a set of arguments, a body of evidence—and the specific form affects what later researchers can do with it. Calculus in Leibniz's notation was not the same intellectual object as Newton's fluxions, even if they overlapped, and downstream work did not treat them identically. If the form matters, she said, then the person matters at least through the form.

M replied that this merely pushes the contingency back one level. The form, he said, is also the sort of thing settled by whoever happens to get there first and by patterns of uptake; if Leibniz's notation won out, that is another contingent fact, not a vindication of the individual. He then anticipated N's next move: she would probably say we still need priority norms to motivate people to do the grinding, uncertain work. But that, he argued, would actually concede his point. If priority is worth protecting for motivational reasons, then it is being defended as a useful social device, not as an accurate record of who was causally the effective origin of the discovery.

N answered that "useful social device" is not the same as fiction. Someone still had to turn a ripe problem into a publishable, usable solution, and a priority norm can track that real contribution—being first to make the discovery available in a form others can build on—even when several people could in principle have done it. She also argued that multiples are noteworthy precisely because they are not the default: for every case where two people converged, there are would-be discoveries that died with their almost-discoverers or were never completed because nobody stayed with the problem. Hindsight, she said, makes outcomes look more inevitable than they were. M countered that this only reinforces his point: if the credit system exists to keep people working on problems, then it is a social institution, not evidence that the credited individual was the cause of the idea.

They ended at an impasse. I would like a point-by-point adjudication: which of the specific claims each of them made are defensible, which should be rejected or modified, where each of them should have conceded ground, and what the dispute is missing.
