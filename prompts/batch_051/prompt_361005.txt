I'm guest-leading an undergraduate practical-ethics session on whether we have duties to wild animals, and I've noticed the same intuition doing most of the work every time: people who will spend an afternoon driving a fledgling with a fishing-line cut to a rehabber will not lift a finger for a fledgling of the same species slowly dying from a naturally occurring parasitic infection, even when the second bird is clearly suffering more. I want one thought experiment that forces this apart properly.

Construct a two-condition case where everything is held fixed except the causal history of the harm: same animal, same kind of injury or illness, same rescuer, same cost, same risk of failure, same ordinary setting — a backyard, a creek, a nature reserve. The human-caused condition should be the milder one and the natural condition the more severe one, so nobody can say severity is doing the work. Keep it non-science-fictional and close off the usual escape hatches: no chance of ecological backfire, no uncertain diagnosis, no predator starvation, no downstream population effects, no hidden differences in the animal's prospects. Then do the full check: what a person who accepts a duty to help in human-caused cases but not natural ones must say in each condition, and the exact principle they have to invoke to justify helping in the milder case while declining in the worse one; what a straightforward harm-minimization consequentialist would say about both; which single feature of the scenario is carrying all the weight; and what happens when you flip that feature. Finish with the strongest move a defender of the leave-nature-alone view could make, and whether it survives being run through both conditions. The case is meant to make the cause-based asymmetry feel less principled than it usually does, but the anti-intervention side shouldn't be a strawman.
