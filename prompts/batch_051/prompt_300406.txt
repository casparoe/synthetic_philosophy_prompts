A friend of mine keeps insisting that intransitive preferences are irrational, and his whole argument is the money pump. His version goes like this: I prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A. So he offers to swap my A for B for a penny, then my B for C for a penny, then my C for A for a penny, and I'm back where I started three cents poorer. I get why that looks bad. But here's what I don't get: if I know in advance how the whole cycle is going to go, I can just refuse whichever trade closes the loop, or refuse to enter the sequence at all. The pump only drains someone who doesn't see what's coming. And isn't a big part of being rational precisely that you don't take a bunch of individually tempting steps when you can see they add up to a bad outcome? So it seems to me the money pump only proves that cyclic preferences are bad for the myopic, not that there's anything wrong with me. What am I missing here—or is the money pump argument quietly smuggling in the assumption that I have to evaluate each trade in isolation, as if I'd never heard of the others?
