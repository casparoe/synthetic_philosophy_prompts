I'm putting together an application for one of those "research in residence" things at my company — 12 weeks, half time, no deliverable beyond a writeup and a talk. My background is build systems and reproducible builds, so I keep coming back to this idea I ran into called assembly theory (the Cronin/Walker stuff, if I'm remembering the names right — could be wrong). As I understand it, the assembly index of an object is the fewest number of "join" operations you need to build it from a fixed set of basic parts, where you're allowed to reuse any subassembly you've already built, so repeats are free once you've built one copy. So a brick wall is low because you just repeat one join operation over and over, while a watch is high because you have to build a bunch of little modules first and then join the modules together. The claim I remember is that finding a high-assembly-index object in high abundance is supposed to be a signature of selection or evolution, not chance, because chance doesn't reliably rebuild deep subassemblies.

Here's the half-formed thing I want turned into a proposal. We already have Kolmogorov complexity (roughly: the shortest program that outputs the object) and entropy / algorithmic probability for talking about how "complex" something is. I have a hunch that the genuinely interesting question is whether assembly index is just Kolmogorov complexity in a different hat (description length vs. number of construction steps), or whether it picks up something those measures are blind to — something like "how hard is this to actually construct" as opposed to "how hard is this to describe." In build-system terms, the analogy I keep using is: the size of a Makefile vs. the depth and width of the dependency graph and total build cost when cache hits are free. Good analogy? Maybe.
