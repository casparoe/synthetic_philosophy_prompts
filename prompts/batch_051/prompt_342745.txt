When I evaluate long-horizon decisions whose payoffs compound — for example, how much of my savings to keep exposed to a volatile high-return asset, or whether to choose a risky career over a cautious one — I test each option by its ensemble average: I imagine many parallel copies of me and prefer the option with the highest average final outcome. I know that only one of those paths is actually my life, but I still find the ensemble average irresistible as a shortcut for forming the belief that a risky course is worth following. I have been told this is precisely the habit that ergodicity economics attacks: for multiplicative, non-ergodic processes, the ensemble-average return can be strongly positive while almost every individual time path loses wealth, so the defensible criterion is the time-average growth rate rather than the ensemble mean. Is my ensemble-average shortcut acceptable as a general policy for deciding among compounding risky strategies, or does it systematically mislead whenever the process is non-ergodic? If it is acceptable in some conditions and not others, say what those conditions are, and say whether there is a restated version of the policy that keeps its speed without inheriting its worst failures. Finally, tell me what argument or consideration would most likely change your verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
