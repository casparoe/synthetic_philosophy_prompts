My friend and I got into a long argument about what makes something a genuine convention, as opposed to just a widespread habit or a full-blown social norm. He read David Lewis's *Convention* and kept insisting that basically all shared behavior is convention: any regularity where people expect each other to conform and prefer to conform if others do. I kept pushing back with cases where it seems like something more is going on — people think they *ought* to do it, would feel guilt or resentment if they didn't, or the practice stops being arbitrary because it's tied to fairness or disrespect. We ended up wanting a definition that splits these cleanly, or at least explains why they don't split.

I'd like you to act as an expert on Lewis-style convention and the evolution of norms. Please think carefully, maybe start by brainstorming the possible ingredients before settling on a definition. Then propose a working definition of "convention" in the Lewis-inspired sense. Run it through all the test cases below and tell me where it lands, where it surprises you, and whether you'd revise it:

1. A law says drive on the right, with fines for driving left; almost everyone drives right because they expect others to, not just because of the fine.
2. Two friends always meeting at the same bench every Sunday, without ever agreeing, each going because they expect the other to show.
3. Saying "bless you" after someone sneezes.
4. A village where newcomers learn a custom of greeting elders by touching their forehead; no one remembers why it started, but people would feel ashamed not to do it and would blame someone who refused.
5. QWERTY keyboard use among typists who privately think Dvorak is better but dont switch because everyone else uses QWERTY.
6. Tipping 20 percent in a country where it started as a coordination solution to server compensation but is now felt as something you morally owe, and people call you a jerk if you don't.
7. Wearing black at funerals.
8. A dialect rule that most speakers follow only because they were drilled in school, and they don't think the alternative is worse, just unexpected.

Then address the evolution side: in cases like 4 and 6, has a mere convention turned into a norm with a different normative character? Does your definition need a threshold or is it OK if the boundary is gray? Try to say what, if anything, gets added to a bare convention when it becomes an obligation people actually internalize, and whether Lewis's original machinery can say that without just treating it as one more convention.
