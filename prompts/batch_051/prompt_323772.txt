In a course on reasons and normativity, our instructor laid out a working test for whether something is a reason, and I have been trying to pressure-test it against a case that keeps troubling me. The test has three conditions. A reason must have representational content: it can in principle be expressed as a consideration that favors or counts against some belief or action. It must be available: a suitably situated deliberator could in principle cite it in justification, rather than its merely operating as a hidden cause. And it must genuinely bear on what it is a reason for: it must support the belief or action, not merely accompany, predict, or produce it. The test works well for standard cases — a wet road as a reason to slow down, a failed control in an experiment as a reason to reject a hypothesis — but I want to see whether it also classifies something more unsettling as a reason.

Consider a liquid futures market in an agricultural commodity, say winter wheat. Imagine a season in which many scattered and individually mundane observations accumulate: a farmer notices frost damage in one county, a shipper hears that planting ran late in another, an exporter sees railcar availability tightening. The price begins to rise within hours, long before any single market participant could have assembled those scraps into a considered judgment about the coming harvest. The movement then does the kind of justificatory work that reasons do: a miller buys more grain now because the forward curve has inverted, a baker insists on a hedge in a contract because the market is signaling scarcity, a government holds off drawing down reserves because the market indicates that supply will be tight. It is natural to say that the price has figured out that next season's supply is constrained, and yet no trader, trading algorithm, or group of traders holds that thought; the conclusion exists nowhere but in the price.

My question is this. Is the consideration carried by the price a reason, under the three conditions above? Go through the conditions one by one, and be explicit about where the case satisfies them, where it fails them, and where the conditions simply leave the matter undetermined. Then tell me what the verdict should push us to conclude: whether the test needs a fourth condition — something like, reasons require a reasoner — or whether the case shows that free-floating rationales, reasons without any holder, are possible after all.
