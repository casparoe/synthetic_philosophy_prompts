At a disciplinary hearing of a professional association, a senior member accused a junior colleague of fabricating performance data in a grant report. The colleague answered that the senior member had falsified trial data in a published paper some years earlier, and closed with: "people in glass houses shouldn't throw stones." In the argument that followed, others restated the point as "he has no standing to bring this accusation," "a hypocrite's charges don't deserve an answer," and "he's in no position to judge." I could tell these were not one claim but several, and that only some of them would actually blunt a true accusation, but the argument moved too fast to sort that out.

I want the analysis I should have had. Lay out the distinct precise claims this family of sayings could be making about blame, criticism, and standing. Group the readings by what they target: the truth of the criticism; the fittingness of the blame itself; the blamer's entitlement to voice it; the fair distribution of blame between two wrongdoers; and the likely effects of letting the accusation proceed. For each reading, state it as exactly as you can, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, and say what work it does or does not do in that room. Then tell me which readings would actually support the junior colleague's deflection, assuming the original accusation is true, and which merely change the subject. Press on one point in particular: whether it matters that the senior member's old wrong was the same kind of wrong — falsification of data — as the wrong now alleged, rather than a merely comparable misconduct.
