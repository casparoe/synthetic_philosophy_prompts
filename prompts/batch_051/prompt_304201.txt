A popular article on algorithmic information theory summarises Solomonoff induction as follows: "Solomonoff solved the problem of induction. He showed that the rational probability to assign to a sequence is a weighted sum over all computer programmes that could have produced it, each weighted by 2 to the minus length of the programme. Because shorter programmes receive exponentially more weight, this formalises Occam's razor: the simplest hypothesis is the most probable. Since a programme's length is just its Kolmogorov complexity, Solomonoff's principle tells us that hypotheses with low Kolmogorov complexity are the ones we should favour. Better still, Solomonoff proved that this predictor converges to the correct probabilities no matter what sequence it is shown. The old philosophical problem of induction is therefore settled, and the only remaining obstacle is that Kolmogorov complexity is undecidable, which prevents us from implementing the predictor in practice." This sounds broadly right to me, but the "no matter what sequence" claim makes me doubtful, and I am unsure whether "undecidable" is even the right term here. Is this a fair summary?
