I'm a journalist who covers biology and the conceptual foundations of science, and I'm working on a piece about why disputes over the definition of life keep recurring. The working definition I bring to interviews is roughly this: a living thing is a bounded system that extracts energy from its environment to keep itself organized away from equilibrium, reproduces with heritable variation, and therefore belongs to a lineage capable of Darwinian evolution. I have no commitment to every clause; I want to know which ones survive contact with hard cases.

The hard case I keep thinking about is a honeybee colony. A colony has a physical boundary, regulates its internal temperature and humidity, forages and stores energy, and reproduces in the only sense that matters ecologically: it sends out swarms and rears new queens. There is also a plausible case that selection acts on colony-level traits such as defensiveness, disease resistance, and foraging strategy, not only on individual bees. But the colony has no single genome and no central controller, and most of its members are sterile workers that cannot themselves reproduce. The puzzle cuts both ways: the worker fails the reproduction clause and would be declared non-living, which almost no one accepts, while the colony passes the ecological version of reproduction and may even be a unit of selection, but is not a single bounded organism in the usual sense. So either the colony and the worker are alive in different senses, or the definition is quietly importing assumptions about what counts as an individual.

Can you go through the criteria one at a time and say honestly where a honeybee colony meets them and where it fails, and whether the colony qualifies as alive in the same sense a worker bee does? Then take up the deeper question: does this case show that something in the definition is carrying more weight than it should—for instance, that reproduction should be attributed to lineages or collectives rather than to bounded individuals, or that boundedness and individuality are doing the real classificatory work while metabolism and evolution mostly trail along—and how would you amend the criteria? If you think the exercise is misframed, for example because I am running together what makes something alive with what makes something one individual organism, I would rather you say that directly and argue for the better framing than politely pretend the test can be applied as written.
