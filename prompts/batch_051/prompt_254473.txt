I teach a graduate seminar on path dependence and historical explanation—about fourteen students and one eighty-minute session—and I want an in-class game that makes the students feel the difference between two claims the literature often runs together: first, that an outcome is sensitive to the order of small early events, and second, that an outcome is locked in because early events select one self-reinforcing equilibrium from several that were genuinely available. I want them to arrive at the distinction themselves, through the game, rather than have me announce it.

Design the activity in full: rules, materials, a timeline, and a payoff scheme that does not use real money. The participants should physically generate a history—for example, by moving tokens between jars under a visible rule of preferential attachment, or by choosing among competing standards subject to local network effects. The game should have two phases. In one phase, small early random differences alter the order and composition of the path, but the process still converges to the same end state; in the other, a threshold or self-reinforcing rule lets the same kind of early random difference determine which of several stable end states gets locked in. Participants should be able to rerun the process, watch different histories unfold from identical starting rules, and then classify for themselves which reruns show mere sensitivity to the past and which show genuine lock-in. The question whether the same outcome would have arrived anyway should be answered by what happens, not by what I say afterward.

Then give me the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto the concepts doing the real work here—increasing returns, switching costs, tipping thresholds, multiple equilibria, and the difference between the past leaving a footprint on the route taken and leaving a footprint on the destination. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it. Finally, be explicit about which feature of historical path dependence the game cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply—in particular, real actors who notice that an outcome is becoming locked in and try to change the rules before it is too late.
