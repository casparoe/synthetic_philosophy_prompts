I am a philosophy professor who works at the intersection of ethics and philosophy of mind, and I want a careful, negotiated analysis rather than a quick verdict. Please take the role of a professional philosopher, think the problem through step by step, and begin by brainstorming the strongest available versions of each side.

Here is the tension. On one side, I am drawn to a version of sentientism about moral status: an entity is owed treatment for its own sake only if it has interests grounded in conscious experience — only if there is something it is like to be it. By that criterion, even a strikingly realistic video-game character is not a moral patient; its cries and recoil are patterns of light and sound with nothing inside, so cruelty toward it may be corrupting or callous but is not a wrong done to it.

On the other side, I have a firm and stubborn intuition that something is morally wrong in a game built around the sadistic torment of a sophisticated simulated person — a character who manifestly suffers, begs, and is degraded. I do not merely find this repulsive; I judge that the torment itself is a proper object of moral criticism, not purely because of its downstream effects on the player or on bystanders.

I need to know whether the theory and the intuition can be reconciled, and if not, which should yield. Give me the strongest case for treating the intuition as a moral datum and the strongest case for debunking it as an artifact of anthropomorphism, simulation-reading habits, or fiction-directed emotion. Then give me the strongest case for standing by sentientism and the strongest case for revising it — including positions such as duties that do not require a patient, moral status for entities that behave as if they feel without having phenomenal states, or locating the wrong in the player's vice or in the expressive meaning of the act. Say which move you would defend, what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, and what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes.
