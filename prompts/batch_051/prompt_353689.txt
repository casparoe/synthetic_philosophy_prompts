Give me a working definition of NIMBYism — not just an expansion of the acronym, but an account of the pattern of local opposition the word is used to name and to accuse — that handles the following cases correctly. In arguments about housing, zoning, and who has a right to the city, I see the term doing two jobs at once: describing where someone stands on a project and condemning the way they stand there. I want a definition that separates those jobs or explains exactly how they are connected.

Here are the cases:

(a) A homeowner in a high-cost city campaigns against a six-story apartment building proposed across the street, citing increased traffic, the loss of afternoon sun in their garden, and the character of the neighborhood. They voted for a regional housing bond and say the city absolutely needs more homes.

(b) A tenant organizer in a gentrifying neighborhood opposes a market-rate tower at the edge of the neighborhood, arguing it will accelerate displacement, while supporting a similar-sized development three blocks away that is majority subsidized.

(c) Residents near a proposed municipal composting facility oppose it because the odor would reach their homes. They say they would support the same facility three hundred meters away.

(d) A city councilmember who campaigns on a build-more-housing platform asks for additional traffic studies and design changes on every project proposed in their ward, and then votes against each one. They have never voted in favor of a housing project in their ward.

(e) An environmental group opposes a coastal development that would fill a rare salt marsh. They also oppose comparable developments along the coast regardless of who lives nearby.

(f) A neighborhood association asks the planning commission to reject the conversion of a vacant office building into a forty-bed homeless shelter two streets away, citing safety and parking, and in the same statement urges the city to build more shelters in commercial districts away from residential streets.

(g) A preservation society opposes tearing down a 1920s building to build two hundred apartments, arguing the building merits landmark designation. The society has also campaigned to landmark buildings in neighborhoods where none of its members live.

My own views: I am confident that (a) is a clear case of NIMBYism, that (e) is clearly not, and that (f) is an especially pure case of it. I am genuinely unsure about (b), (c), and (d). Propose a definition, run it through all seven cases, and say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer; then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. In particular, make it distinguish NIMBYism from three nearby things: ordinary self-interested opposition to a genuinely harmful facility; good-faith project-specific criticism made by someone who happens to live nearby; and abstract support for housing combined with local opposition. I doubt the third is the whole story, since someone can be a NIMBY without ever having supported anything in the abstract. Finally, tell me which of your verdicts you find least secure, and why.
