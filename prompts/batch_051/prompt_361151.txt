I recently reread Plato's Meno, and I found myself bothered by an analogy a teacher of mine used to lean on when explaining why knowledge is better than true belief. In the dialogue Socrates compares true opinions to the statues of Daedalus, which were said to run away unless they were tied down; an untied statue is still a beautiful thing while it stands there, but it will not stay put. A true opinion, on this picture, can guide you just as well as knowledge so long as it is present, but it is liable to slip away; knowledge is true opinion tied down by reasoning about why it is true, and that is what makes it more valuable. My teacher took this to be a complete answer to why knowledge is better than correct opinion, because knowledge, unlike correct opinion, stays with you and can be relied upon. But I suspect the analogy builds its conclusion in. A tied statue is not a better statue than an untied one; tethering merely prevents loss. If knowledge is the tied statue and true belief the untied one, then the analogy seems to show only that knowledge lasts longer, not that it is a better state of mind to be in while it lasts. It also quietly imports the idea that beliefs are possessions we own and that can escape, which seems like the wrong picture. Could you lay out what maps onto what in the statue analogy, where it breaks down, which of the disanalogies actually matter for the question of what makes knowledge more valuable than mere true belief, and whether my teacher's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table? If some other analogy better captures what knowledge adds to true belief, I would like to know what it would be.
