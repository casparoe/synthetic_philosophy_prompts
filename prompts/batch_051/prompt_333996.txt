I have no formal background in philosophy, but I have been trying to settle a disagreement I keep finding in my own thinking about whether there are cases where it is right to believe something for practical reasons rather than strictly because the evidence supports it. The cases that interest me are ones where the belief itself can change what is true, such as a teacher who believes a struggling student will improve, a patient who believes recovery is possible and therefore follows treatment more faithfully, or a person who trusts an unreliable friend and, through that trust, gives the relationship a better chance of becoming trustworthy. One view says these are still just forms of wishful thinking: a belief should answer to the evidence, and the fact that believing would be useful is not evidence for the content of the belief. The opposed view says that when the choice is forced, momentous, and not decidable on the available evidence, and when the belief is partly self-fulfilling, the practical stakes can legitimately settle what one believes; suspending judgment is not a neutral escape, because it too has practical costs.

Please write a dialogue between two proponents of these views. I want both characters to be equally competent and charitable: neither should be a strawman, and each should actually respond to the other’s strongest points rather than talking past them. Have them press each other on whether a belief’s tendency to make itself true can ever count as evidence for its truth, and if not, what other kind of legitimacy it could have. I would also like them to examine whether the two sides are really disagreeing about belief itself, or about something else such as what it means to have “enough” evidence or what it means to act on a belief. Ideally the dialogue should not conclude with a simple compromise; it should end with each character having sharpened their position in response to the other.
