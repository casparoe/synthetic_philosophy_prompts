I'm a phd student working on the ethics of everyday commitments, and I keep chewing on something I've been calling temporal unilateralism. The idea: when two people fix a time to meet, what they've created isn't chiefly a debt of N minutes from the late person to the person waiting. It's a joint entitlement to shape a shared window of time. If you roll up 15 minutes late without a word, the wrong isn't that you cost me 15 minutes — it's that you quietly converted a jointly governed plan into a single-governed one, treating my waiting as a resource you could spend without asking. That would explain why it still grates when I had a book and lost nothing, and why a text saying 'running 20 late' changes the felt wronging even though the delay gets bigger. Is this an actual distinction or just a pompous way of saying rudeness? Has something like it already been worked out under another name — and if so, am I restating it badly? And where does it break? E.g., does it wrongly moralize vague plans, or cases where the waiting really costs nothing, or repeated lateness that's fully mutual? I worry im trying to squeeze a whole theory out of the fact that being kept waiting feels like a small dispossession, but I also can't shake the sense that wether the minutes are trivial or not misses the point.
