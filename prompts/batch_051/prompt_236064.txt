I am a novelist, and over the past several months I have been reading decision theory as background for a book whose protagonist is a professional poker player trying to apply his instincts to early-stage investing. The reading has spilled over into my own convictions, and before I build any of it into the character's interior life, I would like an audit of the position I seem to have ended up with. The commitments are these: (1) the Kelly criterion gives the correct fraction of one's bankroll to stake on a favorable gamble when that gamble will be offered repeatedly over time; (2) expected utility theory is the right general account of rational choice under risk; (3) utility over money is concave — dollars exhibit diminishing marginal value — though I am agnostic about the exact shape; (4) for a one-off gamble, the Kelly criterion is beside the point, and the rational policy is simply to maximize expected utility with one's ordinary utility function; (5) the St. Petersburg gamble should be declined at a sufficiently high entry fee despite its infinite expected monetary value, because the infinite expectation is an artifact of the setup rather than a guide to what one should pay; (6) a person's attitude toward risk should be exhausted by the shape of their utility function over final wealth — there is no separate trait of caution or boldness, and a willingness to take a repeated risk should be fully explicable by final-wealth utility alone.

I cannot tell which of these pull against one another and which merely appear to. Please work through the set and identify the genuine tensions, ranked by severity, with the least costly revision for each; flag any apparent tensions that dissolve under closer inspection; and then say which single revision would do the most to make the whole package cohere. I am less interested in a summary of each commitment than in the structure of the conflicts among them.
