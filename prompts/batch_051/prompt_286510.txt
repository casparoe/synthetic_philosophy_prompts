I have no background in political science or economics, but I follow the debate about AI risk closely, and I have been trying on my own to think about the current AI race — the feeling that labs and governments are sprinting toward powerful AI even though many of the people involved say they would rather everyone slowed down and coordinated. A friend shared a piece by a political scientist named Fearon that gave me a way in. As I understand it, the puzzle is this: war is wasteful, so there is almost always some deal both sides would prefer to fighting; explaining war therefore means explaining why that deal is not made. The piece gives three kinds of obstacle: private information (each side knows things the other does not and has an incentive to bluff), commitment problems (neither side can credibly promise to abide by a deal once the balance of power or the temptation to exploit a head start changes), and indivisibility (the thing in dispute cannot be split).

I built the following argument from that, and its conclusion now looks as if it proves too much.

(1) If two sides would each prefer some bargain to a costly contest, then their failure to reach the bargain must be traced to one of those three obstacles: private information, an inability to commit, or an indivisible prize.

(2) An AI race looks like exactly such a costly contest. The main labs and states would mostly prefer a world in which everyone slowed down and built powerful systems carefully, but they keep spending enormous sums and cutting corners to get there first.

(3) The prize in the AI case is not literally indivisible. Lead time, compute, talent, revenue, safety standards, and regulatory influence can all be split, traded, or compensated for.

(4) Private information also does not look like the core problem, because rival labs know roughly what the others are building, what chips and talent they have, and where the frontier is; they may lack exact details, but they are not operating in the dark.

(5) So the race is driven by a commitment problem. Each side cannot credibly promise to slow down, because once one side has a lead, the temptation and the ability to defect from any slowdown agreement are enormous, and everyone knows this.

(6) But then the same reasoning should apply to every competition in which the parties would prefer mutual restraint but cannot trust one another to show it: a price-cutting contest between two supermarket chains, an arms race, two colleagues staying late at work, neighbors escalating their holiday lights. Each has a costly contest, a bargain everyone would prefer, and a commitment problem standing in the way of that bargain.

(7) And that conclusion seems wrong. A price war between supermarkets is not the same kind of thing as a shooting war, and if a theory of war tells us they are the same kind of thing, then either the word "war" is now doing too much work or the theory explains everything and therefore nothing.

(8) So something in (1) through (6) has to go. If I had to choose what to keep, I would keep the thought that the AI race is mainly a commitment problem, and I would rather give up the idea that this makes it a war. But I do not see how to give that up without also weakening the explanation of actual wars, since the same steps are what got me there.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I do not need references or further readings; I need a diagnosis. If the whole setup is confused — for example, if I am using the word "war" in a way that makes the comparison empty, or if the theory was never meant to draw the line I am asking it to draw — please say so directly instead of rounding the argument into something easier to answer. I would rather get an honest "your premise is false" than a clever repair, and if the right answer is that the argument is valid and I should just accept the conclusion, tell me that too.
