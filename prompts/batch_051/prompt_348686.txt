A friend keeps pushing this argument on me, and I’ve started to think it proves too much, but I can’t find the load-bearing error. Suppose there’s a pill you can take after any major loss that removes grief—the felt pain, the crying, the way the loss colors everything—without impairing memory or your ability to honor the person afterward. No side effects. He argues:

(1) What the pill removes is just suffering, and suffering is intrinsically bad.
(2) Removing grief removes nothing else: you keep the memories, the love, and the capacity to live well; you lose only the pain.
(3) If you can remove something intrinsically bad while losing nothing of value, you should do it.
(4) So after a bereavement you should take the pill, and if everyone should take it, grief should be eliminated altogether.
(5) But a world where no one grieves the people they’ve lost is a world where people love less fully. To love someone is, in part, to be the kind of person who grieves them when they die; someone who takes the pill and feels fine after their spouse dies didn’t love as deeply.
(6) So either grief should be eliminated or love isn’t what we thought it was—and both sound wrong.

I’d most like to reject (5), but every way I try feels like special pleading. Go step by step: which premise or inference is the issue, why did it look right, and what does giving it up cost? And say whether the felt pain of grief and the value of love are actually as separable as the pill story assumes.
