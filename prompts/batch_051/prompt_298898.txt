I'm putting together a unit on Peirce for a course on philosophical methods, and I want to use Sidney Lumet's film 12 Angry Men as a central text rather than as a decorative example. I'd like a committed reading of the film through C. S. Peirce's essay The Fixation of Belief: its four ways of settling opinion — tenacity, authority, the a priori method, and the method of science — together with Peirce's view that inquiry begins in the irritation of doubt and aims at belief constrained by something whose character is independent of what anyone happens to think, and, where it illuminates things, the pragmatic maxim's idea that a conception's meaning is the difference it would make in practice — here, in what a juror is entitled to do with the evidence.

The tension I keep coming back to is this. The film looks at first like a Peircean progression. Juror 8 turns a settled guilty verdict back into a live question, and much of the ensuing deliberation takes the shape Peirce assigns to the method of science: the jury re-enacts the old man's walk, times the El train, tests whether the switchblade was actually as unique as claimed, and treats the marks on the witness's nose as public, checkable evidence. But the deliberation also runs on the very things Peirce thinks the scientific method displaces. Jurors hold their votes out of stubbornness, bend under majority pressure, trust the prosecution's story because it hangs together rather than because it has been tested, and change their minds from fatigue, intimidation, or the wish to get out of the room. And the final verdict is not a settled belief in innocence; it is a shared refusal to claim a settled belief in guilt. That fact looks less like Peirce's picture of inquiry converging on the truth than like fallibilism working under a burden of proof.

I don't want a survey of readings. I want you to commit to a definite interpretive position and defend it against the strongest alternative. The question I want you to settle, not merely pose, is this: is the film best read as a vindication of the method of science and the corrective power of genuine doubt, or as a demonstration that the four methods are never cleanly separable in real, socially pressured inquiry, so that Peirce's account survives best as a normative ideal rather than a description of how belief actually gets fixed? Mark the places where the Peircean lens has to strain or go silent, and say what the film adds — or, if you think it adds nothing, argue that — about persistence, rhetoric, and group dynamics in inquiry that the essays leave comparatively undeveloped.
