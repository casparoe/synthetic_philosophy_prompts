You are a professional philosopher of cognitive science with expertise in both the foundations of computation and the theory of democratic judgment aggregation. Think carefully and do not settle for a quick list of similarities; I want a direct verdict on whether the parallel I have noticed is deep or superficial.

I have been reading a bit of John von Neumann's Silliman lectures, later published as The Computer and the Brain. There he contrasts two strategies for getting reliable behavior out of unreliable components. A digital machine represents information in all-or-none symbols and controls error at discrete stages. The brain, on his account, appears to rely on populations of noisy neurons whose individual firings are unreliable, while the aggregate statistical properties of the population carry the signal. His claim, as I understand it, is that the brain is neither cleanly digital nor cleanly analog but statistical, and that its reliability emerges from redundancy across many imperfect units.

Here is the parallel I cannot decide about. Condorcet's jury theorem says, roughly, that if each voter has an independent probability greater than one half of judging correctly, the probability that a majority of voters judges correctly increases with the size of the group and approaches certainty. This is often framed as reliable collective judgment arising from unreliable individual judgments. That looks, at first glance, like exactly the problem von Neumann is addressing: reliable computation from unreliable components. Both accounts say that the unit-level error is tolerable because the population-level statistic is trustworthy, provided the errors are not too correlated.

But I am unsure whether the identity of structure is real or whether I am just pattern-matching on the word "unreliable." The differences seem substantial. Neurons do not deliberate; voters do. Neuronal codes are continuous, temporally distributed, and population-coded; votes are discrete and propositional. Neural integration is mostly feedforward and spatiotemporally local; voters communicate, persuade, and form coalitions. Neurons do not have interests, incentives, or a common agenda; voters do. Condorcet's theorem also assumes a binary question and a shared standard of correctness, whereas the brain's computational tasks often have no single propositional content and no obvious analog of "voting on the same question."

What I want from you is a precise mapping between the two structures, not a vague comparison. First, state what von Neumann's reliability problem actually requires: what assumptions about noise, redundancy, independence, and signal extraction would have to hold for his neural story to work. Then state what Condorcet's theorem actually requires: what assumptions about individual competence, independence, and binary choice would have to hold for its convergence result. Then tell me how deep the isomorphism is. Are they both instances of one more general theorem about aggregation under independence failures? Or do the differences at the unit level alter the mathematical structure enough that nothing nontrivial transfers? Finally, try to name at least one prediction the analogy would generate on one side that is false on the other, and use that failed transfer to say where the parallel breaks.

I am trying to decide whether von Neumann was, in effect, giving a neural version of the Condorcet jury theorem before that theorem was widely discussed in social epistemology, or whether the similarity is merely a superficial verbal convergence between two things that both happen to involve "many unreliable parts."
