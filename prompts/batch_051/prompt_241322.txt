I am trying to get clear about the logic of the identity claims in the Athanasian Creed (Quicunque Vult), and I would appreciate a close reading of its opening section. The text runs:

For there is one person of the Father, another of the Son, and another of the Holy Spirit.
But the Godhead of the Father, of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit is all one, the glory equal, the majesty coeternal.
So the Father is God, the Son is God, and the Holy Spirit is God;
And yet they are not three Gods, but one God.

Earlier the same section states that the catholic faith is to worship “one God in Trinity, and Trinity in Unity; Neither confounding the persons nor dividing the substance.”

On the most natural reading, the creed commits itself to all of the following: the Father is God, the Son is God, the Holy Spirit is God; the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are numerically distinct persons; and there is only one God. If “is” expresses identity and identity is an equivalence relation, these claims seem inconsistent. Which part of that reconstruction does the text itself push us to revise? Could “God” function here as a kind-term or substance-term rather than an individual-term, so that the three persons count as one God only by sharing one nature? Or does the passage require relativizing identity to a sortal, so that the persons may be the same God while being different persons? What exactly is the “substance” that must not be divided, and does the text’s own wording decide among these options? I am asking only about the formal logic of the passage, not its later theological reception. Keep your answer to no more than 350 words.
