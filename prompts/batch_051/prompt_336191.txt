Running a reading group on Fearon's rationalist explanations for war, and we want to extend the framework to a live question: whether, and through which mechanism, great-power or inter-lab competition over advanced AI is likely to tip into armed conflict. By conflict, include below-threshold uses of force—sabotage of data centers, chip-supply interdiction, exfiltration, preemptive strikes—since that is what an AI race would plausibly produce first. Please produce a decision-tree handout that sorts positions by the mechanism they invoke, built from the logical space of the rationalist framework rather than from a list of labels.

Structure it as follows. The root question separates respondents who grant Fearon's setup (ex post conflict is costly and the parties are minimally unified, instrumentally rational actors) from those who do not; the negative side should get non-rationalist leaves (organizational failure or bounded rationality; ideology, identity, or regime type; pure accident or escalation spirals). On the positive side, branch in this order: first, is there any feasible bargain over the stakes—exclusive control of AGI, shares of capability, timelines, verification, or access—that both sides would prefer to conflict? If not, an indivisibility or winner-take-all leaf. Second, if a deal exists, can the leading or rising party credibly commit to it: no preventive strike against a lab that may soon be ahead, no defection once a deal shifts capabilities, no exploiting a temporary lead? If not, commitment-problem leaves, with preventive-logic and preemptive-logic versions separated where the tree can. Third, if commitment is possible, do the parties' incentives to conceal or bluff about progress, alignment, or resolve make private information the operative problem? If not, the rationalist-baseline leaf: conflict should not occur.

For each leaf give the position's usual name in the rationalist literature, or 'unnamed' if it has none, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment—the one fact about the AI race that most strains it. Keep it to roughly eight to twelve leaves, phrase every branch question so a reader can answer from the case without knowing which leaf they are heading toward, and explicitly flag which pairs of leaves the tree cannot reliably separate. I suspect information-problem and commitment-problem accounts blur in practice, and that preventive versus preemptive logic cannot be read off behavior alone.
