I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend, a committed textualist, about when a court may refuse to apply what a statutory text plainly says. I defended the absurdity doctrine as a narrow safety valve; he kept answering that, apart from correcting an obvious drafting mistake, "absurdity" is just a word a judge attaches to a result he dislikes. The argument made me want to test the criteria I was actually defending.

Here are the criteria I argued a court should apply before it sets aside the plain meaning of a text on absurdity grounds:

1. The text must have a single plain meaning that covers the facts. If ordinary tools of construction resolve the case, the absurdity doctrine does no independent work and should not be invoked.
2. The plain-meaning result must be demonstrably in conflict with the legislature's evident purpose as shown by the statute itself — its structure, findings, or purpose clause — not merely with the judge's sense of fairness or good policy.
3. The conflict must rise to the level of absurdity: the result must be one that no reasonable legislator who deliberately chose these words could have intended. This is stronger than saying the result is harsh, overbroad, or unwise.
4. The corrective reading must remain within what the statutory language can plausibly bear. The court may not write a new rule from scratch; it may only read the enacted words in a way that avoids the absurd application.

Now consider a case that came up tangentially in our argument, and that I could not decide.

Imagine a northern state enacts a statute titled "An Act to Prevent the Introduction of Invasive Aquatic Species." Its purpose section reads:

"The legislature finds that the introduction and spread of invasive, non-native aquatic animals threatens the State's native fisheries and ecosystems, and that these harms are difficult or impossible to undo once a species becomes established. The purpose of this Act is to prevent such introductions."

Its operative section reads: "No person shall knowingly release, or cause to be released, into any waters of this State, any non-native aquatic animal." Violations carry a civil fine of up to $5,000.

A defendant is charged under the Act. He is an aquarium hobbyist who, rather than kill a single guppy he no longer wanted, released it into a pond in a county park. Uncontested expert evidence establishes that guppies are tropical fish that cannot survive the winter in this state and cannot establish a self-sustaining population there. The defendant argues that applying the Act to him is absurd: the Act's own purpose section shows it is meant to stop invasive species, and a fish that cannot survive is, by definition, not invasive. The state responds that the legislature chose the phrase "non-native aquatic animal" instead of "invasive aquatic animal" deliberately, as a bright-line prophylactic rule that spares courts and agencies from litigating invasiveness species by species; enforcing it against a doomed guppy is not absurd but simply the broad coverage the legislature selected.

You are an expert in statutory interpretation and legal philosophy. Think the issue through carefully before answering. Begin by brainstorming two or three plausible readings of the statute and the strongest argument for each side. Then work through the four criteria above, one by one, and determine whether the defendant's case satisfies each. Be explicit about the places where the criteria underdetermine the result. Finally, say clearly whether the case qualifies for the absurdity doctrine as you understand it, and whether the case shows that the criteria themselves need to be revised — and if so, how.
