Im putting together a session for a medical humanities reading group, and were pairing Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go with some material on triage, QALYs, and the value of a statistical life. I want to read the novel through that lens, but I dont want the group to land on the obvious take and stop there.

Here's the angle I want pushed. The world of the novel is, among other things, a rationing scheme: a small number of cloned donors are raised and kept healthy so that a larger number of ordinary people can receive organs. Try reading the people who built that system as if they were trying to run a QALY-maximizing health service, with the clones treated as a supply of quality-adjusted life years. Then ask where that reading fits and where it starts misdescribing the book. In particular, I'd like you to distinguish two things: (a) the system enters the clones' QALYs in the same ledger as everyone else's but assigns them a lower weight, versus (b) the clones' QALYs are never entered at all, because the clones are treated as outside the population whose health gets counted. Which of those is the novel actually running on, and are there scenes that let us tell the difference?

Second, the usual identified-versus-statistical-life point says we overspend on identifiable patients and underspend on statistical ones. The novel seems to flip this: the donors are the ones we actually know (Kathy, Tommy, Ruth), while the beneficiaries stay offstage and statistical. Is the novel making a genuine point about that distinction, or is it just using the machinery of fiction to rig the case against health economics by making us care about particular people? Be honest about that.

Third, for the triage part: triage is supposed to be fair, need-based, and blind to who you are. The novel has pieces that look triage-like (the deferral system, the scheduling of donations, completion, the carers' role), but theyre all bolted on top of a prior decision about who counts. I want to know whether the book is criticizing triage itself, or showing what happens when a triage procedure gets layered onto an already-brutal exclusion.

And please don't just give the reading; push back on it where the book resists it. If Never Let Me Go is really a novel about memory, love, and how people normalize their own exploitation, with the organ-harvesting as a background device, then say so, and tell me which scenes would mislead the group if I framed the whole thing as a rationing novel.
