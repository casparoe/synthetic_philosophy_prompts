I am a PhD student in epistemology working on the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate about testimony, and I have been thinking about how that debate fares once one of its background assumptions is removed: that the testifier is a person. Anti-reductionists hold that being told that p gives the hearer a defeasible, presumptive entitlement to believe p, absent specific defeaters, without requiring prior inductive evidence of the speaker's reliability. Reductionists hold that testimonial justification just is ordinary inductive support for the correlation between report and fact. The canonical cases on both sides involve human speakers: agents with beliefs, communicative intentions, and an epistemic character that can be trusted or blamed. Now suppose a fluent and often accurate large language model assertorically presents p to a user. The surface is nearly indistinguishable from ordinary testimony, but there is no believing agent, no intention to inform, and no one to hold epistemically responsible. Does the anti-reductionist argument — that being told that p is itself a basic, defeasible source of warrant — survive this change? I am not asking whether chatbots literally testify. I am asking which premise was carrying the normative weight — agency, intentional communication, or the bare reliability of an informational channel — and whether the view should be extended, restricted, or restated. Diagnose the argument's fate under this development, and say precisely what would have to be true for it to go through in its original form, or why it cannot.
