The Constitution of the Meridian Philological Society (adopted in 1931 and not since amended) includes the following clause:

Clause 9. A Fellow may resign from the Society by stating to the Honorary Secretary, in person, the words 'I resign from the Society.'

At the annual general meeting, now held by video conference, a Fellow — call her Dr E. — unmutes herself, says nothing, and instead types into the meeting chat, addressed to the Honorary Secretary, 'I resign from the Society.' Her full name appears beside the message, and the Secretary replies in the chat, 'Noted.' Dr E. then leaves the meeting and regards her resignation as complete. The Society does not.

Has Dr E. resigned? The clause predates typed messages, video meetings, and chat windows. Set out what each approach would conclude:

(a) from the plain wording of Clause 9;

(b) from the purpose of the clause, including what the 1931 framers would most plausibly have said if asked about a written message sent during a live meeting;

(c) from a general account of resigning as a performative act — what a statement or writing must be and do in order to bring about a resignation, and whether the Secretary's 'Noted' supplies anything that would otherwise be missing.

Then say which approach ought to decide the case and why, and characterise the others honestly: are they genuine interpretation, or special pleading? Please write your answer in American English.
