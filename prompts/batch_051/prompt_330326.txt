Scheffler argues that what keeps most of our lives meaningful is not the hope of a personal afterlife, as religions have traditionally taught, but our unspoken confidence in what he calls the collective afterlife: the assumption that other human beings will continue to live after we ourselves are dead. He asks us to imagine learning that, although everyone now alive will live out a comfortable natural lifespan, humanity will then die out, whether through infertility or a catastrophe timed to arrive once we are gone. In such a scenario, Scheffler thinks, many of our most important projects would lose their point: cancer research, political activism, the preservation of traditions, perhaps even writing a novel, because these activities derive their meaning from a future they are meant to serve. The piece says this shows that belief in the future of humanity is doing the motivational work that religious believers attribute to heaven and hell, and that personal immortality, by contrast, would be of no value to us. It concludes that on Scheffler's view our deepest commitments are irrational unless we can believe in the indefinite survival of the species, so secular people are ultimately living by faith no less than religious ones.
