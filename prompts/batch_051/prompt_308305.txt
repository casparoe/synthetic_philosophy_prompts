Okay so I'm in a course on early modern theories of money and trade, and I wrote up this argument for a response paper. Now it feels like it proves way too much, and I can't find the exit. Can you tell me where it goes wrong?

(1) If a country's stock of gold or silver increases, domestic prices will eventually rise. (2) Once domestic prices rise, the country's exports become dearer to foreigners and imports become cheaper at home, so specie flows out until the price level falls back into line with it's trading partners. (3) So the balance of trade is self-correcting, and an injection of money can't permanently make a country richer in gold or keep its prices out of line. That's basically Hume's price-specie-flow point. (4) But if that adjustment mechanism always neutralizes changes in the money stock, then money is just a veil: doubling the quantity of money just doubles prices and wages in the end. (5) So monetary policy can have no lasting effect on real output or employment; it only changes nominal magnitudes. (6) But if (5) is true, then monetary expansion can never boost industry, not even temporarily. Yet Hume himself says, in the same set of essays, that when new money first flows into a country it gives trade and employment a boost before prices catch up. (7) So either Hume's price-specie-flow mechanism is wrong, or his short-run stimulus claim is wrong—and either way he's contradicting himself.

I'd most like to keep (1) through (3) and Hume's short-run point in (6), but I don't know what that forces me to give up. Which step or inference do you reject, and why did it look fine? Answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
