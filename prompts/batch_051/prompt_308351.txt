I’ve been rereading Isaiah Berlin’s essay Two Concepts of Liberty and trying to stress-test one of its central arguments against a development Berlin never had to think about. As I understand Berlin, he argues that positive liberty—freedom understood as being governed by one’s own higher or rational self—contains a dangerous internal move. The self gets split into a higher part and a lower, merely empirical part; the higher part can then be identified with something larger than the individual, such as a class, nation, state, or church; and once that happens, coercing actual people can be redescribed as freeing their “real” selves. Berlin treats this as the main mechanism by which positive liberty becomes a justification for tyranny, and he thinks negative liberty—the absence of interference by others—is less vulnerable because it deliberately avoids questions about which self is the true one.

The development I want to bring to this is the rise of recommendation systems, feed-ranking algorithms, and large language models. These systems do not simply satisfy a person’s existing preferences; they shape a person’s sense of which desires are authentically theirs, which higher-order goals matter, and what kind of person they are. They do this through micro-predictions, A/B testing, and reward shaping rather than through overt ideological argument, and they operate mostly below the threshold of conscious belief. So here is the question: does Berlin’s argument against positive liberty survive this development? If an algorithm can nudge someone’s empirical preferences while simultaneously training them to regard the resulting preferences as their own higher values, does that show the positive-liberty corruption Berlin identified can now happen without any tyrant, or does it instead put pressure on Berlin’s contrast by showing that negative liberty is also vulnerable to a different kind of hijacking—one that does not need a social whole to claim to speak for the real self? I’m less interested in a policy conclusion than in what happens to the argument itself. Which premise of Berlin’s is most under pressure, and is there a repaired version of the positive/negative contrast that still does useful work?
