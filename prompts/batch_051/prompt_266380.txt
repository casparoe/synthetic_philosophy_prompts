I work as a software engineer at a mid-sized company that sells a workflow-automation platform, and next month we are formally decommissioning its oldest module, which has been running in some form since the product began fifteen years ago. The occasion will be a small internal ceremony attended by the engineers who have maintained the module over the years and by executives, sales staff, and newer employees who have never seen the code but speak of it with almost superstitious reverence as the thing the whole business is built on. I have been asked to give the closing remarks, five to seven minutes, largely because I have been there longer than most of the engineers and have personally rewritten a good deal of the module.

Here is the difficulty I want the speech to manage. The module has been replaced piece by piece so many times that, as far as anyone can tell, not a single line of the first released version is still running; the database schema has been changed twice, the original programming language has been deprecated, and the engineers now refer to the system by a different internal name, although the executives and sales staff still use the old one. I want the speech to take the Ship of Theseus seriously rather than treat it as a passing joke, because the structure of that puzzle actually dictates what it would be honest for me to say in this room. If there is no fact of the matter about whether what is being retired is the same system that launched fifteen years ago, then I cannot simply eulogise a death, nor simply praise an unbroken continuity, without quietly taking a side. The speech should be built around that tension, and it should land for both halves of the audience rather than becoming a lecture.

I have no formal philosophy background and know the puzzle only in its ordinary telling, about a ship whose planks were all gradually replaced, so please keep the speech free of jargon, named authors, and references to any literature. Please write it in British English, as continuous prose in the manner of a speech script or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and leave the audience with something to feel rather than a set of propositions to accept.
