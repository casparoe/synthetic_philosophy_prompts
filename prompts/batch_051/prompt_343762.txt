After a long argument with a friend about whether ranking historical atrocities is a defensible exercise or an obscene one, I turned the disagreement into the following exam question. Please grade the student answer reproduced below: assign a numerical grade out of 10, and then justify it point by point — identifying which claims are correct, which are mistaken or conflated, what does not follow, and what a full-credit answer would need to add.

Exam question: Assess the claim that ranking historical atrocities — such as the Holocaust, the Armenian genocide, and the transatlantic slave trade — is neither possible nor morally permissible because the very act of ranking is obscene. Distinguish the objections that may be run together in that claim, and evaluate which, if any, succeed.

Student answer: The question bundles several objections. First, there is the feasibility objection: ranking requires a common metric; no such metric exists; therefore single-scale rankings of atrocities are impossible. Partial rankings along specific dimensions — number killed, duration, directness of intention — remain possible, and historians rely on them routinely. Second, there is the obscenity objection proper. Placing horrors on a single ladder implies that there is a common currency of suffering, which fails to respect each event's singularity. I think this objection succeeds for public institutional rankings but not for private reflection, because in private no one's dignity is publicly impugned. Third, there is the standpoint objection, the idea that no one can occupy a position from which to compare. This has little force, because courts compare wrongs whenever they sentence offenders. Fourth, there is the recognition worry, that ranking pressures victim groups to compete for status as the worst-off. That is a genuine social cost, but it does not show ranking is incoherent; it shows only that rankings can be misused. Finally, some philosophers describe these wrongs as incommensurable, by which they mean that the wrongs cannot be compared in any way at all; but that is clearly false, since the Holocaust was worse than a single murder. So the real issue is not comparability but which comparisons we have reason to make.
