I'm trying to figure out how much of a popular account of large language models I should believe. A friend shared a long explainer from a technology magazine — the kind with a confident thesis — arguing that we should stop calling LLMs "agents." The core of the piece runs something like this. Before any fine-tuning, a language model is not trained to have beliefs, goals, or a stable self; it is trained to predict text, and in learning to predict text it learns to simulate an enormous variety of possible authors, characters, and styles. When you prompt such a model, you are not conversing with "the model" itself; you are selecting among these latent personas and letting the selected character improvise. The friendly assistant we meet in a chat interface is simply one more persona, a role the model happens to play because the prompt and the product design cast it in that role. Treating the model as an agent with intentions is therefore a kind of category error — as odd as asking what a novel wants, or whether a stage actor genuinely believes her lines. The author concludes both that attributing agency to LLMs is false in any interesting sense, and that talk of "misaligned LLM goals" rests on the same mistake.

I showed this to a friend who works on these systems, and he said the piece is "half right but sloppy about fine-tuning and about what 'agent' is supposed to mean" — suggestive, but not very helpful. I would like an audit of the summary rather than a one-word verdict. Can you separate what in that account is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what a careful defender of the simulator framing would insist has been overstated or run together? Most importantly, if the overstatements are corrected, does the author's conclusion — that LLMs are not agents and that "LLM goals" is a confusion — actually survive?
