ok ive been turning a distinction over in my head for a while and i want someone to push back on it properly. the debate about whether using AI erodes understanding always seems to be about offloading the answer: you let the model write the code or do the reading, and then you gradually lose the ability to do it yourself, deskilling and all that. i think that's the boring part, and honestly a lot of it is just normal forgetting with a tech scapegoat. the thing i actually think is corrosive is something else, and i want to know if it holds up.

roughly, i want to distinguish three things you can offload. (1) execution — doing the steps. (2) judgment — deciding what matters and what's worth asking. (3) the check — deciding whether your answer is right. my claim is that offloading (1) is mostly harmless, offloading (2) is bad but people at least notice it, and offloading (3) is what quietly eats understanding because it looks like being careful. i know the obvious objection is GPS and pilots — offloading execution obviously does destroy skill there. but my move is that those cases are secretly (3): the pilot also loses the ability to tell when the autopilot's picture is wrong, because the autopilot has been the picture for so long.

the mechanism for (3) is what ive started calling "the auditor's fallacy." you use a model to produce an answer, then you check it — but the standards you check against, and your sense of what a mistake looks like, were themselves supplied by the same model in earlier interactions. so the audit is written by the auditee, and you feel like you're independently verifying the output when the loop is closed. a coder who reviews the model's code by asking it "are there bugs?" is the obvious case. less obvious: a student who gets summaries from a model and then tests their own understanding by generating quiz questions, but the only questions they can generate are rephrasings of the summary. the model is both generator and textbook, so a systematic but subtle error just passes through the check forever and gets baked into whatever "understanding" the person has.

what i want checked: is this a real distinction or am i just relabelling skill loss? is the auditor's fallacy already a known idea under a better name (it feels like it must be)? and is my actual claim right — that offloading the check, not offloading execution, is what actually erodes understanding? give me the strongest case where someone offloads the check and its totally fine, and the weakest case where someone only offloads execution and their understanding still gets hollowed out. if the third category collapses, tell me why.
