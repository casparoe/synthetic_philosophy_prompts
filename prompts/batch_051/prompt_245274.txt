I am an actuary, fluent in my own framework the way one is fluent in a native language and aware that I am an amateur in the philosophical one. For most of my career I treated the vocabulary of my profession as self-sufficient for the decisions it makes: pools, premiums, underwriting, risk classification, adverse selection, moral hazard, reserves, tail risk. That changed a few months ago, when my sister was diagnosed with a rare condition whose treatment costs more each year than most policyholders will claim in a lifetime, and I was asked to join the committee that decides whether our plan will cover it. The committee's argument keeps circling a question I now believe is philosophical, though no one in the room would call it that: whether a small premium increase spread across every member can be justified by the enormous benefit to a very few, or whether there is something wrong with letting a sum of many small burdens outweigh one person's severe claim, wrong in a way our models do not even have a field for.

I have been trying to educate myself in the relevant philosophy, and the debate that seems to get closest is the one between Scanlon's contractualism and its aggregative critics. Let me state my working understanding, and correct it where it is wrong. On the contractualist picture, an act is wrong when it would be disallowed by principles for the general regulation of behavior that no one could reasonably reject; because objections must be voiced by individuals rather than summed across a population, the fact that many people would each bear a small cost cannot by itself justify imposing a severe burden on one person. Aggregative views reject that last step and treat the sum of small costs as exactly the kind of thing that can outweigh a concentrated one.

What I need from you is a translation, not a summary. Restate this debate in the vocabulary I actually work in, premiums and rate setting, expected cost, underwriting and risk classification, exclusions and coverage limits, coinsurance and deductibles, adverse selection, moral hazard, reserves, tail risk, as faithfully as that vocabulary allows. Take the components one by one: reasonable rejection, the individualist restriction on objections, the distinction between a burden borne by a person and a burden spread across a pool, and the idea that a principle's acceptability to each person is what does the justificatory work. For each, tell me whether it translates cleanly, translates only with distortion, or has no expression in my framework at all. Do not smuggle actuarial assumptions into the translation and call that fidelity; flag every place where my framework simply presupposes the aggregative conclusion. Then give me your honest verdict on the residue: does the failure of some elements to translate show that actuarial reasoning is blind to a moral feature it needs, or that the contractualist's anti-aggregation claim is less determinate than it first appears? I need an answer I could carry back into that committee room, not a reading list.
