Gödelian arguments against mechanism of the kind associated with Lucas and Penrose run roughly as follows: any consistent formal system strong enough to express arithmetic contains an arithmetical sentence it cannot prove, but we can stand outside the system and recognize that sentence as true; therefore the human mind is not such a system. The argument was formulated when the main rival picture of the mind was an axiomatic, rule-following system. Today's most capable artificial systems are not presented that way: they are trained on vast corpora, are not axiomatized, are not provably consistent, cannot be identified with any fixed set of inference rules, and routinely assert inconsistent things. Does the Gödelian anti-mechanist argument survive this development? Identify precisely which premise is under pressure, being careful to distinguish what the incompleteness theorems themselves show from what the argument needs them to show, and say whether a repaired version remains that is weaker but still significant, or whether the argument should be abandoned.
