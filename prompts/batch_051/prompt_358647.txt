I serve as an external member of the governing council of a large public university in a country with an English-style funding model: undergraduate tuition is capped at roughly £9,000 per year, most full-time students borrow to cover it, and repayment is income-contingent over a long horizon with unpaid balances eventually written off. The students' union has moved that the council adopt, as the university's formal position in its submission to the government's forthcoming higher-education funding review, the principle that undergraduate education should be free at the point of use and funded from general taxation along German or Nordic lines. The motion rejects tuition fees and student debt outright, and the council is split three ways. One group supports the motion on fairness grounds, arguing that charging for university turns a public good into a privately financed risk, deters debt-averse and low-income applicants, and protects incumbent graduates by shifting the burden onto later cohorts. A second group argues the motion mislocates the incidence of the costs: tax-funded tuition would largely transfer resources to the future graduates who capture most of the wage premium, while income-contingent loans already function as a capped graduate contribution, so the real policy levers are the repayment threshold, the contribution rate, and the write-off terms rather than the existence of debt as such. A third group cautions that the German and Nordic comparison is doing hidden work, because those systems sit inside different admissions, vocational-training, and labor-market institutions, and importing the funding rule would not import the outcomes.

I have been asked to draft the decision memo to be voted on at the next council meeting. The memo should make a definite recommendation: adopt the motion, reject it, or adopt a specific amended position that you formulate, with reasons the other factions can engage with and with the strongest consideration on the losing side treated honestly rather than caricatured. Please do not lay out the options and leave the choice to me; take a position and defend it. Near the end, identify one measurable feature of our own system — something about applications, completion, graduate earnings, or the public finances — that, if it turned out to be convincingly different from what your recommendation provisionally assumes, would most undermine that recommendation.
