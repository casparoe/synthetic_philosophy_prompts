I'm not in school or anything, but I've been going down a rabbit hole about the East India Company lately — how it had it's own army, made treaties, collected taxes, and governed territory — and it got me wondering what actually separates a company from a state. In the group chat someone said a state is basically a corporation that got big enough to claim a monopoly on force, and they pointed to chartered companies like the Dutch and English East India Companies as proof that the line is just a legal fiction. Someone else said no, that's just a metaphor: a real state has public purposes and legitimacy, while a company exists to return profits to shareholders, and no matter how many guards it hires, a private prison or a mining firm with its own security force still isn't a state. I want to audit the analogy properly. If the state is like a corporation, what maps onto what? Territory to property? Citizens to shareholders, customers, or employees? Taxes to revenue, law to contract, war to hostile takeover? Where does the mapping break, and which disanalogies actually matter for the question of whether corporate power counts as political power? Also, if it's mostly a bad analogy, is there a better one, or should we just treat the state as its own thing?
