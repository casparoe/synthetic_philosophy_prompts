I sit on the board of a data-journalism nonprofit that covers science and technology, and in a month I am presenting to our editorial advisory group a thesis I have been developing over the past year: that the debate about whole-brain emulation has been organized around the wrong central question. Most public discussion asks whether an emulation would be conscious, or whether it would be identical to the original mind. I want to argue that those questions are downstream of a deeper one the field itself has flagged but failed to pursue: what exactly counts as a functionally adequate emulation, given that fidelity is always partial and that different scientific uses of an emulation demand different tolerances for distortion.

My audience is not a philosophy department. It is a group of working science reporters and editors, several computational neuroscientists who consult on our modeling coverage, two bioethicists who work on human-subject and animal-research issues, and one member trained in analytic philosophy but employed as a science-policy analyst. They know the standard upload scenarios and the standard personal-identity objections. What they have not read carefully is the primary source that shifted my view. I want the Q&A preparation to center on a passage from Anders Sandberg and Nick Bostrom, *Whole Brain Emulation: A Roadmap*, Future of Humanity Institute, Oxford University, 2008. The passage is:

“The scan-and-simulate approach aims at generating a simulation that is so close to the original that it would share the same essential properties. The success criterion is not that the simulation be computationally indistinguishable from the original for all possible purposes, but that it have a sufficient degree of similarity to the original for the purposes of the emulation.”
