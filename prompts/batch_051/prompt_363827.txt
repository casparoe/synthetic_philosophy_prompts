I am a PhD student working on welfare economics, and I have been trying to work out what I actually think about revealed preference: specifically, when a choice reveals a preference and when it merely reflects a framing effect, a mistake, or a bias. My verdicts on particular cases seem to come apart, and I would like to know whether there is one principle behind them.

1. A colleague says, even when there are real stakes attached to her answers, that she values a short commute far more than a nice view. Yet when she has to choose between two flats, she takes the one with the much longer commute and the better view. I think her choice reveals that she in fact values the view more; her stated priorities are cheap talk.

2. A shopper is offered two identical yoghurts, one labelled 90% fat-free and the other labelled 10% fat. She picks the first. In a later session, with the labels swapped, she picks the other. I think neither choice reveals a preference between the yoghurts; it reveals a labelling effect.

3. A rushed buyer chooses a laptop that is worse on every feature he had himself ranked as important, because the spec sheet was confusing. I think his choice does not reveal his preference; it reveals a mistake.

4. A student books an early exam slot months in advance, but when a late rescheduling window opens the night before, she switches to a later slot. I think the earlier choice is the better guide to her long-run preference, and the later switch is present bias rather than a change in preference.

5. A woman choosing between two restaurants always picks the Italian one over the Thai one. When a third restaurant that she regards as clearly worse is added to her choice set, she switches to the Thai one. I think her original choices revealed a preference for the Italian restaurant over the Thai one, and the later choice is an artifact of the menu, not a real reversal.

I can see that (1) has me trusting a choice against the person's own stated ranking, while (2) and (3) have me refusing to read a preference off a choice, and (4) has me trusting one choice over another choice by the same person. Is there a coherent account of which choices reveal preferences that delivers all, or nearly all, of these verdicts? If there is not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and why? Finally, whatever principle you propose, please say what it predicts for someone who reverses a choice when the same options are merely presented in a different order, and whether it treats utility as the ranking that rationalizes choices or as a welfare ranking that choices only partially track.
