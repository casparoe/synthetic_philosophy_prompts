I'm taking a course on ancient political thought, and we've reached the part of Aristotle's Politics where he argues that some human beings are slaves by nature. I tried to reconstruct his reasoning as a numbered argument so I could see exactly where to push back, but I keep finding that each step sounds more defensible than I expected, even though the conclusion still strikes me as wrong. Here is the reconstruction I've been working with:

(1) Nature is purposive: it fits each kind of thing for its proper work, and it arranges the naturally superior to rule the naturally inferior for the benefit of both. (2) The soul is the natural ruler of the body, and a human being is the natural ruler of a nonhuman animal; in both cases the ruled part is better off for being ruled. (3) Some human beings share in reason only to the extent of perceiving and obeying it, not to the extent of deliberating with it, and they are naturally fit for bodily labor. (4) A human being of that sort stands to a fully rational human being as the body stands to the soul, or as a tamed animal stands to a human being. (5) Whatever stands in that relation is a slave by nature: for such a being, being owned and ruled by a rational person is both advantageous and just. (6) Therefore, if there are human beings of the sort described in (3), natural slavery is just—even if many people enslaved by law or conquest are not natural slaves at all.

I half-suspect the problem is step (4), because the analogy between a less rational human being and a body or an animal looks as though it is doing a great deal of work. But I cannot tell whether the real failure is there, or earlier in the teleology of (1), or in the slide from "beneficial" to "just" somewhere around (5). Is the argument valid as stated? If it is, are the premises true? If it is not, identify exactly which step or steps fail, give the strongest reading of each on which it might be true, and then check whether the conclusion still follows on those readings.
