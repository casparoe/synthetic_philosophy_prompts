I am a retired immunologist, and after a career spent thinking about host–pathogen interactions I have recently come to moral psychology and ethics by way of the literature on disgust. I am drafting a short paper on whether purity and sanctity can survive a debunking account of disgust, and before I circulate it I want an audit of whether the position I hold actually hangs together.

I hold, with varying degrees of confidence, the following claims:

1. Disgust is a biological affect whose original and still-dominant function is pathogen and contamination avoidance. Its elicitors are best predicted by contamination cues, violations of the body's physical boundaries, and reminders of human animality, and only weakly by independent facts about wrongness, harm, or injustice.

2. From this I draw a debunking conclusion: that a practice or class of persons elicits disgust is never itself a reason to judge the practice or the persons morally wrong. Where a moral judgment rests principally on disgust, the judgment should be presumed suspect until it can be regrounded in something other than the aversion itself.

3. Nonetheless, I am not an abolitionist about purity and sanctity. I believe there are genuine values at stake in at least some norms that are, as a matter of psychological fact, standardly carried by disgust: prohibitions on desecrating human remains, on the buying and selling of votes or parental rights, and on some violations of bodily integrity. I take these to protect respect for persons, bodily integrity, and the non-fungibility of certain goods.

4. I also hold a justificatory principle: a moral distinction earns its place only if it can be justified by reasons articulable to someone who does not already feel the distinction's pull. Brute appeals to sacredness — saying that a thing is simply not done, or that its sacredness is self-evident — carry no justificatory weight.

5. I hold, further, that for at least some of the norms in (3) there is a fact of the matter about whether the norm tracks one of the underlying values I listed, rather than merely rationalizing a prior revulsion.

6. Finally, I suspect that moral progress can be partly characterized as the replacement of disgust-based enforcement of norms by reason-based enforcement, while the content of some — not all — sanctity norms survives the replacement.

My question is not whether the position is correct, but whether it is internally stable. Which of these commitments are in genuine tension with one another, which pairs only appear to be, and how should the tensions be ranked by severity? For each genuine tension, please say what would have to give and what the least costly revision would cost me.
