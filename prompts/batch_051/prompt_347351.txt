Put on your game-theorist hat. I want a careful, street-fighting analysis, not a literature review.

Scenario. A firm runs its annual budget negotiation as a centipede game between two divisions. The pot starts at £10,000. On each turn the division whose turn it is can Take or Pass. If it Takes, it gets 60% of the pot, the other division gets 40%, and the game ends. If it Passes, £10,000 is added to the pot and the other division gets the turn. There are six turns in total; if both divisions pass on all six, the pot is split 50/50. Standard backward induction says the first mover takes on turn one, so the growing pot is never touched.

The finance director is fed up with this and proposes a pass-pledge scheme. Before the game, either division can register a binding pledge with the finance office to Pass on a specified future turn (for instance, it's second turn). The pledge is made public. When registering, the pledger must lodge a deposit equal to 40% of the pot that would stand at the pledged turn if everyone passes until then. If that turn arrives and the pledger Takes anyway, the deposit goes to the other division on top of the ordinary Take payoffs. If the other division Takes before the pledged turn, the pledge is cancelled and the deposit is refunded. If the pledger keeps the pledge, the deposit is refunded after the game. Registering a pledge costs a flat £100, paid up front and not refundable.

Analyse how this would actually play out between two rational, self-interested, fully informed divisions. Work through what the pledge does to the backward-induction argument. Who pledges, on which turn, and why? Does the pot get any further than it would without the scheme? What are the failure modes and gaming strategies — for example, pledging to pass when you would have passed anyway, or using a pledge to bait the other side into passing? Can those be fixed without gutting the scheme, or does the cure just push the commitment problem back one level?

Start by thinking out loud about a smaller case (say four turns) before you tackle the full six-turn game. I'd rather see the reasoning than just a verdict.
