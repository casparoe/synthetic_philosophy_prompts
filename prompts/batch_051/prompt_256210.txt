I am a PhD student working on scalable oversight, and the center of my research is the debate protocol: the idea that when a judge is too limited to evaluate a claim directly, two capable agents arguing opposite sides, with the judge free to interrogate them, can reliably surface the truth—because a false position should be harder to defend than to expose once follow-up questions are allowed.

I have noticed that I have imported this idea into my own reading habits, and I cannot tell whether it is a strength or a bias. My policy, stated bluntly: I will not move to a negative view of any proposed alignment technique unless I have seen an actual adversarial exchange about it—a critique to which the technique's defenders have replied on the merits, ideally over multiple turns. A sharply argued critique that draws no public response from the original authors leaves me almost unmoved, and I find myself treating that silence as weak evidence that the critique was answered privately, was confused, or was not worth engaging. I also hold myself to a version of the rule: I will not criticize a proposal in our reading group unless I would be willing to defend the criticism against the proposal's authors directly. Last week I dismissed a lengthy critique of a debate-based oversight scheme largely because the scheme's authors never engaged with it, and a colleague told me I was turning the field's norms of public adversarial exchange into a private epistemology. I suspect there is something to that, but I also know I cannot independently verify most of the claims in this literature, and some filter is unavoidable.

My question is whether this policy is defensible, or whether it is a bias dressed up as a rule. Frame the answer as an evaluation of the policy itself rather than a verdict on any particular paper or author. In particular: under what assumptions does insisting on recorded adversarial exchange track truth rather than mere responsiveness, and when does it systematically fail—when the truth-teller is less rhetorically skilled, when both sides share an unexamined premise, when the exchange is shaped by incentives other than accuracy, or when no one's career rewards engaging with weak but viral criticism? Is my habit simply the debate protocol applied to my own information diet, and is that transfer legitimate given that the protocol was designed for a quite different setting—well-matched agents and a judge who can cross-examine indefinitely? Is an author's silence a rationally usable signal, and at what weight? Finally, is there a corrected version of the policy that keeps its benefits without collapsing into deference toward the combative, the articulate, and the well-connected? Please separate what is settled about debate as an epistemic process from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and please write the answer in British English.
