I teach a philosophy of law seminar and every year I run the students through the standard list of features for a legal fiction: the law asserts some factual claim that everyone involved knows is false, it does so to bring a case under a rule that wouldnt otherwise reach it, the pretense can't be rebutted by evidence, and courts don't actually believe it—they just decline to let reality interfere. Classic cases: a corporation is a "person," an heir conceived before the testator's death is treated as born, a jury "finds" that a lost grant once existed to justify adverse possession. Fine.

Now I'm stuck on the reasonable person. The law asks whether a reasonable person would have foreseen the harm, whether a reasonable person would have felt provoked, and so on. Nobody has ever met her. She is a construct built out of norms of prudence and self-control, and the law treats her verdicts as binding even though she never existed. But on the other hand, the law isnt making a false factual assertion about some actual individual; it is stating a standard. There is no pretense for the court to believe, because the reasonable person is the measuring instrument, not a fact the court is pretending is true.

So: is the reasonable person a legal fiction? Work through the criteria one by one and tell me where she lands. Then go further: if she does qualify, the category starts to swallow every normative standard in the law, and maybe that is right—law might be shot through with fictional entities. If she does not qualify, then the criteria are doing something subtler than they look and I want to know what. Either way, tell me what kind of thing the reasonable person is, and what her case shows about what legal fictions really are.
