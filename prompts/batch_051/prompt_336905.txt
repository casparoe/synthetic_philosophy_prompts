I keep noticing a pattern in how communities handle painful pasts, and I can't find a term for the exact thing. A city keeps every police file from a period of state violence because the victims are owed public remembrance. Thirty years later the same city votes to seal some of those same files for another fifty years, not out of shame or a cover-up, but because they say a decent society also needs what they call a right to a thinner past, so new generations have room to act. In a second case, a granddaughter keeps a shoebox of letters her grandmother wrote before she was disappeared, and every year she deliberately burns one letter, as a duty to the dead, until the archive is gone. In both cases the keeping and the destroying don't look like enemies in a debate; they look like two stages of one practice. Is there an established name for the view that forgetting can be something you owe, not just a failure or a crime? How does it sit next to the duty to remember, to amnesty, and to the older practice of striking a disgraced person's name from public monuments, and where exactly are its edges? If the nearest term only half-fits, draw the boundary rather than hand me a label.
