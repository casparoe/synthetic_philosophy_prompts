Longtime reader of rationalist-adjacent blogs here, and there's a pattern I keep noticing in the wild that I don't have a word for. The clearest specimen is Augustine on lying. You get the absolute rule up front: every lie is a sin, no exceptions, not even to save a life. Fine. But then, in the same breath, you get an elaborate ranking of kinds of lies, from really grave ones (lies about doctrine) down to small ones that hurt nobody, with the bottom rungs described as "still sins, but much lighter." Only the rule never does the actual work—its the ladder. People consult the ladder, see that a particular lie sits near the bottom, and then act as if that's the one you should tell when things get hard, while refusing to ever say the rule has exceptions.

I've seen the same shape with promise-keeping: a blanket "never break a promise," plus a severity ranking from "broken on a whim" down to "broken under pressure," and the ranking quietly decides the cases while the blanket rule stays on the books. It isn't exactly consequentialist weighing, because they insist the bottom-rung act is still wrong; and its not plain absolutism either, since a plain absolutist shouldn't need the ladder except to calibrate how bad to feel. So: does this configuration have a name? If there's an established term, I want to know whether what I'm describing fits it or is only adjacent to it; if there isn't, I'd like a coined term with a definition and boundary cases. A bare label won't help much. I want to know how to carve the thing: is the position even stable, and if the grading does all the deciding, hasn't the person actually given up the no-exceptions rule without noticing? And what exactly separates this from views that openly say "choose the lesser wrong"?
