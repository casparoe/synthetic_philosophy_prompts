I'm a novelist, and I've been chewing on this for years: the best stuff in a story is the stuff the sentences never actually say. You show a character standing at a window and the reader gets the grief or the relief or whatever it is, and no single line spells it out. That's made me wonder about the old mystical question — can what can't be said still be known? Here's the specific thing I want.

Draft the joint statement that two camps could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. Camp A — call them the ineffabilists — holds that there is real cognitive contact with things that language cannot in principle capture, and that it deserves the name knowledge: it can guide action, improve judgment, teach you something. Camp B — call them the discursivists — holds that if a content can't even in principle be said, it can't be a truth, can't be a premise, can't be something two people could check agreement about, so 'knowledge' is just dressing up a feeling. Don't settle for mush; don't write something both would sign because it's vacuous. I want the real document.

Format it like this: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims, specific enough that signing them costs something; (2) a section that states exactly where they still differ, with each camp's position worded in a way that camp would accept, not a caricature of it; (3) a table of cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side — with, for each crux, a case or kind of evidence both sides agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance.

While you do that, keep the layers seperate: not-yet-said vs. can't-be-said-by-me vs. can't-be-said-in-principle; propositional knowing-that vs. knowing-how vs. knowledge by acquaintance; and the novelist's case — a reader who comes away from a story newly able to notice a certain kind of self-deception, but who couldn't state what the story taught them. I'd also want you to say, straight out, whether some of this fight is really about the word 'know' and some of it isn't, instead of dissolving the whole thing into semantics or refusing to admit any of it is semantic.
