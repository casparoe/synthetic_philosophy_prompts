I am a journalist who covers the science of ageing and the intellectual culture around it, and I want to pressure-test a position I have drifted into over several years of reporting.

The position is not primarily a normative one. I do not claim that radical life extension is obviously desirable, or that the standard objections to it fail on the merits. The position is explanatory: I have come to think that the most influential objections to substantially extending healthy human lifespan — the tedium objection, the claim that a life needs a bounded narrative to be meaningful, the worry that longer lives would ossify institutions and block generational turnover, the concern about intergenerational injustice, and the charge that pursuing it is hubristic or unnatural — are, in the form they usually take in public debate, largely rationalisations of the status quo. People begin from the brute fact that a human life normally lasts several decades, adapt their values and narratives to that horizon, and then generate reasons that make the horizon bearable or even precious. I suspect the real drivers are status quo bias, hedonic adaptation, fear of raising hopes about a prize that may never arrive, something like terror management, and the formation of adaptive preferences of the sort that make the unattainable look not merely forgiven but chosen. I have also come to think that the polemical term "deathism", used by some longevity advocates, points at something real even when it is deployed unfairly.

I am aware that this is itself a debunking position, and that debunking positions can be self-insulating: once opponents have been explained away as rationalising, every further argument they offer can be dismissed as more rationalisation. So I want to know what it would actually take for me to be wrong.

Please treat the explanatory claim — that the prominent case against life extension is best accounted for as motivated cognition of roughly this sort — as the object to be tested. What would count against it? Which observations, demographic or cross-cultural patterns, psychological findings, philosophical distinctions, or individual cases would be hardest for it to accommodate? What does the debunking account predict that rival accounts of the same opposition do not? Sort the defeaters roughly from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about what a defender of the debunking account should themselves concede counts against it, as opposed to what its critics merely say. Tell me too whether the position, as I have stated it, has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and, if it has, whether that is a problem for an explanatory claim of this kind.

I would personally be most moved by two things: evidence that people who have seriously and repeatedly imagined living well for a very long time nevertheless converge on something like the tedium or narrative objections; and a careful argument showing that those objections do not in fact depend on the psychological mechanisms I have named.

Finally, I would like you to say explicitly what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. Please write the response in British English.
