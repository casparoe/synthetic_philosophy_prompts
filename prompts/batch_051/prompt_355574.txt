In my course on Wittgenstein and rule-following, our discussion group built an argument that seems to prove too much, and I cannot find the exit. We were working through Kripke's skeptical reading and the "quus" example. Define quaddition, written ⊕, so that x ⊕ y = x + y when x and y are each less than 57, and x ⊕ y = 5 otherwise. Suppose that I have never previously dealt with numbers ≥ 57. The argument is:

(1) If I mean addition by "+", then some fact about me—my past usage, my mental history, or my dispositions—must rule out my meaning quaddition by "+" instead.

(2) My past usage does not rule this out: every sum I have ever computed involved numbers below 57, so it is equally compatible with addition and with quaddition.

(3) My mental history does not rule it out: I have never before considered cases involving numbers ≥ 57, so I formed no instruction or representation that distinguishes the two functions.

(4) My dispositions do not rule it out either: I have no actual dispositions for sums beyond the range I can manage, my in-range dispositions are liable to error, and any idealized version of those dispositions is well-defined only if we already know which function I meant.

(5) So nothing about me determines that I mean addition.

(6) Yet when I am asked for 68 + 57, I answer 125 unhesitatingly and regard that answer as following from the meaning of "+"; someone who meant quaddition would instead have to answer 5.

(7) Therefore there is no fact of the matter about which function I meant, and meaning talk of this familiar kind is threatened.

I want to resist the conclusion. Which premise should be rejected, and what does rejecting it commit one to? Please lay out what a refined dispositionalism, a non-reductionism about meaning facts, and the skeptical solution each have to give up, and say which diagnosis is most defensible.
