I have no academic background in philosophy or linguistics, but I have been thinking on my own about what it would mean to say that a word has a meaning if a machine could arrange the whole vocabulary into a geometric space. Recently I read a popular essay that argued these word-embedding systems do not merely make useful predictions; they settle a philosophical dispute. Before I repeat its claims to other people, I would like to know whether the argument is fair.

As I remember it, the essay's reasoning went like this. Programs such as word2vec and GloVe are given a large corpus of ordinary text and are asked only to place each word at a point in a high-dimensional space so that words that appear in similar contexts land near one another. The system is given no dictionary definitions, no explicit grammatical rules, and no direct information about the world; everything it learns must come from patterns of co-occurrence. Even so, the essay pointed out, simple arithmetic on the resulting vectors appears to recover meaningful relationships: the vector for king minus the vector for man plus the vector for woman lands close to the vector for queen, and parallel results hold for country-to-capital pairs, tenses, and other relational patterns. From this the author concluded that a word's entire semantic contribution just is its location in the space, so that meanings are literally geometric objects. Since the representation is built purely from distributional statistics and still performs so well, the essay went on, this is an empirical confirmation of the idea that the meaning of a word is its use, and a refutation of the older view that meanings are definitions inside a speaker's head. It finished by suggesting that meaning does not require any contact with the world beyond the corpus itself: a word's meaning is fully fixed by the company it keeps within language, and understanding is, in principle, only a matter of carrying out the right vector arithmetic.

On a first read I found this exciting and largely convincing, but I suspect the chain of reasoning moves rather quickly, though I cannot yet pinpoint exactly where the trouble is. Could you go through my reconstruction of the essay and tell me which parts are accurate, which are useful simplifications, and which parts a careful philosopher of language or a semanticist would dispute, and whether the final conclusion actually follows from the documented successes? I am not asking for a reading list; I am asking for an audit of the reasoning itself, so that I can tell which of the essay's claims I can safely pass along.
