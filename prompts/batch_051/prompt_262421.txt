A group I hang out with keeps saying that a trained model is like a chef who has only ever read recipe books: no matter how creative the output looks, everything it makes is a recombination of recipes it already saw, so it can never make a genuinely intuitive leap beyond its training distribution. It's used to argue that when a model appears to have a novel scientific hypothesis or a new mathematical idea, the right response is to say it's just interpolating in a high-dimensional space between points it absorbed during training. I'm not convinced that the analogy is doing the work. It seems to smuggle in a picture of the training data as discrete, labeled, recipe-like items, and then treats "leap beyond the distribution" as if the distribution were a bounded shelf and the model were a little guy who can only reach between shelves.

I was reading Turing's Computing Machinery and Intelligence, and in the section on Lady Lovelace's objection he makes a closely related move. He says there that the view that machines cannot give rise to surprises rests on a fallacy: once a fact is presented, we cannot easily see how it could have followed from the programme, so we say it wasn't in the instructions, but our own intellectual surprises are produced by processes we cannot fully trace either. So I want you to audit the chef/recipe-book analogy in light of that Turing argument, without just doing usual pro/anti LLM talking points.

What maps onto what in the analogy, exactly? Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the training data, the recipe, the chef's skill, the kitchen, the ability to taste and adjust, and what would count as a genuinely new dish that isn't just a combination from the books. Then tell me where the analogy breaks in ways that actually matter for the conclusion, separate from the places it breaks but the conclusion survives anyway. In particular: does the analogy secretly assume that input-output pairs in training are like examples in the same way recipes are examples, and does that assumption hold for large language models? And after the audit, say whether the original claim — models can only recombine, not leap — survives or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole tim.
