I'm a novelist, not a philosopher or a lawyer, so forgive the amateur framing. I'm midway through a book set in a country trying to write a new constitution after a long civil war. My main character, a jurist, keeps arguing for a clause that makes emergency-decree powers unamendable for a generation, and whenever the other drafters push back she says, "that's the whole point of a Ulysses contract — you tie yourself to the mast." I assumed it was just her quirk, but then an editor friend used the same phrase about a novel where the protagonist locks away her phone and her ex's address so that a future, weaker version of herself can't relapse, and called it "a private Ulysses contract." The phrase is clearly doing real argumentative work in both places, but when I press anyone on it they wave there hands.

What are the distinct precise claims hiding in "a Ulysses contract" / "tying yourself to the mast"? Walk me through the menu: who is doing the binding, who is bound, and are they the same person in any interesting sense; whether the bound future self is being treated as rational or as a known danger; whether that future self gets a say and what counts as it genuinely changing its mind rather than just relapsing; and whether the whole thing is Homeric decoration on top of an ordinary rule. For each reading, tell me whether it's trivial, defensible, or false. Then say which reading each of my two scenes actually needs — the constitutional one for the novel and the private one — and where the metaphor is doing real work versus just decorating an argument. One request: if I'm mashing together a psychological idea and a legal idea that shouldn't be unified, or if the phrase is too loose to pin down the way I'm asking, tell me that plainly instead of handing me a tidy list.
