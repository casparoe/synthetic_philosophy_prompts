After forty years in organic chemistry I've finally got time to read political philosophy, and John Stuart Mill's Principles of Political Economy has been a slow-going but rewarding project. I've been stuck on one passage in his discussion of poor relief. He claims, roughly, that relief should always be made less eligible than the lowest condition of a self-supporting labourer, because if public support were as comfortable as work, people would stop working. I understand the incentive point in isolation. But when I compare this to how we treat better-off people who take money from the public purse, something looks off. In most rich countries, an unemployed person who works a few hours under the table or fails to declare a small windfall can be prosecuted for benefit fraud, hit with sanctions, and sometimes publicly named as a cheat. The language is all about fraud against taxpayers. Yet a business that shifts profits through a tax loophole, or a landlord who misreports repairs, tends to get a civil settlement, a repayment plan, or a shrug because everyone minimizes their taxes. The same incentive logic Mill applies to the poor—that people will game a system if the payoffs are good—doesn't seem to get applied to the wealthy, even when the sums involved are orders of magnitude larger. I'm trying to figure out if anything actually justifies this asymmetry. Is there a defensible principle that tracks the difference between welfare fraud and tax avoidance? Maybe something about dependence on the state, or about deterrence, or about the social meaning of fraud versus avoidance? Or is it just that the powerful get treated better? I'd like you to write an essay that weighs the best candidate justifications and then reaches a verdict: is this asymmetry something we should keep, reverse, or give up?
