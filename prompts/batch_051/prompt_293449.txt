I've been chewing on Hofstadter's strange-loop account of the self, the idea that the "I" is a self-referential symbol, and I want someone to pressure-test it against something that barely existed when the view was being worked out: large language models. Here's how I understand the account, so you can correct me if I'm off. The claim is that the self isn't a soul or a special mental substance; it's an abstract pattern that arises when a symbol-processing system (a human brain, mostly) builds a symbol for itself and that symbol gets caught up in the very processes it represents. The "I" is the knot in a loop: high-level symbols point down at the low-level machinery, and that machinery in turn gives the high-level symbols their causal role, so the whole thing closes on itself. On this picture the self is real the way a whirlpool is real, not an illusion, but it doesn't exist apart from the dynamics that make it up.

Now here's what bothers me. A modern language model seems to do a lot of the surface features of this without obviously having a self. It's trained on an enormous amount of human text, nearly all of it shot through with first-person writing, so it learns to produce "I" in the right places. If you prompt it that it's an AI assistant, it will describe itself, its limitations, its reasoning process; it can catch mistakes in its own drafts, say how confident it is, and talk about what it "thinks" would happen if it answered differently. Architecturally, a transformer's self-attention means each token's representation is computed partly by weighting the other tokens in the context, including tokens the model itself produced moments earlier. So there's a real sense in which the model's state is built out of references to its own prior state. But it still seems wrong, maybe wildly wrong, to say a single forward pass has an "I" in Hofstadter's sense. There's no continuing point of view across conversations, no body, no life; the self it talks about feels more like a character implied by the text than a loop running through the actual machinery.

So my question is what this does to the strange-loop account. Not whether LLMs are conscious, which I know is a separate question. I want to know whether the arguement that the self is this kind of self-referential symbol survives the existence of systems that do self-reference-ish things on the surface and in their architecture but are not obviously selves. Does the argument still go through? Does it need a repair, and if so, what's the repaired version, and which premise exactly got patched? Or is it in trouble, because we now have a clean-ish case of self-reference without a self?
