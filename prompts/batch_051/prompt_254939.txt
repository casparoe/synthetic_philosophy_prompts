Analyze the Newton–Leibniz priority dispute through the lens of the inevitability thesis: the claim that major discoveries are, in an important sense, inevitable once their conceptual and technical preconditions are in place, so that near-simultaneous discovery should be expected and the identity of the discoverer is partly a matter of contingency. The salient facts are these. Newton worked out his version of the calculus privately in the mid-1660s and published little about it for decades; Leibniz developed his version independently in the mid-1670s and published first, in 1684 and 1686; a bitter priority dispute followed, including formal charges of plagiarism, and the later consensus credits both with independent discovery. Work through the case twice. First, use the thesis to read the episode: what does it explain well—the overlap in result, the differences in notation and framework, the timing—and what does it imply, if anything, about how credit should have been assigned? Second, reverse the lens and use the episode to test the thesis: which features does the thesis miss or misdescribe, and where does it fall silent, especially about the priority dispute itself and the way the two men's different situations and habits of publication shaped the outcome? End with an honest verdict. Does the case confirm the thesis, show it to be incomplete, or reveal that it conflates the inevitability of a result with the attribution of credit to a person? And does the episode show that it did not matter who made the discovery, or that it mattered a great deal—and to whom?
