I'm taking a philosophy of law course and we've been going through Hart and Fuller, and the whole debate felt way to abstract until I tried to run it on a dumb dispute in my own co-op. Our bylaws contain this rule, passed in 1987 after a fight over someone's homemade satellite dish and some mayoral campaign signs:

"No sign, awning, antenna, or other object shall be attached to or placed upon any exterior wall, window, or door of the building without the prior written consent of the board."

Recently, a resident used a pressure washer to clean a slogan into the thick grime on the rear brick wall—by removing dirt in a pattern, she made "SAVE THE RESERVOIR" visible from the street. She never attached or placed any object on the wall; she only removed grime in a selective pattern. The board says she "placed" a message on the wall. She says that if the rule reaches this, then it would also reach any non-uniform cleaning, which is absurd and can't have been what anyone meant.

I'm less interested in who wins the practical dispute than in how each interpretive approach pulls. The text strains around "placed upon" because nothing was placed. The purpose the board keeps citing is preserving the building's uniform appearance and preventing physical damage, and the pressure-washed message arguably does the first even though it involves removal. I doubt the people who wrote the rule in 1987 ever imagined reverse graffiti, and a reasonable new resident reading the bylaw probably wouldn't expect it to apply, but they also might not expect a readable slogan to appear on the wall by any method.

Where would Hart and Fuller actually seperate on this? Would Hart see this as a penumbral case—"attached" and "placed upon" have a clear core of physical objects, but this is at the fringe—so the judge or board just has to exercise discretion? And would Fuller say you can't stop with the text and then dress up the leftover decision as "discretion," because you instead have to interpret the rule in light of its point within the co-op's scheme of governance, and that's what tells you whether the message falls within its reach? Or is that a mangling of both positions, and the real Hart–Fuller disagreement lies somewhere else? Please answer in American English.
