Act as a professional philosopher who works on moral philosophy and the metaphysics of persons. I want you to actually work the problem, not give me the textbook answer. Think carefully, and before you commit to a position, brainstorm three or four candidate principles that could explain the asymmetry I'm about to describe, then stress-test each of them against nearby cases.

Here's the asymmetry, and its always struck me as undertheorized.

On the one hand, most people are perfectly comfortable saying that a person can be wronged after they're dead. If you make a deathbed promise to a friend that you'll publish her novel, and once she dies you burn the manuscript, we don't just think you wronged her grieving family. We think you wronged her. Same thing if an executor quietly spends an estate in defiance of a will, or if someone spends decades slandering a dead scientist's reputation. We talk as though the dead person's interests survive her death, and a fair amount of law (wills, burial instructions, defamation of the dead) seems built on that.

On the other hand, almost nobody thinks a person can be wronged before they exist. If a couple delays having a child for twenty years, and the child who is eventually born is worse off because of the delay, nobody says the child was wronged by the delay. If a person's life goes badly because of events that took place a century before they were conceived, we don't say they were personally wronged by those events. Point to the time before someone existed and call it an injury to that person, and people treat you like you've made a category mistake.

So ordinary judgment says: after death, you can still be wronged, but before you exist, you can't. Both are stretches of time when the person doesn't exist. The Epicurean line — that when you're not there, there's no "you" left to be harmed — cuts against the first judgment at least as hard as it supports the second. The obvious fix is that a dead person acquired interests while alive and those can outlive her, while a not-yet-existing person has no interests yet to reach backward. But I'm not sure the fix survives pressure: what exactly is an "interest" that outlives its owner, and why can't a future person's interest in a longer or better life reach backward the same way?

I want the strongest principle you can build, and an honest verdict on wether it works — should we keep the asymmetry, give it up, or reverse some cases? And which way does the Epicurean consideration actually push: toward extending rights to the not-yet-existing, or toward giving up posthumous wrongs? Don't give me a reading list, just reason it through.
