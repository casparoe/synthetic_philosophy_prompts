I'm a journalist who covers economics and the history of ideas, and I'm working on a feature about Adam Smith's account of empire. I keep coming back to his diagnosis in the Wealth of Nations: the American colonies weren't a huge net profit center for Britain; the real winners were politically connected merchants who got a protected market while the home country carried the costs of defence and administration. He also thought the status quo was unstable, and that Britain had exactly two viable long-run arrangements—either full union with the colonies (actual representation in Parliament, taxed in proportion like everyone else) or a cordial separation in which Britain accepts that the colonies where never going to stay subordinate anyway. Everything in between, he thought, was a costly illusion kept alive by the people who profitted from it.

I want to use that as a lens on the imperial crisis of the 1760s and 70s, the one that ends in the American Revolution. So give me a disciplined two-way analysis. First, theory onto episode: who are the players in Smith's terms, what options did he think Britain faced, what did his framework say Britain should have done once conciliation failed, and which features of 1765–1776 does it explain well (say, why the dispute kept coming back to representation rather than just tax rates, or why the empire stayed worth more to a narrow interest than to the British public). Then, where does Smith's account misdescribe or miss things that plainly mattered—the colonists as political actors with their own constitutional ideas, slavery, the speed of escalation, whatever you think he underweights. Second, episode onto theory: the colonies were lost and Britain didn't collapse, and within decades American trade mattered more than it had under the old monopoly. What does that post-1783 record do to Smith's account—confirm it, refute it, or mark a case he never anticipated? I care less about a verdict than about keeping the mapping honest; say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.

End with a one-sentence statement of your overall assessment, on its own last line starting "Bottom line:".
