I've come to accept the block universe on the strength of the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, and I want to know how exposed that conviction is. The position I hold is this: past, present, and future events exist tenselessly; the universe is a four-dimensional block; the "now" is not a mind-independent marker of what exists but an observer-dependent slice, analogous to "here"; and temporal passage is not a fundamental feature of reality.

The argument, as I understand it, runs as follows. Special relativity teaches that simultaneity is not absolute: for spacelike-separated events, different inertial frames order them differently. Take an event that lies in my future at spacelike separation from me. In my frame it is in the future; in some other inertial frame it is simultaneous with my here-and-now, and so—on the assumption that to be real is to be simultaneous with a real observer's present—it is already real. Since physics privileges no inertial frame, and since what exists cannot depend on how one is moving, the event exists simpliciter. By chaining such "now" slices through suitably chosen observers, the conclusion extends to future events more generally. The natural endpoint is eternalism: past, present, and future are equally real, and the distinction among them is a matter of standpoint.

I want a map of what would count against this view, not merely a list of standard alternatives. Specifically: which premises or inferences in the above argument could fail, and which of those failures are live options rather than merely formal possibilities? What experimental or observational results—from tests of Lorentz invariance, quantum foundations, cosmology, or the study of time's arrow—would tell against the block universe, or against the move from relativity to eternalism, and which results would leave the position untouched? In particular, what does the block view predict or entail that its rivals do not, and where would those predictions show up? Which commitments elsewhere in metaphysics, such as about persistence, causation, agency, or the openness of the future, would be fatal for a block theorist, and which merely awkward? Distinguish what the view's own defenders would concede as counting against it from objections they can absorb at low cost. Also assess honestly whether the position has been insulated: has it been qualified in a way that would let it survive anything, and if so, is that a defect or a reasonable feature of an interpretation of a physical theory? Finally, tell me which consideration should most move me—that is, what would actually change my mind or force me to weaken the position—and where the reasoning above is most likely to break down.
