I am grading an undergraduate philosophy of punishment exam and would like a second opinion on one student's answer. The exam question was:

"Explain the contrast between retributivist and consequentialist theories of punishment, and discuss the following proposal: the consequentialist should answer the question of why we have a system of punishment at all, while the retributivist should answer the question of who may be punished and how much. Does adopting this proposal dissolve the objection that a consistent consequentialist would sometimes have to punish the innocent? Why or why not?"

The student's answer, reproduced from the exam booklet, was:

"Retributivism says that punishment is justified because the offender deserves it; it looks back to the crime rather than to future consequences. Consequentialism says the reverse: punishment is justified only insofar as it produces future goods such as deterrence, incapacitation, and reform. Taken as answers to the same question, these theories conflict, but they should not be taken that way. The consequentialist's claim is about why the institution of punishment should exist at all; the retributive principle enters only at the point of distribution, requiring that punishment be imposed only on the guilty and not exceed what they deserve. Once the theories are separated this way, the standard objection that consequentialism would punish the innocent collapses. The consequentialist is not committed to scapegoating because a retributive side-constraint rules it out before any utility calculation begins. Indeed, the objection is less than coherent: 'punishment' is by definition inflicted on an offender, so 'punishing the innocent' is a contradiction in terms. Retributivism, properly understood, is therefore not a rival to consequentialism at all. It is simply a constraint that any sensible consequentialist should build into the practice, and the apparent debate between the two camps is largely a verbal muddle."

Grade the answer out of 20 for its philosophical content, and give me the point-by-point comments that justify the grade. Address exactly where the answer is right, where it overreaches or conflates distinct claims, and which parts of the exam question it handles well or badly. Be sure to assess the two-level division of labor the student appeals to, and whether treating 'punishing the innocent' as a contradiction in terms dissolves the standard objection or merely changes the subject. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the grade: which further argument or consideration would lead you to revise it upward or downward, and where your grading is least certain.
