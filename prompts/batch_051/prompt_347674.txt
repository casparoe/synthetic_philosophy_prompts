I am a retired natural scientist—my own field was physics—who has only lately begun reading the philosophy of time, and I have come upon a question I want worked out carefully. I have been comparing two routes to the block-universe picture: the explicit argument from the relativity of simultaneity known as the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, and the geometrical picture Hermann Minkowski presents in his lecture "Space and Time." After introducing the world-point and the world-line, Minkowski writes:

"The whole world appears to be resolved in such world-lines, and I may just anticipate, that according to my opinion the physical laws would find their most perfect expression as mutual relations among these world-lines."

As I understand it, the Rietdijk–Putnam argument runs as follows. For any two space-like separated events, there is some inertial frame in which they are simultaneous; if an event is real just when it is simultaneous, for some possible observer, with that observer's here-and-now, then every event is real for some such observer, and past, present, and future must all exist on the same footing. Please make the strongest argument you can for the position that this famous argument is, for the eternalist, largely redundant. Argue that Minkowski's remark that the whole world resolves into world-lines, taken literally, already treats the entire history of a material point—earlier and later portions alike—as given together; that the Rietdijk–Putnam argument reaches the same conclusion only through an observer-relative present, which is precisely the notion a block-universe view should dissolve; and that the detour matters, because it leaves eternalism with a premise about what counts as "real now" that its opponents need not accept. Let the quoted sentence carry the argument: interpret what it means for the world to be "resolved in such world-lines," apply that reading to the Rietdijk–Putnam reasoning, and test whether your argument survives the objection that Minkowski's world-line talk is only a mathematical device rather than a claim about what exists.
