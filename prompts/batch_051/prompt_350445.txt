I am preparing a moot problem for a course on private governance and the limits of the state, and I want to press on how far a contractual right of exit can be stretched. Consider the following clause from the founding charter of a hypothetical charter city, the Meridian Free Port, drafted in the early 2000s by drafters who imagined a small commercial enclave of expatriate entrepreneurs and their families:

"Any Resident may withdraw from the Free Port by delivering a signed Notice of Withdrawal to the Registrar. The Notice shall specify an effective date not less than thirty days after delivery. On that date the Resident shall cease to be subject to this Charter and to the jurisdiction of the Port Authority, and no fee, fine, tax, or other penalty shall be imposed on him in respect of the withdrawal itself."

For two decades the clause was administered as a right to leave: residents gave notice, sold or removed their property, and departed. The Charter contains no procedure by which a district may alter the Free Port's boundaries except by agreement with the host state. Now the Northshore district, a contiguous residential area of roughly three hundred households inside the Free Port's boundaries, has submitted a single Notice of Withdrawal signed by the district's residents. They do not intend to move. Their position is that the Charter's jurisdiction is contractual and consensual: once they withdraw their consent, the Port Authority retains no authority over them or over the freehold land they own, and that land simply ceases to be part of the Free Port in any jurisdictional sense. They intend to remain physically in place, to continue using the port's roads, water, and electricity under access agreements they have proposed unilaterally, and to stop paying the port's land-value levy and complying with its building and fire codes. The Port Authority's counsel replies that the clause was drafted for persons leaving the territory, that the drafters never contemplated a district attempting to secede from within, and that the district's reading would let any neighborhood dissolve the city piecemeal while still consuming its infrastructure.

How should the Registrar rule on the validity of the district's Notice, and what does the clause actually permit in this case? I am less interested in a single conclusion than in seeing the interpretive approaches separated: what the plain words of the clause say, what its evident purpose would dictate, what its drafters would most plausibly have said if asked about a collective in-place withdrawal, and what a reasonable resident would have understood the clause to mean at the time. Where these readings diverge, which should govern, and does it matter that the residents are acting collectively rather than as individuals, or that they intend to keep using the port's infrastructure?
