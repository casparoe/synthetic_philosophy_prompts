In rationalist and ML-adjacent discussions I keep seeing the Wolpert–Macready no-free-lunch theorem deployed as a formal stand-in for Hume's problem of induction. The argument, as I've usually seen it run, goes roughly like this: averaged uniformly over all possible target functions, every learning algorithm has the same expected generalization error, so no inductive bias is intrinsically better than any other, so a preference for any particular way of generalizing from past data to the future is ultimately a leap of faith, and induction has no rational foundation. I've been trying to work out whether this argument survives the development that has dominated machine learning in the years since it became popular — the empirical discovery that the actual distribution of problems is nothing like uniform over all functions, and that success comes from strongly biased learners. Deep networks with built-in assumptions like compositionality, locality, and translation invariance generalize very well across the kinds of tasks the world actually poses, even though under the theorem's uniform distribution they would do no better than any other algorithm, including random guessing. The theorem is silent about non-uniform distributions, and once the distribution is concentrated on structured targets, biased learners can dominate unbiased ones.

My question: does the no-free-lunch objection to induction collapse because it was only ever a theorem about a distribution we do not face, or does it survive in a relocated form — with the problem of induction reappearing as the problem of justifying the assumption that the world's tasks have that structure, or of justifying the particular structured prior an architecture encodes? Please say exactly which premise of the original pessimistic argument is under pressure, whether the practical success of biased architectures really bears on it, and, if you think a repaired version of the no-free-lunch challenge to induction still bites, state it carefully and say what it does and does not rule out.
