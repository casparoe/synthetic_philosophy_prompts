I keep coming back to an argument that seems to settle, in a few lines, the question of whether anyone ever genuinely does something for someone else. If it is right, then a morality that tells me to set aside my own interest for another person's sake is asking me to do something no one can do, and the only honest ethics would be a disciplined pursuit of self-interest. So I want to know whether the argument actually holds up. I suspect one of its words is being made to do two jobs somewhere, but I cannot put my finger on where.

(1) Whenever a person acts deliberately, she does what she most wants to do at that moment; if she had wanted one of the other options more, she would have taken that option instead.

(2) Doing what you most want to do is acting in order to satisfy the desire that moves you.

(3) To satisfy a desire of yours is for a state of affairs you wanted to come about, and when it comes about through your action, you have gotten what you wanted.

(4) Getting what you wanted through your own action is a benefit to you: the world now matches your preference, and the world going the way you want it to is something good for you.

(5) Therefore, every deliberate action aims at bringing about a benefit to the person acting. It follows that when someone gives money to a stranger or jumps into a river to save a child, she is not really aiming at the other person's good; she is aiming at a benefit to herself, so the altruism is only apparent. Psychological egoism turns out to be true.

Take this step by step. For each numbered statement, say whether it is true as written; for each move from one step to the next, say whether it follows. If the argument fails, identify the first step at which it fails, name the exact word or phrase that is doing two jobs, distinguish the reading on which that phrase is true from the reading the conclusion requires, and say what the argument would prove if the faulty phrase were replaced by the correct one. End with a line beginning "Verdict:" that gives the number of the first failing step, or says the argument is valid if you think it goes through.
