A friend in my reading group has an argument I can't shake, even though it seems to prove too much. We were arguing about whether you can ever tell what an AI system is "really" optimizing for, and she laid it out like this:

(1) The objective and search space of an optimizer aren't written down anywhere we can just read; if we can know them at all, we infer them from behavior. (2) But any finite pattern of inputs and outputs is compatable with a pile of different objective/search-space pairs: a system that always takes the shortest route could be minimizing travel time, or maximizing the number of turns it avoids, or running some other search entirely that just happens to land on the shortest route each time. You can always retrofit an objective that makes the observed behavior look optimal. (3) So behavior underdetermines both the objective and the search space. (4) Therefore a claim like "the model is optimizing for engagement rather than truth" is never justified — not just hard to establish, but ruled out in principle — unless you can read the reward function directly out of the code. (5) But that can't be right. We do sometimes catch a system optimizing for something hidden from its behavior: a chess engine with a buggy evaluation might sacrifice pieces because its search effectively rewards captures; a thermostat with a miswired sensor ends up trying to track the wrong variable. And in ordinary life we infer goals from behavior constantly without reading anyone's mind. (6) So the argument rules out too much; some objective-ascription has to be possible.

She was using this to argue that behavioral audits of AI systems are hopeless. I don't believe that, but I can't find the step where it goes wrong. I'd rather reject (4) than (5), but then I owe an account of how behavior can justify saying what something is optimizing for even when it doesn't pin down a unique answer. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
