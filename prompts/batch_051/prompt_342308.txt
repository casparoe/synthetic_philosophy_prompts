In Nicomachean Ethics VII, Aristotle argues that the weak-willed person is in a distinct state from the self-indulgent person: the weak-willed person judges correctly what to do but is overcome by appetite, whereas the self-indulgent person takes the wrong action to be choiceworthy. Behavioral economics now commonly explains many such failures as hyperbolic or quasi-hyperbolic discounting, in which immediate rewards receive disproportionate weight, so a person’s choice reverses when a temptation moves from future to present. On that model, there is no simultaneous conflict between a still-active right judgment and a recalcitrant appetite; there is only one time-inconsistent preference function. Does Aristotle’s argument for akrasia as a distinct condition survive this development? Identify which premise of his account the discounting model bears on, say whether the distinction between akrasia and self-indulgence can be recovered in discounting terms, and if not, whether the philosophical category of weakness of will should be revised or eliminated.
