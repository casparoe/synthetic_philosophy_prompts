I'm the head of the long-horizon screening unit at a foundation that funds "civilization-scale" technology projects. Every eight weeks I get maybe a dozen proposals to triage—space-based solar, direct air capture grids, planetary biochar, programmable matter, asteroid mining, that sort of thing—and I have to give the board a one-page score on whether each one moves civilization to a genuinely higher capability level or just scales up existing capability or rearranges money. The board members are smart but not philosophers, grants range from $5M to $60M, and I have about 90 minutes per proposal with no outside experts. I want a reusable screening procedure, not a memo about one specific decision.

The theoretical frame is Kardashev plus Barrow. Kardashev sorted civilizations by total energy harnessed: Type I planetary, Type II stellar, Type III galactic. Barrow's inward extension complicates that by saying a civilization can also advance by manipulating matter and energy at finer and finer scales—molecular, atomic, subatomic, down toward the Planck scale—not just by reaching outward for more watts. So I want the procedure to treat advancement as having at least two axes: one for how much a project changes the civilization's usable energy budget, and one for how much it changes the precision of control over matter, energy, or information. A project can score high on one axis and low or even negative on the other, and the procedure needs to say what to do in those cases instead of averaging them into mush.

You are an expert on the philosophy of measurement and strategic technology assessment. Please think carefully and start by brainstorming possible axes and pressure-testing them on tricky cases before giving the final procedure. Then deliver the actual decision procedure: a checklist or decision tree in words, with steps in order, inputs I can pull from a proposal, thresholds or rules of thumb, tiebreakers, and a one-page scoring format I can reuse. It has to be usable by someone without the theory, so define each axis operationally—what would count as evidence in a project description or technical annex.

The procedure has to handle some hard cases explicitly: rebound effects (more efficient tech that leads to more total consumption rather than less), projects that lower gross energy use but increase control, projects that are dual-use or have long-tail catastrophic risk, and projects that are effectively subsidies to existing infrastructure wrapped in futurist language. Mark clearly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say what the procedure cannot do.

After laying out the procedure, apply it to three hypotheticals:

1. A distributed network of small modular reactors that does not increase global primary energy but greatly improves resilience and local control.
2. A planetary-scale direct air capture and ocean alkalinity grid that consumes a large fraction of current global energy but stabilizes biogeochemical cycles and buys time.
3. An orbital solar constellation that triples total energy available but uses a fragile, highly centralized architecture and strips rare earth materials.

Make sure at least one of these—ideally the one I've been calling our flagship—comes out as "not an advancement" by your procedure, and show how a disappointed board member would plausibly object to that result and how the procedure should respond.

Write the final answer in American English.
