Heres an asymmetry I can't shake. If someone arranges their whole life around becoming the best pianist they can be — practicing seven hours a day, skipping weddings, letting friendships die, reading nothing that isn't about music — I mostly admire the dedication, even if I'd find the person exhausting. If someone arranges their whole life around becoming the best moral agent they can be — every spare dollar to effective charities, no luxuries, no lazy weekends, every decision run through an impartial calculation of how to do the most good — I'd call them a saint and also find them a bit alarming, and I'd much rather have dinner with the pianist. Most people I know have some version of that reaction. And it's not just about single-mindedness, because single-minded pursuit of money reads as greed, not as admirable discipline. The weirder thing: morality is the one kind of excellence we expect from everyone — nobody thinks a non-pianist is failing by not practicing — yet it's also the one whose maximal pursuit makes us uneasy. Is there a principle that generates both the admiration for the pianist and the unease about the saint, or just a psychological story about being afraid of being judged? Give me the strongest candidate principle you can, test it against a saint with a genuine sense of humor who actually enjoys helping people, and tell me whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up — and, at the end, whether maximal moral goodness is something we should actually want for people, including ourselves.
