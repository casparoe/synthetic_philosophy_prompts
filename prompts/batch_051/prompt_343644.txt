I recently began serving on a committee that allocates a scarce, life-saving treatment across a hospital system, and the work has forced a tension in my moral thinking that I can no longer postpone. I have long been drawn to a Scanlonian, individualist account of wrongness: an act is justifiable only if it is justifiable to each person on terms no one could reasonably reject, and one person's serious harm cannot be outweighed by the mere sum of many smaller harms to others. The anti-aggregative core of this view has always seemed to me its chief virtue. It explains why we may not harvest one healthy person's organs to save five, why a person is not a container of value whose claim can be diluted by other people's claims, and why each individual has a complaint that must be answered from her own point of view rather than averaged away.

But the committee's decisions keep confronting me with cases in which numbers seem to matter in a way my view cannot easily capture, and I do not experience these judgments as concessions I make against my principles; I experience them as judgments my principles, if true, would have to endorse. If an equipment failure forces a choice between treating one critically ill patient and treating five equally critically ill patients, I would save the five, and I believe I should. Likewise, if we must choose between imposing a certain serious harm on one person and imposing a very small risk of the same harm on a sufficiently large number of people, my theoretical commitments tell me the scattered risks must not be summed to outweigh the one certain harm, yet at some point I cannot believe the right decision simply ignores how many people are exposed. The numbers seem to have moral force, and I want to know whether the theory can grant them that force without surrendering what made it attractive.

I do not want a slogan. I want to know whether the conflict I am describing is real or whether it rests on an underdeveloped reading of the theory. The standard contractualist reply, as I understand it, is to deny that numbers carry intrinsic weight and to allow them at most a derivative role, perhaps as a tie-breaker when individual complaints are equal in seriousness. Work out the strongest version of that reply, and press on it honestly: does it avoid aggregation, or does it merely relocate it under another name? Then tell me what would have to be true for the intuition to be the thing I demote or abandon, what would have to be true for the theory to be amended, and whether there is a precise reading on which both the individualist restriction and the numerical judgments survive. I want a negotiated verdict, not a victory for one side.

Please structure your answer as follows.

1. The theory, stated precisely. State the strongest version of the Scanlon-style view I am attracted to, make its anti-aggregation principle explicit, and identify exactly which of my judgments it seems to rule out or leave unexplained. (About 200 words.)

2. The intuitions, stated precisely. State the strongest formulations of my two numerical judgments — save the five in the equal-conflict case, and let large numbers of small risks count in the risk case — including their exact scope and limits. (About 200 words.)

3. The case for the anti-aggregative theory. Give the most compelling arguments for the individualist restriction, and explain what systematic virtues it buys: simplicity, unification, a non-arbitrary account of why some tradeoffs are forbidden. (About 350 words.)

4. The case for letting numbers count. Give the most compelling arguments for treating numbers as morally relevant in their own right, including any reasons to think my anti-aggregation intuition is a debunkable artefact rather than a datum. (About 350 words.)

5. The negotiated verdict. Say whether the apparent conflict survives a careful statement of the theory. Evaluate the tie-breaker strategy, and any other compatibility fix, with an eye to whether it is a genuine solution or disguised aggregation. Then specify what would justify each possible move — amending the theory, demoting the intuition, or reinterpreting one of them so the conflict dissolves — before saying which I should accept and what residue of discomfort, if any, I should expect to keep. (About 400 words.)
