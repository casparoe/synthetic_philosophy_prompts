I teach an undergraduate seminar on philosophy of mind and psychology, about eighteen students, and we have a seventy-five-minute meeting coming up on the debate between cognitive/appraisal theories of emotion and somatic theories. I want to run an activity that makes William James's central challenge vivid, rather than just having students argue over it abstractly. The passage I want the session built around is this, from William James, "What is an Emotion?" (1884):

Without the bodily states following on the perception, the latter would be purely cognitive in form, pale, colourless, destitute of emotional warmth. We might then see the bear, and judge it best to run, receive the insult and deem it right to strike, but we could not actually feel afraid or angry.

Design a classroom activity or game, ideally requiring no special equipment beyond things I can print or ordinary classroom furniture, that gets students to feel the force of James's claim. The activity should have them make some real choices, perform some bodily or expressive manipulations, and report or reflect on their experience, so that they notice a contrast between judging that a situation is, say, sad, threatening, or absurd, and actually feeling the corresponding emotion. I want rules, materials, a step-by-step sequence, and a debrief that maps what happened back to James's argument. The debrief should also flag where a somatic theory would have to handle objections: for example, patients with reduced peripheral feedback who still report emotion, or the possibility that the activity only shows that bodily states modulate emotion rather than constitute it. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, especially participants who won't cooperate, find it silly, or report exactly what they think I want to hear, and say how to handle that without giving the game away.
