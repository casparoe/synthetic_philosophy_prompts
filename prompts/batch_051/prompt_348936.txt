The standard argument in moral uncertainty says an agent ought to maximize expected choiceworthiness: you list the live ethical theories, assign each a credence, have each theory score the available options, and pick the option with the highest credence-weighted score. The argument assumes, among other things, that the uncertain reasoner has a fixed list of candidate theories and that those theories compare their favored options on some common scale.

Here's the development that seems to put pressure on that. The reasoners who are practically morally uncertain today are often not single humans with a tidy list of theories. They're large language models, or people making decisions by leaning on large language model outputs. A trained model's moral "commitments" are a latent, statistically learned amalgam of a huge corpus of human moral talk—some of it considered, some of it inconsistent, some of it just status- or identity-flavored speech. There's no obvious way to go inside and read off which theories the system is uncertain between, or what credence it assigns to each. It may not represent ethcal theories as discrete objects at all; the "theories" are spread out across its weights, mixed up with mere style and tone.

So here's the question: does the maximizing-expected-choiceworthiness argument survive this change in who or what the uncertain reasoner is? Is a premise doing the real work destroyed, or just harder to apply in practice? Can the argument be repaired into something weaker but still useful—something like a discipline you impose on an opaque system from the outside? I want to know what happens to the argument itself, not what policy we should adopt about deployable models.
