I write about climate and weather for a general-audience magazine, and a few years back the county flood-control district stuck me on its citizens' advisory board specifically because they wanted someone who could read a methods section without fainting. We're now being asked to approve a 30-year flood-infrastructure plan. The district's hydrologists ran a 14-member regional climate-model ensemble to project changes in extreme rainfall; on the metric that actually drives the levee design—the 100-year, 24-hour rainfall total—11 models cluster around a modest 8% increase, while 3 models project increases near 25%. The three high models are annoying because their also the ones with the most physically sophisticated treatment of thunderstorms, which is exactly what matters for extreme rain around here. The consultants recommend designing to the ~8% cluster: they say the ensemble 'majority' should carry the day and the high models are a different statistical population, maybe not even worth including. A few retired-engineer members of the board think that's reckless: once you know three credible models say 25%, you can't just set that aside because it got outvoted.

I'm on the hook to draft the decision memo. Please act as a philosopher of science who thinks about climate models, ensembles, and decision-relevant uncertainty. Start by brainstorming, without trying to be decisive yet, the distinct questions we'd need to work through (e.g., what does agreement among models actually show; when is it OK to discount outliers; what should a decision be robust to; how much should engineering conservatism just absorb the spread). Then give a clear recommendation the board can vote on, including the strongest argument for the losing side, and write it as an actual memo with an actionable bottom line.
