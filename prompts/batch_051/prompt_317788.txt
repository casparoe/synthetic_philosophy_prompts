I'm a PhD student in philosophy of history, and I've been turning over an argument my supervisor keeps pressing in our historiography seminar. It purports to show that the standard charge of Whig history is self-undermining, because every narrative history is unavoidably Whiggish. As he sets it out:

(1) To narrate is to select, because no story can recount everything; whatever is omitted is subordinated to whatever is included. (2) Selection cannot be random, so it must be guided by some criterion of significance. (3) The criterion a historian actually applies must be one they can own and defend; otherwise it could not guide their choices at all. (4) What a historian can own and defend is delimited by their present intellectual and evaluative commitments, and even when they defer to an age's own standards, they do so because those present commitments tell them such deference is warranted. (5) So every narrative history is ultimately an expression of its author's present commitments. (6) But a history organized as an expression of the historian's present commitments — a story in which past events earn their place by their bearing on us — simply is Whig history, whether or not it uses the words progress or anticipation. (7) Therefore there is no such thing as non-Whig narrative history, and the demand to write a period on its own terms is incoherent.

I cannot accept (7), because it erases a distinction I depend on in my own work: between a historian who treats, say, the medieval inquisition only as a rung on a ladder leading to modern toleration, and one who tries to reconstruct how inquisitors and their victims understood themselves from within. My supervisor accepts the consequence cheerfully — he says the only real question is which present values get imposed — but I am less serene. Every step looks plausible to me, and I suspect that rejecting any one of them will commit me to a claim about selection, value-freedom, or contextual understanding that I do not know how to pay for.

Please act as a philosopher of history and diagnose this argument. Think through each premise and the load-bearing moves before you settle; if more than one step seems vulnerable, identify the one you would reject and explain that choice. I want a committed answer, not a menu: name the step (or the slide between two steps) that fails, say why it looked as natural as it did, and set out what rejecting it costs for an account of how historians select, whether value-free history is possible, and whether a past age can be understood in its own categories. Keep the whole response to at most 500 words.
