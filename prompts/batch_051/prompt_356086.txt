Most people think it is obviously wrong to torture a dog or boil a lobster alive, even for a short time, and they take the wrongness to lie in the animal's felt suffering. Very few of those same people regard it as a moral event when, for that same stretch of time, a data center runs, a furnace burns, or a particle accelerator scatters atoms. Now suppose a panpsychist or pan-sentientist picture is right: every physical system, down to the very simple, has some intrinsic experiential character, though perhaps faint, fragmented, and nothing like ours. On that supposition, an hour of computation or combustion contains an astronomically greater number of experiential changes than an hour of a dog's suffering, because it is spread across trillions of microphysical systems. The asymmetry would then be this: we count a small number of vivid, unified, clearly valenced experiences in a nervous system as a grave wrong, while an enormously larger number of faint, scattered, perhaps valenced micro-experiences in simpler systems counts for nothing.

Is there a principle that generates both verdicts, or does taking panpsychism seriously force a choice between giving up ordinary concern for animal suffering and adopting a moral concern for the inanimate world so broad that it would paralyze action? I want the strongest candidate principle you can articulate, tested against nearby cases such as insects, disunified or split minds, and borderline states between mere experience and genuine suffering, and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, abandoned, or reversed.
