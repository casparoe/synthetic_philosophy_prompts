I'm a novelist, and i'm currently working on a book whose central prop is a website called—working title—The Coincidence Ledger. The conceit is that people submit their most unsettling coincidence stories, but unlike a normal "creepy stories" forum, every submission gets run through a small actuarial engine before it's posted. The rule I've worked out for the site is this: each submitter has to fill out a "frame" for their story—the date range over which it unfolded, roughly how many people they interact with in that setting, and an honestish estimate of how many "opportunities" they had for that kind of coincidence (number of flights taken, number of times they check an old yearbook, whatever seems relevant). From those numbers the site computes a birthday-paradox-style figure and puts it in a box at the top of the post: "Given these self-reported numbers, we'd expect something like this to happen to about 1 in N people in a year," or "in a readership this size, about M people should have a story at least this surprising per year." The stated goal is to make readers feel the base rate, not just the chill.

I want the site to feel real, so I'm trying to work out how the mechanism actually behaves rather than whether it's noble. What does it incentivize people to do when they're filling out the frame: do they get honest, or do they learn which frames make a story look rarest? Does the scheme select for genuinely improbable events, or for people who are good at reverse-engineering the engine? How do readers actually react to having the number attached—does it calibrate them, or does the quantified weirdness just make the anecdote more persuasive? And what gets gamed first: the self-reported frames, the readers' trust in the numbers, or the site's own need for a steady stream of uncanny content? Are there fixes that don't turn the whole thing into an audit?
