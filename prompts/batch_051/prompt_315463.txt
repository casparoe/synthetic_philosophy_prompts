I'm giving a seminar paper next month defending the claim that states can be proper targets of apology for wrongs their predecessor governments committed a century or more ago — not just as a shorthand for "we, the current citizens, are sorry," but because the state itself, persisting as an institutional agent through regime changes, can owe the apology. My paper leans pretty hard on the analogy between state continuity and corporate continuity: the institution, not the set of people running it, is what carries the relevant identity over time. And I claim an apology does normative work that reparations alone don't, because it re-sets the terms of the ongoing relationship between the apologizing state and the wronged group.

The audience is a mixed workshop: mostly legal historians and transitional-justice people who are going to worry I've built the whole thing on the fiction that a state is the same entity across generations, plus one political theorist who thinks apologies are cheap talk and the only thing with real weight is material redress and institutional reform. Put together my Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections in rougly the order I'll actually get them, phrased the way this crowd would put them (not the way a moral philosophy seminar would), with a note on which ones have real force, what I should concede up front, a two-minute reply for each, and the weakest point in my paper I should be prepared to defend rather than hope nobody notices. And tell me the question I should be most afraid of, and why it's the one that could sink the whole talk.
