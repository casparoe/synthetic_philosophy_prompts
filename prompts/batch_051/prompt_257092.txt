Consider David Lewis's account of convention, on which a regularity R in a population is a convention only if it is common knowledge that, in almost every instance, everyone conforms to R, everyone expects everyone else to conform to R, everyone prefers to conform to R given that the others do, and there is some alternative regularity R' that could have served in place of R had it become established. Take the familiar case of a convention to drive on the left. Now vary it: on any given road, a randomly drawn fraction ε of drivers are visitors who do not know the local side and choose left or right at random; locals know ε but cannot tell visitors apart, and all of this is common knowledge. For which values of ε, if any, does the left-driving regularity still satisfy Lewis's conditions, and where exactly does it first fail? If the strict reading says it fails for any positive ε, say whether a natural probabilistic weakening of the expectation condition would preserve it, and whether that weakening still distinguishes conventions from mere regularities. In your answer, indicate how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
