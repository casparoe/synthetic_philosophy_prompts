I've been trying to pin down what people mean when they say a proof is understood, and I've ended up with a parallel I can't evaluate on my own. In algorithmic information theory there is a familiar way of making understanding precise: you understand a string to the extent that you can produce a short program that outputs it, a program much shorter than the string itself. It occurred to me that this might capture exactly what separates genuinely understanding a proof from merely having procedural fluency with it. A student with procedural fluency can be walked through a proof and verify that each line follows from the previous ones; they can execute the proof the way a machine runs a transcript. A mathematician who understands the proof, by contrast, seems to hold it in compressed form: a small set of structuring ideas—a symmetry, an invariant, a fixed-point argument, a potential function—from which the whole proof can be regenerated on demand. On this picture, understanding a proof is just having found a description of it that is much shorter than the proof itself, and the gap between fluency and understanding is the gap between executing a transcript and possessing something like its source code.

I can already see that this identification must fail somewhere. There are very compressed proofs—terse one-liners that jump from a clever substitution straight to the conclusion—that almost no one would say they understand without substantial unpacking. And there are proofs people can verify line by line, and even reproduce, while still insisting that they don't see why the theorem is true. But maybe those are simply cases where a short description is present yet not the right kind of compression, or where the program is short but the interpreter is missing. I want to know whether the core idea is salvageable. Is the parallel between proof-understanding and compressibility deep or superficial? Where exactly does the identification break, and what does its failure reveal about what understanding a proof is over and above having an economical way to regenerate it?
