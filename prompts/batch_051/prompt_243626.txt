I am a senior script editor at a historical documentary production company. My department reviews draft scripts every week under tight deadlines, and the first-pass readers are junior staff with no training in historiography or the philosophy of history. Over the past year we have deliberately pushed scripts away from "great man" history and toward non-human causes — epidemics, climatic shifts, volcanic eruptions — and I have come to think this has produced a different failure mode. Drafts either invoke the non-human cause as decoration that does no real explanatory work, or they slide into environmental determinism, treating climate or disease as destiny while human institutions, choices, and accidents quietly vanish. These decisions determine what our audience is told about why the world looks the way it does, so the failures are not cosmetic. I want a procedure my readers can run on any script that gives causal weight to a non-human factor, so we can tell whether that factor is being treated as a genuine historical actor with real causal work, as a mere background condition, or as a deterministic force that silences human contingency.

Please write me that decision procedure. It should:
- state the questions a reader asks, in order;
- specify, for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as failing it;
- say where the procedure stops with "revise" or "reject";
- mark the steps that are evaluative rather than empirical, and say who must make the call;
- be usable by a junior reader in roughly half an hour, using only the script in front of them.

Then run the procedure on three draft claims we are currently fighting about:

1. A draft on the French Revolution claims that the 1783–84 Laki fissure eruption in Iceland dimmed European skies and shortened growing seasons, and that the resulting hunger "broke the monarchy's back" and "triggered" the Revolution.

2. A draft on late medieval England claims that the Black Death of 1347–51 so reduced the labor supply that it "shattered feudalism" and "gave birth to" wage labor and the modern labor market.

3. A draft the team is attached to, on the Classic Maya collapse, claims that a series of severe droughts rendered that collapse "inevitable"; every human response in the script is presented as a doomed improvisation against a climate that had, in the script's words, "already written the ending."

Make sure at least one of your worked examples is a case where the procedure tells us something we would rather not hear, and end by saying what the procedure cannot catch — the places where a checklist will feel as though it has settled a question that is really about narrative values, and where I should hand the decision to a person.
