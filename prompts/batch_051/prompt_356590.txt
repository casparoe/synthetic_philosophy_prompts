You are a professional philosopher specializing in probability and formal epistemology, brought in as a consultant for a quantitative team that must set prior distributions under real deadlines. I am the lead modeler in the forecasting and risk unit at a large insurer. Several times a month, my team has to set priors for quantities we have never directly observed — for example, a severity distribution for a new kind of claim, a base rate for a rare operational failure, or a parameter in a climate-loss model before any internal data exists. We usually have at most half a day per prior, the stakes are high (capital requirements, pricing, regulatory review), and every prior is signed off by our chief actuary, who is statistically sophisticated but impatient with anything that looks like metaphysics.

Several of my colleagues have been pushing the maximum entropy principle as our default: among all distributions consistent with what we actually know, choose the one with the highest entropy, and treat that as our prior. They call it the least opinionated choice, and they like that it can be defended to auditors as non-arbitrary. I am skeptical that it is always as clean as that, and I want a procedure that tells us when maximum entropy is genuinely appropriate and when it is quietly making subjective choices while wearing the costume of objectivity.

Your deliverable is a reusable decision procedure my team can actually run before setting a prior. Ground it in the substance of objective Bayesianism and the philosophy of probability — the idea that there may be rationally privileged priors, along with the strongest worries against that idea — but translate it into operational steps for people with strong statistical training and no philosophical vocabulary. I want the reasoning itself, not references.

Specifically, the procedure should:

- State the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "do not use maximum entropy for this quantity," and where it does not stop but instead hands the decision to a human because the remaining question is a modeling judgment or a values judgment rather than a fact.
- Be explicit about which steps are genuinely mechanical and which are judgment calls dressed up as computation — for example, the choice of the space over which entropy is maximized, or the reference measure used on a continuous parameter.
- Flag known failure modes. In particular: treating a constraint that is itself estimated from noisy data as if it were exact; the fact that on a continuous parameter the "least informative" maximum-entropy distribution can change when the same quantity is re-described in a different coordinate system; and the gap between "consistent with what we know" and "known with certainty."
- State honestly what the procedure cannot settle. It should not pretend to settle whether there is a uniquely rational prior in general; it should only identify cases where maximum entropy earns its claim to objectivity and cases where it does not.

Before writing the final procedure, spend some time brainstorming the most dangerous ways the checklist could go wrong — cases where it would say "use maximum entropy" but the resulting prior would mislead us — and build guards against at least two of those into the procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

1. A claims-severity flag takes one of four levels (negligible, minor, moderate, severe), and a reliable external industry audit gives us an estimate of the expected dollar cost per flagged item. We need a prior over the four levels for a new product with no internal history.
2. We need a prior over a positive continuous rate parameter, such as the annual frequency of a specified operational failure mode per 10,000 accounts. We have no hard constraints. The team proposes maximizing entropy over the positive reals, expecting to get something like a flat or exponential distribution and calling it objective.
3. A subject-matter expert tells us the mean time to a particular type of IT degradation is around 40 days, give or take, based on a small set of vaguely analogous incidents. The team wants to treat 40 days as an exact mean constraint and maximize entropy subject to it.

Write the procedure in numbered steps with plain-language explanations at each step, and then run the three cases through it. If the procedure tells my colleagues "no" on a case they expected to pass, say so plainly and explain why that is the procedure working rather than a bug.
