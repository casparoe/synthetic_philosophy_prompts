I follow a rule about aesthetic judgment, and I want to know whether it is rationally defensible. I readily treat other people's testimony as evidence in factual and practical matters, but I never let it count as evidence about aesthetic quality: when friends praised a film I had not seen, I took their praise as giving me no reason to expect it would be good, and when a reviewer I respect argued that a novel I admire is badly constructed, I lowered my opinion only after my own rereading convinced me. I apply this across literature, film, and music, waiting for first-hand experience and then mostly trusting my own response over articulate reviews. I know this cuts me off from the accumulated judgment of others and can make my tastes look stubborn or willfully uninformed. Is this policy defensible, or is it bias dressed up as a point about the first-person character of aesthetic judgment? Is there a weakened version that keeps what is right about it while letting me learn from other people? Please answer in American English, indicate how confident you are in your claims, and single out the point you are least sure of.
