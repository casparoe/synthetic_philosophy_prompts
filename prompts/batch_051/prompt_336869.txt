I've been reading around the two-factor theory of delusions for an essay, and I built myself a tidy little argument to explain why someone might think delusions aren't really beliefs. Now I'm worried it proves too much. It runs like this.

(1) On the two-factor theory, a tightly circumscribed delusion such as Capgras forms when something goes wrong twice: an anomolous experience supplies a strange candidate content (factor one), and a separate failure stops the person from rejecting that content (factor two). The second factor is standardly described as a deficit in belief evaluation.

(2) If belief evaluation is defective in that way, the person should keep endorsing the delusional content even when they are given decisive evidence against it. That fits the cases: someone with Capgras syndrome can agree that the woman in front of them has the same face, mannerisms, and memories as their wife, and still insist she is a double.

(3) But a belief, as opposed to an idle imagining or going along with a claim, just is the sort of state that is eventually responsive to evidence. A state that survives any amount of counterevidence isn't a belief; it's something else.

(4) So delusional contents, on the two-factor theory, aren't beliefs.

(5) Now the part that bothers me. The evidence resistance in (2) isn't exotic. People keep their political opinions when the numbers go the wrong way, keep thinking they're above-average drivers after an accident that was plainly their fault, keep believing a partner is faithful despite the messages on the phone. If (3) is the test, then none of those are beliefs either.

(6) But obviously they are beliefs: partial, self-serving, stubborn beliefs, but beliefs all the same.

(7) So either (3) sets the bar too high, or delusions are beliefs after all, or there's a difference between the ordinary cases and delusion that I haven't named. Something has to give.

I'd like to keep (3), since it seems to capture why delusions feel different from ordinary thinking, and I'd like to keep (6) as a point of common sense. But if I keep both, I can't see what the two-factor theory is really explaining. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
