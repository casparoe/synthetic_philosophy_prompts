I recently read a magazine article about the ethics of insect welfare, and it presented something called the scale argument in a way that left me unsure whether I should trust its version of the argument. According to the article, the reasoning goes roughly as follows. At any given moment, there are on the order of a quintillion insects alive, far more than all humans and farmed animals put together. Science cannot yet tell us whether insects are conscious, and for most kinds of insects the probability that they can feel anything at all seems small, perhaps one in a hundred. But when one multiplies that small probability by the enormous number of insects, the expected number of insects capable of suffering is still staggeringly large, vastly larger than the number of dogs, pigs, and cattle whose suffering we already try to prevent. The article then said that we should act as if insects are suffering on a massive scale, even though we cannot say which individual insects are conscious. It concluded that anyone who accepts this argument ought to prioritize insect suffering over the suffering of familiar vertebrate animals.

I found the arithmetic easy to follow, which makes me inclined to accept the core of the argument, but I suspect the article may be making it do more work than it can bear. I would like to know whether this is a fair summary of the scale argument. Which parts would a philosopher defending the argument accept, which parts are standard simplifications, and which parts, if any, would be regarded as misstatements? In particular, does the final conclusion about prioritizing insects over familiar animals actually follow from the accurate parts of the summary, or is that the article's own addition?
