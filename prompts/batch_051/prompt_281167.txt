I've spent my career in the natural sciences, but since retiring I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group, and last week two members nearly fell out over Simpson's paradox. I'd like someone to referee the actual argument, because I think both of them were partly right and neither could see the other half.

The numbers they were shouting about where these. Two hospitals, call them Westgate and Dunmow. At Westgate, 180 of 200 mild patients survived and 20 of 100 severe patients survived, so 200 of 300 overall. At Dunmow, 96 of 100 mild patients survived and 75 of 300 severe patients survived, so 171 of 400 overall. Rounded: Westgate 90% mild, 20% severe, 67% overall; Dunmow 96% mild, 25% severe, 43% overall.

Bill started.
(1) "Westgate is obviously the better hospital. If I'm having an emergency, I want the place where more people walk out alive: 67% against 43%. The rest is statisticians making excuses."

Margaret came back.
(2) "That's Simpson's paradox. Split by severity and Dunmow is better for mild patients, 96% to 90%, and better for severe patients, 25% to 20%. It only looks worse overall because it treats far more of the severe cases — 300 of its 400 are severe. If I turn up with a given condition, mild or severe, I'd rather be at Dunmow."

(3) Bill: "But before I've been seen, I don't know whether I'm mild or severe. For someone choosing in the dark, the aggregate is the right probability: 67% beats 43%. You're asking me to condition on information I don't have."

(4) Margaret: "You'll know soon enough, and severity on arrival is fixed before the hospital does anything to you. The fair question is how two hospitals handle the same patient. By your reasoning, a clinic that only took people with splinters would look like the best hospital in the country."

(5) Bill: "That's not a bug, it's the point. If Westgate gets easier cases because it doesn't have a trauma unit, then for a random member of the public Westgate really is the safer bet. The severity mix is part of what you're choosing, not a confounder to be adjusted away."

(6) Margaret: "The severity mix is a confounder for hospital quality. 'Which hospital is better' is not 'which hospital has the luckiest catchment.' And your random member of the public isn't a random draw from the pooled total of both hospitals anyway; if you wanted the right aggregate you'd have to standardise to some real population, not just use the crude totals."

At that point they both turned to me. I did a fair bit of statistics in my working life, but the causal and philosophical side of it is newer to me, and I genuinely couldn't sort out which moves carry weight. Adjudicate it move by move: for each numbered move, say whether it's right, wrong, or half-right and why, then give an overall verdict — who has the better of it, and what are both of them missing?
