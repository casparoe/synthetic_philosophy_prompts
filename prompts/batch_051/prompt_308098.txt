I became a trustee of the small family foundation my father ran until his death, and I now chair it. At next month's meeting we have to settle our largest contested grant of the year: one remaining award of five hundred thousand dollars, with two finalists. The first is a seasonal malaria chemoprevention program in West Africa with years of evidence behind it: multiple randomized controlled trials, independent replication, and audited delivery costs. Its cost-effectiveness arithmetic, which our program officer has checked, says our grant would prevent on the order of a hundred child deaths. The second is a small research group working on early detection of engineered pandemics. The scientists are serious and have one promising prototype, but there is no field evidence that it works. Their own model, worked out in a long technical appendix, estimates a one-in-a-million chance that their project prevents a pandemic that would otherwise kill one billion people. The expected value is therefore about a thousand deaths averted, roughly ten times the first proposal. The trustees are split. One camp says the arithmetic is decisive and that refusing the research group is a failure of nerve dressed up as prudence. The other camp says this is expected value fanaticism, or a Pascal's mugging: any applicant can claim some tiny probability of an enormous good, so a policy of following such estimates lets the most extreme story win the money.

Draft the decision memo I should circulate to my fellow trustees before the meeting. Begin with your recommendation in the first sentence, and argue for it the way a serious trustee would: take a definite position—fund the malaria program, fund the research group, or do something in between under clearly stated conditions—and do not merely lay out the options and leave the choice to them. Give the strongest consideration on the losing side an honest run before you explain why it should not prevail. Please write the memo in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a formal email or letter.
