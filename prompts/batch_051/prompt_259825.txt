I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, so I'll lay this out the way I'd lay out a bug report: the setup, the test cases, the observed outputs, and the question I can't resolve on my own.

The setup: my spouse and I are both nonbelievers, and my spouse's parents are devout Christians. We have two children, an eight-year-old daughter and a six-year-old son. We are raising them without religion, but we want them to stay close to their grandparents and cousins, who practice. Over the years I've noticed that my reactions to ordinary family situations fall into a pattern, and I'd like to know whether there is one principle behind them, or whether I'm applying two principles that sometimes conflict (roughly: don't lie, and preserve the children's place in the family) without noticing where the conflict is.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. My mother-in-law asks me directly whether I believe in God. I say no, plainly. I think hedging would have been wrong. Fine as is.

2. I've told my daughter that if a relative asks whether she believes in God, she should answer truthfully. When her grandmother asked, she said, "I don't know yet." I thought that was fine, probably good.

3. At a holiday dinner, my eight-year-old announced to the whole table, "Mommy and Daddy say God isn't real." I thought that was not fine, and afterward I told her: it wasn't wrong because it was false, but because it was volunteered to score a point at someone else's table. Answering a direct question truthfully is one thing; using the truth as a cudgel is another.

4. My father-in-law asked me to say grace before a meal. I declined and asked him to do it. I think that was right; saying the words myself would have been speaking as if I believed them.

5. At a family funeral, though, I joined the responsive prayers and said the creed with everyone else. I feel fine about that, even though I don't believe the words. Sitting silent would have turned my unbelief into a statement in a moment that was about someone's death, not about me.

6. I let my children attend services with their grandparents when they stay over, and I sent them to vacation Bible school with their cousins. I'm fine with this. They experience it mostly as family, music, and ritual, and when they come home with questions we answer them honestly.

7. My daughter told her grandmother, "I pray every night," to make her happy. She does not pray. I thought that was not fine, and I told her afterward that she doesn't need to perform belief to be loved, and that I don't want her learning that her place with her grandmother depends on saying the right lines.

8. A devout teenage niece took my daughter aside and asked her, one-on-one, whether she wanted to "ask Jesus into her heart." I thought that was not fine, in roughly the same way I'd object to an adult pressing an eight-year-old into any life-changing declaration. I don't think relatives should put a young child in that position, on any side of the question.

Then there's the case I keep going back and forth on. My in-laws currently believe, or strongly assume, that we are raising the children in the faith. We have never corrected them, because it hasn't come up as a direct question. The children are young enough that this false belief hasn't yet forced a practical collision, but it will: eventually a grandparent will expect a first communion or some other statement of faith. Is leaving that assumption in place a kind of lie? Should we correct it now, or wait until it collides with a concrete request?

What I'd like from you: (a) the principle, or small set of candidate principles, that best fits verdicts 1 through 8, stated precisely, with each clause tied to the specific case that motivates it; (b) what that principle implies for the uncorrected-assumption case, and for one or two further cases of your own choosing that would put real pressure on it; and (c) an honest list of which of my verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate — and whether I should drop a verdict rather than complicate the principle to keep it. I care more about ending up with a rule I can actually explain to my children than about saving every one of my current gut calls.
