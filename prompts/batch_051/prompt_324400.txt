Here's what I can't get straight. The classic argument for the moral equality of combatants says that regular soldiers on both sides are morally permitted to kill each other even when one side's war is unjust, because the ordinary soldier isn't in a position to judge whether their side's cause is just—they've been conscripted or socially pressured into it, they're fed propaganda, and they're trained to obey rather than to second-guess what the war is for. But the soldier in the field today isn't living in the information blackout that picture seems to assume. A lot of contemporary armies are volunteer forces, people enlist after the war has already been publicly argued about for years, and a soldier can sit on base streaming independent reporting and footage from the war before they ever deploy. If someone signs up for a war they had good reason to think was unjust, and they plausibly could have not signed up, does the moral equality argument still cover them? And does it still cover the enemy soldier on the other side, who may have had far fewer options and much worse information? I'm wondering whether the argument survives this unchanged, or needs repair—say, it's now only true for conscripts and the heavily propagandized, or it's become a legal convention rather than a moral claim—or whether it's just dead for volunteer militaries. Be specific about which premise in the old argument is actually doing the work here, and what a repaired version would have to give up.
