One analogy I keep noticing in science-education writing, and which is doing genuine argumentative work there, is that a student's persistent misconception is like a scientific theory in miniature. The picture is roughly this: the student holds a small set of core commitments (for example, that continued motion requires a continuing cause, or that heat is a material fluid that is transferred between bodies); they use these commitments to generate explanations and predictions across a range of cases; they patch apparent counter-examples with auxiliary assumptions rather than revising the core; and merely being told the correct answer leaves the structure untouched. The pedagogical conclusion drawn from the analogy is a strong one: since the student is in the grip of a theory, teaching science properly means inducing something like a conceptual revolution — confronting the student with anomalies, making the target theory intelligible and plausible, and replacing the old structure wholesale rather than correcting single errors one at a time.

I would like an audit of this analogy, not a glancing dismissal of it. In particular: (a) give the correspondence table — what in the learner's state of mind plays the role of the theoretical posits, the observational consequences, the auxiliary hypotheses, the scientific community, the accumulating anomalies, and the revolution; (b) identify the main disanalogies, with particular attention to features of a scientific theory that the analogy quietly imports into the learner's mind but that may be absent there, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the pedagogical use of the analogy from those that are merely decorative; (c) give a verdict on whether the conclusion survives. If you think the systematicity of student errors does not license the full conceptual-revolution teaching model, say which weaker pedagogical conclusion does survive, and offer a better analogy if there is one. You may help yourself to anything you know of the knowledge-in-pieces tradition, of ontological-category accounts of misconception, or of the conceptual-change literature, but the audit should stand on the mapping itself rather than on citations; I do not want a reading list. Please write your answer in British English.
