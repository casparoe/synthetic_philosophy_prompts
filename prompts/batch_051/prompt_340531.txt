I'm a novelist, and after a painful thing with a freind I've ended up with an argument I can't shake. Becuase if it's sound I have to let the whole thing go, and if it's not I want to know exactly which step is rotten, not just a general 'that feels off.'

The background: I'd been working on a novel for over a year. The core was a specific conceit — a city where memories are physically deposited in shared objects, and the plot runs on who has handled your past. Six months ago I laid the whole thing out for my friend over coffee, including the twist I was proudest of. Last week he sent me a finished story with the same premise and the same twist, saying he'd 'just started playing with it' and it came out its own thing. I was furious; he said I still have my novel, so what did I lose?

His argument, as best I can put it:

(1) To own something is to have the right to stop others from using it without your permission.
(2) But an idea isn't like a physical object: if someone else uses an idea, you still have it whole and can use it exactly as before.
(3) So when a person takes your idea and builds something on it, nothing on your side has gone missing — you haven't been deprived of the thing itself.
(4) And if nothing has been taken from you, no theft has happened.
(5) So he didn't wrong me; he just used an idea, and my anger is really just anxiety about not being first.

I can't find the hole, but step 5 feels like it's carrying more than the earlier steps earned. I keep worrying there's a slide from 'nothing was taken' in one sense (I still have the idea) to 'nothing was taken' in a stronger sense (I lost nothing worth being upset about). Walk me through what the argument actually establishes. Is it valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and what the corrected version of that step would be. And be straight with me: even if his conclusion is true, does the argument get him there?
