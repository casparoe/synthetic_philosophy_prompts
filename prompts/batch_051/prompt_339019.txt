I used to do materials science, and now that I'm retired I've been trying to get a handle on moral philosophy. A friend who teaches philosophy told me deontology has this old "paradox" at its core and that it basically undermines the whole view. I've been turning it over, and I can't tell if he's right or if he's just being clever. He laid it out as a numbered argument:

(1) A moral rule against doing a certain kind of act, like lying or killing, has to be justified by something about that act itself — otherwise the rule is just arbitrary.
(2) The most natural thing about the act itself is that it is bad: the world is worse off in some respect whenever such an act is done.
(3) If an act is bad in that way, then more of it is worse than less of it.
(4) So if you can do one such act yourself and in doing so prevent several other people from doing it, you should do it — you'd be making the world less bad overall.
(5) But deontology says you must not do it, even when that would minimize the total number of such acts.
(6) Therefore deontology tells you both to minimize the act and not to minimize it, which is inconsistent.

I keep trying to find the flaw, but step 1 seems right to me, and step 3 is just arithmetic. My friend says this shows that anyone who believes in rights or side-constraints is really a consequentialist in denial, because the only possible rationale for a rule is that the act-type is bad and bad things should be minimized. Is he right? If not, exactly which step fails and what should the correct premise be? I don't need a survey of the literature, I just want to know whether this particular argument goes through or whether there's a place where a deontologist can legitimately dig in.
