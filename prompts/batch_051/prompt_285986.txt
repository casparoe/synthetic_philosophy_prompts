I'm giving a workshop paper next month at my department's development economics seminar and I already know it's going to be a bloodbath. My line is that the institutions explanation for why some countries are rich wins the empirical fight partly because institutions are much easier to code than geography or culture, not because the evidence actually rules the alternatives out. Institutions get a clean composite index with annual or at least regular variation; geography and culture have to be either coarse dummies or they get absorbed into fixed effects or shoved into the residual, so they never get credited. So the regressions and the big natural experiments end up reporting 'institutions did it' partly as an artifact of what's measurable, and geographic or cultural channels never get a fair shot at entering as anything but a control. I'm not claiming institutions don't matter; I'm claiming the estimated magnitude is inflated by a measurement asymmetry, and the headline identification strategies mostly buy their exclusion restriction by assuming geography washes out rather than demonstrating it.

The audience: mostly empirical development economists and economic historians, at least half of whom have built careers in the institutions paradigm, plus one historical geographer who works on disease and agricultural suitability, and one junior person doing norms-and-persistence who everyone else treats as a nuisance. Prepare me for Q&A. I want the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased pretty much the way these people will phrase them; for each one, a short reply and an honest note on where that reply is weak; which objections genuinely damage my argument; what I should concede immediately; what I should hold firm on and why; and the question I'm praying nobody asks. Don't give me citations or a reading list; make it a prep sheet I can memorise on the train.
