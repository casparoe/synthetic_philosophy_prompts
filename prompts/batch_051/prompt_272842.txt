I've wound up running a philosophy circle for retired scientists in my town, and our next session is on discounting the future. I've been reading Ramsey on this lately — the idea that pure time preference, discounting someone's wellbeing just because it happens later, is more a weakness of the imagination than anything you can defend — and I want the group to actually feel that, not just nod along. There'll be about 14 of us, we have 90 minutes, nobody has economics or formal philosophy training (retired chemists, geologists, one civil engineer), and there's a room we can move around in.

What I'd like you to do is design a runnable game or activity, with genuine small stakes (chocolate, coffee vouchers, poker chips that convert into wine at the end — your call), where participants make a sequence of choices about rewards arriving at different times and get tripped up by thier own impatience. I don't want the usual would-you-rather-have-100-dollars-now-or-110-in-a-year questionnaire: I want the discounting to happen in what they do, so that by the time we name it, they've already done it. The payoffs should be arranged so that at the start almost everyone makes a plan for later rounds, and then, when those rounds actually arrive, most of them want to break it — the preference reversal should happen to them, not be read to them.

The thing I keep chewing on is how to seperate pure time preference from the perfectly good reasons to discount the future: risk that the future won't arrive, opportunity cost from using the resources earlier, and the fact that later people (or later selves) might be richer. So the activity should have at least one condition with real uncertainty and one without, or some comparable trick, so that in the debrief we can point at the residue left once those are controlled for. That residue — sheer preference for sooner because it's sooner — is what I want them to squirm about.

Please lay out rules, materials, exact payoff numbers, timeline, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto exponential vs hyperbolic discounting, Ramsey's point, and time inconsistency. Be honest about what the activity can't reproduce (I assume you can't fake a forty-year horizon in ninety minutes) and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. No lecture up front, and no naming the concepts until the debrief.
