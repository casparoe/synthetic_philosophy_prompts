I'm a retired geologist and I've only recently started reading philosophy seriously, so forgive me if this is a standard move. I built a six-step argument that seems to show *better than* isn't transitive, and I can't find the flaw. It's a spectrum argument — my old field was full of gradual transitions, wich may be why I trust it.

(1) If A is better than B all things considered, and B is better than C all things considered, then A is better than C all things considered.

(2) Other things equal, if a person has abundant unscheduled free time, trading one hour a week of it for the removal of one serious concrete risk to their safety makes their situation better all things considered.

(3) Take a chain of situations S0 to S100. S0 is a life with 100 hours a week of unscheduled free time and almost no safety. Each S(i+1) is exactly S(i) except that one further hour a week becomes monitored or scheduled and one further serious risk is removed. S100 is a life with zero unscheduled hours a week and complete, monitored security.

(4) For every i from 0 to 99, the person at S(i) still has abundant free time; one more hour can't be the thing that turns abundance into scarcity. So by (2), each S(i+1) is better than S(i) all things considered.

(5) By repeated application of (1), S100 is better than S0 all things considered.

(6) But S100 is not better than S0. A completely monitored life with zero unscheduled time is bleaker than an open, risky life with lots of self-directed time.

So something has to give. I'm tempted to say (6) is the weak link, but I find I can't actually move myself off it. Tell me exactly which step fails and what the corrected version of it would look like, and whether anything in this argument really threatens the transitivity of better-than.
