I spent most of my career as a solid-state physicist, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy with a small group of former colleagues; our current topic is the philosophy of time. We keep returning to a single apparently simple question: what does it mean to say of an event that it is real now? I realize that 'now' is a contested term, and that special relativity makes it more difficult rather than easier, since it tells us that simultaneity is not absolute and that observers in relative motion slice spacetime into different sets of concurrent events. I would like a working definition of 'real now' as applied to events, one that does not simply beg the question in favor of either presentism or the block universe.

The definition should get the following cases right, or at least give a defensible verdict on each. A knuckle rap on my desk at this moment is real now; my retirement dinner last year is not. A supernova in a galaxy ten million light-years away whose light reaches my telescope tonight is seen now, but by ordinary reckoning the explosion itself happened ten million years ago, so the seeing and the seen seem to have different statuses. A flare on a planet five light-years away that, in my rest frame, coincides exactly with the rap is present by my frame's assignment, even though I cannot know of it for five years. If a spacecraft passes Earth at that same moment moving toward the planet at a substantial fraction of light speed, the Lorentz transformation places a different, later event on the planet in the spacecraft's simultaneity slice, so the flare itself is not part of the same present for the spacecraft. Then there is tomorrow morning's coffee, at nine o'clock, here at home: clearly future in my rest frame, yet the argument usually attributed to Rietdijk and Putnam appears to show that one can choose an event far enough away to be simultaneous with my rap in my frame, and spacelike separated from the coffee, and then find a moving observer for whom that distant event and the coffee are simultaneous; if every observer's present is as real as mine, tomorrow's coffee is already real. Finally, consider the familiar Andromeda-style case, without the storybook details: two people pass each other on the street; for the one walking toward the Andromeda galaxy some particular event there has already occurred, while for the one walking away it has not yet occurred. Is there a fact of the matter about whether that event is present?

Please approach this as a philosopher of physics teaching a graduate seminar would. Before committing yourself, work through the main available positions, such as a frame-relative present, a purely local 'here-now,' presentism, and the eternalist block-universe reading, and say which of them your definition embodies and which it must reject. Then propose the definition, run it through each case, and tell me where it gives no clear answer or yields a verdict you find surprising. If your first attempt fails, revise it and say which cases changed their classification between the earlier and the later definition. I am more interested in a definition that is honest about its own costs than in a forced settlement, so please close by identifying the verdict you find least secure. Write as continuous prose in the manner of an email or letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables, even though the cases would naturally invite a list.
