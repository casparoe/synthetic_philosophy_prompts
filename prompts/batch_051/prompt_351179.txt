I recently spent a long evening arguing with a friend about the AI-safety-by-debate proposal: two AI systems argue opposite sides of a question while a human judge watches, and this is supposed to let the human supervise systems far more capable than the human. My friend defended a skeptical view—that for any fixed level of judge competence, a sufficiently good liar can construct a case the judge finds convincing even when its conclusion is false. I tried to press that worry into a numbered argument, and now I cannot tell whether it is sound or where exactly it overreaches. My friend also kept saying that follow-up questions would let the judge drill down; I could not decide whether that answers the worry or merely pushes it down a level.

Here is the argument as I reconstructed it:

(1) A debate between two agents lets a human judge identify the more truthful answer only if, whenever the dishonest agent offers a misleading argument, the honest agent can produce a rebuttal that the judge finds more convincing. (2) A rebuttal is found more convincing than a misleading argument only if the judge can see that some specific step of the misleading argument fails—that a premise is false, an inference invalid, or some material consideration omitted. (3) A judge can see that a specific step fails only by checking that step against the subject matter itself, which is just the kind of checking the debate was supposed to reduce to manageable pieces rather than eliminate. (4) Yet for any judge with fixed competence, a sufficiently capable arguer can construct an obfuscated argument: a chain in which every individual step, examined from the judge's vantage point, looks plausible, so that no single step can be pinned down as the failure, even though the conclusion is false. (5) Therefore, faced with an obfuscated argument, the judge cannot see that any specific step fails. (6) Therefore, the honest agent cannot produce a rebuttal the judge finds more convincing. (7) Therefore, debate does not reliably let a human judge identify the more truthful answer.

I keep going back and forth about steps (2) and (4), and I also suspect the argument assumes the judge must reject a bad argument by locating its bad step, when a rational judge might instead reject the whole package on holistic grounds—for instance, because the conclusion is independently far less plausible than any of the premises. Is the argument valid as it stands? If it is not, identify the first step that fails and say what a corrected version of that step would have to assert for the argument to go through. If it is valid, say which premise you find weakest and what the strongest case for it would be.
