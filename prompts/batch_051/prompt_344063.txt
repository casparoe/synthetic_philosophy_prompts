I hold a view of social norms in the tradition of David Lewis's account of convention. On this view, a large and important class of norms—including many now treated as morally binding—began as arbitrary solutions to recurring coordination problems: several rules would have worked equally well, but once one became salient and each person expected the others to follow it, conformity became the best response to expected conformity and the rule became self-enforcing. It persisted, spread, and was taught to newcomers not because anyone judged its content best or right, but because precedent and common expectations made deviation costly; over generations this origin was forgotten, participants moralized the rule and credited its content with an authority that the coordination story says belongs only to the equilibrium, and apparent improvements in norms often strike me as path-dependent drift rationalized after the fact. I do not claim this explains every norm, but I treat it as the default and best explanation for the large class of norms it fits, and I think the felt necessity of many norms is systematically misleading about how they arose.

I want to know what would count against this view, not the standard objections but the evidence, arguments, or possible cases that should actually move me. Rank them from the considerations that would be most damaging—potentially fatal or forcing a major restriction of the view—to those that are merely awkward. For each one, state the consideration and whether a committed defender of the Lewisian picture would concede that it counts or would try to explain it away, and on what grounds. Reply as a bare numbered list of exactly five items, each item one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding text.
