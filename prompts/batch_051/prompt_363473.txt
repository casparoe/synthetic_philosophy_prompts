In An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding (1748; 1777 edition), Section V, Part I, David Hume writes:

"Though we should conclude, for instance, as in the foregoing section, that, in all reasonings from experience, there is a step taken by the mind, which is not supported by any argument or process of the understanding; there is no danger, that these reasonings, on which almost all knowledge depends, will ever be affected by such a discovery."

I teach a graduate philosophy of science seminar on the problem of induction and its formal descendants, and I need a classification instrument for a session that pairs this passage with a stripped-down no-free-lunch result for predictive rules. The result runs as follows. Take the measure over infinite binary sequences that treats each next bit as an independent fair coin toss. After any finite history, the conditional chance that any deterministic rule guesses the next bit correctly is exactly one half, so every rule — the straight rule that projects observed frequency, the counter-inductive rule that bets against it, the rule that always guesses heads — has identical expected accuracy. A rule can beat another only when possible futures are weighted non-uniformly; the entire advantage of one rule over another therefore lives in the choice of prior over futures, not in the rule itself.

For this session, please write a decision tree that sorts a respondent into a named position on the question: what, if anything, does this no-free-lunch result show about the problem of induction Hume states?

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of labels. Make the first split a question the respondent answers about the quoted passage: whether they take Hume's claim that these reasonings will never be affected by the discovery as (a) evidence that a justifying argument was the wrong thing to demand, (b) a merely psychological prediction that leaves the normative problem fully standing, or (c) a challenge to supply some non-demonstrative warrant for induction. Every subsequent internal node should be a claim or a concrete choice the respondent can answer without knowing which position it leads to, and should be phrased so that it discriminates among the positions below it.

The leaves must include at least these positions: the Humean sceptic; the Reichenbach-style pragmatic vindicationist; the subjective Bayesian; the objective Bayesian who assigns higher prior probability to simpler or algorithmically compressible sequences; the Popperian deductivist who denies that science needs inductive extrapolation at all; the Humean naturalist who treats custom as a fixed habit of mind whose success in this world is enough; and the measure-sceptic who denies that the uniform weighting deserves to be treated as the natural neutral prior against which inductive rules must be judged.

For each leaf, give exactly one sentence on its most awkward commitment. For any combination of answers the tree reaches that no named position occupies, say briefly whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or apparently unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot fully separate and state the additional question that would separate them. Aim for seven to nine questions and six to nine leaves, something a student can work through in ten minutes and that depends only on the quoted passage, the theorem sketch above, and the standard positions named here. Do not assign any secondary reading or citations.
