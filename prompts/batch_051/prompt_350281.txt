I am a novelist drafting a scene set in a hospital chapel. Two characters — a chaplain and a physicist keeping vigil for her father — fall into conversation about what God could be. The chaplain holds that God is radically transcendent: wholly other, not one more item in the world, never present in the way an object or a feeling is present. The physicist finds that empty; she says that if God is to mean anything to her, God must be encountered within the world — in its order, its beauty, its depths — which is to say, immanent. Write their dialogue. It should be a genuine exchange, not a set-piece debate: each should answer the other's strongest formulation, concede what can be conceded, and press where the other's view is weakest. Let them test the views against concrete difficulties — how a wholly transcendent God could be related to a world that changes and suffers; how a wholly immanent God could be anything more than the world itself or our own best aspirations; and whether the analogy of an author, nowhere inside the story yet present in every line, resolves the dilemma or merely restates it. Neither character should be a strawman; each should be able to make the other's view look attractive. The conversation need not reach agreement — it should end where honest, intelligent disagreement naturally rests.
