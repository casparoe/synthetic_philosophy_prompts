I recently joined a giving circle whose members have been arguing about how to weigh long-term, low-probability interventions against near-term interventions with more certain payoffs, and one member, whom the rest of us treat as the quantitative authority, offered what he presented as the definitive diagnosis. His summary ran as follows:

"Expected-value reasoning commits you to fanaticism. The rule says to take each possible outcome, multiply its value by its probability, and choose the action with the highest total. So a one-in-a-million chance of saving a million lives has the same expected value as saving one life for certain, and a one-in-a-billion chance of saving a trillion lives is a thousand times better than either. Once you accept that, you should be routing your money into long-shot projects that might prevent human extinction rather than into bednets or deworming pills, which only guarantee modest, near-term benefits. And there is no principled stopping point, because this is simply what follows from the axioms of decision theory: anyone who uses expected values at all is stuck with the fanatical conclusions. Indeed, a mugger could name a payoff large enough to make the expected value infinite, and then you would be rationally obliged to hand over everything you own for a one-in-a-trillion shot at it. What this shows is that expected-value reasoning is a fine heuristic for ordinary decisions, but it breaks down precisely when probabilities become tiny and stakes become enormous, so it should not be trusted for high-stakes, long-run philanthropy."

Since I am the one responsible for drafting our group's allocation recommendation next month, I want to know how much of this to trust. I suspect the summary is not entirely fair — the claim that the axioms force all of this strikes me as too strong, and I have never understood whether the example with the mugger is supposed to involve an infinite payoff or merely a very large one — but I cannot make the underlying issue precise. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what a careful defender of expected-value reasoning would accept, what they would flag as a conflation or an overstatement, and what survives of the final conclusion once the corrections are made? I would also like to know whether the mugger argument is best understood as a problem about infinite payoffs, about unbounded utility functions, or about something else, and whether one can reject the fanatical conclusion while still accepting expected-value reasoning in a suitably qualified form.
