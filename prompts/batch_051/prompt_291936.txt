I am preparing a recorded interview for a student-run philosophy journal with a philosopher who defends the maximin rule as a general principle of rational choice under ignorance: whenever probabilities cannot be assigned, choose the option whose worst outcome is least bad. He is not defending it only as the special principle Rawls uses behind the veil of ignorance; he says the same rule should govern ordinary irreversible choices, from medical decisions to career changes.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having him restate the position. Include at least one question on each of the following, and for each, a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give: Harsanyi's objection that a rational party behind a veil of ignorance should assign equal probability to occupying each person's position and maximize expected utility; whether minimax regret—minimizing the worst regret rather than securing the best worst outcome—is a more plausible rival; how to individuate options and outcomes so that the worst case cannot be gerrymandered by redescription; and the asymmetry between one-off and repeated decisions under ignorance. End with one question about what evidence or cases would make him abandon the rule.

Please organize the response in these sections, in order: 'Opening questions' (2 questions), 'The Harsanyi challenge' (2 questions, each with a follow-up), 'Rival rules and regret' (2 questions, each with a follow-up), 'Outcome individuation' (2 questions, each with a follow-up), 'One-off versus repeated choice' (1 question with a follow-up), and 'What could change your mind' (1 question). After the questions, add a headed note, 'Settled versus contested' (3–4 sentences), separating what is settled in decision theory from what remains genuinely disputed between maximin, minimax regret, and expected utility, so that the questions do not quietly presuppose a contested reading as consensus.
