I'm a machine learning engineer who works mainly on sequential decision-making problems — multi-armed and contextual bandits, reinforcement learning for treatment assignment, recommendation, and adaptive experimentation — and I've been reading around in the ethics of randomized controlled trials, especially the clinical equipoise literature. I keep finding myself reaching for my own vocabulary to make sense of the debate: policies, environments, arms and contexts, exploration versus exploitation, regret, posterior uncertainty, exploration bonuses, safety constraints. But I worry that the translation is doing more work than I realize, and that I'm quietly dropping parts of the argument that don't fit my formalism.

The position I want translated is roughly this: it is ethically permissible to randomize patients between treatments in a clinical trial only while there is genuine, honest uncertainty in the relevant expert medical community about which treatment is better — the condition usually called community clinical equipoise. This is typically contrasted with a stricter requirement that the individual treating clinician herself be genuinely uncertain between the arms, and with a looser view that randomization is acceptable whenever a trial could produce knowledge that a regulator or health system needs, even if some clinicians already suspect one arm is inferior. The tension the position is trying to manage is between the clinician's obligation to the particular patient in front of her and the social value of obtaining reliable causal evidence for future patients who do not yet exist.

Restate the core claims, distinctions, and arguments of that debate in the vocabulary of bandit algorithms and reinforcement learning as faithfully as you can — policies, states and contexts, arms, exploration and exploitation, regret, priors and posterior uncertainty, constraints, and so on — and then tell me what did not survive the translation. Which claims map cleanly onto the apparatus, which can be carried over only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all? In particular, I want your view on the residue: is it a sign that the RL vocabulary is too thin to express something that matters ethically, or a sign that some of the ethical claims are doing less work than they appear to do when stated in their home vocabulary? Be honest about where the formal framework itself smuggles in assumptions — for example, about who pays the exploration cost and who reaps the information gain — rather than treating those assumptions as neutral.
