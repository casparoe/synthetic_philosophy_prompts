Imagine a foundation that accepts unsolicited project proposals and must rank them strictly by expected value, where a proposal's expected value is computed from the proposer's own numbers alone: the probability that the project works, multiplied by the number of lives it would save if it did. The foundation is barred from discounting a proposal because its probability is tiny, its mechanism is speculative, or it lacks precedent, and it may not cap the number of lives a proposal may claim to save. A proposal may be rejected only on two grounds: the stated numbers do not multiply out as claimed, or the probability estimate contradicts a reference class the proposer itself supplied. Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a moral position. Analyze what the rule incentivizes among proposers, reviewers, and the foundation's leadership; how it would be gamed in practice; what kind of portfolio the institution would converge on over time; and whether its failure modes can be repaired without abandoning the underlying commitment that no positive probability is too small to matter if the stakes are large enough.
