I'm taking a course on personal identity and practical reason this term, and I keep returning to the question of self-binding. Consider three practices.

First, psychiatric advance directives, sometimes called Ulysses clauses. A competent person with bipolar disorder signs a directive specifying that, if she enters a future manic episode and refuses treatment, she may be treated involuntarily. Many legal systems will honor that earlier instruction over her contemporaneous refusal, on the grounds that the earlier, competent self was in a better position to decide for the later, impaired self.

Second, ordinary contractual precommitment. A person accepts a loan with an early-repayment penalty, signs a lease she cannot exit cheaply, or agrees to a non-compete clause, knowing that her later self will wish she had kept her options open. Courts routinely enforce these arrangements against the later preference.

Third, constitutional precommitment. A constitution entrenches certain rights or imposes a supermajority requirement for amendment, so that a current legislative majority is disabled from doing what it presently wants because an earlier generation bound it.

But the same legal systems refuse to enforce contracts of self-enslavement, agreements to sell a vital organ, or irrevocable waivers of all future treatment. So the law honors the authority of the earlier self over the later self in some cases and not others, and it does so in both the personal and the political cases.

I want to know what picture of the self, of autonomy, and of trans-temporal authority this whole practice presupposes. Does honoring an advance directive presuppose that the later self is, in some robust sense, the same person as the earlier one, and is that compatible with a more reductionist view on which the later self is only connected to the earlier self by overlapping psychological links? Does the practice presuppose that autonomy can be legitimately exercised by restricting one's future options, rather than only by choosing at the time of action? Which rival views does it quietly rule out, and which views would each still license the practice? Finally, are the two strands of doctrine coherent together: can a legal system consistently honor a Ulysses clause while refusing to enforce a contract of self-enslavement, or do those doctrines presuppose inconsistent things about identity and freedom — and what would the law have to abandon if we instead held that only present, unbound consent counts?
