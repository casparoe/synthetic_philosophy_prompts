I run a decision theory unit inside an intro philosophy course — 24 undergrads, one 60-minute session — and after spending the last month in a giving circle that kept getting pascal's-mugged into weird conclusions, I want students to actually feel why expected value reasoning goes fanatical, not just have me tell them. Design an in-class game where participants are repeatedly offered a choice between a sure thing and a lottery ticket whose chance of paying out is small but whose payoff keeps growing, so that the lottery is always the expected-value winner. The thing I want built in: whenever a student sets a cap or says 'no payoff that big is worth that probability,' the rules should let the facilitator restart the ladder one probability notch down or one payoff scale up, so the stopping point starts to look arbitrary unless the student can defend a principled cutoff. Use tokens with announced probabilities from actual cards or dice, no real money, include a round where the same choice is repeated many times and averaged so students can see whether the law of large numbers changes what feels right, and give a rough timeline for the hour. The debrief should map the moment people start refusing the ticket onto the live options — bounding utility, ignoring outcomes below some probability, rejecting expected value maximization for one-shot choices, or biting the fanaticist bullet — and should be honest about what the token game cant reproduce about the real thing, like probabilities that are themselves guesses and huge payoffs that land on strangers rather than the player. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and what to say in the debrief to make the distinction between choices about your own tokens and fanaticism about other people's welfare land.
