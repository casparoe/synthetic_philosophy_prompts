I have recently begun helping to teach an introductory biology course, and in preparing a session on natural selection I have found myself unsettled by an asymmetry in how fitness is treated compared with other explanatory dispositions. We would not accept the explanation that opium puts a person to sleep because it has a sleep-producing power, where that power is defined simply as the tendency to put people to sleep; yet evolutionary biology routinely explains a trait's spread by saying that it was fitter, while also defining fitness in terms of the survival and reproductive success that the explanation is supposed to account for. This is, of course, the tautology objection: if fitness is measured by actual survival and reproduction, then the claim that the fittest survive looks empty. My question is whether anything justifies this asymmetry. Why should the circularity be tolerated, or dissolved, in evolutionary biology when a parallel move would be rejected elsewhere, for instance in chemistry or in ordinary causal explanation? Or is the asymmetry only apparent, because fitness should be understood not as actual success but as a propensity—a probabilistic disposition to leave offspring in a specified environment? If you take that line, please address the harder version of the objection: a propensity of this kind, unlike a plainly observable disposition such as fragility, can typically be estimated only from the very outcomes it is meant to explain, so how does the propensity reading avoid becoming a sleep-producing power dressed up in statistics? I would like a verdict on whether the asymmetry is justified, but before you give it, set out the strongest case for the opposing answer. Please keep your response to at most 500 words, and write in British English.
