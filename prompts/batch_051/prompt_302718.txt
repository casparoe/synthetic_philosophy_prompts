I was at the pub last night with two mates, Lex and Priya, both of whom are way to into decision theory. We was meant to be watching the football, but they got into a long argument about counterfactual mugging and a variant Priya calls XOR blackmail, and I ended up listening rather than watching. I'd like you to adjudicate the argument. For context, the classic counterfactual mugging is: Omega, a perfect predictor, flips a fair coin. Heads: it gives you £10,000 if it predicted you would pay £100 on tails. Tails: it asks you for £100. No precommitment is allowed.

The back-and-forth went like this. (1) Lex said the mugging is easy: you shouldn't pay. In the tails branch the heads branch didn't happen; paying £100 is just a loss, because the only reward is in a branch you're not in. (2) Priya replied that if you wouldn't pay in tails, Omega predicts that, so on heads you get nothing. The policy of paying in tails has expected value £4,950 before the flip, while refusing has £0, so you should be the sort of agent who pays. (3) Lex: Omega's prediction is fixed by the time you're asked. Your current action can't change what was predicted. The ex ante calculation is about choosing a disposition, not about what to do after you've seen tails. (4) Priya: The disposition and the act aren't separable. If your disposition says to pay in tails, then paying now is just what that disposition outputs; you can't collect the ex ante benefits while refusing ex post. (5) Lex: I can, because the benefits are in a counterfactual branch. A decision theory has to tell you the best act in your actual branch, not the best disposition for branches you aren't in. (6) Priya then brought in a variant she calls XOR blackmail. Omega runs two copies of you, one in the real branch and one in a simulated branch, and each copy is told it is the real one. In each branch Omega demands £100 and threatens to leak a damaging secret. But the leaks are cross-wired: in the real branch the secret is leaked iff the simulated copy refuses; in the simulated branch the secret is leaked iff the real copy refuses. Paying in your branch does not affect whether your own secret is leaked; it only affects the copy's. Do you pay? (7) Lex said that in this version, if he thinks causally and treats the simulated copy as already fixed, paying doesn't cause his own secret to be safe, so he'd refuse. But if both copies run the same algorithm on the same information, then his paying means the simulated copy pays too, so his own secret is safe; his refusing means the simulated copy refuses too, so his own secret leaks. So paying is better even for his own branch once he takes the logical correlation seriously. He admitted that felt like a point for Priya, but he still thought the two cases differed. (8) Priya said that's exactly the same cross-branch structure as the mugging. In heads, the reward to the heads-copy depends on what the tails-copy would do. So if you pay in the XOR case to protect the simulated copy, you should pay in the mugging to secure the heads reward. (9) Lex said the cases differ. In the XOR case both copies actually face the choice, so the correlation is between two live decisions. In the mugging only the tails-copy acts; the heads-copy would merely receive money or not, and Omega's prediction is already fixed. Also, preventing a harmful leak is different from chasing a forgone gain. (10) Priya said expected utility doesn't care about the gain/harm distinction, and "already fixed" is just causal thinking; the logical correlation is still there because Omega predicts your algorithm.

Please adjudicate move by move: say for each of (1)–(10) whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Also say what, if anything, both of them are missing. Then give an overall verdict on who is more right and on exactly which points. At the very end, on a final line, put a short machine-readable summary beginning "Verdict:" with your overall judgement.
