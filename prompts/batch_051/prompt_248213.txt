I am drafting a novel in which the matriarch of an isolated mountain village has spent decades arbitrating its disputes. I need to write the scene in which she must rule on a matter that will become a turning point, and I want her ruling to follow from the person her earlier decisions have established rather than from what the plot requires.

Her earlier decisions, as I have written them:
- A miller had used false weights against every customer. She made him confess before the whole village and repay double, and refused to reduce the penalty despite his confession and repayment.
- A niece had told a dying neighbor that his estranged son had returned and forgiven him, though the son had not. The matriarch declined to censure her, saying such a lie was mercy in its proper place.
- She forbade shortening the midwinter vigil or moving it indoors during a bitter frost, insisting the vigil keep its ancient form or cease to be itself.
- She refused to let the village store seed grain in a disused shrine even in a lean year, on the ground that the shrine’s sanctity could not be traded for utility.

The new case: a young scholar born in the village returns after years abroad with documents showing that the founding event the vigil commemorates did not happen as the village’s story tells it. He asks permission to read his account publicly and correct the record. The matriarch knows the vigil’s meaning may not survive the correction, but suppressing it would mean knowingly preserving a false history.

I have been unable to settle whether she treats truthfulness, tradition, and purity as ends in themselves or as instruments of some deeper concern, and this case seems to force the question. What would she decide if she is to remain consistent with the person her earlier rulings reveal? Which earlier ruling is the closest analog? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the choice, what principle would each option attribute to her, and what further case would best distinguish those principles?
