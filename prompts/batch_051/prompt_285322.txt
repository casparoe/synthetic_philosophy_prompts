I follow a long-running forecasting discussion group, and a thread from last week keeps coming back to me. The question concerned whether a labor stoppage at a major Pacific port and a fire at a semiconductor packaging plant would both occur before the end of the year. The base rates on offer were 30% and 20%, and one experienced forecaster treated those two estimates as if they were independent, multiplying them to get roughly 6% for the compound event. His stated reason was "the presumption of independence": absent specific evidence of dependence, he said, one should multiply probabilities, because "independence is the null hypothesis" and the burden lies with anyone who wants to posit a correlation. An objector replied that this gets things backwards, since both events are plausibly driven by common upstream conditions—tight global logistics, high energy prices, or a general overheating in a few key markets—so ignoring even weak dependence would systematically distort the joint estimate, perhaps understating the probability of simultaneous failure.

I have watched this move circulate in rationalist-adjacent contexts for years, and I have never seen it pinned down. As used there, the phrase seems to slide between a computational convenience, a prior over joint distributions, a rule about who bears the burden of proof, and a maximum-entropy idea that may not imply what people think it implies. I am not asking you to resolve the object-level question for the port and the plant. I would like a menu of the distinct precise claims that "the presumption of independence" could be making, from the trivial to the substantive to the false, with a verdict on each. For each reading, please say whether it actually licenses multiplying base rates when the only objection is an unsupported but plausible common-cause story. If no precise reading of the slogan does the work this forecaster was asking it to do, I want to know that too, and what weaker or more defensible slogan should replace it in his comment.
