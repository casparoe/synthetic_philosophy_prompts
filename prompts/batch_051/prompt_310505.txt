I was up late last night arguing with a friend about whether thought experiments in science are just polished intuition pumps or whether they can actually move our knowledge forward without any new observation. He kept saying they're just arguments dressed up in pictures, and I kept insisting that undersells them — and the conversation kept circling back to the same example, Galileo's two-stones argument against the claim that heavier bodies fall faster. I want to run a class session where the students actually feel the force of that argument from the inside, rather than being told about it.

The setting is a philosophy of sience seminar, 14 students, 80 minutes. I'd like a structured activity — a game, a role-play, or a forced-choice deliberation — where the students have to commit themselves to the heavier-falls-faster view and then discover, through their own commitments, that they cannot issue a single coherent prediction for the case Galileo actually uses. Specifically, I was rereading the section in Galileo's *Dialogues Concerning Two New Sciences* where Simplicio starts out maintaining that a heavy body falls faster than a light one, and Salviati gets him to consider a light stone tied to a heavy stone: the pair is heavier than the heavy stone alone, so it should fall faster, but the light stone should also retard the heavy one, so the pair should fall slower. My students should run into exactly that tension themselves. By the end, I want them to see that the strength of the argument is not that it proves the correct law of fall, but that it makes the proportionality claim internally unstable — and to have a real foothold for asking whether that can count as acquiring knowledge.

I don't want any lecture up front and I don't want the name Galileo dropped until the debrief. Please design the activity so that the rules and materials make the contradiction appear as a consequence of their own decisisions — for example, by having them play an Aristotelian academy that must issue predictions for a demonstration before the Grand Duke. Include a timeline, materials, what you predict will go wrong the first time it is run and how to handle it, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity to the parts of the thought experiment. Also be frank about which feature of Galileo's original argument the activity cannot reproduce and what the facilitator will have to supply after all.
