I'm a fourth-year math PhD student and I've somehow ended up giving the departmental teaching seminar next month. My talk is on how we teach the first real analysis course (the one with sequences, limits, Bolzano-Weierstrass, continuity, compactness, and the basics of uniform convergence). My position, which I have to defend in the Q&A, is that the course should be motivation-first: each major theorem should be approached through concrete examples and a failed naive proof or a counterexample, and only after that should the epsilon-delta / open-cover machinery come in as the thing that fixes the failure. I'm not against rigor; I think rigor should arrive as the payoff rather than be the starting point, and that front-loading it is a big reason students check out.

The audience is my department: mostly pure mathematicians, mix of analysts and non-analysts, several of whom wrote the current analysis lecture notes. They value mathematical maturity, they think the point of analysis is learning to prove things, and a couple of them have complained that high school and calculus teaching is already to hand-wavy and its our job to fix that. There's one senior analyst who is genuinely curious about teaching but worries about standards, and one younger faculty member who will probably defend rigor-first as what seperates math from engineering.

Give me a prep sheet for the question period. I want the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way this crowd would phrase them. For each objection: how damaging it is, what to concede immediately, what to hold, and a roughly two-minute reply. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me where my own position is softest so I can prepare that defense instead of hoping they don't notice. If there's a single reply that has to land for the whole talk to survive, flag that too.
