We've been going back and forth in an AI alignment reading group about process-based versus outcome-based supervision (roughly: scoring each step of a model's intermediate reasoning instead of only the final answer), and it seams to me the whole debate is misframed. I've got a half-sketched essay I'd love a second pair of eyes on before I do anything with it. The claim I'm kicking around is that process-based supervision isn't really an alternative to outcome-based supervision — it's outcome supervision with the verdict moved earlier in time and applied at higher resolution. When a labeler marks a step of the chain-of-thought as "good," they're either checking that the step is locally valid (a valid inference, a correct algebraic move) or they're checking that it's the sort of step that tends to show up in trajectories that end in good final answers. The first check can't do the advertised job, because a sequence of locally valid steps can start from a subtly wrong premise and march straight into a confidently wrong answer, with the flaw nowhere visible in the step-by-step logic. So in practice the labeler quietly leans on the second check: a step is good insofar as it's promising, and "promising" just means "the kind of step that would belong to a good outcome." But that's outcome supervision again, just distributed over timesteps. On this view the real fork isn't outcome-versus-process at all; it's which outcome is functioning as the yardstick, and how finely you slice the credit assignment.

The obvious pushback is that a step can be good even when the whole episode fails — the student sets up the integral correctly and then makes a sign error at the very end — so the process verdict is doing something an outcome verdict can't. My reply so far is that the labeler is just grading a local outcome (did this substep accomplish its sub-goal?) rather than the global one, and that "sub-goal" just means "outcome at a smaller scale." But I'm not sure that reply is as strong as I want it to be, because then "outcome" gets stretched until basically everything looks like process supervision from one vantage point and nothing does from another, which might mean the distinction is doing no philosophical work and only marketing work. Maybe that's the right conclusion, but it feels too quick.

So here's what I'd like comments on, in roughly this order of interest. First, is there a third story about what makes a process step good that isn't reducible to either local validity or outcome-promise — something like domain-general epistemic virtues (acknowledging uncertainty, stating assumptions, checking units) — and would it actually escape the argument? Second, is the "sub-goals are just smaller outcomes" move a real problem for someone who wants process supervision to be a distinct kind of supervision, or am I just relabeling things to win? Third, where's the weakest premise, and what's the most plausible patch? And finally, if you had to defend the opposite thesis — that process supervision is a genuinely different kind of supervision, not merely finer-grained outcome supervision — what would your best one-paragraph steelman look like? Be brutal; I'd rather find out the essay is confused now than after I've written the whole thing.
