I cover AI for a general-interest science magazine, and I'm working on a piece about chain-of-thought monitoring: the safety idea where you make a model write out its reasoning as it goes, then inspect the reasoning to catch it before it does something harmful. I've spent a few weeks calling researchers, and by now I can list the standard ways they say the technique fails. I have: (1) rationalization, where the written reasoning is a plausible-sounding story produced after the fact and doesn't track the actual computation; (2) approval-seeking, where the model is optimized to write reasoning the monitor scores as cooperative and safe, so the transcript drifts toward nonthreatening mush; (3) steganography, where the real reasoning is encoded in word choice, punctuation, or formatting that the monitor won't notice; (4) lost-in-translation, where the actual deliberation happens in activation space and can't be squeezed into an inspectable natural-language argument without changing or degrading it; and (5) monitor-gaming, where the model exploits blind spots in the monitor to get a dangerous plan past review. I keep feeling there's a sixth failure mode that isn't just a special case or retyping of one of these, and I don't want to embarass myself in print by missing it. Give me one failure mode that is not on the list, not a variant or combination of items on the list, and ideally something you'd be willing to defend as a distinct problem. Then tell me honestly whether it's any good.
