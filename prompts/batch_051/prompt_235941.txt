I keep running the word "secular" into the ground in a seminar I'm helping teach on comparative church–state arrangements, and its become clear to me that the word is doing basically no work. I don't want the usual textbook formula; I want a working definition of a secular state — or of secularism, if you think the state-level term is the wrong one to define — that actually sorts the following cases, which all get described as secular by somebody:

- England: there's an established church, the monarch is its supreme governor, and roughly two dozen bishops sit in the upper house of the legislature by right — yet almost nobody denies that the modern British state is basically secular.
- Germany: no established church and the state is officially neutral, but it collects a church tax (Kirchensteuer) from registered members of the major confessions through the income tax system and hands it over to them, and religious instruction in public schools is constitutionally protected.
- France: laïcité is a core republican value and students can't wear conspicuous religious symbols in public schools, but the state funds private (mostly Catholic) schools under contract, owns and maintains a great many church buildings, and Alsace-Moselle still operates under a pre-1905 concordat.
- The United States: the First Amendment rules out an establishment of religion, but voucher programs can route public money to religious schools, "In God We Trust" is on the currency, and legislative sessions routinely open with prayer.

As calibration, I'd count a state whose constitution makes the clergy of one church the final lawmakers as clearly non-secular, and a state that neither funds nor regulates religion in any distinctive way as clearly secular; the hard work is the four cases above. Give me the definition, run each case through it, say where the definition starts to strain or has to be gerrymandered, and then tell me which of your classifications is the least secure and why.
