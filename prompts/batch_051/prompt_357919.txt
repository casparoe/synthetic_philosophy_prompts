I'm taking a media law course this term and got into a running argument with a housemate who works at a credit-scoring startup about whether predictive ratings should ever count as defamation. A legislator we both follow has floated a "safe harbor" rule: any published statement about a person or small business is catagorically an opinion—not actionable as defamation—so long as it (1) is expressed as a numerical rating or probability, (2) is about future behavior within a stated time window (something like "repayment risk over the next 12 months"), and (3) links to a methodology page describing how the number was computed. My housemate says this would unlock honest, useful predictions; I suspect it would mostly launder factual smears through the future tense. I keep turning it over and can't tell weather the safe harbor does real conceptual work or just moves the defamation fight one level down.

I want to treat the proposal as a mechanism rather than take a side. What would it actually do to the people publishing ratings and forecasts, to the people being rated, and to what counts as a "factual" claim about someone's past? Some specific wrinkles I'd like chased down: a forecast like "35% chance this landlord will be sued within a year" seems to rest on embedded factual claims (past evictions, pending complaints)—are those embedded claims still actionable, or does the safe harbor swallow them? Can a forecast even be proved false; if the year passes and no suit comes, has the statement been falsified, or was it just a probabilistic claim that landed on the unlucky branch? What stops a publisher from recasting any ugly assertion (someone is a fraud, will default, can't be trusted around kids) as a number with a time window and a plausible-sounding methodology page? And who bears the costs if the rule cuts too far in either direction—do rated parties have any non-defamation pressure valve, like a right of reply or a correction mechanism, that would matter here?

Finally, ifyou think the safe harbor has failure modes, try to patch them—for instance by making embedded factual premises separately actionable, or by requiring forecasts to be auditable or calibrated—and say whether the patch survives contact with publishers who would rather say the bad thing as a number than say it outright.
