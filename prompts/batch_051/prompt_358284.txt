I’ve been trying to get my household budget under control, and two friends have given me sharply different versions of what is at stake in frugality. One says I have a moral duty to be frugal myself—that there is something defective about the person who sees small waste and does not care. The other says frugality has no standing on its own; what matters is whether resources are directed to the best overall use, so the real requirement is to promote efficient, frugal allocation, whether or not I practice it personally. I went back to Benjamin Franklin’s The Way to Wealth, and what struck me is how much a single passage can be read in both ways. In that passage, Father Abraham warns a crowd that small daily expenses, even ones that look too trivial to matter, quietly drain a household over time; he suggests that a family can keep up a comfortable front while being ruined by unnoticed leaks. That treats frugality as a discipline of attention and self-restraint, but the argumentative force comes from consequences.

You are a professional moral philosopher who works on the structure of duties and values. I would like the deliverable to be a specification, not a verdict: before deciding whether Franklin is right, I want to know what an adequate theory of the normative significance of frugality would have to get right. Please start by brainstorming a wide list of candidate requirements, then narrow it to the ones that survive scrutiny. In each case, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and say whether it is negotiable and why. In particular, make sure the list covers (though you are not limited to) the following:
- the boundary cases that distinguish frugality from nearby but different things such as miserliness, asceticism, efficiency, prudence, and sustainability;
- the distinction between an agent’s own frugality and the social promotion of frugality, and whether a theory may collapse that distinction without loss;
- what empirical or economic facts a theory cannot contradict, such as whether “the paradox of thrift” counts as a genuine constraint;
- what kind of case would most clearly expose whether a theory is smuggling a deontological reading of frugality into a consequentialist one, or vice versa.
End by saying which single requirement does the most work in separating the viable theories from the popular but inadequate ones, and why. A table is fine.
