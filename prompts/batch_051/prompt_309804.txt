Charles Darwin, in the fourth chapter of The Descent of Man, writes: “But these feelings and services are by no means extended to all the individuals of the same species, only to those of the same association.” I want a working definition of moral progress that treats that sentence as a description of the natural starting point of moral concern, and that uses the expansion of concern beyond such associations as one central criterion—without collapsing into simply equating progress with any enlargement of sympathy. Test your definition against these cases: the abolition of chattel slavery; the extension of the franchise to women; a law prohibiting cruelty to farm animals; a parent who favors their own children in a scholarship competition; a nationalist trade policy that benefits co-citizens while harming foreigners; a person who extends legal rights to an AI system but not to a human fetus; and a moral reform that later turns out to rest on false empirical beliefs. Say which your definition counts as progress, which it does not, and where Darwin’s sentence leaves the definition underspecified; then revise or defend the results.
