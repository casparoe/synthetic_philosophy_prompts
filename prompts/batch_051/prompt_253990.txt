I've been turning over the Chase and Sanborn bit in Dennett's essay Quining Qualia. I might be mangling the details from memory, but as I recall it: two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn, both end up disliking a coffee they have been tasting for years. Chase says the coffee itself has not changed, his taste has gotten more refined. Sanborn says his standards have not changed, but the coffee now tastes different to him. Dennett's point, I think, is that the two stories are behaviorally indistinguishable, and even the tasters themselves could not settle from the inside whether what changed was the flavor or their reaction to it. He uses this to argue that the idea of a private directly introspectable quale is doing no explanatory work.

Okay, here is the parallel that keeps bugging me. In ML monitoring, when production model outputs get worse, engineers split the explanation into data drift (the inputs changed) and concept drift (the relationship between inputs and labels changed) plus sometimes rater drift: the scorers' standards moved. Some of this can be settled by keeping old inputs, old labels, and old model snapshots. But for open-ended generation evaluated by humans, it is often impossible to tell whether the model got worse or the raters got pickier. That feels exactly like Chase and Sanborn, except the stakes are about a trained system rather than a private quale.

Is this a deep structural parallel, or does it just look like one? And does the ML version cut for or against Dennett? On one hand, you might say: in ML there is at least an objective fact to aim at, because you could in principle have kept exhaustive logs; the distinction is only epistemically hard. On the other hand, for many uses of a model, the bitter truth is that there is no stored label for what counts as good output, so the distinction is not just hard but underdefined. Walk me through the mapping. I want you to say whether the cases share a real structure, where the analogy breaks, and whether the ML case shows qualia are an incoherent posit or just that we often lack evidence. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, no preamble or restating the question. Also, when you talk about Dennett and the qualia literature, separate what is actually settled from what is contested among philosophers; I know Dennett has his critics and I don't want a response that acts like his view is the consensus.
