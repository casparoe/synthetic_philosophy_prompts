Consider the following argument concerning the color phi phenomenon and Dennett’s claim that the Orwellian/Stalinesque dispute rests on a mistake about what there is to explain.

In the standard color phi setup, a red spot is briefly presented at one location, and shortly thereafter a green spot is briefly presented at a nearby location. With appropriate timing, observers report seeing a single spot move from the first location to the second and change from red to green somewhere along its path. Against that background, consider this argument:

(1) The observer’s report is shaped by information from both stimuli: the later green stimulus contributes to the reported red-to-green transition.

(2) There are only two possible stories about when the redness enters the observer’s conscious experience. Either the observer was conscious of the red spot around the time of the first flash, and the later green flash caused a retrospective editing of the relevant memory or experience (the Orwellian account), or the observer was never conscious of the red spot as such, because the relevant experience was delayed until after the green flash arrived and was constructed with the red-to-green transition already built in (the Stalinesque account).

(3) These two stories therefore differ over a straightforward factual question: whether the observer was conscious of red before the green flash occurred.

(4) A factual question about the timing of an experience can in principle be settled by probing the observer at the relevant time.

(5) Hence the dispute can be settled by probing the observer in the interval after the red flash but before the green flash. If the observer can report or respond to the red spot during that interval, the Orwellian account is correct; if the observer cannot, the Stalinesque account is correct.

(6) Therefore the Orwellian/Stalinesque dispute, far from being an ill-posed pseudo-dispute, is empirically decidable; color phi merely shows that we need temporal probes of consciousness fine-grained enough to catch the red experience before the green flash overwrites it.

Is this argument valid? If it is, say so and explain what makes the conclusion follow. If it is not, identify exactly which step or steps fail, explain why the failure matters, and state what a corrected version of the argument would or would not establish.
