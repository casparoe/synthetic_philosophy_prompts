I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and lately I've been down a rabbit hole about a research program called factored cognition. The idea is that the way to get good reasoning out of a group, a language model, or whatever, is to break the thinking into pieces. The setup is sometimes called humans consulting humans: a head researcher takes a hard question, hands sub-questions to helpers who each see only their own little corner, combines their answers, and then repeats recursively whenever one of the sub-questions turns out to be too hard on its own. The theory behind it makes a strong claim: any deliberative task can in principle be decomposed that way, and a tree of small, mostly context-limited sub-agents can do at least as well as a single brilliant mind trying to hold the whole problem in view at once. That's genuinely attractive to me. It gives you a decision procedure, it matches how a lot of real organizations already think, and it promises to scale reasoning past the limits of any one head.

But I keep running up against a stubborn intuition that the moments of thinking I'd least want to lose don't look like that at all. They look like a bunch of seperate considerations suddenly snapping into a single picture: seeing how the pieces of an argument reinforce each other all at once, or realizing that two plausible-sounding answers are really the same answer from two angles. I want to say there's a difference in kind between a manager weighing reports from people who each only understood a fragment and a single mind that holds the whole thing at once. A tree of specialists can hand you a dossier; it can't have the insight. A principle keeps pulling at me—that the one who judges has to see the whole—even though I can't state it nearly as cleanly as the factored-cognition people state theirs.

So that's the clash: a theory with real systematic virtues on one side, a firm intuition it contradicts on the other. Don't just tell me who wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes—what's the strongest debunking story for why integrated insight feels so special when it's really just the visible summary of a factored process underneath—and what it would take for the theory to go or be amended, say because some inferences require joint attention to everything at once and no decomposition preserves their quality. Or is there a compatibility reading where the theory is only an idealization about division of labor, not a claim about what deliberation is or what a judgment consists in? Give me the strongest version of each move and tell me which you'd give up, which you'd amend, and which compatibility reading seems most honest.
