I'm putting together a film-and-philosophy unit on empty names and truth in fiction, and I want to use Stranger than Fiction (2006) as the bridge between the two halves: the semantics of names that do not refer, and the question of what is true according to a story. The film gives me a lot to work with. Harold Crick, an IRS auditor, begins hearing a woman's voice narrating his actions in third person; he learns that the voice belongs to Karen Eiffel, a novelist writing a book in which he is the protagonist, and that her sentences appear to make things true about him, including his impending death. The literary theorist he consults tries to settle whether Harold is in a comedy or a tragedy, as though identifying the genre would determine what will be true of him. I want to read this situation as a nested-fiction thought experiment: Harold is a real person within the film's story and a merely fictional character within the novel inside that story, while from our seats he is a fictional character in a film.

Could you help me work out that reading in a way that is faithful both to the film and to the philosophy of language? I want to know where the film dramatizes distinctions that theories of empty names and truth in fiction actually draw—for instance, the difference between the conditions under which a name refers and the conditions under which a sentence is true in a fiction—and where the film trades on confusions a careful philosopher would want to keep separate. What would a Millian, a descriptivist, and a pretense-theorist about fiction each have to say about Harold Crick, about the claim that Harold Crick will die in the novel, and about our own outside-the-film claim that Harold Crick does not exist? How does the nesting of fictions change the standard textbook cases, such as whether Sherlock Holmes refers and whether it is true that in the stories Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street? I would also like an honest account of where the film resists the philosophical reading, the scenes or plot moves a theorist would find sloppy or that push back against the theory. The paper I am building toward asks what the film adds to this debate that argument alone does not, so I want to avoid reducing it to an illustration; where should the reading concede that the film is doing something more than, or different from, the theory?
