Question: Historians sometimes describe the outbreak of the First World War as "overdetermined." In a short essay, explain what overdetermination means in a historical explanation, and discuss what it does and does not imply for how we should explain why the war broke out in August 1914.

Student answer: Overdetermination is when an event has several causes each of which would have been sufficient on its own to bring it about. The First World War looks like a textbook case: the alliance system, militarism, imperial rivalries, and nationalism had each pushed Europe toward a general war by 1914, so even without the assassination of Franz Ferdinand some other spark would have set off the same war around the same time. The shooting in Sarajevo is therefore not really an explanatory cause; it is just the trigger that released a pressure that was already there. It follows that in an overdetermined event it makes no sense to ask which cause was more important, because the outcome would have happened without any single one of them. The correct explanation should therefore list the background conditions that made war possible, without trying to rank them or pick one out as decisive. A monocausal account, like blaming the alliance system alone, would be false.
