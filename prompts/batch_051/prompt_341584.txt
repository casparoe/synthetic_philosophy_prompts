I've been retired for a few years now. I spent my career as a physicist working on disordered systems, where the whole game is that the ensemble average and the time average come apart — in a non-ergodic system the ensemble average can be a number no single system every visits. So when I got into philosophy of economics after retiring, the thing that lit me up was that same point applied to ordinary decisions: a lot of what we call "rational" is only rational if you average over an ensemble of parallel copies of yourself, when what you actually get is one trajectory through time.

The toy example I can't stop thinking about: a fair coin gamble where heads multiplies your wealth by 1.5 and tails by 0.6. The expected value after one round is (1.5 + 0.6)/2 = 1.05, a tidy 5% gain. But if you actually repeat the bet, the typical path is half heads and half tails, which multiplies your wealth by 1.5 × 0.6 = 0.9 every two rounds. Per round that's a growth factor of sqrt(0.9) ≈ 0.949 — a bit over 5% lost per round. The 1.05 describes the average over possible worlds; the 0.949 describes you're actual trajectory. Both numbers are "right," they just answer different questions.

Here's my counterfactual. Suppose the United States, starting tomorrow, requires that any financial product (insurance, payday loans, brokerage and retirement products, mortgages), any employment contract involving equity, and any informed-consent form for an elective procedure or long-term medication display two numbers at the top, side by side: the expected value and the time-average outcome (the geometric growth rate of the relevant quantity if the choice were repeated). The numbers are computed by a new public agency that people trust about the way they trust the National Weather Service, and they can't be buried in the fine print. But nobody is forbidden from taking the bet, and nobody has to act on the numbers.

I want your reasoned prediction of how this plays out over twenty years — mechanisms, incentives, and second-order adjustments, not a story. Get into the weeds. What happens to casinos and state lotteries? To insurance markets (this one I can argue both ways, so I want to see the actual causal chain)? To startup formation and the willingness of a 40-year-old engineer to leave a salary for equity? To payday lending? To how people think about vaccines and screening tests? Which predictions are you most confident in and why, and which would flip if people mostly ignore or distrust the displayed number? And where does the whole reform get gamed or backfire in a way its designers didn't intend — for instance, what does the agency even do about one-shot decisions like a single surgery, where there isn't really a time average at all?
