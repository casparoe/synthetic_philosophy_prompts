I have been turning over a point in the philosophy of privacy that keeps collapsing whenever I try to express it in the vocabulary I actually think in at work. I am a software engineer on a health-data platform and a longtime reader of rationalist blogs, so my default toolkit for privacy is the one used in information security and privacy engineering: confidentiality, integrity and availability; authentication and authorisation; the principle of least privilege; data minimisation; threat modelling and attack surface; and, more recently, de-identification and differential privacy. A colleague of mine, who came to the subject through moral philosophy rather than engineering, insists that this vocabulary misses the thing that matters most about privacy, and she pointed me to the following passage from Samuel D. Warren and Louis D. Brandeis's "The Right to Privacy" (Harvard Law Review, 1890) as the clearest statement of what she means:

"The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury."

She reads that passage as a claim that privacy is not fundamentally about control over information flows or about confidentiality, but about a socially created need for retreat and about a distinctive kind of harm — mental pain and distress caused by exposure — that is meant to weigh more heavily than bodily injury. Here is what I want, and it should be a systematic translation rather than a verdict or a defence of one side. First, restate the position defended in that passage as faithfully as you can in the information-security and privacy-engineering vocabulary I have just described, treating the passage's specific claims — the need for retreat from the world, heightened sensitivity to publicity, and the comparison between publicity-induced mental pain and bodily injury — as the things to be translated rather than explained away. Second, give me the residue report: which claims map cleanly onto access control, confidentiality, consent as a control, or risk and harm metrics; which claims translate only by being distorted; and which claims have no counterpart at all in the way a security engineer thinks. Third, tell me whether that residue indicates that my vocabulary is missing something morally important, or that the passage is less precise than it looks. My prior is that the apparent gap will turn out to be either a verbal disagreement or a genuine blind spot in the engineering framework, and I want to locate the crux precisely rather than paper over it.
