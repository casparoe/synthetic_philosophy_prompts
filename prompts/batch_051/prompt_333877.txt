Suppose a capital-sentencing statute, enacted in 1960, reads: "A sentence of death shall not be imposed unless the court is convinced beyond every doubt that the defendant committed the capital offense, and unless the court further finds that no punishment short of death would satisfy the need for retribution or deter others." When it was drafted, the only alternative to execution was imprisonment with possible parole, and courts treated guilt as something a witness's word or a visible physical match could establish with certainty. The statute is now applied in a jurisdiction that has since created life imprisonment without parole. The defendant was convicted on a single eyewitness identification. A recently developed probabilistic DNA analysis of the murder weapon recovers a full profile from an unknown person and none from the defendant; the prosecution's expert testifies that, if the defendant had handled the weapon, there is a 4% probability that no detectable amount of his DNA would remain. The judge accepts that testimony. Does the statute permit a death sentence in this case? Argue it from the plain words, the purpose of the provision, what its drafters would most plausibly have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader of the code would now take it to mean; then say which of these approaches should control and why.
