I'm a PhD student in development ethics, and I keep hitting the same wall when I teach or write about veganism. The standard definition of veganism says it's a way of living that tries to exclude animal exploitation, but then it tacks on a hedge about doing that only to the extent that it's feasible in your circumstances. In a seminar on food justice last week, someone pointed out that this feasibility clause is doing a huge amount of work, and that it basically collapses in some contexts: in a rich country, "practicable" can look like full avoidance, while for someone food-insecure in a place with few plant-based options, the same ethical commitment might cash out as eating some animal products simply because the alternatives are worse or unavailable. My suspicion is that the word is doing two different jobs—describing a pattern of consumption on one hand, and marking an ethical stance on the other—and scarcity sort of pries those two apart.

So here's what I'd like from you. Give me a working definition of "vegan" (or, if the cleanest answer splits it into two definitions, a dietary one and a stance one, that's fine) and run it through the following cases:

1. A middle-class person in Chicago who avoids meat, dairy, eggs, honey, leather, and cosmetics tested on animals, and has no trouble doing so.
2. A person in suburban Ohio who eats factory-farmed burgers and cheese daily and never treats animal products as an ethical issue at all.
3. A subsistence fisher in coastal Bangladesh whose family eats fish daily, because rice and lentils doesn't supply enough protein and the plant proteins they could buy are priced like luxury goods; she knows about veganism and says it's a rich person's position.
4. A Maasai pastoralist in Kenya who keeps cattle, drinks milk, and occasionally slaughters a goat; crops don't grow well where he lives, and his herd functions as his savings account.
5. A single mother in a Mississippi food desert who wants to go vegan. Her shopping options are a corner store with cans and chips and a McDonald's. She has a child, she works two jobs, and her apartment barely has a usable kitchen.
6. Someone with severe allergies to legumes, nuts, soy, and gluten who eats eggs and oysters to get by.
7. Someone who keeps rescued backyard hens as pets and eats their eggs.
8. An Iñupiaq hunter in a village where store-bought food is flown in and costs several times what it costs in Anchorage, who eats bowhead whale, seal, and caribou.
9. Someone who takes a daily medication that contains lactose and gelatin and for which there is no realistic substitute.
10. Someone who is otherwise fully plant-based but eats oysters and mussels because he's convinced bivalves aren't sentient.
11. Someone who could easily be vegan but drinks milk in his tea at his grandmother's house because refusing would cause a huge fight.

Please propose the definition, run it against all eleven cases, and say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer; then either revise the definition or stand behind the surprising verdicts. I think case 1 is unambiguously vegan and case 2 unambiguously isn't, and I genuinely don't know how to handle 3, 4, 5, 8, and 11. I'd much rather you commit to one definition and defend it than lay out the options and leave me to choose. Also, please write in American English. Thanks!
