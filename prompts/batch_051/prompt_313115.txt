I teach a third-year undergraduate PPE seminar with 18 students, and I have one 90-minute session coming up that I want to devote to judgment aggregation, specifically the discursive dilemma. I do not want to introduce the idea by name or explain it in advance; I want the students to enter as participants in a committee and only afterwards see what they were inside. Please design a runnable classroom activity, including a timed debrief.

The students should come away seeing two things. First, that a group of people who are each fully consistent in their judgments can nonetheless collectively endorse an inconsistent set of propositions, and that this arises from the aggregation procedure rather than from anyone's mistake. Second, that every natural repair of the conflict — voting on the premises alone, voting on the conclusion alone, granting someone authority to settle it, or relaxing a structural principle — requires giving up something they were probably taking for granted about fair collective decision-making.

Constraints. There should be no lecture up front, and the term discursive dilemma should appear only in the debrief. The activity must work with 18 students in 90 minutes. Since 18 is too many for one panel, break them into small groups so that everyone participates, and have the groups work the same case in parallel where feasible, then compare how they resolved it. The centerpiece should be a role-play: each student is assigned a set of consistent judgments about the elements of a structured decision, with no outside facts they need to research, and the voting is arranged so that the majority's positions on the separate elements conflict with the majority's position on the overall verdict. A second phase should then force each group to commit to a procedure for how the committee ought actually to rule and to defend that procedure, with the rule that they must live with the result their chosen procedure yields for the case before them. Please specify the exact case, the assigned judgment profiles, the ballot format, the prompts for each phase, and a schedule for the session.

The debrief should map concrete moments from the activity onto the components of the dilemma: the connected propositions, individual consistency, premise-level majorities, conclusion-level majority, and the clash between them. It should also anticipate at least three things that will predictably go wrong when this is run — for instance, students insisting that further deliberation and a re-vote would dissolve the structural problem, or trying to change their assigned views to make the conflict disappear — and it should say how I should handle each. Be explicit about what the activity cannot establish on its own: I assume a single worked profile does not show that the problem is general, so the force of the impossibility-style result may have to be supplied by me, and I want to know how to do that briefly without letting a lecture take over the session.

If there is a natural way to make the same structure reappear one level up, with each group's chosen procedure treated as a judgment to be aggregated by the whole class, include it — but only if it sharpens the point rather than blurring it.
