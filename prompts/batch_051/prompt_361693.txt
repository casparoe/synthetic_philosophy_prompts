I've been arguing with a friend about the toxin puzzle — the one where an eccentric millionaire offers you a million dollars if, at midnight, you genuinely intend to drink a harmless but sickening toxin the next afternoon. You don't actually have to drink it to get paid, and everyone involved knows you won't. My friend insists this is just a failure of imagination: of course you can decide to intend something unpleasant. I think the whole point is that intending isn't that kind of voluntary act, and I'd like to send him a piece of satire that makes the point better than I can.

Write a deadpan startup onboarding FAQ, around 650–750 words, for a company called Intendr (or another vowel-heavy name) that sells a "Toxin Readiness Program." The pitch: a high-touch subscription that coaches you into forming the midnight intention, guaranteed, with no side bets, no penalty clauses, no hiring someone to force you to drink — none of the usual workarounds. To an outsider it should read like normal startup self-help, but to someone who knows the puzzle, every answer should quietly reveal that the service can't do the one thing it charges for. Its method drifts between predicting you'll drink, wanting to be the kind of person who drinks, repeating the story until the brain scan can't tell the difference, and administratively redefining "intention" so the detector passes. Include one customer review from a client who got the million and still isnt sure they ever intended anything. The jokes should land the real issue — the gap between intending and expecting, and the idea that you can make yourself intend at will — without the FAQ ever saying so outright.
