I've been going back and forth with a friend about longtermism — the idea that because the future might contain trillions of people, the most important thing our generation can do is steer the long-run trajectory of civilization. The friend's worry, and honestly one I haven't fully been able to shake, is that once someone believes the stakes are that big, democratic norms and transparency start to look like small print you can ignore if you have to. People who think they're saving trillions of lives, he says, are exactly the kind of people who eventually decide the public can't be trusted with the truth and that a little authoritarian shortcut is fine. I keep coming back to the Manhattan Project as the closest real test of this.

So I want you to analyze the Manhattan Project through this lens. Run it as a case study for the claim that existential-stakes or far-future reasoning tends to license control. I know the broad story — the race with Germany, Los Alamos, the secrecy, the huge budgets, the decision to drop the bombs, the start of the nuclear age — so you don't need to retell it, just do the mapping. Who were the players and what did they think the stakes actually were? What got hidden or overridden, and was it justified just by the wartime emergency rather than anything like concern for the far future? Did civilian oversight actually hold, or did it get captured by the people who knew the secret? Then be honest about wether the analogy to longtermism breaks — the bomb was about an immediate rival, not about trillions of future lives, and that might matter in ways that make longtermism more safe or more dangerous. And give me a bottom line: does the episode, on net, make you more or less worried that serious far-future conviction would license authoritarian control? Dont give me both sides hanging in the air — I want the analysis to go both ways and then land somewhere.
