I sit on the Curriculum and Assessment Committee of a nondenominational theological seminary, and I need to circulate a decision memo ahead of Thursday's meeting. A biblical-studies professor has proposed a new elective, Number and Meaning in Scripture, to count toward the Master of Divinity exegesis requirement. The syllabus has three parts: a literary unit on numbers such as seven, twelve, and forty as recurring compositional and theological motifs; a historical unit on numerical exegesis in late antique and medieval Jewish and Christian traditions, including gematria and related letter-number methods; and a methods workshop in which students learn modern Bible-code search techniques—equidistant letter sequences and word-value numerology—by running the searches themselves on both biblical and control texts. The proposing professor argues that the third unit is where the real critical lesson lies: students discover from the inside how easily impressive-looking patterns can be produced from almost any text. Two committee members want to approve the course essentially as written; two want to send it back, on the grounds that it awards the same academic credit to the study of a real interpretive tradition and to exercises in what they regard as engineered confirmation bias, where multiple comparisons get mistaken for meaning.

In drafting the memo, I am deliberately setting aside any dispute about the inspiration or authority of scripture. The question is narrower and harder: when should a numerical pattern count as an interpretation of a text rather than a pattern found in it? Whatever your bottom line is—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—please give the strongest version of the losing side an honest run rather than a straw man. The committee also needs criteria that a future committee could apply to similar courses without relitigating this one from scratch, and the memo should engage the professor's pedagogical claim directly: that teaching a dubious method hands-on is the best way to inoculate students against it.

Please draft the memo I will circulate under my name, and structure it in exactly these sections, in this order, with these approximate lengths: (1) Summary and recommendation, 150 words; (2) The proposal and the committee split, 200 words; (3) The case for approving the course, 250 words; (4) The case for rejecting or restricting it, 250 words; (5) Criteria for distinguishing symbolic interpretation from pattern-hunting, 300 words; (6) Recommended conditions or amendments, 200 words; (7) Bottom line the committee can adopt, 100 words.
