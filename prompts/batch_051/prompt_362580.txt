I'm a high school senior writing a paper on what a theory of rational belief has to account for, and I want to start from a checklist of requirements before I commit to any particular view. I'm interested in a theory adequate for a careful but finite person managing a large body of fallible information, not for an idealized reasoner.

The paper keeps circling around two cases, and I don't yet see what a theory has to do to get both of them right. First, the lottery case: suppose there is a fair lottery with a million tickets and exactly one winning ticket. It seems rational to believe of any particular ticket that it will lose, since it is overwhelmingly likely to lose. It also seems rational to believe that at least one ticket will win. But if rational belief is closed under conjunction, then from my million individual loser-beliefs I could infer that every ticket will lose, which contradicts my belief that one wins. So something has to give: the closure principle, the idea that high probability suffices for rational belief, or the idea that I can rationally hold all of these beliefs at once.

Second, the preface case: an author has checked every sentence in her manuscript and rationally believes each one. In the preface she writes that, given her track record and the length of the book, she is sure at least one sentence contains an error she missed. This, too, seems rational. Yet the set of things she accepts is inconsistent: she accepts each sentence of the book and also accepts that at least one of those sentences is false. Again, something has to give, but it is not obvious what.

The two cases look structurally similar to me, so I suspect an adequate theory should treat them with the same machinery unless it can point to a principled difference between them.

For that paper, I want you to write the desiderata: the list of requirements any adequate theory of rational belief or rational acceptance must meet to handle these cases and others like them. For each requirement, give the case or principle that makes it a requirement. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any theory and which are only the preferences of one camp. Flag any pairs or larger groups of requirements that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the available theories.

Then, briefly, score the main candidate views against your list: the fixed-probability-threshold view, the view that rational belief need not be closed under conjunction, the view that outright belief is different in kind from degrees of confidence, and the view that these cases merely show that the word 'belief' is context-sensitive. Keep the scoring short; the list and the reasoning behind it are what I most need.
