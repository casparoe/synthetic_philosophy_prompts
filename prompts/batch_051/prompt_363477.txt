Lately I’ve been stuck on the bit in Sidgwick’s *Methods of Ethics*, Book I chapter IV, where he’s arguing against the view that all desire is really desire for pleasure. His point—as I read it—is that desire is often directed at something else (winning, finishing, understanding, being with a person, whatever), and the pleasure comes as a sort of side effect. I think he also says, in the same chapter, that this is what makes the paradox of hedonism more than just a bit of folk wisdom: making pleasure the direct target undermines the state that produces it. I’ve come to hold a pretty strong version of this: the paradox is a real psychological regularity, not just a cautionary metaphor, and it has normative bite for anyone defending hedonism as a theory of motivation or value.

Now I want to know where this view is exposed. What would have to be true for the Sidgwickian line to be wrong or at least overstated? I’m not interested in the standard textbook objections that get refuted a hundred times; I want the live defeaters: empirical findings, distinctions, counterexamples, or arguments that should actually move me. Rank them from decisive to merely awkward, say which ones a committed Sidgwickian would concede count against them and which they’d dismiss, and be honest about whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—and if so, whether that’s a problem, or maybe fine for a psychological generalization of this kind.
