I've always said the doing/allowing distinction matters for moral responsibility, but when I list my actual verdicts on cases, my gut doesn't seem to respect it half the time. I want you to tell me what principle, if any, is actually tracking my verdicts, and which of them I'd have to give up to keep things simple. Below are my cases and verdicts.

1. A runaway mine car is heading toward five workers. I can flip a switch to a siding where it will kill one worker. I think flipping is permissible, maybe even required.
2. Same mine car, but the only way to stop it is to push a large man off a walkway onto the track. Wrong.
3. I own a factory. I remove a safety guard from a cutting machine to speed up production, and a worker loses a hand. Wrong, full stop.
4. I own the factory. I learn a guard has broken; I don't replace it because shutting the line for a day costs too much, and a worker loses a hand. Wrong, and honestly I don't feel it's less wrong than 3, even though I "only" allowed it.
5. I'm a random pedestrian walking past the factory, and through a window I can see a worker about to feed his hand into the broken machine. I could bang on the glass and warn him at no risk to myself, but I don't. Wrong, but much less wrong than 4.
6. I'm in another country, and I decline to donate ten dollars to a fund that would have covered the repair, knowing my refusal means the injury isn't prevented. Not wrong, or only barely wrong.
7. A doctor withdraws a ventilator from a competent patient who has clearly and repeatedly asked to be taken off it. Fine.
8. A doctor never puts the same patient on the ventilator when their breathing fails, because the patient made the same request. Fine, and I see no difference between this and 7.
9. A babysitter sits by a pool and lets the toddler she's responible for drown because she doesn't want to put down her phone. Terrible — about as bad as if she held the kid under, I think.
10. A stranger on the beach sees the same toddler drowning in shallow water and does nothing, though pulling the child out would be easy and risk-free. Wrong, but not as terrible as the babysitter.

The pair that bugs me is 5 and 6: two strangers, both can prevent a harm at effectively zero cost, but I rate the one who's physically present as genuinely wrong and the distant one as barely wrong. Maybe presence matters, but that seems like it should be morally irrelevant. Is it?

I'd like you to give me one principle — or two or three candidates — that covers these verdicts, state precisely how each clause is motivated by a case, tell me which of my verdicts it can't keep without bending, and say which principle you'd bet on. Also give verdicts on a few cases I didn't list, especially one where a person starts pulling someone from a well and then lets go halfway (is that doing or allowing?).

For the format: after the prose, include a table with five columns: Case, My verdict, What a flat doing/allowing principle would say, What your preferred principle says, Match to my verdict. Then include a JSON object with these fields: "preferred_principle", "clauses" (a list of strings, each naming the case or pair that forces it), "verdicts_overturned" (a list of case numbers and the changed verdict), and "predictions" (a list of objects with keys "case" and "verdict" for the new cases).
