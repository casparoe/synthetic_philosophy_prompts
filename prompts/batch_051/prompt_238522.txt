Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps about the semantics of indicative conditionals. The first camp, the propositionalists, holds that an indicative conditional "If A then C" expresses a proposition: it is true or false at each possibility, can be negated and embedded, and has an unconditional probability. For them, Lewis's triviality results show that the Equation P(A→C) = P(C|A) cannot hold in full generality for any propositional conditional connective; their project is to explain why the Equation nevertheless holds approximately over a large fragment of unembedded, factual conditionals, and to say exactly where it fails. The second camp, the probability-first theorists, holds that the Equation is the primary datum a theory of "if" must explain; for them, the same results are best read as a reductio of the claim that conditionals express propositions at all, and conditionals are instead devices for expressing a credence under a supposition.

The two camps share the following background, which their joint statement may take as agreed. Ordinary speakers' degrees of belief in unembedded, factual indicative conditionals closely track the corresponding conditional probabilities. Formally, let → be a conditional connective defined on a Boolean field of events, and say P satisfies the Equation when P(A→C) = P(C|A) whenever P(A) > 0. The triviality phenomenon is this: suppose the Equation is required to survive conditionalization, so that P(A→C | B) = P(C | A∧B) whenever P(A∧B) > 0, and suppose there are disjoint events D and E with P(D) > 0, P(E) > 0, and P(D∨E) < 1. For any A, C with P(A∧C) > 0 and P(A∧¬C) > 0, applying the requirement under C and under ¬C gives P(A→C | C) = 1 and P(A→C | ¬C) = 0, so by the law of total probability P(A→C) = P(C); the unrelativized Equation then gives P(C|A) = P(C). Setting A = D∨E and C = D yields P(D | D∨E) = P(D), hence P(D∨E) = 1, which contradicts P(D∨E) < 1.

Write the joint statement itself. It should include: (i) a numbered list of agreed claims, stating what both camps accept about the data, the proof, and the constraints on any adequate account, without losing ground for either side; (ii) a disagreement table whose rows give, for each point of divergence, the propositionalist's formulation and the probability-first formulation, each in wording its own side would willingly sign; (iii) no more than four cruxes — sub-questions such that settling them in favor of one camp would genuinely move the other — with each camp's prediction recorded in advance; and (iv) a brief section identifying any part of the dispute that turns out to be merely verbal, if any does. Do not adjudicate the dispute; produce the document the two camps could co-sign.
