I'm a novelist, and I keep getting dragged into arguments about whether you can still admire the work of a writer who did monstrous things. I recently read a piece by an editor I respect arguing that you can't seperate the work from the artist, and along the way he summarizes the separatist view like this: "The separatist holds that a work's moral defects are never relevant to its value as art, because art invites a detached, disinterested contemplation that is nothing like our attitudes toward real people. On this view, calling a novel immoral is a category mistake, like calling a triangle melancholy; the only proper judgments are aesthetic ones such as unity, complexity, and inventiveness. So the separatist is committed to saying that a technically brilliant novel that celebrates child abuse is as good a novel as an equally brilliant humane one, and that the artist's own crimes cannot count against the work, because the work is a self-standing object severed from its maker the moment it's finished."

I'm sympathetic to the separatist side, but that summary doesn't feel like what I actually believe, and I can't tell where the editor has made it too easy to knock down. Fine-tooth it for me: which clauses would a serious defender of the autonomy of art accept, which ones overstate or conflate, and at the end tell me whether the view as he describes it is one I should keep defending or whether there's a better, more modest version.
