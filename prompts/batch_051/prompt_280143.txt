Suppose a linguist proposes three criteria for something's being a grammatical rule: (i) it maps a base form to an inflected or derived form; (ii) it applies over a well-defined class of items, extending productively to novel ones; and (iii) the output is composed from the base's own stem. Now consider the English comparative pair good and better. Better expresses the same grammatical relation as taller does to tall, but it is suppletive: it shares no stem material with good, it belongs to no productive pattern, and it is unique to this one adjective. Is the relation good → better a grammatical rule under the proposed criteria? Work through each criterion honestly. Where the case fails a criterion, say whether that should count against the criteria, against a clean division of grammar into rules and exceptions, or against the demand that better be generated by a rule at all. Finish by saying how you would revise the criteria, and whether the strongest revised version still captures what the original criteria were after. You may use the parallel case go → went, where the past tense of go is not built from go, to sharpen the verdict.
