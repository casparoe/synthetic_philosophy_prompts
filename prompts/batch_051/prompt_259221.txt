I sit on the Standing Panel on Subsidiarity of a federal republic with strong regional self-government, which hears complaints from the regions against national statutes alleged to intrude into matters properly left to the regions. Next term we must rule on a new case, and I want a recommendation built from our own precedents rather than loose talk of subsidiarity. Our record so far is as follows. We upheld a national statute imposing uniform emission ceilings on large power stations, on the grounds that air pollution crosses regional borders and endangers public health. We struck down a national statute requiring every region's public libraries to open on Sundays, holding that opening hours are a local cultural matter and that no adequate reason had been shown for displacing regional choice. We upheld a national statute laying down minimum fire-safety standards for hotels and assembly halls, noting that travellers and conference visitors move freely across regions and that basic life-safety protection should not depend on geography. We struck down a national statute establishing a mandatory Day of the Federation with regional parades, describing it as an expressive and symbolic measure rather than a proper regulation. Finally, we upheld a national statute prescribing a single emergency telephone number and call-routing protocol, because emergencies disregard regional borders and a patchwork of systems would endanger life.

The case now before us concerns a national statute requiring every region to provide wheelchair-accessible voting at all polling stations. Two regions submit that the conduct of elections is a regional competence and that the statute addresses no cross-border spillover. What should we decide if we are to remain consistent with ourselves? Please set out the principle or principles that fit our record, identify the closest precedent, state the ruling each candidate principle would support, offer your best prediction with reasons, and name the further case, not currently before us, that would best discriminate among the candidate principles.
