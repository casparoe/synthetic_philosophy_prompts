I'm preparing a handout for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of biology seminar. Most of the students are biology majors with no background in the philosophical literature. The session concerns a single question: in what sense, if any, does DNA carry information, and is "the genetic code" literally a code? I'd like you to write a classification instrument for this debate — a decision tree, or a taxonomy in which every branch is labeled by the question that separates it from its siblings — that takes students from their answers about concrete cases to a named position.

Please build the instrument from the logical space rather than from a list of names. First identify the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions within that space, then flag any combinations that are unoccupied or underpopulated, saying briefly whether each is empty because the resulting view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The work should be in the questions: each branching node should be a concrete case or claim that a student can answer without already knowing the philosophical labels, so the instrument sorts people by what they believe about cases rather than by what they call themselves.

The leaves should be the following six positions, characterized roughly as follows; you may refine the names, but do not cite authors, and do not ask the students to recognize the labels in advance:

- Literal-code view: the genome really does contain encoded instructions, and the cell's translational machinery reads semantic content off the DNA the way a reader reads a message.
- Causal-information view: genes carry information only in the covariation or correlation sense, the way a barometer carries information about an approaching storm, and calling DNA a code is loose talk about chemical causation.
- Instructional or program view: genes carry information in a sense stronger than bare correlation but weaker than full semantic content; DNA functions as a program, recipe, or set of instructions in development.
- Parity or developmental-systems view: there is no principled reason to privilege DNA as the locus of information; genes, cellular machinery, and the environment all carry information symmetrically, and code talk smuggles in an unjustified privilege for the gene.
- Teleosemantic view: a DNA sequence has content in virtue of its evolutionary function — what it was selected to do — even though no one sent the message and no intentional reader decodes it.
- Metaphorical or fictionalist view: the phrases "genetic code" and "genetic information" are useful heuristics or fictions; the literal truth, whatever it is, belongs to causal description alone.

The branching questions should make use of at least the following three cases or claims, and you may add others:

- That the mapping between nucleotide triplets and amino acids appears chemically arbitrary and is not universal: mitochondrial genomes, ciliates, and some other lineages use alternative assignments. This should force a choice about whether arbitrariness is enough for something to be a code or whether the mapping is merely historical contingency.
- That DNA is transcribed and translated only by machinery — polymerases, ribosomes, tRNAs, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases — which the genome itself is widely said to specify, so whatever reads the code is partly among the things the code is supposed to describe.
- That many developmental outcomes do not depend on DNA alone; maternal factors, epigenetic marks, and environmental inputs make the phenotypic effect of a given sequence context-sensitive, which presses on the idea that a sequence carries a single determinate instruction.

At each leaf, give the position's name and exactly one sentence stating the most awkward commitment the view cannot avoid. If the tree cannot cleanly separate two leaves — a literal-code view grounded in semantic content and a teleosemantic view grounded in selected function may converge on every diagnostic question, for example — say so explicitly and judge whether the blur is a defect in the tree or a real ambiguity in the debate. Aim for about eight diagnostic questions, and take the six views above as the leaves; if some combination of answers leads to a position no one defends, label it as empty and diagnose it briefly rather than inventing a seventh leaf.

Please write the handout in American English.
