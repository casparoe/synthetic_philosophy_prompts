Take the superior-orders defense at the Nuremberg trials as a test case for Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities (PAP). I know the rough history, so dont spend too long on it: after the war the Allied tribunal tried senior Nazi officials, many of them said they were only following orders and would have faced severe consequences — maybe death — if they had refused, and the tribunal refused to let that count as a full defense, though it could mitigate. What I want is the slow, careful mapping, in both directions.

PAP says a person is morally responsible for what he did only if he could have done otherwise. Frankfurt's counterexamples are meant to show that's false: a counterfactual intervener is standing by, ready to force the agent's decision if the agent shows signs of deciding differently, but the agent decides on his own anyway, and we still blame him. First, run the Nuremberg defendant through each one. Under PAP, what exactly would the defendant need to establish for the superior-orders defense to succeed, and what would the prosecution need to establish to defeat it? In particular, what does the enormous cost of refusing do to the 'could have done otherwise' test — is the requirement literal alternatives, or does severe duress count as a kind of inability? Then switch to the Frankfurt picture: once alternative possibilities are dropped, which facts about the actual sequence of intention and action are doing the responsibility work, and what happens to the fact that the regime would have forced or killed him if he had balked? Does that fact stop mattering, or does it matter in a new way?

Then the part I actually care about. In the standard Frankfurt setup the agent is unaware of the intervener, or at least is not acting because of him. A Nuremberg defendant is acting because of the standing threat — that's the whole point of the defense. Is that a real disanalogy, meaning Frankfurt cases simply are not models of real coercion, or does the superior-orders defense fail anyway, just for a different reason than PAP would give? And finally, look at what the tribunal actually did with that defense: does it look like they were groping toward something Frankfurt-ish without knowing it, or does it show that our actual practices of blaming still lean on alternative possibilities in a way the counterexamples cant reach? Be honest about where the Frankfurt analysis of the episode breaks down or goes silent.
