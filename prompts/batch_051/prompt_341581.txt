I am trying to decide whether to accept a job offer, and I keep going back and forth about how much further searching is rational caution rather than avoidable delay. The offer in front of me is good enough that I could happily take it, but it is probably not the best position I could get if I kept searching for several more months. A friend who has been mentoring me through the process insists that a genuinely rational decision would lay out every available alternative, trace the likely consequences of each, and compare them all before choosing; he says anything less is deciding on vibes. I have been reading Herbert A. Simon’s Administrative Behavior, and I am tempted to treat the following passage as permission to stop:

“The function of knowledge in the decision-making process is to determine which consequences follow upon which of the alternative strategies. It is the task of knowledge to select from the whole class of possible consequences a more limited subclass, or even (ideally) a single set of consequences correlated with each strategy.”

Is that a fair use of Simon’s point? I want to know whether his account of knowledge and decision actually supports accepting a satisfactory alternative before an exhaustive comparison is complete, or whether I am just borrowing respectable language to rationalize ending an unpleasant search. What would a defensible satisficing procedure look like in my situation, and what would distinguish it from self-deception?
