I'm a novelist, and I'm drafting a little utopian-social-fiction thing about a town that runs itself on moral arithmetic, so I want to make the mechanism feel real rather than allegorical. The town's charter says anyone who wants to move in gets scored by a rotating admissions comittee of current residents: they write down the applicant's expected lifetime well-being on a 0-to-100 scale, and the applicant is admitted if and only if adding them raises the *total* summed well-being of everyone in the town compared to refusing them, even by a point. The founder, a retired actuary, assumed bigger total meant better. My protagonist serves on this committee and begins to notice it keeps pushing through cheerful but very low-score applicants—a hermit who's mildly content alone, a person with a chronic illness who will need decades of care—because each of them still nudges the sum upward—while they reject high-score applicants who'd lower the total only because they'd pull down the average. Meanwhile a faction wants to amend the charter to the opposite rule: admit only if the new person raises the *average*, so the town gets "better," not just larger. I keep hearing the two sides argue it as a question of whether a bigger town of slightly less happy people matters more than a small one of very happy people, and I don't want my novel to pick a side—I want it to show the machine running.

Treat the charter as a mechanism and walk me through how each version would actually play out over, say, fifty years. Under the total rule, what kinds of people get selected for and what does the population drift toward? How do residents game the fact that they're the ones writing the scores—logrolling on applicants they like, bribes, lying an applicant's score down to keep a rival out or up to get a family member in? What about having children, splitting off into a second town, or courtship aimed at high scorers? Then do the average rule. Where does each rule break first, and can my protagonist patch either one—say, with a minimum-score floor, or a multiplier for children, or anonymous scoring—without turning the town into a completely different institution? I care less about which rule is morally right and more about which one's failure modes are more interesting to live inside.
