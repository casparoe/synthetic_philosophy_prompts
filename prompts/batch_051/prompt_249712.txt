I just came from a long argument with a friend about whether an ant colony can literally be said to have a mind—the Hofstadter figure of Aunt Hillary, where the colony is treated as a single minded thing. My friend's position was that the usual analogy is more than a heuristic: the colony is a brain, the ants are neurons, and the coordinated behavior of the colony constitutes a single mind. But I have been reading Leibniz's Monadology, and this passage keeps troubling me: "There must be simple substances because there are compound substances; for the compound is nothing else than a collection or aggregatum of simple substances." If Leibniz is right that a compound is only an aggregatum of simple substances and that true unity must be sought in what is simple, then how far can the ant-colony-as-brain analogy really carry us? Give me the correspondence table for the analogy—what plays the role of neuron, signal, representation, memory, and perceiver—then identify where the mapping breaks. Specifically, can a colony made up of already composite living ants be the kind of unified subject that a mind would require, or is Leibniz's aggregatum point exactly where the analogy smuggles in a unity it has not earned? Does my friend's literal conclusion survive once the disanalogies are laid out, and is there a better analogy for what Aunt Hillary is supposed to be?
