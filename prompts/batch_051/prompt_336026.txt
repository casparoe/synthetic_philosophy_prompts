I've been teaching a grad seminar on ritual and moral psychology, and one of my students has gotten very attached to a strong reading of the scapegoat mechanism. Her claim, roughly, is that when a community piles its accumulated guilt or free-floating aggression onto a substitute — an animal, a prisoner, an outsider — and then expels or kills it, the guilt is actually transferred to the target and discharged along with it. Not just depicted, not just performed, but moved around like a kind of stuff. I can't tell how much of that is meant as a causal description of something in people's heads and in the group, and how much is just an evocative way of saying that the ritual gives everyone a shared story.

So here's what I'd like you to do. Design the best test you can of the claim that the transfer is real rather than merely narrative. Be concrete: what population, what manipulation, what outcome measures, and what pattern of results would push you toward "yes, something is actually being moved" versus "no, the public performance is doing all the work." Then tell me explicitly what the test would not settle even if it came out exactly as you hoped. I'm thinking here of questions like whether the community's relief is caused by the scapegoat now carrying the guilt, as opposed to being caused by the simple fact that the ceremony is over and everyone has signaled closure; whether a group can be a bearer of guilt in the first place; whether the scapegoat is the genuine object of the bad feeling or just a convinient prop. Give me the residue, not a victory lap.
