Grammatical categories are natural kinds, because they show up again and again across languages and have stable semantic cores. Nouns prototypically pick out objects and verbs prototypically pick out events, so the noun/verb distinction is a universal semantic distinction, not just a convention. This is confirmed by the fact that every language has some way of distinguishing nouns from verbs morphosyntactically. Case is likewise a natural kind: Latin's six cases carve up semantic roles neatly — nominative for agents, accusative for patients, dative for recipients, ablative for source/instrument, and so on — and other languages just realize the same underlying system in different ways. Adpositions are also a natural kind because they express spatial relations, which are universal: in, on, under, and so on are the same categories everywhere, with the same boundaries. So the conventionalist view is too pessimistic; the categories look different only because morphology surfaces differently, but the underlying categories are the same.
