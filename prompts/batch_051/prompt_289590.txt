A friend who works on the anthropology of punishment has been pressing an argument on me that I cannot find a way out of, and I want to know which step is the one to give up.

(1) Scapegoat rituals perform real work. A community under strain selects a substitute — a marginal figure or an animal — imputes its troubles to that figure, then kills or banishes it; afterwards the community is calmer, more unified, and the accumulated guilt has in some sense left with the victim.

(2) The ritual's effectiveness does not depend on whether the substitute actually caused the troubles. What carries the community's blame away is that the community takes the victim to be guilty. The victim functions as a vessel for transferred guilt rather than as a culprit; indeed, a scapegoat is often chosen precisely because it cannot retaliate and the community can discharge its aggression on it safely.

(3) Criminal punishment, in its public and ceremonial aspect, runs the same mechanism. A community whose sense of order has been breached fixes on a figure it takes to be an offender, denounces and penalizes that figure in public, and the community's sense of violated order is repaired. Collective blame is concentrated on a single body and expelled with it.

(4) If the mechanism that does the social work is the same in both cases, and if the ritual case shows the mechanism operates without any requirement of the victim's actual guilt, then what justifies punishment must likewise be the mechanism, not the offender's actual guilt. Actual guilt enters only as a proposition the community must believe in order for the ceremony to function.

(5) It follows that if a community could be brought to believe that a randomly selected innocent person was guilty, and it carried out the ceremony with conviction, the punishment would be justified; the person's innocence would count only as a defect of stage management rather than as an injustice.

(6) That conclusion is intolerable. So somewhere in (1)–(5) a premise is false or an inference does not go through.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1) and (2): the ethnographic picture feels robust, and I do not want a theory of punishment that must deny it. I am less attached to (3) as a claim about all of punishment rather than its ceremonial surface. But I need the actual diagnosis: where does the argument break, and what account of punishment am I left holding once I have priced the rejected step and its replacement?
