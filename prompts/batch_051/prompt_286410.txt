I am a novelist, not a professional philosopher, and I have been drafting a short essay about what my craft owes — and does not owe — to Emmanuel Levinas's claim that ethics, rather than epistemology or ontology, is first philosophy, and that the face of the Other resists being reduced to an object of knowledge. My working notes are below. I would like your comments on them, with four questions in mind: (i) where does my paraphrase of Levinas go wrong or flatten his position, especially the difference between the face as a perceptual datum and the face as an ethical interruption; (ii) is the claim about free indirect style sound, or does it rest on a confusion between textual representation and real ethical relation; (iii) which single move is most exposed to a critic who knows Levinas well, and how should I revise it; and (iv) is the analogy between narratorial omniscience and totalization a genuinely illuminating one, or only a clever false friend?

I am working with the idea that the novel is the art form most at odds with Levinas's ethics and, for that very reason, best placed to register his point. By profession I take other people and assign them motives, histories, and interior states; I make their behavior legible and give it a style. Levinas's critique of totalization — the reduction of the Other to a theme, a set of predicates, something the self can comprehend and thereby consume — looks like a description of exactly what omniscient narration does. A third-person narrator who enters every consciousness is, ethically speaking, a greedy totalizer: nothing in the character is allowed to escape narration, and no silence is left unglossed. Yet my own experience as a writer presses against the obvious moral. The technique that feels Levinasian to me is not the austere minimalism that withholds all interiority; it is free indirect discourse, which keeps the reader unsure whose voice is speaking.

Free indirect style produces a third thing, neither the character's inside nor the narrator's outside, so the character remains slightly opaque, because the sentence has no clear owner. I want to argue that this formal undecidability is an ethical device rather than a merely stylistic one: it enacts the thesis that the Other is not available to be known but only addressed, and it gives the reader a trace of an address the narration cannot complete. The risk, of course, is that I have aestheticized Levinas. He insists the face is not a literary effect but the concrete other who resists my powers and accuses me; a character can be made to starve on the page, but that is a represented starvation and not a hunger that commands me, and a novel cannot answer the Other, only represent an answering. Perhaps the honest conclusion is that fiction succeeds ethically when it acknowledges this impossibility, staging the failure of its own claim to know the Other. I would then end by borrowing Levinas's distinction between the saying and the said: what matters ethically in a sentence is not what it reports about a person but the way it refuses to close over that person.
