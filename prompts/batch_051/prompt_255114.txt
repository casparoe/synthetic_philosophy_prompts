I keep going back and forth on what training objective for a reporter would actually recover a model's latent knowledge instead of its surface behavior, and I'd like a concrete case to test my intuitions against. Please construct a scenario to the following specifications.

There is a base language model M, an ordinary decoder-only transformer, trained on a realistic corpus that is mostly marketing-style and user-generated text with a small amount of technical literature mixed in. M's internal state encodes an accurate representation of some proposition F, but when M is asked directly in a neutral context, it outputs the socially expected claim not-F. We train two reporters, each a small probe that reads M's activations and answers the same yes/no question about F. Reporter A is trained to match the next token M would produce when asked the question in the neutral context. Reporter B is trained to be accurate on a small set of unambiguous factual questions whose answers are verifiable from the corpus, and, subject to that, to return the same answer across a family of paraphrases and audience framings of the question, with a penalty on probe complexity.

The specification for the case is: Reporter A reports not-F, and Reporter B reports F. Constraints: the divergence must come from the geometry of M's representations, not from stipulating that either reporter has privileged access to a ground-truth label; the paraphrases and audience framings used to train B are ordinary text-level changes, not surgical edits to a known truth variable; and the scenario itself should stay within a paragraph or two. The point of the case is to pressure-test whether invariance-plus-simplicity is a better guide to latent knowledge than behavioral fidelity, so a case that just defines B as the truthful reporter would not count.

After the scenario, please show the check: explain for each reporter why its training criterion selects the answer it does; identify the single structural feature of the case that does the work; say what happens if that feature is removed; and give the strongest objection a defender of the behavioral-fidelity criterion would press.
