I'm TAing a philosophy of language course and working through a pile of homework answers on vagueness. Before I write comments on one of them I'd like a second read. Here's the question I set:

Explain how epistemicism (Williamson) and supervaluationism (Fine, Keefe) treat the following two sentences, relative to a standard sorites series for "bald" running from a clear case with 0 hairs to a clear case with 100,000 hairs. Sentence (1): there is some n such that the person with n hairs is bald and the person with n+1 hairs is not bald. Sentence (2): for a borderline case m, the person with m hairs is either bald or not bald. For each view, say whether the sentence comes out true, false, or neither, and why. Keep it to 500 words.

Heres what the student wrote:

Epistemicism says vagueness is a matter of ignorance, not semantic indeterminacy. There really is a sharp cutoff in the series, fixed by our use of "bald," even though we can't tell where it is. So sentence (1) is true on epistemicism: some particular n is the last bald case; we just can't know which. Bivalence holds, so the borderline case m is either bald or not bald, and sentence (2) is true as an instance of excluded middle. The reason we can't know the cutoff is that "know" is itself vague: near the boundary, it's indefinite whether we know, so the ignorance falls out of the vagueness of our epistemic vocabulary.

Supervaluationism is a three-valued logic. A sentence is true if it's true on every admissible precisification of "bald," false if false on every one, and takes a third, indeterminate value when the precisifications disagree. For sentence (1), every precisification draws a line somewhere, so the existential is true on all of them and hence supertrue. So supervaluationism gives the same verdict as epistemicism here, and their really only differ on a side issue about what speakers can know. For sentence (2), the borderline m is bald on some precisifications and not bald on others, so each disjunct takes the third value, and the whole disjunction "either bald or not bald" inherits that third value. So supervaluationism rejects the law of excluded middle for borderline cases, unlike classical logic.
