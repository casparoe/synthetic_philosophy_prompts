Across AI research there is an emerging de facto practice of treating machine-generated commentary on other machines as genuine interpretation: one model scores another's outputs (LLM-as-a-judge), a red-team model probes another model and reports what it found, and automated interpretability systems produce explanations of what a target model's internal components are doing, explanations no human has independently verified. I want you to reconstruct the philosophy of interpretation this practice presupposes. In particular: does it presuppose that interpretation is a third-person, instrumental achievement — producing glosses that predict and control the target's behavior — rather than something requiring the interpreter to share the target's situation or grasp its reasons from the inside? Does it presuppose that a system can be a competent interpreter without itself being able to do or understand what it interprets; and if so, what accounts for the asymmetry between interpreter and target? Most of all, press on the regress. The interpreting model is itself opaque, often as opaque as the model it is meant to illuminate, so the practice can look as though it replaces the problem of understanding a model with the problem of trusting a second model about the first. Is that a genuine bottoming-out — for instance, because interpretation bottoms out in downstream usefulness rather than in any interpreter's understanding — or is the regress merely postponed? Reconstruct the implicit theory carefully, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, note whether its evaluative and explanatory strands rest on the same conception of interpretation, and say what would have to change in the practice if the correct account of interpretation were instead something like: understanding requires being the kind of agent that can be answerable for its glosses, with normative commitments at stake beyond maximizing agreement with a reward signal.
