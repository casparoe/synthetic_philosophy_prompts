I am presenting a paper at a philosophy department colloquium next week and want to be prepared for the question period. The talk defends the following thesis: the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism fails against any version of the view that keeps two distinctions in place — the distinction between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure, and the distinction between the commitment to maximising the good and a particular rule for adding up individual welfare. In outline, my argument is as follows. Rawls's charge is that utilitarianism extends a principle of rational choice for a single life over time to society as a whole, thereby treating distinct persons as if they were one. I maintain that this charge depends on taking the impartial spectator literally rather than as a representational device for impartial ranking, and that, read as intended, the device does not presuppose a real person whose intrapersonal trade-offs are projected onto society. The residual complaint — that unweighted summing can ride roughshod over those who are worst off — is, I argue, an objection to one additive aggregation rule, not to the utilitarian family as such: a strictly concave transformation of individual utility (a prioritarian social welfare function) retains the maximising structure while giving extra weight to the worse off. What remains, I conclude, is either an intramural dispute among consequentialists about distributive weights, or a deontological appeal to agent-centred constraints that requires independent support and no longer counts as an objection to the utilitarian framework as such.

The audience is a philosophy department colloquium of roughly twenty-five people: a senior Rawlsian contract theorist who thinks I am misreading the original position; a Kantian working on the second-personal standpoint who will press the charge that the framework still treats persons as mere means; a welfare economist sympathetic to Harsanyi-style arguments who will say that once I adopt a concave transformation I have stopped being a utilitarian; a population ethicist who will ask whether the concave move remains stable when the size of the population varies; and a group of graduate students, many close to effective altruism, who will defend straightforward sum-ranking and think I have conceded too much to the Rawlsian early in the talk.
