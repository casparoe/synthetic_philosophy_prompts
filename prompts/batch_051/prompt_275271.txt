I want to get them to the point where they can distinguish an event being multi-causal from its being overdetermined, and can say why the difference matters for how we explain.

Don't explain the concept to me; plan how I should teach it. What idea has to be in place before the technical notion of overdetermination even makes sense (I'm guessing something like necessity vs sufficiency, or the "but for" counterfactual test)? What example should carry the whole session, and what does it need to have and lack? I was thinking the classic firing squad, but that might be too clean and ahistorical; if so, what historical case would work better without dragging in too much factual baggage? I want a case where two causes are each plausibly sufficient on their own, not just jointly necessary.

Then walk me through the misconceptions in the order the students will actually hit them: the "that's just many causes" conflation, the idea that if an event was overdetermined then no individual cause mattered, and probably the move of using overdetermination to say "so my pet cause is just as good as any other." Where in the sequence should I head each one off, and with what quick move?

I also need one question I can put on the board at the end that tells me whether they actually got the concept rather than just repeating the word. And tell me what I should deliberately not get into (causal preemption, the metaphysics of events, whatever) so the section doesn't turn into a philosophy seminar.
