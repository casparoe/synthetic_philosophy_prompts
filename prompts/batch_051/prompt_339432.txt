I keep coming back to an argument with a colleague about whether 'disease' is a biological fact or a value-laden category. His line is that once you fix age, sex and species, a condition is a disease just in case some part fails to perform its evolved function, and that this is a purely factual matter. I suspect values enter much earlier: in deciding what the function of a system is, in picking the reference class, and in deciding which mismatches between organism and environment count as defects. I can even imagine two people who agree on every lab value, every piece of anatomy and every bit of natural history, and still disagree about whether someone is diseased. I'd like to stop trading intuitions. What would be the strongest test you can design of the claim that disease judgments are partly evaluative rather than purely descriptive? Be concrete about the design — what cases you'd vary, who you'd ask, what you'd measure — and say what pattern of results would move you in each direction. Then be clear about what the test could not settle, even in principle, about where the boundary of disease actually lies. Please write your answer in British English.
