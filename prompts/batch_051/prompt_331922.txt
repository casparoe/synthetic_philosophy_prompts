I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about South Korea's fertility collapse and the government's pronatalist response. My friend kept insisting that a shrinking population is self-evidently a disaster and that heavy state spending to raise the birth rate is obviously justified; I found myself unable to shake the thought that this assumes, without argument, that a larger, or at least non-shrinking, population is better. We got nowhere, and I want to think through the issue properly.

I would like you to act as a professional moral philosopher specialising in population ethics and the ethics of public policy. Please work through the task carefully, and begin with some brainstorming before you commit to a final analysis.

Analyse South Korea's recent pronatalist turn — the declaration of a demographic emergency, the expansion of childcare subsidies, parental leave, housing support, and campaigns aimed at raising marriage and birth rates — as a case study in population ethics. Treat the episode as a test of the theory, and the theory as an instrument for reading the episode. The fertility rate is far below replacement and among the lowest in the world; take these facts as given and do not spend time on empirical demography.

I want the analysis to run in both directions. First, use population ethics to read the episode:

- Reconstruct the ethical assumptions needed to justify the policy. Does it rest on a total view, an average view, a person-affecting view, or something else? Be precise about who is supposed to benefit, and whether future merely possible people can have moral claims.
- Set out who the affected parties are and what standing each has: existing parents, existing non-parents, children who would be born under the policy, future generations, and the state. Then say what the different theoretical positions imply the state should have done.
- Consider whether the costs of pronatalism — the care and labour-market burdens it places disproportionately on women, and the resources diverted from other ends — are distributed in a way any of the standard views could endorse.
- Say where the theory is silent or indeterminate. In particular, can population ethics rank a larger future population against a smaller one without controversial assumptions, and what does the non-identity problem do to the claim that the policy benefits the very people it brings into existence?

Then run the analysis in the other direction and say what the episode shows about the theories:

- Is South Korea's framing a natural application of one position in population ethics, an anomaly for others, or a sign that the theories do not fully capture what is at stake in real demographic policy?
- Does the episode expose an ambiguity between valuing lives and valuing living standards that the theories resolve, or merely rename?
- If the goal is to avoid the harms of a falling population, immigration is an obvious alternative that changes population without creating new people. What do the different population-ethical views imply about the choice between pronatalism and immigration, and does the South Korean emphasis on births rather than migration encode a moral judgment the theories can explain, or one they must take as given?

Do not force the theory to speak where it has nothing determinate to say. Give me a disciplined mapping — implied axiology, affected parties and their standing, options and their costs — and then an honest account of which features of the episode population ethics explains, which it misdescribes, and which it leaves untouched. Where possible, say what the analysis would have to assume for the episode to look like a confirmation of one of the standard views.
