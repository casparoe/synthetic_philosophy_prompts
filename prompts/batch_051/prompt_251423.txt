I've been convinced more then I expected by the functional-programming case against mutable state: the same expression can mean different things depending on when it's evaluated, two names can point to the same object and interfere through aliasing, and equational reasoning stops being safe because replacing equals by equals fails the moment something can be updated behind your back. The view has real systematic virtues, like compositionality and local reasoning. But I can't shake the intuition that this asks me to pretend the world isn't made of things that change. If I put a letter in an envelope and seal it, the envelope is now in a new state, and if I hand it to you, there's one envelope we're both talking about, not two copies of an envelope-value. That feels like a fact about identity and time, not just an engineering preference.

I don't want to be told which side wins. Walk me through what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there's a reading of 'state' and 'identity' on which I can keep both — objects that genuinely change, and reasoning that stays compositional. What does each option cost, and what would justify each move?
