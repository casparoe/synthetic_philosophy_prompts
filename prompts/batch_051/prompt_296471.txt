I am a novelist building a near-future world that follows from one altered social fact rather than from authorial convenience. The altered fact is this: after a decade of public argument over suffering-focused ethics, a wealthy, stable, mid-sized democracy — call it the Commonwealth — amends its constitution by referendum so that the sole permissible ground for state action becomes the reduction of severe suffering. The amendment is lexical: no gain in happiness, dignity, or liberty may justify any increase in tier-one suffering, while even a small reduction in tier-one suffering may justify large sacrifices of other goods. A Standing Commission on Suffering maintains a national Suffering Register that scores suffering by tier, duration, and certainty; the Register initially covers humans and farmed animals, and a later judicial ruling extends it to wild animals. Every proposed statute and regulation must be accompanied by a Suffering Impact Statement, and courts may strike down any measure on the basis of the Register alone.

I want this projected forward over roughly forty years, but as social theory rather than narrative: a chain of reasoning about mechanisms, incentives, and constraints. Reason through how the Register gets gamed and captured; what happens to palliative and psychiatric medicine, to agriculture and meat production, and to public goods whose value is almost entirely the creation of positive experience; how the wild-animal ruling interacts with conservation and with the idea of intervening in nature; how courts handle the antinatalist and end-of-life implications of the asymmetry; and how neighboring states, capital, and labor markets respond. For each major prediction, say whether it follows robustly from the incentives or is only plausible, identify which choices by the framers do the real causal work, and state the assumptions whose failure would falsify the projection. End by naming the two or three points where the world is most likely to reverse itself, and why.
