For a democratic theory seminar I have been working on the boundary problem — the question of who ought to be included when a polity draws its demos. I would like an analysis of a rule I am considering, treated as a mechanism rather than as a proposal to endorse. The rule: for every decision a polity takes by vote, any person anywhere may register to vote on that single decision by attesting that the decision would affect their interests above a materiality threshold, set at a ten per cent change in expected real income; a standing panel screens registrations only for bare plausibility; and those admitted are struck from the roll once that decision has been voted on. Walk through how this would actually behave. What would it incentivise among ordinary voters, organised interests, the politicians who draft the questions put to a vote, and the panel members themselves? Where is it gamed first — for instance, by splitting or bundling decisions to move people just below or above the threshold, by strategic attestation at scale, or by a polity tailoring its agenda so that its permanent demos remains manageable? What does it do to the connection between representation and a stable electorate, and which of its failure modes could be fixed without gutting the mechanism?
