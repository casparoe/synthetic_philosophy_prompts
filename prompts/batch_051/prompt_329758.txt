Just got back from a long argument with a friend about the problem of old evidence, and he walked me into this argument I cant get out of. He said:

(1) Bayesian confirmation says evidence E confirms hypothesis H just in case P(H|E) > P(H).
(2) If E is old evidence, then E is already known, so P(E)=1 for the relevant agent.
(3) But if P(E)=1, Bayes's rule gives P(H|E)=P(H), so old evidence can never confirm anything.
(4) The anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion was known for decades before Einstein's general relativity predicted or explained it, and clearly it counts as one of the major confirmations of the theory.
(5) So Bayesian confirmation theory can't make sense of a central kind of scientific evidence.
(6) Therefore Bayesian confirmation theory is either false or incomplete in a way that isn't a small technical fix.

I want to say something like: the mistake is that "known" isn't an absolute starting point, because you can ask what the evidence would have supported relative to a background that didn't already include it. But then I don't know how to state that without making the Mercury evidence merely a hypothetical, and my friend says that's just abandoning the claim that the actual history of science provided real confirmation.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which premise should I give up, and what does the fix cost? Also, please separate what is settled among people who work on Bayesian confirmation from what is genuinely contested, because my friend pitched this as if all Bayesians agree the problem is fatal, and I doubt that's right.
