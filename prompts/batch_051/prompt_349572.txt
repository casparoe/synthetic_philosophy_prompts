I'm working on a novel and I want the central argument in it to actually hold together, not just be intellectual set dressing. The protagonist works at a longtermist foundation — the kind of outfit that will happily spend serious money on an intervention with, say, a one-in-ten-thousand chance of nudging the long-run trajectory of intelligent life, on the grounds that there are so many possible future lives that even a tiny probability swamps the cost. She's completely fluent in that kind of expected-value reasoning. But in one scene a colleague brings up the simulation argument, the idea that if advanced civilizations run ancestor simulations then the odds we're in one are overwhelming. And she treats it as a fun late-night idea that doesn't change anything about her decisions. The colleague calls her on it: by her own style of reasoning, shouldn't a substantial probability that the future is being hosted, or has already been settled by beings whose interests aren't ours, matter enormously for which actions are worth taking? She doesnt have an answer, just an instinct.

So the asymmetry I want to pin down: why is it rational to let tiny probabilities of influencing astronomical numbers of future people drive present choices, while it's also rational to treat a comparable — maybe much larger — probability that the whole future is already under someone else's control as a curiosity that changes nothing? Is there a principle that generates both attitudes, or is she just letting radical conclusions in when they flatter her and keeping them out when they're inconvenient? Give me the strongest possible defense of her instinct, test it against nearby cases (say, a lottery that's explicitly run by a host, or a future that's already fixed by someone else's plan), and then give me an honest verdict. If the asymmetry doesn't survive, tell me what she should actually think — I'd rather write her as consciously wrong than accidentally incoherent.
