I am the chief of staff in a large American city's mayor's office. Roughly once a week, a department head or a member of the mayor's staff brings forward a proposal that would ordinarily violate a moral rule we accept — misleading the public about a health risk, breaking a public commitment, placing covert surveillance on a community without a warrant, concealing a budget concession until after an election — on the ground that some pressing public good requires it. The office currently decides these case by case, under time pressure, with no shared framework, usually by whoever happens to be in the room. I want that replaced with a procedure my staff can run in under 48 hours, even though none of them has philosophical training, with the mayor signing off only on proposals that clear the early stages.

By "the problem of dirty hands" I mean the line of thought running from Machiavelli through Weber to Walzer: political life sometimes requires a responsible officeholder to do something that is simultaneously the best thing to do, all things considered, and a violation of a moral rule that would otherwise bind anyone; the wrong is not canceled by the justification; and part of what marks the justified offender is that they do not launder the act as clean — they acknowledge the wrong and accept its residue and its follow-on duties. I want this view operationalized, not a pure cost–benefit screen and not a clean-hands rule that refuses every such proposal.

Write me a decision procedure in the form of an ordered checklist: the questions to ask in order, the threshold each answer must meet, where the procedure stops with a "no," where it hands the decision to the mayor as an irreducible value judgment, and where it imposes follow-on duties such as disclosure, accepting responsibility, or a post-decision review. Built into the checklist, I need these distinctions preserved rather than smoothed away: a hard tradeoff with regrets is not the same as a justified wrong that remains a wrong; necessity is a demanding test, not a label officeholders apply to their preferred course; and the residue of having done wrong cannot be converted into another unit of cost and weighed away. Mark explicitly the steps where judgment cannot be eliminated and the places where the procedure is most likely to misfire.

Then run the procedure on three cases, tracing the path each takes and stating its verdict and any residual obligations: (1) the police commissioner wants to place a covert listening device in a mosque community center without a warrant, on a credible but not yet sufficient lead that an attack is being coordinated there; (2) the public health director wants to publicly downplay how easily an emerging disease spreads for about a week to prevent hoarding and hospital overcrowding while surge capacity is expanded; (3) the mayor's own office wants to quietly approve a tax abatement and rezoning negotiated with a large employer to keep it from relocating, reversing a campaign promise, and to conceal the concession from the council and voters until after the next election. Do not soften the outcome if one of these cases turns out badly for my office.

Respond in American English. Keep the entire response, procedure and all three cases included, to no more than 750 words. Number the checklist items, and set each worked case under a heading that names the proposal.
