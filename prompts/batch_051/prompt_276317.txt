I've been kicking around a term I want to use in a piece, and I'd like it stress-tested before I put it in print. I call it 'borrowed ergodicity.' Ergodicity economics makes the point that for a repeated multiplicative gamble, the number that matters are the time average of one person's path, not the ensemble average across many people at once: the ensemble average can be positive while nearly every individual path goes to ruin. But the decisions I cover tend to be one-shot — one marriage, one migration, one startup. So I've been using 'borrowed ergodicity' for the move of justifying a single-shot choice with time-average logic that only applies if the choice is rerun many times. Example: a friend won't take a one-off coin flip that pays +50% on heads and costs 40% on tails, because 'over many repeats the geometric mean is negative.' He's right about the repeats, but there are no repeats, so he's borrowed an average from a process he won't live. Then I caught myself doing the mirror-image thing: citing a population-wide average to nudge an individual toward a one-time health decision, which borrows the ensemble instead. My question: is this a real distinction or am I just relabeling single-stage vs multi-stage decisions, or expected value vs expected utility? Where does it break — is 'one-shot' even a coherent category when a life is a long run of small gambles?
