I keep returning to an argument that seems to prove that Latin and Italian are the same language, and I cannot see which step to give up. I want the diagnosis to be more than hand-waving about vagueness.

Take the chain of speech communities running continuously from first-century BCE central Italy to present-day Italy: at each stage, a generation of children natively acquired the speech of the previous generation, and over the centuries that speech changed into what is now called Italian.

(1) The language at the start of the chain is Latin, and the language at the end is Italian, and these are distinct languages. The two endpoints are mutually unintelligible and their grammatical systems are systematically different: the earlier system has a case system and no obligatory articles, the later system has no nominal case inflection and obligatory articles, and so on.

(2) For every adjacent pair of generations in the chain, the earlier and the later generation spoke the same language. There was no first generation of Italian speakers: each generation differed from its predecessor only in the way contemporaneous neighbors in a single speech community differ from one another, and no community in the chain switched codes.

(3) Sameness of language is identity, and identity is transitive: if the speech of generation A and the speech of generation B are the same language, and the speech of generation B and the speech of generation C are the same language, then the speech of generation A and the speech of generation C are the same language.

(4) By chaining (2) and (3) along the lineage, the language at the start is the same language as the language at the end. That is, Latin is Italian.

(5) From (1) and (4), Latin both is and is not Italian.

I have priors I do not want to give up easily. I do not want to reject (2) by positing a hidden sharp cut somewhere in the chain, since that seems empirically false. I do not want to reject (1), since it seems to amount to denying that historical linguistics ever distinguishes one language from another. And I do not want to reject (3), since that appears to be tampering with identity itself. Yet one of these, or some step in the move to (4) or (5), must fail.

Which step gives, and why? In your answer, spell out what a language must be — a historical continuant, a synchronic system, a social practice, a cluster kind — for that rejection to be principled rather than ad hoc, and price the revision to ordinary and technical talk about languages. If the right conclusion is that "when did Latin become Italian?" has no determinate answer, explain what that indeterminacy does and does not imply for the reality of the categories "Latin" and "Italian" and for periodization generally.
