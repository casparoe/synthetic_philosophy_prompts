I keep going back and forth on a bunch of Mary's-room variants and I want to know if one principle captures my verdicts, and which of them it forces me to give up. Classic Mary — black-and-white room, all the physical and functional facts about color vision — learns something when she first sees red: she learns what it's like. I want to say that's genuinely new knowledge, not just a new ability, because afterwards she can sit in the dark and think 'so that's what I was missing,' and that thought is newly true for her. A man born blind who knows all the same facts and later gains sight is the same case to me: he learns what red is like. But a wavelength detector with all those facts stored in it doesn't learn anything the first time red light hits it; it just shifts into a new state. And a normally sighted person who learns, for the first time, the whole photochemistry of why a tomato looks red does learn a new fact, but not a new what-it's-like — so I don't want to just cash the whole thing out as 'learning a fact.' Meanwhile I dont think that zapping Mary's cortex from inside the room, even if it induced the very brain state that actually seeing red would induce, would count as her learning what red is like; the input has to come in through the eyes the normal way. Is there one principle behind all of these? Which verdict does it make me drop? And what would it say about someone who has seen red only in paintings and then sees a ripe tomato in direct sunlight for the first time — do they learn something new, or not?
