I'm the token philosopher of biology down the hall, and the biology department has me giving a seminar next month. The room will be mostly organismal biologists, a few microbiome people, one or two multilevel-selection people, and exactly no philosophers, so they'll read me as the humanities person telling them their core concept isn't as hard-edged as they'd like. My talk is tentatively called "The holobiont is not an organism, and thats fine." I'm going to grant all the empirical stuff up front: the bobtail squid's light organ doesn't develop without Vibrio fischeri, aphids depend on Buchnera for essential amino acids, some termites depend on their gut protists, and I'll wave at leafcutter ant colonies and the Portuguese man o' war as nearby cases. My argument is that the standard candidate criteria for being an organism—metabolic integration, co-development, shared environment, co-transmission, shared fitness—pull apart in these cases, and none of them tracks the boundaries biologists actually reach for. So "organism" is a pragmatic category, not a natural kind; holobionts and superorganisms are real, but real relative to the question you're asking. I expect pushback about units of selection, about whether I'm smuggling in a single-genome prejudice, and about immune systems as the line between self and nonself. Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way those biologists would actually phrase them, each with a short reply and a note on where that reply is soft, what to concede, what to hold, and the weakest point in my own position I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. And since I want to hand chunks of this to my grad students afterwards, separate what's settled empirical fact from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this—dont present a contested take as the consensus.
