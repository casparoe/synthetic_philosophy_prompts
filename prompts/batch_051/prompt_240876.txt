I teach a small upper-level seminar called Democracy, Choice, and Fairness — 14 students, mostly philosophy and econ majors, one 80-minute session. We've done Arrow, and next up is the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, but I don't want to open with the theorem. I want the students to live through the problem that motivates it: any decision rule that accepts arbitrary individual rankings over three or more alternatives, always respects what the group unaminously wants, and dosen't hand the decision to a single person will give somebody, in some profile, an incentive to misreport their ranking. And this is a structural fact, not a bug in whichever nice rule we happened to choose. I'd like an activity where they first vote under a rule that feels fair, then some of them notice they can get a better outcome by misrepresenting their preferences, then the class tries to patch the rule and keeps failing — every patch either turns out to be manipulable in some new case or forces them to give up something they cared about, like letting one person's ballot decide everything or only ever deciding between two options. They should feel the pull of gaming the rule before anyone names it, and the debrief should map the moments in the game onto the conditions of the theorem. No lecture up front, and ideally nobody says the words Gibbard–Satterthwaite or strategy-proof until the debrief.

Please commit to one concrete design rather than offering a menu: exact number of rounds, alternatives, ballots, assigned preferences or payoffs, roles, timings, and debrief questions. If it helps, the running case can be the seminar itself deciding how to spend a fixed activities budget among three things some of them actually disagree about — say a guest speaker, a day trip, and a fund for a class journal — but pick a sharper example if it makes the manipulation easier to see. Structure the response in these sections, in order, with rough lengths: (1) Setup and materials, around 150 words; (2) The activity as participants experience it, around 400; (3) Facilitator moves and timeline, around 250; (4) Debrief, around 400; (5) What predictably goes wrong and how to handle it, around 200; (6) What the activity can't reproduce and the teacher must supply, around 150.
