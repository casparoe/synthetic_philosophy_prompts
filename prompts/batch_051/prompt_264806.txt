I am a novelist rather than a philosopher. My current manuscript turns on a civil suit in which the central dispute is whether an omission — a neighbor's failure to water a garden during a heat wave — can properly be said to have caused a death. A friend who read the draft warned me that all my characters sound like the same mildly confused person whenever the topic of causation comes up. I want to fix that by giving each character a distinct, genuinely defensible theory of what causation is, and I want none of them to be a caricature. I also do not trust myself to label the positions correctly, because the relevant distinction — roughly, between accounts that analyze causation through counterfactual dependence and accounts that treat it as the product of possible interventions — keeps slipping away from me when it is stated abstractly.

Please act as a professional philosopher who works on causation and who is used to explaining these debates to a careful lay audience. Begin by brainstorming, but the final deliverable should be a sorting quiz under the working title "Which Theory of Causation Do You Actually Hold?" It should have seven to nine questions, each centered on a concrete vignette, and each with two or three plain-language answer options that no answerer would recognize as a position label. The questions must cover, though not necessarily in this order: a brick thrown through a window as a baseline; a victim who suffers two simultaneous, independently sufficient injuries, such as two arsonists lighting separate fires; a bullet that arrives before a heart attack would have killed the victim; a neighbor's failure to water a plant; and an act that prevents something which would itself have prevented the outcome, for example someone draining water that would have put out a burning fuse. You may add one or two vignettes of your own if some distinction would otherwise remain untested.

Then give a scoring key that maps each answer pattern to one of at least five positions: a counterfactual or difference-making account; an interventionist or manipulationist account; a mechanistic, production, or process account; a regularity account in the INUS sense (an insufficient but non-redundant part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition); and a primitivist, contextualist, or otherwise deflationary position. Build the key from the logical space of answer combinations first, and then locate the named positions in that space; if a combination is unoccupied, say briefly whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two positions cannot be separated by the questions as written, flag that explicitly instead of inventing a difference. The quiz should sort respondents by how they actually judge the vignettes, not by which label they like, so no question should ask whether they find counterfactuals or interventions more plausible. The intended audience is intelligent readers of literary fiction with no philosophical training, so keep every question free of jargon and do not cite, quote, or name any author or work.
