You are a professional philosopher of science who specializes in confirmation theory and the problem of underdetermination of theory by evidence. Please think through the following argument slowly and carefully; before settling on a diagnosis, consider two or three different places where the reasoning might break, test them against each step, and then give your final analysis.

A colleague presented the following chain of reasoning in a seminar, and I have been unable to find the flaw. Each step seems sound on its own, but the conclusion strikes me as absurd.

(1) For any finite set of data points and any curve that fits them, there are infinitely many other curves that fit the same points perfectly and diverge wildly elsewhere. For example, adding a term c(x−x1)(x−x2)…(x−xn) to the original curve changes nothing at the observed points but can bend the curve arbitrarily between and beyond them.

(2) Scientific theories are in the same situation. A theory is, in effect, a high-dimensional curve fitted to the finite evidence we have so far, and for any such theory there will always be endlessly many rivals that fit all the evidence equally well while making different predictions about cases not yet observed.

(3) If infinitely many rivals fit the evidence equally well, then the evidence itself cannot discriminate among them; judged purely by fit with observation, they tie.

(4) Whatever then breaks the tie — simplicity, elegance, naturalness, prior probability, tradition — is not evidence for the chosen theory. It is a background preference we impose on the data.

(5) Therefore no theory enjoys more evidential support than any grue-like rival that matches everything observed so far and then flips in its predictions for the unobserved cases.

(6) So, for example, our preference for established physics over the hypothesis that established physics holds only until next Thursday, after which the gravitational constant triples, is not grounded in the evidence at all.

(7) But that cannot be right. If any empirical preference is rationally grounded, that one is.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep (1) and (7); beyond that I am unsure what I can safely give up. Please identify the exact step or transition that fails, explain why it looks innocuous, and tell me what I am committed to if I reject it.
