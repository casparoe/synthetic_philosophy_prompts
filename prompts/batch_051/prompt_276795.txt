A popular history of the philosophy of mathematics I have been reading summarizes Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations roughly as follows. Lakatos set out to show that mathematics is a quasi-empirical discipline: it grows through conjectures, proofs, and counterexamples rather than through deduction from fixed axioms. The book is cast as a classroom dialogue about the Eulerian formula V - E + F = 2. A teacher and students produce a proof, then construct an awkward polyhedron — hollow, multiply connected, or otherwise irregular — on which the formula gives a different value, and the theorem is revised to exclude that sort of object. According to the article, Lakatos's conclusion is that mathematical conjectures are tested against particular cases and abandoned or revised when a counterexample falsifies them, much as a scientific theory is set aside when the data turn against it. That, the author says, is what Lakatos meant by calling mathematics quasi-empirical: a theorem is secure only so long as no counterexample has surfaced, so our confidence rests on a kind of induction over the cases examined, not on proof.

I had remembered the argument differently — as having something to do with tactics I think he names monster-barring, exception-barring, and lemma-incorporation, and with the idea that counterexamples do not refute a good proof so much as force the proof's concepts to be stretched.
