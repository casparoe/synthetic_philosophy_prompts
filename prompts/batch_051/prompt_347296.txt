Question: Explain tranquilism — the view that only the absence of craving and suffering matters — and evaluate the objection that it cannot accommodate irreducibly positive goods such as love, understanding, and aesthetic experience.

Student response: Tranquilism claims that the only thing good for its own sake is the absence of craving and suffering; anything else can be good only as a means to that absence. For example, a loving relationship is good only insofar as it removes the longing for intimacy, and understanding is good only insofar as it removes the discomfort of confusion. This leads directly to a criterion of right action: one should act so as to minimize the total amount of craving and suffering. Tranquilism is therefore a form of negative utilitarianism — the view that the right act minimizes total suffering — except that it adds the elimination of craving to the elimination of pain. Given this, positive goods turn out to have no value at all, so the objection that love or understanding is valuable for its own sake cannot get off the ground.
