I'm a PhD student in the history of programming languages, and I keep getting stuck on a passage from Dijkstra's talk "On the Cruelty of Really Teaching Computing Science." He is arguing that computers are a radical novelty, and that when you meet a radical novelty, stretching old concepts and analogies over it just gives you misleading metaphors. Then he writes: "Coming to grips with a radical novelty amounts to creating and learning a new foreign language that can not be translated into one's mother tongue." I always read this as the strong version of the claim that programming languages matter: a formal notation is not just a tool for instructing a machine, it is a new conceptual language a person has to learn, and learning it changes what they can think.

Now I'm sitting in a lab where the coding agents write most of the code from English prompts and the PhD students mostly read diffs and summaries; quite a few never open the underlying source files at all. If that becomes normal, does Dijkstra's argument survive? I can see three reads: (a) the argument still goes through, but the "new foreign language" has moved one level down into the model, so it is the model that gets the cognitive benefit of formalization and the human gets left with the mother tounge; (b) the argument becomes a reason to force humans to stay in the loop at the level of code, not just prompts; or (c) natural-language prompting has itself become a new formal language with its own untranslatable idioms, and "foreign language" was never really about formality. I care less about predicting coding jobs than about whether the premise — that coping with this particular novelty requires a learned formal notation — still has teeth.

Please say how confident you are in what you end up claiming, and single out the point you are least sure of.
