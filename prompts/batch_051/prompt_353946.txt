I am a PhD student in philosophy working at the intersection of aesthetics and emotion theory, and I have drafted a short essay on the paradox of tragedy. I would appreciate comments on the draft, particularly on the distinction I draw in the third paragraph and on whether my criticism of conversion-style views is fair. I have included the draft below, followed by a few specific questions. I am less interested in whether you agree with the position than in whether the argument hangs together and whether the two questions I separate really need different answers.

Draft

In discussions of tragic pleasure, two questions are often run together. The first is motivational: why do people seek out and sit through works that represent suffering, death, and injustice, when they avoid such things in real life? The second is experiential: why do they describe the experience of such works as pleasant or satisfying, even while reporting genuine sadness, pity, or horror? I suspect these are not the same question, and an answer to one need not answer the other.

Consider a person who rereads a novel with a disastrous ending. We can explain the rereading by appeal to curiosity, reacquaintance with the language, or a liking for the formal construction. None of that yet explains why the overall experience feels, to the reader, good. The work might have been pursued for reasons other than felt pleasure and still be felt as pleasant. So the first question can be answered by non-hedonic motives. The second cannot.

I propose to distinguish two layers of affective response. First-order responses are the emotions directed at the represented events: fear for the protagonist, grief at the death. Second-order responses are responses to one's own first-order states: for example, satisfaction that one's fear is proportional to what the work invites, or pleasure in the precision with which the work elicits the response. The pleasure of tragedy, I suggest, belongs primarily at the second order. It is not the sadness that pleases, nor even a transformed sadness; it is the recognition that one has responded fittingly, and that the work has produced that response with economy.

This lets me object to conversion accounts in their traditional form. Hume, in his essay Of Tragedy, argued roughly that the painful passions excited by a tragic scene are converted into pleasure by the predominant force of beauty and eloquence in the representation. But if the second-order view is right, the pleasure does not need to be produced by converting the painful passion. The painful passion remains painful; what pleases is a different state, directed at the pain as well fitted to its object. The two states can coexist without one having to dominate or transform the other.

A further advantage is that the second-order view can explain why badly made tragedy does not please even when it is equally saddening. It is not that the sadness is unsuccessfully converted; it is that the sadness is not felt as apt. The grief is still grief; it simply earns no commendation.

Questions I would particularly value comments on:

1. Is the separation of the motivational question from the experiential question already present in the literature, and if so, where does my formulation differ?
2. Is my reading of the conversion metaphor fair to Hume's actual mechanism, or does his appeal to eloquence already anticipate the second-order point?
3. Does the second-order view over-intellectualize the experience, given that viewers may not report any thought about the fittingness of their emotions?
4. What would be the strongest counterexample from tragedy, perhaps a case where the first-order pain itself seems to be the locus of the pleasure?
