I have been collecting my verdicts about broken small commitments — promises to show up, to meet at a set time, to do a small favor — and I want to know whether any one principle lies behind them. Here they are.

(1) I promise my aunt I'll go to my cousin's birthday dinner and then skip it when a more attractive invitation arrives. I'm sure that's wrong, and not trivial; it seems like a betrayal of trust.

(2) I agree to meet a friend at 3:00 and arrive at 3:15 because I was scrolling on my phone and left late. I think I did something wrong, though small; the wrong is that I treated the friend's time as not worth planning around.

(3) Same lateness, but the bus was delayed. I don't think I did anything wrong, yet I still owe an apology for the time the friend spent waiting.

(4) I promise to water a neighbor's plants for a week, forget one day, and nothing dies; the neighbor never finds out. I still think I did something wrong, even though nobody was harmed.

(5) I tell an acquaintance "we should hang out sometime" and never follow up. I don't think that's a broken promise at all; the phrase doesn't really commit.

(6) I tell a classmate "I'll text you about the homework tonight" and then simply forget. I think this is wrong but very mild; it's more carelessness than a serious letdown.

(7) I promise a friend I'll come to their low-stakes club meeting, then take a last-minute movie invitation instead. The friend later says they didn't even notice my absence. I still feel I did something wrong.

(8) The case I keep going back and forth on: I promise my younger brother I'll come to his Saturday soccer game, but on Friday a teacher offers me a one-time slot to practice for a college interview. I think I should take the interview, but I don't know whether I owe my brother more than an apology, or whether the promise was even binding once this opportunity appeared.

Is there a single principle that delivers all of these verdicts, or close to it? My tentative candidate is that the wrong of breaking a small commitment depends on whether the other person reasonably relied on me and was let down — but (4) and (7) seem to pull against that. Please state the best principle you can find, say which cases motivate each part of it, apply it to a further case you haven't seen me list, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
