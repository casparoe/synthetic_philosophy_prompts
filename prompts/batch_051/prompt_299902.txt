I am presenting a paper next month to a joint history and philosophy workshop on historical moral evaluation. My thesis is that the 'man of his time' defence should be reconstructed not as a general exemption from judgment but as a two-part excusing condition, and that once reconstructed this way it is far less exculpatory than historians often assume. In my formulation, a historical figure is excused for an act that we now condemn only if (1) the agent's social-epistemic environment made the wrongness of the act non-salient or failed to make a feasible alternative salient, and (2) no available source of dissent within that environment, of the kind the agent could reasonably be expected to have encountered and understood, put the agent on notice. I argue that Thomas Jefferson fails this test because he lived among contemporaries who condemned slavery, including some he read and corresponded with, and because his own writings show awareness of the tension between slavery and the principles he endorsed. Christopher Columbus fails for a similar structural reason: the treatment of the indigenous people of the Caribbean was contested in his own time, including by people within his own voyages, and he had access to legal and theological frameworks that made the alternative intelligible. Winston Churchill is the mixed case in my paper: his views on empire and race were conventional for his class, but there were also live critics within his own political world, and the question is whether the second condition is too demanding if we require him to have taken seriously critics he had reason to dismiss.

The workshop will have two dominant audiences. The first is intellectual historians who think retrospective moral judgment is anachronistic and that we should understand historical actors in their own terms rather than assess them by ours. The second is analytic moral philosophers who think moral responsibility is time-indexed but who also believe that the possession of a live alternative is the central excusing condition. I am worried the historians will reject the very idea of a test for excusing conditions, and the philosophers will accuse me of smuggling in a presentist standard through the notion of what an agent 'could reasonably be expected to encounter and understand'.
