I've been trying to work out whether I actually think there's a moral difference between lying and merely misleading someone, and my gut verdicts on cases keep refusing to line up. Here's where I've ended up, case by case.

- A stranger at the door asks where my flatmate is. I think he means to hurt him. I say "he's not here." Fine.
- Same stranger, same question, but I say "he said he'd be out all afternoon" (true at lunchtime, but he's currently hiding in the loft). Also fine, though I'd feel slightly more uneasy about this one than about the straight lie, which surprises me.
- My gran asks if I like the scarf she's knitted me. "It's lovely." Fine.
- Same gran, same scarf, but I say "I've been after a mustard-yellow scarf for months" (true, though not for the reasons she'd assume). Fine, maybe a touch less fine than the lie — again, the opposite of what I'd have predicted.
- A job interviewer asks why I left my last role. "I left voluntarily" (false; I was sacked). Not fine.
- A different interviewer asks whether I was ever put on a performance plan. "I passed every performance review I had" (true; six passed reviews, and I was still sacked weeks later). Not fine. But less bad than the outright lie, and I couldnt tell you why.
- A friend asks what I think of their novel manuscript. "It's brilliant" (I think it's unreadable). Not fine.
- The same friend asks whether they should quit their job to write full-time. "I never tell people to quit their jobs" (true as a policy, though I'd have broken it for a book I believed in). Fine — even though it leaves them just as deceived.
- A former employee asks me for a reference. I write "she was never once late in four years" (true; she was also hopeless). Not fine, even though every word is true. It feels like lying by omission, if that's a thing.
- My cousin asks if I'm seeing anyone seriously. "I'm seeing someone from work" (true; we've lived together for two years). Not fine.
- Poker. I bluff along with everyone else. Fine.
- Under oath: "did you meet Mr X on the 14th?" I answer "I was in Glasgow on the 14th" (true, and I did meet him — in Glasgow). Not fine. I'd call that perjury in all but letter.

The one I keep flipping on: I'm a manager, and at a town hall an employee asks whether their team's jobs are safe. I know redundancies are coming next financial year. I say "there are no redundancies planned for this quarter" — true, but I know what they're really asking. Half the time I think that's clearly wrong; the other half I think it's just careful under-promising.

Is there a principle — one principle, or a small set — that gets all of these, or nearly all? Does it treat lying and misleading as morally on a par, or does it leave room for the felt difference, and is that difference just about how much we trust someone's words? What does the principle say about the town hall case, and which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? Give the principle precisely, with the case or clause in it that each of my judgements is pushing you towards, and don't quietly drop the awkward ones — say which of my verdicts have to go if they have to. Please write your answer in British English.
