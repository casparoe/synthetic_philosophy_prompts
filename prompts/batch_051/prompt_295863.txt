The following is my own attempt to defend a practice I encounter constantly in climate impacts work: treating the spread of a multi-model ensemble as a probability distribution over future change. I find the argument persuasive, but I half-suspect it cheats somewhere, and I cannot identify the step at which it goes wrong—if it goes wrong at all. Please evaluate it step by step.

(1) Each model in the ensemble has passed the basic test of reproducing the observed 20th-century global-mean surface temperature record within the range of observational uncertainty.
(2) For a target quantity such as equilibrium climate sensitivity—the long-term global-mean warming that eventually results from a doubling of CO2—the models in the ensemble produce a spread of values.
(3) We have no principled, target-specific reason to trust any one of these models more than any other.
(4) If we have no principled reason to trust one model more than another, then our uncertainty about the target quantity is properly represented by treating the models' outputs as equally weighted samples from the space of plausible climates.
(5) Therefore, the spread of the ensemble's outputs is a defensible estimate of our uncertainty about the target quantity.
(6) Therefore, decision-makers should treat the ensemble distribution—its central tendency and its spread—as the decision-relevant uncertainty, for instance by planning around the interval between the ensemble's low and high values.

My suspicion is that the trouble is around (3) and (4), but I cannot tell whether (3) is simply false, whether (4) makes an illicit move from an inability to rank the models to a requirement to weight them equally, or whether the argument is valid as far as it goes and is only misused downstream. Please state whether the argument is valid as written and, if it is not, identify the first step at which it fails. Then give the correct version of that step, and say what the nearest true claim is. In particular, explain what an ensemble would have to satisfy—concerning the independence of its members, their shared assumptions, and how they were constructed—for the move from an inability to rank the models to equal weighting to be sound.
