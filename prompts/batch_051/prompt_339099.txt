I'm part of a small team building a bank's fraud-detection system, and we have to choose between two versions of the same model. Version A is right 96% of the time on our held-out data, but no one can say, in any particular case, what drove its decision; the best we can do is point to a weighted combination of several hundred weak signals. Version B is right 91% of the time and always emits a short plain-language reason for each decision — for example, "the account's activity pattern changed suddenly after a long dormant period, and the recipient bank is in a high-risk jurisdiction." The bank acts on these flags, freezing accounts and filing reports, and customers sometimes ask why. My co-founder wants to ship Version A. His argument is that the bank needs the most accurate decision available, not the most agreeable one, and that my preference for explanations is aesthetic: a plausible-sounding reason attached to a wrong answer is no better than a wrong answer, and neither explanation nor understanding changes whether any given transaction was actually fraud. I feel he is missing something, but I keep failing to say what it is.

There is also a complication. When we tried to close the accuracy gap by training Version B on more data, its accuracy rose to about 93.5%, but we discovered that its plain-language reasons no longer tracked what the model actually responded to. Changing a feature mentioned in the reason often left the decision unchanged, while changing a feature the reason never mentioned could flip it. The reasons still read well; they just do not correspond to what is doing the work. My co-founder says this settles it: the reasons are cosmetic, so sacrificing two points of accuracy for them is irrational. I find myself retreating to the line that we should be able to understand our own system, and he replies that "understandable" is doing no real work beyond "soothing."

Help me figure out what, if anything, is wrong with his position, and what I should actually do.
