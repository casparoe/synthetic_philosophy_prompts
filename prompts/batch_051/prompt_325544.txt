I got into an argument with a friend about Wang Yangming's idea that knowledge and action are one. The way I've seen it explained is that you don't really know something is right unless you actually do it: if you know you should help someone and then walk past them, you didn't truly know the thing you thought you knew. I like that way of putting it, but the more I think about it the more it seems to lead to two weird places.

First, blame. If I fail to do the good thing, Wang's view seems to say I never really knew it was good in the first place. But we normaly let people off the hook when they act out of ignorance. So doesn't his doctrine end up giving everyone a blank check to excuse bad behavior? Second, starting out. Before I've ever done a kind act, I can't really know kindness in his sense, so it looks like I'd never have a reason to do the first kind act. Or is the move that just wanting to or thinking about the act already counts as action? Because then it feels like the word "action" stops meaning anything and we're all acting every second.

I'm sure I'm reading him wrong somewhere but I can't locate the mistake myself. Please walk me through what the unity claim actually does and doesn't say, in plain language, without assuming I know any philosophical vocabulary.
