I'm in a little reading group on the problem of divine hiddenness, and last week one of the members kept coming back to a picture of God as a parent who hides behind the sofa while a toddler plays: the parent is still there, still watching, but stays just out of sight because the child needs to learn to look around, to call out, and to cope with a minute or two of felt aloneness without dissolving. Her point was that God's hiddenness isn't evidence against a loving God — it's the sort of thing a loving parent would do to draw a child into a more mature relationship. I don't think this works, but I keep getting tangled in which parts of the picture are doing the argumentative work, and I'd like to write a proper critique up for the group's blog.

Could you audit how far this analogy actually carries? Give the correspondence table first — parent, child, the hiding place, the rules of the game, the moment the child is supposed to speak up, the end of the game — then go through where the mapping breaks. In particular, I'd like to know what the picture quietly assumes about the child's background knowledge (that there is a parent at all, that the parent will come back, that the hiding has a point), and whether those assumptions are precisely the things at issue when people talk about nonresistant nonbelief. Then say whether the conclusion she wanted — that divine hiddenness is compatible with, or even an expression of, perfect love, so that Schellenberg-style arguments miss the point — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was riding on the analogy all along. And if there is a better analogy, tell me what it would need to look like.
