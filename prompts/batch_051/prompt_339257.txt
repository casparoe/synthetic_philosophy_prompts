I serve on my company's open-source license compliance committee, and I need to circulate a decision memo before the committee's next meeting. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophical background, and the other committee members are similarly practical: they want a defensible recommendation we can act on, not an essay.

The case in front of us concerns our proprietary workforce-scheduling application. One of its components, the constraint-solving engine, was built by taking SolveCore, a GPLv3-licensed constraint solver, and running it as a local daemon. The application sends the daemon a serialized scheduling problem over a Unix socket, and the daemon returns candidate schedules. The two processes ship together in our installer and run on the customer's machine. We publish our patches to SolveCore under GPLv3, so the solver side is compliant; the open question is whether our proprietary application is itself a derivative work of the solver under the license, which would mean we could not distribute it on our current proprietary terms.

The engineering team says these are plainly separate programs. They live in separate repositories, build independently, run as separate processes, and share no address space. Nothing from SolveCore is compiled or linked into our application. On their view, the two programs communicate at arm's length, the way a proprietary script invokes an unmodified GPL command-line tool, and shipping them together is a permissible aggregate of independent works.

The rest of the committee is not convinced. The serialization format our application uses is not a designed interchange format; it is a direct dump of SolveCore's internal C data structures, mirroring the solver's private struct layout, field order, and padding in a header inside our application's repository. Our application also parses SolveCore's internal debug output to steer its own search heuristics, and it relies on undocumented details of the solver's variable ordering and warm-start behavior. No other backend could be substituted without substantial rework. On this side of the table, the process boundary looks nominal: the two parts leak implementation details into one another constantly, and each is close to useless except as part of a single combined unit.

Draft the committee's decision memo. It should begin with a concrete recommendation: approve the release as planned, block the release pending licensing changes, or approve it only under specified conditions. The reasoning should make explicit the conceptual question at stake, namely what makes one program end and another begin, and it should give criteria that distinguish this integration from an ordinary pipeline that shells out to an unmodified GPL utility, so we can reuse the analysis in future cases. The memo must engage the strongest consideration on the losing side and explain why it does not prevail. It should end by specifying the immediate next action the committee should direct the engineering team to take. Please do not ground the recommendation in case law, citations, or outside legal commentary; the committee needs a first-principles analysis based on the license's basic categories and our own technical judgment, since outside counsel is not available before the release decision.
