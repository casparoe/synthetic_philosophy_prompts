I am a novelist. For the past several years I have taught an upper-level interdisciplinary seminar for philosophy majors and fiction-writing students called The Name and the Named: proper names, reference, and the conventions by which people come to bear the names they do. I'm setting the final exam, and I need one essay question that cannot be answered well by reciting Kripke on rigid designation, together with a model answer of roughly 800 words and a marking scheme out of 25 marks.

Act as a philosopher of language who is expert in the semantics of proper names—Millian, Fregean, and causal-historical views—and experienced at writing assessments that separate students who understand the issue from those who have memorized the labels. Take your time. First, in your head, brainstorm several candidate questions and reject any that could be handled by summarizing standard examples such as Hesperus/Phosphorus. Do not include this brainstorming in your reply.

The question should be built around the following asymmetry. In English and in many other naming systems, given names and family names behave quite differently. Given names are bestowed on individuals, are freely changeable, and sit naturally as bare singular terms: "Nadia arrived." Family names are inherited, are changeable only under narrow legal conditions, and move easily into determiner and plural frames: "the Halperins arrived," "a Halperin was here," "no Halperin missed the meeting." Frames like these are strained for given names used to refer to a single person. The core Millian claim at stake is that a proper name contributes nothing more than its bearer to the proposition expressed. The question must force students to decide whether this given-name/family-name asymmetry is a semantic one—family names are really predicates or kind terms, while given names are genuine singular terms—or a metasemantic one about how the two sorts of label are socially assigned and transmitted. A good answer will have to distinguish, and use, three things: (i) the meaning of a name, (ii) how a name's reference is fixed, and (iii) the social rules governing who may bear a name.

Please write the three components in order: the actual exam question as you would hand it to students; the model answer; then the marking scheme. The marking scheme should spell out which specific moves an A answer must contain, which omissions make a B, and which confusions should cost most marks—for example, treating pluralizability as sufficient to settle the semantic question, or treating facts about how a name is transmitted as if they were part of its meaning. Do not include citations, footnotes, or a reading list; students and graders will refer to positions and arguments by name only.

Begin your response with the exam question itself. Provide no preface, no framing, and no commentary before or after the three requested components.
