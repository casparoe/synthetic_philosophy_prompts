In a graduate seminar on the politics of technology, I pressed a student on the slogan she had used in discussion — "Guns don't kill people; people do" — and found that she could not state the several distinct theses the sentence might be asserting without sliding from one to another. The episode convinced me that the slogan deserves a careful disambiguation, and I want one written out.

The context in which the slogan was doing argumentative work was this. We were considering whether a recommender system could have a politics of its own in Winner's sense: whether its design embodies and enforces particular distributions of power rather than merely serving the interests of the people who deploy it. The student invoked the slogan to resist that idea. On her use, the algorithm is an instrument, and whatever it does should be attributed to designers, platform owners, and users rather than to the artifact.

So the text to be disambiguated is the slogan itself, understood as it functions in that sort of argument. It can be read as a causal thesis about where agency lies between an artifact and its makers and users; a moral thesis about who is responsible or blameworthy; a legal thesis about liability; a design thesis to the effect that artifacts have no ends of their own but only those imposed on them; a political thesis to the effect that artifacts are not proper objects of political criticism, only the human systems around them; and perhaps others. For each reading I want three things stated precisely: what exactly the reading asserts; whether it is trivial, false, or substantive and defensible; and whether it actually establishes the conclusion the slogan was being used to draw in the seminar — that a recommender system cannot have politics in Winner's sense.

I am less interested in the provenance or sociology of the slogan than in the architecture of the positions it lets a speaker collapse. A good answer will lay the readings out as a menu with verdicts attached, and will say plainly which readings do the intended argumentative work and which merely borrow the slogan's common-sense authority without supporting that conclusion.
