A long-standing position in science education holds that students' systematic errors in physics, biology, and chemistry are best explained by their possessing intuitive theories — coherent alternative frameworks, such as an impetus theory of motion or the belief that matter disappears in combustion — and that instruction should aim to create dissatisfaction with these theories and replace them with the correct scientific theory. A more recent line of research challenges the coherence assumption: the same student often applies a naive idea in one context and a much more sophisticated idea in a closely related context, and elements of the supposedly wrong theory can serve as productive resources for learning rather than as obstacles to be replaced. Does the misconceptions-as-theories argument survive this development, or does it need repair? I am asking specifically which premise the context-dependence evidence bears on — the claim that errors are systematic, the claim that they reflect a single coherent underlying theory, or the claim that the right instructional strategy is replacement — and whether the view can be rescued in a weaker form that still earns the label theory. Also indicate where your verdict is least robust: which empirical result about consistency across contexts, transfer, or learning outcomes would most change your answer, and why.
