I'm not an academic; I've just been reading on my own, and I've gotten stuck in a loop about Malthus. A friend and I keep arguing about a simple version of his idea: population can increase without any fixed limit, while the food supply can grow only by fixed additions, so at some point population must outrun food, and a check -- famine, disease, or war -- has to force a balance. Since Malthus's time, that check has not arrived in most places; living standards mostly rose instead. My friend still says Malthus was right but merely early: the check is postponed, not canceled.

When I try to spell out his reasoning, I get an argument that worries me:

(1) The Malthusian claim predicts that a check is inevitable, but it gives no latest date by which the check must occur.
(2) If a prediction gives no latest date, then no matter how long we observe and fail to see the predicted event, those observations remain compatible with "it is still coming."
(3) So no finite body of observations can disconfirm the Malthusian claim.
(4) A claim that no finite body of observations can disconfirm cannot be tested by evidence.
(5) So the Malthusian claim cannot be tested by evidence; it is only the thought that, eventually, something must give.
(6) But the same reasoning would make untestable every claim about an eventual limit: that an economy cannot grow forever on finite resources, that an unchecked epidemic eventually exhausts its susceptible population, that a leaking dam eventually fails. Those all look like proper empirical claims to me.
(7) So either one of these steps fails, or almost every long-run limit claim is untestable. That conclusion seems to prove too much.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I most want to know exactly where the line falls between a theory being flexible about timing and a theory being empty.

- "cost_of_rejecting": what we give up if we reject or qualify that step.
