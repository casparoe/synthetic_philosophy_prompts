I'm giving a seminar next month to a biology department's behaviour and evolution group, defending the line that the handicap principle, as it's usually taught, is really a tangle of at least three different mechanisms—index signals, differential-cost signals, and signals kept honest by receiver retaliation—and that a fair chunk of the textbook evidence offered for the handicap principle turns out to support one of the others once you draw the distinction. My rough argument is: (1) a cost that every signaller pays equally can't keep a signal honest, it just lowers everyone's fitness in the same currency; (2) what actually does the work is either that the signal is physically tied to the trait (an index), or that low-quality signallers pay a higher marginal cost, or that cheating gets punished after the fact; (3) so the classic examples like stotting or the peacock's train mostly land in the index or differential-cost bins, and the slogan that honesty requires cost is either false or needs heavy caveats.

The audience is mostly behavioural ecologists in their forties and fifties who grew up on Zahavi and treat Grafen 1990 as the canonical proof that the handicap principle can work; there's also one younger theorist who builds costly-signalling models, and an economist visitor who keeps saying this is all just Spence's job-market signalling with extra steps. I'm expecting the usual questions to be less hostile then confused—they'll say Grafen already made the distinction I'm claiming to make, or that manipulative experiments on ornament size and immune costs settle the empirical case, or that if I take away cost I owe them an alternative explanation for honesty.

Include what I should concede up front, what I should refuse to concede, and the one question I should be praying nobody asks. Oh, and write the whole thing in British English, please.
