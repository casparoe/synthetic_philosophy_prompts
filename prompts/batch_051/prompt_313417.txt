John Locke's argument for property in the Second Treatise of Government says you come to own something from the common stock of nature by mixing your labour with it. The passage I keep coming back to is chapter five, section 28: "Thus the grass my horse has bit; the turfs my servant has cut; and the ore I have digged in any place, where I have a right to them in common with others, become my property, without the assignation or consent of any body. The labour that was mine, removing them out of that common state they were in, hath fixed my property in them." I want to know whether this arguement survives when the thing being appropriated is personal data. In the original setting, the resource is lying around in nature unowned, the labour runs one way, and taking it leaves less for everyone else. Data about a person is different: it is non-rival, it only exists as a trace of someone, and the "labour" is split between the person generating the raw traces and the company whose employees and models collect, clean, and aggregate them into profiles, the way Locke's servant cuts turfs for the master. So: does the argument still go through here, and if it does, who ends up with the property — the company that did the mixing, the person the data is about, or both? If it doesn't go through, which premise is actually under pressure: the common-stock starting point, the labour-mixing rule, or the self-ownership premise? And is there a repaired Lockean view that keeps something weaker but still captures the intuition that a data broker shouldn't own my clickstream just because they hoovered it up?
