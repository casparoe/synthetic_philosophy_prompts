I help run a reading group whose members come mostly out of the rationalist blogosphere, and the question that reliably splits us is whether the causal inefficacy of individual contributions to large collective harms—a single flight, a single steak, a single vote in a noncompetitive district—should change what one is morally required to do. The argument keeps collapsing because the phrase "my action makes no difference" slides between a claim about imperceptibly small influence, a claim about a redundant or overdetermined contribution, and a claim about not being the decisive cause, and different people draw quite different conclusions without noticing they have changed sense.

We want to build a diagnostic instrument in the style of a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz: something like a political-compass test but less gimmicky, with eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete case and two or three forced-choice options. The cases should include at least: buying a transatlantic flight; being the only vegetarian in a large city; voting in a race decided by tens of thousands of votes; being signatory 9,999 of 10,000 on a petition whose practical effect triggers only at 10,000; joining a consumer boycott no one else joins; buying an offset for an individual flight; and showing up as the fiftieth volunteer for a task that needs only three. Phrase every option as a reason or judgment about the case rather than as a self-identification, so that a reader does not need to know any philosophical vocabulary to be classified. The scoring key should sort answer patterns into the underlying positions in the logical space: at minimum, a straight expected-value act-consequentialism, a threshold or triggering view, a Kantian-type generalization view (act as every similarly situated person acted), a participation- or complicity-centered view that refuses the individual-causal frame from the start, and a view on which the action is expressively or virtue-theoretically important but not outcome-obligatory. Present these as leaves located by combinations of answers, not as predetermined categories into which the questions are reverse-engineered to fit. Where a combination of answers is logically available but no one defends it, say whether it is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or plausibly just unexplored. Finally, flag which positions the case-based quiz cannot cleanly separate—I suspect the participation view and the expressive view will blur together on the boycott and petition cases—and say one question you would add if you were allowed an eleventh. I care more about diagnostic validity than elegance; if a question tracks two distinctions at once, mark it clearly rather than papering it over.
