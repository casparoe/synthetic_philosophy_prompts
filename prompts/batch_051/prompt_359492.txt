I am taking a philosophy of physics course this term, and we have been working through a unit on symmetry principles and conservation laws. I find myself torn between one of the theories we have been examining and a standing philosophical intuition that I cannot seem to dislodge. On the theory side, I am drawn to the claim that symmetries do explanatory work in physics. Noether's theorem, as I understand it, associates every differentiable symmetry of a system's action with a conserved quantity: time-translation symmetry with conservation of energy, spatial-translation symmetry with conservation of momentum, and rotational symmetry with angular momentum. What attracts me is that this framework takes a miscellaneous list of conservation principles and shows them to be reflections of one underlying structural fact about the equations. It seems to say not merely that energy is conserved, but why it is conserved: because the laws are invariant under translations in time. The view is elegant, unifying, and gives a principled answer where another approach would simply post a brute fact for each conservation law.

Yet I also have a firm intuition that explanations must do something the symmetry view does not obviously do: they must identify what brings a phenomenon about, the causal story or mechanism. A mathematical theorem that maps formal symmetries onto conserved quantities can feel more like a redescription of the physics, or a demonstration that two features necessarily co-occur, than an account of why the one obtains because of the other. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the causal-mechanical intuition to be the thing I give up, what it would take for the symmetry-based account to be the thing I amend or abandon, and whether there is a way of reading "explanation" on which both the unifying power of symmetry and the causal intuition survive.
