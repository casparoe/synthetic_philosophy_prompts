I have been trying to live by a rule that I drew from W. K. Clifford's essay "The Ethics of Belief" (1877), and I want to know whether the rule is defensible as stated, or whether I have turned a guarded point about responsibility into a blanket epistemic habit that needs restating. The passage I keep returning to is this:

"The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him."

The habit I have built on it is this: in my own thinking, I try not to let any belief settle until the evidence makes it compulsory, and I extend the same standard to other people. If a friend arrives at a conclusion partly because it is consoling, because loyalty to a group seems to require it, or because suspending judgment would be painful, I treat the belief as epistemically tainted even when it later turns out to be true — because Clifford's point seems to be that a lucky outcome does not launder the process by which the belief was formed. A friend who admires William James tells me that my policy is not the neutral, strictly evidential rule I take it to be. On her reading of James, a rule of never believing anything until the evidence is compulsory is itself a choice made by the passional side of our nature, one that ranks the avoidance of error above the pursuit of truth, and it quietly decides options that cannot be postponed without already taking a side by treating suspended judgment as the only respectable outcome. I find this objection plausible but also too convenient, because it seems to let motivated believing off the hook exactly where it is most dangerous.

Is my habit defensible, and if not, what is the restated version that keeps what Clifford was right about while conceding what James was right about? Does James's objection defeat the rule itself, or only my mistaken claim that the rule is intellectually neutral? Could you give me the conditions under which "wait for compulsory evidence" is itself a way of rigging the outcome, and the conditions under which it is the only honest policy available?
