I work on a fraud-detection team at a payments company. We currently gate transactions with a hand-written rules engine that declines about 3% of volume. I trained a machine learning model on our historical transaction logs—where the fraud labels come from chargebacks and manual review of transactions the old rules let through—to estimate, for any incoming transaction, the probability that it is fraudulent. The model performs well on held-out data from the same period.

Our risk team now wants to replace the rules engine with a policy that automatically declines the 10% of transactions with the highest model scores. They plan to justify this change by reporting the model's average predicted fraud rate within that top 10% as the amount of fraud the new policy would prevent. They also want to record each declined transaction whose model score exceeds some threshold as a fraud that we prevented.

I have a nagging sense that the model's probabilities are being made to mean something they don't mean, because the model was trained on data generated under the old rules and only on transactions the old rules let through. But I can't articulate the problem precisely. My manager's reply is that a fraud probability is a fraud probability: if the model says a transaction has a 90% chance of being fraudulent, that's a property of the transaction itself, and it doesn't matter which policy produced the training data.

Who is right? I'd like a rigorous account of what my objection amounts to, and a clear statement of the assumptions under which the risk team's two uses of the model would be valid or invalid.
