I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, and this argument keeps getting rehearsed in our applied ethics reading group. I've got a working draft of a paper pushing back against the standard criticism of sweatshops, but I cant tell whether the argument is actually valid or whether I've talked myself into it. Could you act as an expert on exploitation and labor ethics and stress-test it for me? Please think carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming the different ways "better off" and "harm" could be read before you go through the steps one by one. Answer in American English, please.

Here's the argument:

(1) To wrong someone through an economic exchange is to harm them, i.e., to leave them worse off than they would otherwise be.
(2) In a typical case, the sweatshop worker is better off with the job than she would be under her next-best alternative — that's why she takes it and stays.
(3) So the exchange leaves the worker better off, not worse off.
(4) The exchange leaves the employer better off as well, and there is no third party to the transaction itself.
(5) So the exchange harms no one, and therefore wrongs no one.
(6) A transaction that wrongs no one cannot be exploitative in any morally objectionable sense.
(7) Therefore there is nothing morally wrong with offering, taking, or buying sweatshop labor — at least with respect to the exchange itself.

Assume for the sake of argument that the empirical claim in (2) is true; I want to know whether the argument goes through even then. Is it valid? If it is, is it sound? If not, which step or steps fail, and do any of them fail because of a slide between two senses of "better off" or "harm"? I half suspect its valid and I just dislike the conclusion, but I keep feeling like the move from (2) to (5) is doing something sneaky with baselines. Pinpoint it.
