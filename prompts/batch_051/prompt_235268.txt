I help direct the research program at a foundation concerned with the long-run safety of artificial intelligence, and the deepest disagreement on our strategy committee is not over any particular technical result but over two rival pictures that keep getting called tool AI and agent AI. We use the labels loosely, so I will fix them here. By tool AI I mean systems whose contribution runs through outputs — answers, written analyses, code, proposed plans — that a human reviews and decides how to act on; the system does not set its own subgoals, take actions in the world, or optimize against an objective beyond responding faithfully to a request. By agent AI I mean systems that are given objectives and then act over time to bring them about: they choose which sub-tasks to take on, which levers to pull, and they adjust their behavior in light of the results.

I want to put our disagreement on a more rigorous footing, partly to see whether the rest of the committee is even disagreeing about the same thing. Please give me your own credence in the following proposition: By 2040, the systems responsible for most of the economically significant expert work in law, software, medicine, finance, and engineering will still be tool-like rather than agent-like in the sense just fixed — that is, their contribution will predominantly run through outputs a human reviews and acts on, rather than through the system's own extended pursuit of outcomes. Treat the proposition as adequately resolved if a reasonable survey of where expert-level work gets done and who or what carries out the decisive steps shows the value as predominantly tool-mediated or predominantly agent-mediated.

The number matters less to me than the reasoning you use to get it. Decompose the proposition into the distinct sub-questions your credence depends on — at least these four: (1) Is the tool/agent distinction a real boundary or a matter of degree, and if the latter, where exactly do you draw the line for the proposition above? (2) Can high-level capability be decomposed into non-goal-directed services, or is the core of planning and judgment under uncertainty itself goal-directed in a way that makes the tool-like posture unstable once systems get good enough? (3) How strong are the competitive, organizational, and user-facing pressures to hand systems more autonomy regardless of what safety-minded researchers prefer? (4) Does removing the system's agency actually reduce total risk, or does it mainly relocate the risk to the human operators who must execute the plans — and does that affect whether the tool-like approach is actually sustained? For each, give a rough probability (or a probability over a small set of relevant possibilities), show how the sub-questions combine to produce your final number, and state which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to. I am currently at roughly 0.6 on the proposition; two colleagues I respect sit at roughly 0.35 and 0.75. Tell me where the disagreement among the three of us most likely lives, and tell us what evidence in the next five years should most move our numbers toward one another.
