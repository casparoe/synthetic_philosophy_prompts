ok so i have this rule i follow whenever people want to change something, and i want to know if its actually defensible or just me being scared of change. the rule is: i won't support getting rid of a rule, tradition, or feature of a system unless someone can first explain what it was originally for. not what they guess it's for now, but what actual problem it was solving when it got put there. like last year our school tried to kill the two-week late work policy where you can still get half credit. the people who wanted it gone said it just rewards procrastination. but when i asked why the policy existed in the first place, nobody on the committee actually knew. teachers guessed, but the guesses contradicted each other. so i voted to keep it. my logic was: if you don't know why a thing was put there, you don't know what breaks when you remove it. and if nobody remembers the reason anymore, that might just mean the reason got forgotten, not that there never was one. i think this is basically that chesterton fence idea, maybe burke too, but i sort of arrived at it myself.

i'm not saying never change anything. if someone can give me the original reason, then we can argue about whether that reason still applies. but i treat "nobody knows why this exists" as a point in favor of keeping it, not a point against it. is that right? cause i can see it would've blocked alot of good changes, like when the original reason was stupid or the thing is just genuinely pointless now and everyone can see it. so is there a corrected version of this rule that keeps the good part without making me vote to preserve every bit of junk we've forgotten the purpose of? and how do i tell from the inside whether i'm using the rule to think, or just using it to defend whatever's already there because that feels safer?
