I keep coming back to the following argument. My instinct is that something in it is too quick, but each step looks right on its own, and I want to know exactly where, if anywhere, it fails.

(1) Condorcet's jury theorem says: if a group decides between two options, exactly one of which is objectively correct, and each member has probability greater than one-half of voting for the correct option, and each member votes independently of the others, then as the group grows larger, the probability that a majority votes for the correct option approaches certainty.

(2) Many of the questions voters actually face are like this. They are binary and have an objectively correct answer: "Will a roundabout at this intersection reduce serious crashes?", "Did the new curriculum raise or lower reading scores?"

(3) On such questions, each voter is at least slightly more likely than not to answer correctly. If that were false, then ordinary citizens would be no better than coins, and it would be hard to see why anyone should consult them rather than toss one.

(4) Voters' judgments on such questions are independent in the sense the theorem requires. They read different outlets, belong to different social networks, and reach their conclusions through their own reasoning; no one is literally copying another person's ballot.

(5) Therefore, by (1), when a large electorate decides such a question, the majority is almost certainly correct.

(6) So if a large majority judges one of these questions one way and I judge it the other, I should defer to the majority — even when my own reasons still feel strong. A private argument on my side does not overturn the near-certainty that the majority has the truth.

Which step, if any, fails?
