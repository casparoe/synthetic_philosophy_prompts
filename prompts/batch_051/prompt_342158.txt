I'm drawn to a clean view about belief: you can't believe at will, and ought implies can, so there can't be a duty to doubt—"you should have doubted" is just an epistemic evaluation of the belief, not a moral one. It's tidy, and it keeps epistemic and moral norms from bleeding into each other. But I can't shake the intuition that someone who never reopens a question because the answer flatters him, or who looks away from evidence he half-knows is there, has failed in a way that feels more serious then merely having an inaccurate belief—it feels like a failure of thinking itself, and a culpable one. So which gives? If I keep the theory, do I have to say the intuition is just a scope confusion between "should have known" and "should have believed otherwise"? If I keep the intuition, do I have to abandon ought-implies-can, or can the duty attach to attention and inquiry rather than to belief directly? Give me the strongest version of each option, what it costs, and whether a reading of "doubt is a duty" survives once that distinction is drawn.
