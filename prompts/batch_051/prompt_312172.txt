In teaching philosophy of language, I still teach Russell’s theory of descriptions as the best first approximation to the semantics of *the F*, and I am especially attached to the way he handles the uniqueness implication. In “On Denoting” (1905) Bertrand Russell writes:

“It remains to interpret phrases containing *the*. These are by far the most interesting and difficult of denoting phrases. Take as an instance ‘the father of Charles II was executed’. This asserts that there was an x who was the father of Charles II and was executed. Now *the*, when it is strictly used, involves uniqueness; we do, it is true, speak of ‘the son of So-and-so’ even when So-and-so has several sons, but it would be more correct to say ‘a son of So-and-so’. Thus for our purposes we take *the* as involving uniqueness.”

I want to know what would count against the position in that passage: roughly, that a sentence of the form *the F is G* is true only if there is exactly one F and that F is G, and that apparent uses of *the* where uniqueness fails are loose or governed by a less strict convention. I am not asking for the usual objections recited in passing. I want a map of the evidence, arguments, and natural-language data that should move a committed Russellian, ranked from decisive to merely awkward. Which cases cannot be handled by saying the description is incomplete, contextually restricted, or used loosely? Which patterns of projection under negation, modals, and propositional-attitude verbs tell specifically against Russell’s quantificational picture, rather than merely showing that natural language is messy? What would a defending Russellian have to concede, and what should they continue to dismiss as pragmatic? I would personally be most moved by evidence that the uniqueness implication is not part of truth-conditional content but still cannot be explained away as an implicature, or by evidence that definite descriptions sometimes behave like genuine referring terms in ways that quantifiers never do. Is there a clean result that would show Russell’s analysis collapses into a referential or predicational theory, or has the view been qualified enough to survive such results? And if it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, is that a defect in this part of semantics, or simply what we should expect from a theory of natural language?
