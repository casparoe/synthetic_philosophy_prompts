I keep going back and forth on something and could use a second opinion. I'm a software engineer, never took a philosophy course, but I've been reading Nozick recently — the entitlement theory and the Wilt Chamberlain argument. As I understand it, he's saying a distribution isn't to be judged by its pattern, like how equal it is, but by the history of how people got what they got. If everyone acquired their holdings justly and then transferred them justly, the final distribution is just no matter how lopsided it looks.

Here's the asymmetry I can't shake. Picture a million fans each voluntarily paying a dollar to watch a basketball player. Taken one at a time, none of those payments feels wrong: the fan gets exactly what they paid for, the player isn't defrauding anybody. But then the camera zooms out and the player is a million dollars richer while a million people are each a dollar poorer, and that gap now looks unjust to me — I'd probably be okay with the state taxing some of it away to help the worse off. So I'm judging the endpoint as a problem even though I approved every step that built it. Nozick's move seems to be: if you don't like the final pattern, stop pretending the steps were fine — you have to find something wrong upstream, either with the initial holdings or with the transfers themselves.

What I want to know is whether that procedural-versus-end-state asymmetry can be defended. Is there a real justification for holding the whole to a seperate standard than the parts that produced it — like cumulative effects, background injustice, social costs of large inequality, or something about the difference between evaluating acts and evaluating states of affairs — or is the discomfort with the big gap just an illusion I should give up? And if I give it up, should I go the other way and say my approval of the individual payments was the thing that was too quick?
