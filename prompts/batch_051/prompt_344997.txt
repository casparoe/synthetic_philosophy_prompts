Classical decision theory and most of economics evaluate a gamble by its ensemble average: imagine a million parallel copys of you each taking it, and average what happens. There is a well-known line of objection, associated with Ole Peters and others, that for a single agent who takes a gamble repeatedly what actually matters is the time average—how the agent's own wealth grows or shrinks over time—and these two averages can come apart dramatically. Take a coin flip that multiplies your wealth by 1.5 on heads and 0.6 on tails: the ensemble average is positive, but over repeated flips an individual's wealth collapses toward zero with probability one. My question: does expected-utility reasoning survive this point in a repaired form, or is it dead for any decision problem that repeats over time? Pin down exactly which premise or assumption of the standard framework the time-average challenge bears on, and say what the surviving principle should be called if maximising expected utility has to be replaced. Please write the answer in British English.
