You are a moral philosopher who works on the ethics of consumption, virtue, and the demandingness of morality. I am drafting a short statement of my views on frugality for a workshop on consumption and moral character, and I would like you to stress-test the set of commitments below before I circulate it. Take your time, and begin by brainstorming the most charitable reading of each commitment before testing them against one another; then reason step by step.

Here are the commitments as I would currently state them:

1. Frugality, as I understand it, is a disposition to avoid wasting what one has. It is not a duty to minimize spending, and it is compatible with large expenditures that are put to real use.
2. My reason for valuing frugality is consequentialist at bottom: wasteful consumption drives unnecessary resource extraction and ecological damage, and those harms are what make wastefulness wrong. I do not think there is a basic, self-standing duty to conserve for its own sake.
3. It follows, and I accept, that there is nothing wrong with an expensive wedding, hobby, or meal as such, so long as the person can genuinely afford it and is not neglecting some more important obligation to others.
4. All the same, I think someone who spends lavishly on themselves while knowing that other people lack basic necessities displays a defect of character, even when that spending does not cause or worsen the deprivation and does not violate any duty of aid.
5. I also think that, in our present environmental situation, promoting frugality through norms, policies, and institutions that curb consumption is morally more important than practicing frugality in one's own life, because the effects are so much larger.
6. But I hold that a public figure who campaigns for frugality or sustainability while personally living extravagantly is a hypocrite, and that the hypocrisy undercuts the value of their advocacy in a way that matters morally, not merely aesthetically.
7. Finally, I do not think I should go around judging or lecturing my friends about their spending choices. My frugality is a standard for how I live, not a license to police other people's consumption.

I want an audit of this set rather than a defense of it or a general lecture on frugality. Please go through the commitments pairwise, or in whichever small clusters actually matter, and tell me which pairs are in tension, which are only apparently in tension, and how severe each real tension is. For each real tension, say what the cheapest fix would be and what that fix would cost. Then tell me which single revision of one commitment would do the most to make the whole set cohere, and what residual strains would remain. I am not certain where the problems are, and I would rather not bias the audit by saying which of these I am most attached to. As you go, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers working on this material, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
