I keep using a phrase I can't quite defend, and I want to know whether its a real idea or just a habit of speech. I call them free-floating rationales: reasons for doing something that aren't anybody's reasons, at least not yet. A loose step on the stairwell is a reason to fix it even if the landlord hasn't noticed and no tenant has complained. I want to say the step's being broken is itself the reason, not just that someone would have one if they looked. And the idea does more work than that: I want to say the rationale for a rule can outlive the people who made it, so that even after the original point of the rule is gone, the reason to follow it is still there, hanging around the institution. I also catch myself saying a workplace has reasons to reorganize that no one working there has ever entertained, or that a character in a novel had a reason to leave even though the author never wrote it and no reader ever thought it.

Here's my trouble. I'm not a Platonist about reasons — I don't think reasons are objects floating around in the world. But once I talk this way, I can't tell what I've added to the plain claim that there are facts about what would be good to do. If free-floating rationales are just 'it would be better if,' then my phrase is a metaphor with delusions of grandeur. If they're something more, what are they attached to if not a mind? And I want to say a free-floating rationale can sit there forever unnoticed, but then why call it a rationale at all — a rationale nobody rationalizes with sounds like a recipe nobody cooks.

So: is this a real concept, or just a metaphor doing the work of 'objective reason' in worse words? What does it buy me over saying reasons are facts about what would be good? And where does it break — what would it force me to count as a reason that I shouldn't?
