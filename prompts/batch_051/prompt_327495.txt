I'm taking a media law course this term, and our moot court problem has me going back and fourth because the precedents feel like they point both ways at once. The problem gives us the defamation track record of a single made-up state supreme court, which has been unusually interested in cases about ratings, rankings, and forecasts. So far the court has held that: (1) a restaurant critic's line that a bistro served "gloppy, overpriced risotto" was non-actionable because "gloppy" and "overpriced" are subjective evaluations that can't be proven false; (2) a credit agency's downgrade of a town's bonds from A to B-plus, announced with the phrase "fiscal management is deteriorating," was protected even though the town claimed the agency had miscopied its audit figures, because a letter grade and a vague evaluative phrase are opinions; (3) a stock analyst's line "I'm lowering my price target on Nimbus because its 10-K buries lease obligations in a footnote" was actionable only on the clause about burying lease obligations, while the price target itself was struck as opinion; (4) a magazine's "Fifty Worst-Run Towns in the State" list, which ranked a town first and added "the deficit tripled under the current manager," was protected as to the ranking but actionable as to the deficit sentence; and (5) an apartment site's bare "WalkScore: D" with no supporting text was protected, because a bare number or letter rating isn't an assertion of fact.

Our panel has to decide the next case: a hotel-rating app displays the Grand Meridian with a checkmark and the headline "Verified Health Score: 41/100," captioned "computed from health department violation counts, complaint records, and inspection reports as of this month," but it doesn't list any individual violations. The hotel says the app's database merges its records with those of another Grand Meridian across the state line, so the 41 is generated from the wrong hotel's violations entirely. I need to write the majority opinion, and I want to be consistent with those five rulings. Should we treat the verified health score as a protected rating/opinion, or as an actionable statement of fact? I'm convince at least two defensible rules fit all five precedents and they come apart on this display. What are those rules, which one do you think a court following these precedents would actually adopt, which of the five prior cases is the closest analog, and what principle would we be committing to either way? Also, what single additional fact about the display would do the most to separate the two rules?
