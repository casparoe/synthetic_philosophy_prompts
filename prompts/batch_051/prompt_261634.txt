I've been trying to reconstruct an argument I first saw in a conversation about imprecise probabilities, and it still bothers me enough that I want a careful diagnosis rather than my own hunches.

Suppose my degrees of belief are imprecise: they're represented by a set P of probability functions, all of which I treat as permissible, and I update by ordinary conditionalization applied to every member of P. Let H be some proposition about which I'm imprecise, with my credal interval for H being [0.25, 0.75], and let C be the outcome of a fair coin toss that I regard as completely evidentially irrelevant to H. Each p in P assigns C probability 0.5, and my imprecision about H is matched by a total lack of settled opinion about how H and C are correlated, so P contains functions on which H is positively correlated with C, negatively correlated with C, and independent of C. Here is what troubles me:

(1) Learning the outcome of a process I regard as evidentially irrelevant to H should not leave me more uncertain about H than I was before. (2) The set-and-condition rule permits every probability function consistent with my prior bounds, including those exhibiting strong correlations between H and C. (3) Conditioning on C=Heads then produces a posterior set in which H ranges over the entire interval [0,1]: for any value q in [0,1], some permissible prior has p(H|Heads)=q. (4) So the framework turns an irrelevant observation into complete ignorance about H. (5) The only apparent escape is to restrict P from the outset so that H and C are stochastically independent in every permissible function. (6) But that is a precise correlation judgment imposed on a supposedly imprecise framework, and once you impose such independence constraints wherever the same problem arises, the representor stops representing genuine imprecision. (7) So imprecise probabilities are either incompatible with ordinary conditionalization, or they secretly collapse into precise probabilities.

That conclusion seems far too strong, but I can't locate the exact move that lets the argument earn it. I'd most like to keep the representor-and-conditioning picture, and I suspect I should give up something in the neighborhood of (1), but I want to know what I'd be paying. Please respond as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: name the step that should be rejected, explain why it initially looked acceptable, describe the main alternative ways out, state what each alternative costs, and give your bottom-line judgment on what the argument does and does not show.
