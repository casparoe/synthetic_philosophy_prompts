I am a retired experimental scientist, and in my recent reading in epistemology I keep turning over a parallel that I cannot decide about. The philosophers' bootstrapping problem observes that a belief-forming process cannot be used to certify its own reliability: to argue that perception is reliable by appealing to a long record of perceptual reports, each of which was itself accepted on the strength of perception, is to move in a circle. The neighboring easy-knowledge problem observes that from a single ordinary perceptual judgment — that the table is red, for instance — together with closure under known entailment, one seems able to rule out a radical alternative such as being a brain in a vat presented with a red-looking table, without having acquired any independent evidence against that alternative. Both patterns are widely treated as suspicious.

In the laboratory I spent my career enforcing what looks like the same prohibition. We never calibrated a thermometer by comparing it with its own uncorrected readings; we did not estimate a detector's error from the detector alone; we did not validate a fitted model on the data used to fit it; and we did not rule out a confound by re-analyzing the same possibly-confounded dataset. The common rule was that a source of information cannot vouch for its own accuracy; support must come from something independent of the source under test.

I can see two readings of the resemblance. On the first, the philosophical cases simply are the same circularity as self-calibration, and the laboratory prohibition is its working expression. On the second, the analogy is largely verbal, because in science an independent standard is in principle available — a primary reference, a second instrument based on different physics, a held-out dataset — whereas one cannot step outside one's own cognitive faculties; there is no epistemic analogue of a primary standard, and the epistemologists are not warning against a fallacy so much as marking a boundary condition of our cognitive situation.

I would like you to do more than choose between these readings. Lay out, in as much detail as it can bear, the mapping between epistemic self-support and experimental self-validation: what corresponds to the instrument, the reading, the track record, the independent standard, and the calibration certificate. Then say exactly where the structures match and where they come apart, and identify something the parallel would predict if taken seriously that is false of either science or epistemology. Is 'independent of the source under test' doing the same work in both domains, or is that phrase only a superficial echo? And if anything does transfer, what, if anything, should epistemology learn from metrology's distinction between calibration and validation, or from the way a new primary standard gets established before there is any independent check on it?
