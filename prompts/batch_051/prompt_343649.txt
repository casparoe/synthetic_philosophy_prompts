In his essay "Of the Standard of Taste," David Hume makes the following observation before he develops his account of the good critic:

"The sentiments of men often differ with regard to beauty and deformity of all kinds, even while their general discourse is the same. There are certain terms in every language, which import blame, and others praise; and all men, who use the same tongue, must agree in their application of them. Every voice is united in applauding elegance, propriety, simplicity, spirit in writing; and in blaming fustian, affectation, coldness, and a false brilliancy: But when critics come to particulars, this seeming unanimity vanishes; and it is found, that they had affixed a very different meaning to their expressions. In all matters of opinion and science, the case is opposite: The difference among men is there oftener found to lie in generals than in particulars; and to be less in reality than in appearance. An explanation of the terms commonly ends the controversy; and the disputants are surprized to find, that they had been quarrelling, while at bottom they agreed in their judgment."

Hume can be read as proposing a claim about where disagreement is located: in matters of taste, apparent agreement at the level of general terms is partly an artifact of shared vocabulary, while the real disagreement emerges when critics judge particular works; in matters of opinion and science, by contrast, real disagreement tends to reside at the level of general principles, and much of it disappears once key terms are explained.
