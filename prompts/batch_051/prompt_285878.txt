I'm recently retired from geochemistry and have started reading the French materialists and their hangers-on, and I keep coming back to Émilie du Châtelet's *Discours sur le bonheur*. As I understand her, she claims that a person cannot be happy without strong passion, but also that most passions are traps, because they attach us to things outside our control and they only work by keeping us in a state of illusion about ourself or the future. Then she seems to make the love of study the exception: a passion that gives you absorption and desire without depending on luck or other people's good opinion. I don't want a summary of her position, though. I want your actual credence that the underlying psychological thesis is true: that durable human happiness requires at least one strong passion, and that this passion must either be of a kind that does not depend on external uncontrollable outcomes, or be fueled by enough illusion that you falsely believe you control or deserve those outcomes. Decompose it. Give me a number for each of these and then the combination: (a) that apathy or tranquility is insufficient for happiness for most people; (b) that passions directed at external goods bring more unhappiness than happiness on average; (c) that the love of study or inquiry is really distinctive in supplying passion without the usual dependence on external outcomes; (d) that her view is partly a rationalization of her own priviledged situation and won't generalize to people with less control over their time and circumstances. Then tell me which of those sub-questions your final number is most sensitive to, and what evidence would move it most.
