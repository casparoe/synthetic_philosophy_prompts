I've been writing exhibit labels for a small natural history museum this year, and I keep noticing a weird pattern in how we talk about the past — and it's not just us, it's basically every science museum and documentary I've seen. The displays march forward in time, and almost every label frames an old thinker or culture in terms of what they got right by our standards: a medieval bishop is called "ahead of his time" for sketching something like the Big Bang, an eighteenth-century naturalist "anticipated" natural selection, a Greek philosopher "prefigured" atomic theory. But the same exhibit quietly skips everything in those people's work that now looks wrong, and it never really asks why smart people held those wrong views for so long. A historian friend of mine rolls her eyes at this and calls it Whig history — writing the past as a ramp that leads up to us.

I'm trying to work out what this practice actually commits a museum to. What view of knowledge, or progress, or history does it presuppose? Does it presuppose that ideas are only worth displaying if they're recognizable ancestors of current science, that history has a direction, that present understanding is the right standard to measure the past against? And is the practice even coherent, given that we also put up labels saying science is self-correcting and that all theories are provisional? I'd like to know what would have to change in how the exhibit is laid out and written if you actually dropped those presuppositions — would it just become a museum of dead ends, and is there a way to display the past that doesn't smuggle in the assumption that it was all headed toward us?
