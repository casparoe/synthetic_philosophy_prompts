I'm prepping for my 19th-century seminar and I keep tripping over a stretch in Mill's On Liberty, early in Chapter III. He argues, roughly, that people should be free to try out different modes of life, because human beings differ and a way of living that suites one person may not suit another. Then he pushes further: even when someone's chosen style of life looks odd or irregular, we should let them try it, since the bare fact that a custom is established tells us nothing about whether it fits this particular individual. And then he says a person whose desires and impulses are their own has character, whereas someone whose impulses are not their own has no more character than, say, a steam engine. I used to read this mainly as an epistemic point: experiments in living give society information about ways of flourishing, so they are valuable as a discovery mechanism. But now I'm not sure whether Mill also means that individuality is valuable for the person who has it, apart from any social payoff. Can you help me work through what this passage is actually doing: is the criticism of conformity primarily about people being unfree, or about them being useless as sources of novelty? And if he intends both, are these separable arguments or just two sides of one argument?
