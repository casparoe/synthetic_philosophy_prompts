Construct a thought experiment that forces apart two rationales for funding an apparently useless academic field. The point of the case is to test whether the familiar debate over 'useless' scholarship has more structure than a clash of slogans; a case that simply stipulates that a field has no instrumental payoff and is intrinsically valuable will not count as meeting the specification. The two rationales are:

1. The instrumentalist rationale: a field earns its place in a university by contributing, directly or through a credible long-run pathway, to practical goods such as technological capacity, economic productivity, health, or policy competence — goods whose value can be recognized even by someone with no intrinsic interest in the field.

2. The intrinsic-value rationale: at least some systematic forms of inquiry are worth supporting because the activity, its internal standards, and what it lets a community understand are valuable in themselves, independently of downstream practical benefit.

Design one realistic case, containing a specific field of study, a research program within it, and a concrete funding decision, such that an instrumentalist must recommend defunding while a proponent of the intrinsic-value rationale must recommend funding, even though both parties are fully informed and share the same evidence. The case may not rely on religious or partisan content, on direct practical benefits produced by practicing the field (such as performance, therapy, or community outreach), on public ignorance of the field, or on a discipline so contrived that it has no serious claim to being one. In particular, the divergence must trace to a difference in the theories of value, not to different probability assignments about future payoff.

After the scenario, (a) trace the reasoning each rationale uses to reach its verdict; (b) identify the single structural feature of the case that does the discriminating work, then remove it in a companion variant and explain whether the disagreement disappears, reverses, or becomes less sharp, and what that shows about where the intrinsic-value rationale draws its line; and (c) present the strongest attack an instrumentalist could mount against the original case and say whether it succeeds. If it succeeds, revise the case so that it withstands the attack without using a forbidden device; if no such revision is possible, say so explicitly.
