Next month I'm presenting a short paper to my department's criminal law theory workshop, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense. The paper is on the paradox of blackmail: where it would be lawful for me to reveal a true and damaging fact about you, and lawful for me simply to ask you for money, yet when I make the revelation conditional on payment the whole becomes blackmail and is criminal. My thesis is that the puzzle dissolves once we reject an aggregation principle for permissions: a permission to disclose and a permission to bargain do not automatically compose into a permission to make disclosure the alternative to payment, because composition changes what the act is. The conditional threat is not the sum of its parts; it is a new act with a different coercive structure. The audience is mostly analytic philosophers of criminal law who take the paradox seriously, plus two law-and-economics people who think the real content is that legalizing the package would create bad incentives to dig up or manufacture damaging information, and one deontologist who will press the claim that the victim is used as a means and that my view says too little about wrongful threats. Prepare me for the objections I will actually get, roughly in the order they will come, phrased the way this exact audience would phrase them, with what to concede, what to hold, and where each reply is soft. Include the question I should most hope nobody asks and say what to do if it comes.
