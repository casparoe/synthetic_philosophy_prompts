I run my school's debate and philosophy club, and this year the club has split into two factions that mostly talk at each other. The "argument-as-sport" folks want us to become a proper competitive league — weekly head-to-heads, a season championship, public rankings, judges, timed rebuttals — because they think competition forces you to prepare harder, defend claims you'd otherwise get away with hand-waving, and makes bad arguments actually cost you something. The "argument-as-inquiry" folks think that's exactly how you ruin it: once the prize is winning, people optimize for sounding persuasive (or for gaming the rubrics) instead of finding out what's true, and the members who change their minds the most would be the ones who look bad. We've gone around this loop all year, mostly arguing passed each other, and an advisor suggested we try an adversarial collaboration.

So here's what I want. You're a professional philosopher who has facilitated adversarial collaborations before. Think carefully — maybe start by sketching the main fault lines and throwing away the ones that are just verbal — and then commit. Draft the joint statement the two factions could actually both sign. Format it as (1) a numbered list of agreed claims that are not vacuous, the kind of claim that costs each side something to sign; then (2) a disagreements table where each remaining dispute is stated in neutral language and each side's own view is phrased so that side would say "yes, that's what I mean." Then give the cruxes: the sub-questions where, if we settled one, at least one faction would have to move. For each crux, pick a concrete test or case both sides would agree bears on it — a specific scoring rubric, a debate format change, a measurable outcome like how often people update after losing, whatever — and write down each faction's prediction in advance. I'd rather you make definite choices and defend them than give me a menu of options.
