I am a novelist who teaches in a university writing program, and I have become puzzled by a tension in the way our program handles memoir. In workshop, students writing about their own lives are told that memory is not a recording; they must select and rearrange, decide what kind of narrator they will be, and give the "I" on the page an arc, a voice, and a point of view. A memoir is praised when this constructed narrator is more vivid and coherent than the author could ever be in the room. But the same institution calls these courses nonfiction. If a student is found to have invented a scene, merged two relatives into one figure, or compressed a year into an afternoon, the piece is withdrawn from our competitions and the student appears before a small honesty panel, accused of passing off fiction as fact. The practice therefore seems to say, in the classroom, that the remembered self is something made, and, before the panel, that the made thing must answer to a real self that exists independently of the making. What theory of the self does this practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that a self is not a fixed item that memory reports, but something like a narrative center of gravity, an abstraction posited to unify a life story? Or does the panel secretly depend on a substantive self that narrative must not betray? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the two halves of the practice are coherent together, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits, and tell me what would have to change in the workshop or the panel if one of those views of the self were carried through consistently.
