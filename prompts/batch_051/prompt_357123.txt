I'm running an informal two-hour workshop on game theory and cooperation for philosophy grad students, maybe 12 to 16 people, and I want to spend the second hour on something most of them won't have heard of: program equilibrium. The rough idea I'm working from is this: in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, instead of each player just writing down cooperate or defect in secret, each player submits a little deterministic decision rule (a kind of mini-program), and before any rule is run all the rules are made public, so each rule can condition its output on what the other rule says or does. The alleged punchline is that mutual cooperation can become an equilibrium even though the game is still one-shot and anonymous — e.g., a rule that says it cooperates if and only if the other rule would cooperate against it, otherwise it defects, so the two rules prop each other up. I half-remember that the formal version has to finesse circularity somehow, and I don't want a lecture on the formalism up front.

What I'd like is a runnable activity that makes the group discover that move themselves rather than me explaining it. They already know the basic prisoner's dilemma and have just played a few normal one-shot rounds with payoff numbers, so the failure of cooperation should feel settled and a little bleak. Then let them rewrite the game's move: they submit rules instead of actions, under pseudonyms so its not about reputation, the rules get posted where everyone can read them, and I execute them. I want rules, materials (index cards? a handout with the payoff table and constraints on what a rule may say?), and a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money — maybe points that convert to candy or just bragging rights and a leaderboard. It should be set up so someone is likely to stumble onto conditional rules that mention the other rule, and so the facilitator can actually evaluate every matchup without ambiguity. What I want them leaving with is a felt sense that cooperation can self-stabilize when decision rules are mutually readable, not just that they heard a clever argument. Then a debrief that maps specific moments onto the formal idea (including why reading the other rule does work that just choosing an action can't), says what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it — I'm picturing rules that don't halt or are too vague to execute, or someone annoyed that a rule that always cooperates did worse than a conditional rule — and is honest about what the physical version can't reproduce (I suspect free program-checking and guaranteed termination are doing a lot of quiet work in the formalism). Don't name the concept until the end. Keep the whole thing, debrief included, to 900 words max.
