I'm in a reading group on value theory, and we keep returning to what gets called the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: the warm bath, the malicious glee, the relief of a headache lifting, and the satisfaction of finishing a proof have very little in common, yet hedonism seems to require them to be good in virtue of one shared feature. I've been sympathetic to hedonism because it promises a single scale on which to weigh hard choices, and last week a more committed hedonist in the group offered an analogy that seemed to rescue that promise. Pleasures, he said, are like money. A dollar and a rupee don't look alike, and you can't spend a rupee in Chicago, but each has a rate at which it exchanges into a common measure, and that exchangeability is what makes them the same kind of economic thing. Likewise, he went on, pleasures differ endlessly in object and felt texture, but each exchanges into a single hedonic value. That, he argued, is all the hedonist needs to say that every pleasure is good for the same reason and good in amounts that can be compared and summed; the heterogeneity objection dissolves, and the additive calculus he was using to weigh lives and policies is licensed.

I want the analogy audited rather than politely accepted or brushed off. What maps onto what — the distinct currencies, the exchange rate, the mint or issuing authority, the account balance, purchasing power, inflation, counterfeiting? Where does the mapping break in ways that matter for his conclusion, as opposed to ways that merely show no analogy is perfect? I'm especially worried that money is a social institution whose value is purely instrumental — a medium for getting what you actually want — whereas the hedonist takes pleasure to be the final thing wanted; and I'm unsure an exchange rate between the pleasure of a joke and the pleasure of a proof could be anything more than a metaphor. Does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table — that the diversity of pleasures poses no objection because every pleasure translates into one commensurable hedonic value — or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? And if it fails, is there a better analogy that does the hedonist's work without importing the same problems?
