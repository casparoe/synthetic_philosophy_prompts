I sit on the board of a regional water authority, and on Thursday we vote on whether to issue bonds for a storm-surge and stormwater tunnel system with a design life of 150 years. The engineers' cost estimates are no longer contested; what is contested is the discount schedule. Under our current policy, a uniform 5% annual rate, the benefit–cost ratio is 0.6 and the project fails. Under the alternative the finance staff were asked to test, 3.5% for years 0–50, 1.5% for years 50–100, and 0.5% thereafter, the ratio is 1.3. The advocates of the alternative argue that pure time preference, discounting a future benefit merely because it lies in the future rather than because later generations will be richer, the asset will be obsolete, or the benefits are uncertain, has no justification in public decisions with century-scale consequences. Defenders of the current rate reply that our ratepayers and bond markets express steep positive time preference, that a near-zero long-run rate would make every speculative far-future project look attractive, and that the policy would tax people alive now for benefits to unborn generations who may be much wealthier and may not want the asset. Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete discounting policy plus a bottom-line vote on the bond issuance, explain in plain terms what pure time preference does and does not justify, and engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side.
