I follow a rule that I think of as a working version of the pragmatic maxim, and I want to know whether it is defensible as an epistemic policy or is merely a bias disguised as a principle. Before I let myself treat a dispute as a real disagreement, or before I commit myself to one side, I require that someone state what conceivable difference it would make to future experience or practice if one side rather than the other were true. If no one can state such a difference, I do not declare the dispute meaningless; I simply refuse to spend further attention or credence on it, and I treat the two positions as different temperaments or different names for one working reality. This policy recently led me to bow out of an argument in my own field between two theoretical constructs that are by everyone's admission behaviorally indistinguishable so far, though differently motivated. My colleagues thought I was dodging an important question, and one said I had smuggled in the verificationism the field claims to have abandoned. Is the rule defensible as a policy for allocating belief and attention rather than as a theory of what words mean? Does it nevertheless collapse into verificationism in practice, even if I disavow that theory? And is there a corrected version of the rule that keeps its economy without foreclosing, prematurely, the possibility that a credible difference might still be found?
