I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar on self-deception, and the instructor has a policy of not stating her own account until the final meeting. Instead, she issues rulings on particular cases, and I've been keeping a log of them. So far she has ruled as follows. A gambler who can recite the arithmetic showing that his betting system has negative expected value, yet remains convinced that the system gives him an edge, is self-deceived. A patient awaiting a biopsy result who decides the lump is almost certainly benign, and who is genuinely shocked when the result comes back malignant, is not self-deceived, because the evidence available before the call did not yet point clearly either way. A husband who has strong evidence that his wife is faithful but becomes convinced she is having an affair, and who is made miserable by that belief, is not self-deceived, since the false belief is not one he was motivated to hold. A woman whose boyfriend is in fact unfaithful, who deliberately deletes his messages without reading them and sets herself daily reminders to "trust him," and who by June reports that she fully believes him, is self-deceived. And a student who keeps telling himself, "I've already failed, so studying is pointless," while continuing to study hard, and who says he does not actually believe he has failed, is not self-deceived—that is more like a self-soothing performance. I think there are at least two principles that fit her rulings so far, and I cannot tell which one she is running.

Next week she will rule on this case. A doctoral candidate is told by her committee that her central argument has a serious gap. She takes the objection seriously for several weeks and tries to write replies, none of which work. Over the summer, without forming any plan to do so, without avoiding her notes, and without seeking out sympathetic readers, she finds that each rereading makes the objection look more like a misreading, until by fall she genuinely believes the objection rested on a confusion. Her desire to be finished clearly did the psychological work, but she never caught herself doing anything strategic, and she would deny ever having deceived herself.

Predict how the instructor will rule on the doctoral candidate. Lay out the candidate principles that fit her log so far, say what each one predicts for the new case, and give your best guess with the reasons for preferring it. Then identify a further case I could propose to her whose verdict would settle which principle she is actually running. Tell me where your prediction is most likely to fail, and what kind of clarification or additional ruling would change your mind. Please write the response in American English.
