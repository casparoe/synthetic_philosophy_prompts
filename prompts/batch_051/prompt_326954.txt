A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about Hannah Arendt's account of Eichmann, and I came away convinced that the best way to sharpen our disagreement is to read a film through the thesis rather than keep arguing about the trial in the abstract. The film is Jonathan Glazer's The Zone of Interest, drawn loosely from Martin Amis's novel. It follows Rudolf Höss, commandant of Auschwitz, and his family through their deceptively normal domestic routines: gardening, a child's birthday, a promotion, visits from family, all in a house whose garden wall is the wall of the camp. The film never shows the interior of the camp; what reaches the audience is mostly off-screen: gunshots, barking dogs, furnaces, screaming, smoke, and the characters' matter-of-fact references to what they are administering.

I want a careful critical reading of the film through Arendt's banality-of-evil thesis, as developed in her book on the Eichmann trial. As I understand that thesis, Arendt's unsettling claim was not that Eichmann was an exception, but that he was unremarkable: not a sadist and not a fanatic, but ambitious, conventional, careerist, and radically thoughtless, so immersed in clichés and administrative procedure that he never meaningfully took up the perspective of the people his work would destroy. My friend objected that The Zone of Interest actually pushes back on this. In his view, Höss is not thoughtless in the relevant sense: he knows exactly what the camp is, he discusses the details of transport and crematorium capacity, and then reads bedtime stories and plans vacations. That looks less like an absence of thinking than like an active, willed segregation of the domestic from the administered, and the film's power comes precisely from showing that the segregation takes effort and produces its own strains: the mother-in-law who leaves, the wife's panic at the prospect of being moved, the children's blankness. I am not sure my friend is right.

Please write an essay that treats both the film and Arendt with as much fidelity as possible. Where does the film genuinely illustrate her claim about the ordinariness of the perpetrator, and where does it show something her category of thoughtlessness does not capture, such as knowing compartmentalization, instrumental processing, or deliberate emotional insulation? Is the family in the garden made exemplary of the banality of evil, or does the film make Höss a different kind of perpetrator from the desk-bound Eichmann, with different responsibilities and a different relation to the killing? Do not resolve the tension too early: if the most honest reading is that the film simultaneously confirms and complicates the thesis, keep both verdicts in play and say what the film contributes that reading Arendt alone would not.
