I am taking a philosophy of mathematics course which has just turned to the question of the actual infinite, and something from last week's tutorial has been nagging at me ever since. We had been working through Hilbert's hotel and the familiar point that the even numbers can be put into one-to-one correspondence with the natural numbers, so that a proper part of a collection turns out to stand in a relation we normally reserve for wholes of the same size. A classmate kept objecting that none of this could be right, because the whole is greater than the part: the evens are strictly contained among the naturals, so there must be fewer of them, and any construction that suggests otherwise is just a trick of description. Our tutor pressed him to spell out what 'greater' means once it is detached from finite collections, and he more or less replied that greater is greater; the tutorial moved on before anyone had actually untangled it, and I realised I could not say with confidence where the slogan has force and where it runs out.

I would like a careful unpicking of the maxim that the whole is greater than the part as it gets deployed against actual infinities. Please lay out the distinct precise claims someone could be making with it — claims about finite wholes only, claims about proper parts and one-to-one correspondence, claims about whether 'greater than' has one sense or several — and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false once actually infinite totalities are admitted. Then identify which of those readings, if any, would actually license dismissing Hilbert's hotel as a trick, and which of them a finitist, who denies that any actually infinite totality exists, could consistently accept. I am less after a verdict on Cantor than a menu of disambiguations I can take back to the tutorial, so I can see exactly how much work the slogan is doing and how much is being done by an unexamined slide between senses.
