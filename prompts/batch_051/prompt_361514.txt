I chair the student conduct committee at a small liberal arts college. Our handbook, last revised in 1987, still prohibits "public displays of affection beyond holding hands" in the student center. I've been here 20 years and its never once been enforced; two former deans of students have said they didn't know it was on the books. Last week a student filed a complaint against a same-sex couple who, frankly, were doing nothing that would raise an eyebrow at any coffee shop nearby. Some committee members want to enforce the rule because it's still in the handbook. Others want to treat it as void through desuetude — the idea that laws or rules can lose their force through prolonged non-enforcement and public reliance on that non-enforcement. I'm aware that desuetude is normally a doctrine about statutes, not student handbooks, and that there's a live worry: if a committee can let a rule die through neglect, what does that do to the rule-of-law idea that rules bind until they're repealed?

Draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the hearing. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, say which body — the committee, the student government, the faculty senate, the trustees — should formally strike or revise the rule if that's your recommendation, and engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side. I also want one explicit paragraph on whether this case is better understood as desuetude or just selective non-enforcement, because those two look different to me.
