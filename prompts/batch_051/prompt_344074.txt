I'm taking a social choice theory course and also sitting in on an epistemic game theory seminar, and the two just collided in a way I can't shake. In social choice we proved Gibbard–Satterthwaite: with at least three candidates, if a voting rule is deterministic, defined on all strict preference profiles, and every candidate can win under some profile, then making truthful ranking a dominant strategy forces the rule to be dictatorial. You literally cannot build a non-dictatorial election where it's always in everyone's interest to just report their real ranking. But the seminar covered proper scoring rules, like the Brier score, where you ask someone to report a probability distribution over some future event, then score them once the event is observed. Under a strictly proper scoring rule, a person maximizes expected score by reporting their true credences. So honesty is a dominant strategy, and it doesn't collapse into anything dictatorial — in fact there's no aggregation problem at all. The parallel I keep seeing: both are mechanisms for truthfully eliciting an internal attitude, a ranking in one case and a credence distribution in the other, by making lying weakly dominated. One is a famous impossibility theorem, the other is a standard tool. I want to know whether this parallel is deep or superficial. Where exactly does the map between them break? Is it just that Gibbard–Satterthwaite deals with ordinal preferences over three or more alternatives and no cardinal score to optimize, while a proper scoring rule leverages a verifiable outcome and a cardinal reward? Or is the real difference that voting has to combine many reports into one social choice, whereas a scoring rule pays each individual separately? And here's a prediction I can't test: if the analogy were deep, you'd expect either a Gibbard–Satterthwaite-style impossibility to show up for eliciting rankings via scores, or a Brier-style escape hatch for voting that gets non-dictatorial strategy-proofness. Does either actually happen, and what would that tell me about the parallel?
