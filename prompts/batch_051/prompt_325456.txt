I've been turning over a parallel I can't decide about, and I'd like a second opinion. The anti-commodification line in the repugnant markets literature says putting a price on certain goods — kidneys, votes, queue spots, grades, surge pricing after a hurricane — doesn't only trade them, it corrupts the practice that gave them their value: once you can buy a kidney or a place in line, the meaning of the thing changes, and what had been a gift or a civic act becomes a sale. Goodhart's law in policy and machine learning says that when a measure is turned into a target it stops being a good measure, because people start optimizing the metric rather than the underlying quality it was supposed to track. Structurally these look the same to me: introduce an incentive or an exchange, and the incentive feeds back into and degrades the very thing it was meant to record or allocate. But I can't tell whether that's a deep identity of structure or the kind of analogy that sounds deep and isn't. In the Goodhart case the damage seems to come from the metric being a lossy proxy that agents game without improving the target. In the commodification case the damage is supposed to be something else — a change in norms, motives, or the social meaning of the good, not slippage between a proxy and its target. I'd like you to state the mapping precisely, say what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer from one to the other, and then name something the parallel predicts that turns out false so I can see where it breaks. If it holds up I'd like to use it in a paper on repugnant markets; if not I'd rather find out now than in a referee report.
