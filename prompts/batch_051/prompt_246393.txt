I'm a retired chemist who fell into philosophy of language a couple years ago, and I keep tripping over the same thing. In my old life, a law with exceptions wasn't a law. If I said "the reaction goes to completion, except when it doesn't," I'd be laughed out of the room. And when I read about historical linguistics, the old sound-law people seemed to agree with me: sound change is regular, and the apparent counterexamples are just analogy. Fine. But then the same field talks about synchronic grammar, and it says English has a past-tense rule — add -ed — and then calmly lists go/went, sing/sang, bring/brought, hit/hit as exceptions to that very rule. That sounds like "the reaction goes to completion, except when it doesn't" to me. So I built an argument, and now I can't find the exit. It goes:

(1) A genuine rule admits no exceptions; a generalization with exceptions is a tendency or a list, not a rule.
(2) The English past tense has exceptions to the -ed pattern: sing/sang, bring/brought, hit/hit, and go/went — where go/went is even worse, because the past form doesn't share a stem with the present (suppletion).
(3) So the past tense is not governed by a genuine rule.
(4) You can't save it by saying the irregulars follow their own rules. Either each irregular form is its own rule, which is just a list wearing a lab coat, or you group them (sing/sang, ring/rang, drink/drank), but those groups have exceptions too (bring/brought, not brang, at least in my English; think/thought), and no rule at all gets you from go to went.
(5) What goes for the past tense goes for every grammatical "rule" I can find: plurals have mice and oxen, comparatives have good/better, and so on.
(6) So English has no genuine grammatical rules.
(7) But that can't be right. The wug-style tests and everything that's come after show that children and adults extend the -ed pattern to nonsense verbs they've never heard — blick -> blicked, and even new verbs like "to Google" -> "googled." That's the signature of a rule, not a list of stored forms.
(8) So English has a genuine past-tense rule after all, contradicting (3).

Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? My gut says keep (1) and keep (7). I dont want to water down "rule" until it just means "tendency," and I don't see how to deny the productivity data. But then I can't see how (3), (4), or (5) survive.
