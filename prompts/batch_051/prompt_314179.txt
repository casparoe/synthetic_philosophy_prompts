I've been trying to get clear on a debate that has run for decades without converging, and I've started to suspect the impasse isn't where the participants say it is. The question is whether psychopaths know that what they do is wrong. The knowledge-deficit view says they do not. On this view, whatever verbal facility psychopaths display is hollow or parroted: their justifications on moral/conventional tasks differ from typical people's, they tend to treat moral rules as authority-dependent rather than harm-based, and their use of the word wrong is not backed by the aversive responses that make an utterance a genuine moral judgment. The motivational-deficit view says the opposite: psychopaths know perfectly well that what they do is wrong — in interviews and structured tasks, adults with psychopathy can identify moral violations, label them wrong, distinguish them from mere convention-breaking, and give harm-based justifications. What they lack is not knowledge but caring: wrongness does not engage their emotions or constrain their choices, and their behavior tracks immediate advantage rather than the judgments they can state. Both camps now appeal to what looks like the same body of experimental results, so the disagreement no longer seems to be about what the data are.

My working hypothesis is that the debate is really about the criterion for knowing that an action is wrong: whether correct explicit classification is sufficient, or whether an emotionally grounded evaluative understanding has to be in place. But I can see two other live options: a hidden empirical disagreement about whether psychopaths' correct verbal classifications are produced by the same underlying processes as other people's, and a disagreement about which sense of knows matters for moral responsibility. What kind of disagreement is this, actually?
