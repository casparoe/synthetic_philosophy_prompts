Assume, for the sake of the prompt, that the Hofstadter line on the self is basically right: the "I" is a pattern, not a thing; a self is a strange loop that can in principle run at lower resolution in other people's heads; and people who know you well host degraded copies of you, so in a literal sense some of your selfhood survives, or keeps unfolding, in them even after you die. I've spent the last year getting convinced of this, and now I want to write a book about it — not a summary of Hofstadter, but something that pushes past the parts everyone already argues about. I have maybe a year and can only do justice to two or three big questions. What I want from you is an agenda, not a survey and not a reading list. Which three open questions or underexplored implications of this picture deserve the year most, and why its those rather than the standard moves? Rank them. For each, say what I'd get if I made real progress on it — a changed view of grief, a testable claim, a different picture of moral responsibility, whatever — and defend the ranking with explicit criteria: tractability inside a year, how many other questions hinge on it, how surprising or load-bearing the payoff would be. Then tell me what would make you reshuffle the order. If there's a distinction most people writing on this miss, bake it into the ranking rather than tacking it on at the end.
