An argument has been bothering me because each step looks right, but the conclusion seems to prove too much. It runs:

(1) Self-control is exercised only where a desire conflicts with one's better judgment; where there is no conflict, there is nothing for self-control to do.
(2) Where such a conflict exists, self-control consists in acting against a desire that one's own judgment condemns.
(3) Acting against a desire that one's own judgment condemns is either repression (treating the desire as an alien force to be silenced) or mere prudence (treating it as one's own but strategically restraining it).
(4) Repression is not a virtue, and mere prudence is not a virtue of character.
(5) Therefore self-control is never a virtue of character.
(6) But temperance is self-control directed at the bodily appetites, and temperance is a virtue of character.
(7) So temperance both is and is not a virtue of character.

I cannot accept (7), yet I cannot see which step fails. I would most like to keep (6), since it sounds definitional, but then the failure must lie somewhere in (1)–(4). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost the remaining claims?
