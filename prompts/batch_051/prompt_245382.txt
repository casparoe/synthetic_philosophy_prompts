In my Chinese philosophy seminar we've been going back and forth between Mozi's impartial care (jian'ai) and the Confucian insistence that care has to be graded, and I keep coming back to one particular objection from Mencius. The way I read him, Mencius's argument isn't just that impartial care is hard; it's that it's self-defeating. Care mostly gets produced inside special relationships, and if you try to care equally for your own mother and a stranger's mother, you don't end up with equal care — you end up with thinned-out care, because you've reasoned yourself out of the special attachments that actually do the work. The text mostly treats this as a fact about human psychology and social life, but I'm starting to wonder whether it secretly depends on the setup of the time, when care work was mostly done inside households by kin.

So here's what I want to ask. Suppose we now live in a world where a lot of care can be delivered impersonally: a tax-funded childcare and eldercare system, mandatory insurance pools where strangers take on each other's risks, anonymous charitable clearinghouses, professional care workers paid by the state or by mutual aid funds. Does Mencius's argument survive that shift? Is the objection a claim about human motivation that institutions can't touch, a claim about how care gets produced (which institutions might handle fine), or some mix of both? I'm interested in what happens to the argument itself, not in wheter we should all go be Mohists or Confucians today.
