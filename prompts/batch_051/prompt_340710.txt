I retired from geology a few years back and have been working through the philosophy shelf I never had time for. Goodman's new riddle of induction keeps bugging me, maybe because I spent a career around actual emeralds. The setup, as I understand it: every emerald we've examined so far is green, so induction seems to liscense the prediction that all emeralds are green. Now define a predicate grue as follows: a thing is grue if it is examined before the year 2100 and is green, or if it is not examined before 2100 and is blue. All our observed emeralds are grue too, since they were examined before 2100 and turned out green. So the same evidence seems just as well to support the prediction that all emeralds are grue, which says emeralds first examined after 2100 will be blue. The riddle is to say what principled reason we have to project green rather than grue.

So far my notes contain five candidate solutions. (1) Goodman's own: only project entrenched predicates — green has a long record of successful projection, grue has none. (2) The natural-kind line: green picks out a real join in nature, grue is a gerrymandered conjunction, and induction should track the joints. (3) The simplicity line: the grue hypothesis carries an extra time index and is therefore more complex, so Occam's razor favors green. (4) The Bayesian line: we just assign a much higher prior probability to the green hypothesis, and no special rule about grue is needed. (5) The Carnap-style line: relative to a language whose primitive predicates are purely qualitative, grue is positional rather than purely qualitative, so a sound inductive logic would not treat it on a par with green.

I'd like one more candidate solution that is genuinely different from these five — not a restatement, not a special case, and not a mashup of two of them. It should ideally diagnose a different point in Goodman's argument where the trick gets done. Then tell me honestly whether it actually solves the riddle or just relocates the problem. If you think those five are all there is, I'd rather hear that and why than get a sixth that is secretly one of the five in a hat, but only do that after actually looking for a sixth. My real interest is geting clear on the structure of the problem, not the history.
