I am compiling objections to a legal market in kidneys from living, consenting adult sellers. The objections I already have are these: (1) the exploitation/coercion objection—poverty either makes sellers' consent less than voluntary or lets buyers wrongfully take advantage of their desperation; (2) the commodification objection—putting a price on kidneys corrupts the meaning of the body or of human dignity, in a way not reducible to the harms the transaction causes; (3) the harm-to-seller objection—nephrectomy carries real and partly long-term medical risks, and sellers in poverty are least able to bear them; (4) the crowding-out objection—paid procurement would erode altruistic donation and the gift-based norms that sustain organ transfer; (5) the inequality objection—a market would let the rich buy priority and consolidate a class of poor organ providers; (6) the slippery-slope objection—legalizing kidney sales makes later extensions to other organs or to riskier forms of selling the body more likely; and (7) the black-market objection—legalization would not dissolve trafficking or transplant tourism and might normalize or entrench them. I take these to be the standard objections. Give me one further objection to a legal live-donor kidney market that is genuinely different from all seven—not a rewording, special case, or combination of any of them—and then tell me honestly whether it is any good.
