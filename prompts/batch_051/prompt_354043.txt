Here's a rule I follow in arguments, and I'm honestly not sure if it's a serious demand for rigor or just a trick to avoid ever getting cornered. Whenever someone gives me an argument, I won't accept the move from their premises to their conclusion until they spell out the rule of inference they're using and justify that rule. And if they justify it by invoking some more general rule, I ask them to justify *that* one, and so on. In practice it comes up less with formal arguments and more with everyday ones: someone says the evidence "supports" their view, I ask what principle gets us from evidence to support, then why that principle tracks the truth, then why *that* one does, and off we go. Nobody ever makes it past step two, so I usually win by infinite regress, which is starting to feel less like winning and more like a bug in how I argue. I know the standard line about Carroll's tortoise—Achilles keeps adding something like "if the premises are true then the conclusion follows" as an extra premise and the tortoise still won't take the step—is that rules of inference aren't premises, your supposed to just accept them or not. But that seems like it just relocates the question: if an inference rule can't be justified by a premise, what *is* its justification, and am I ever entitled to demand one? Is there a principled place to stop asking "why is that inference good," or is my policy just a fancy way of never having to update? Is there a version of it that's defensible, and how would I tell from the inside whether I'm doing epistemology or just filibustering?
