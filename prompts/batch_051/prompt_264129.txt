Here's a puzzle I keep picking at. In the Platonia dilemma, twenty people are each given the same offer: if exactly one of them sends a reply by the deadline, that person gets a billion dollars; if zero people reply, or more than one does, nobody gets anything; and there's no way for them to coordinate. Replies are free to send, and delivery is normally treated as perfect. The standard superrational answer in that setup — calculate the strategy that does best for you under the assumption that the other nineteen, being identical to you, adopt the same strategy — is for each person to send with probability 1/20.

Now vary just one thing. Suppose each person can still send at most one reply, but each reply has an independent 20% chance of never arriving, and everybody knows these odds. So a reply actually arrives with probability 0.8, and failures are independent across people. Still twenty players, still a billion dollars, still no coordination, still free to send.

Run the superrational calculation again. Does the unreliable channel just force the sending probability up a bit, and by how much exactly? I want the algebra, not just the number. Then generalize: if each reply arrives with probability q, what's the superrational probabilty of sending? Is there a reliability threshold below which the mixed-strategy answer stops making sense — where "send with probability 1/(20q)" would ask for a probability above 1 — and what should the superrational player do then? And, separately, does the plain game-theoretic (non-superrational) analysis shift at all when delivery is less than perfect: for instance, does "exactly one person sends" stop being a Nash equilibrium?
