I’m trying to write an essay about whether machines can make genuine intuitive leaps, not just recombine things from their training distribution. I keep coming back to Poincaré’s bit in *Science and Method* where he’d been hammering away at Fuchsian functions, gave up, went on a trip, and then as he stepped onto a bus the solution arrived fully formed. He describes it as evidence of unconscious work—ideas combining below the surface while he wasn’t attending to them. I want to argue that if we take Poincaré seriously, then the line between “recombining old ideas” and “genuine novelty” is suspicous, because his breakthru also looks, from the outside, like recombination plus a very strong feeling of suddenness. But then I worry that’s too deflationary and maybe it misses the thing that actually matters about creative novelty, which is that the person is changed by it and can use it going forward.

Could you act as an expert in philosophy of mind, creativity, and machine learning, and give me comments on the following essay skeleton? Please think carefully before answering. Maybe start by brainstorming a few ways someone could push back on my main claim, then tell me which parts are actually interesting and which are dorm-room metaphysics. Here’s the skeleton:

1. Open with Poincaré’s bus incident as a case where a human breakthrough has the external shape of recombination: he already knew the elements—Fuchsian functions, prior dead ends, the transformations involved—and the new combination arrived without explicit step-by-step reasoning.

2. Claim: when people say LLMs can’t leap beyond their training distribution, they often use a double standard. For humans, they treat the felt suddenness of insight as evidence of something non-mechanical; for machines, they treat the same surface features as evidence of mere interpolation.

3. Introduce a distinction between weak novelty (a new combination that solves a standing problem) and strong novelty (a new combination that changes the space of problems). Ask whether Poincaré’s leap was weak or strong, and whether any LLM output could ever be strong in the same sense.

The novelist angle: maybe strong novelty isn’t a property of the output itself but of the agent’s subsequent relation to it—how it reorganizes memory, style, future intentions, the kind of thing a writer means by “finding my voice.” If so, the interesting question isn’t whether the machine had a Poincaré moment, but whether it has the right kind of continuity for that moment to reorganize anything.

5. End with a risky thought: our own sense of “genuine insight” might be a trained response to certain internal signals. Which would mean an LLM with a different architecture could have its own version of the bus moment that we wouldn’t recognize as such.
