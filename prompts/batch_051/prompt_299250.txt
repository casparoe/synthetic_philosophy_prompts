I am a novelist, and a puzzle that began in my own revision practice has hardened into a view I should like tested. While working on a novel I noticed that many facts about the story world — a character's childhood, the exact layout of a house, the words of an offstage quarrel — are never written down, and often I have never decided them myself. I have come to hold what I call the Ink Standard: an unwritten fact is true of the story only if its truth would leave a different trace in ink, that is, only if some possible sequel, prequel, close reading, or revised edition would be forced to treat it otherwise than its negation. Where two rival backstories are such that no actual or possible text could ever be made to differ on account of them, there is no fact of the matter; the question is empty rather than merely undecided.

I suspect the standard travels beyond fiction. In a scientific theory the data are the text and the postulated unobservable entities are the backstory: on my view, a theory's real ontology consists in exactly that hidden structure without which the accessible record would have been different, and two theories whose hidden structures are discernible by no possible observation differ merely in notation, not in what there is. Information that could not in principle disclose itself in the record is information about nothing real.

I am aware this sounds close to verificationism, to structural realism, and to fictionalism, and it may be one of them in less writerly dress. Give me an honest assessment: is the principle coherent as stated; does it already exist under a better name; and what are the three strongest objections, with some judgement as to whether any is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable?
