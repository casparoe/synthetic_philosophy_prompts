Please explain, in careful conceptual terms, what exactly the gradualness in a gradual-replacement uploading scenario is supposed to contribute to the survival of the original person. In the usual description, each neuron is replaced one at a time by a functionally equivalent artificial unit while the subject maintains an unbroken report of consciousness, forms new memories, and exhibits ordinary psychological continuity throughout the procedure. Many people have the intuition that this procedure preserves the person, whereas a destructive scan-and-copy upload kills the original and creates a distinct person. But it is unclear what premise licenses the asymmetry. If survival is fixed by a functional or psychological relation, then both procedures should have the same result; if survival requires the persistence of the biological substrate, then the gradual procedure destroys that substrate piecemeal; and if survival requires continuity of consciousness, then the defender of the intuition owes an account of whether the relevant continuity is preserved at the whole-brain level even though individual neurons are being replaced, and why that level rather than the constituent-neuron level is the relevant one. Please explain what the gradual-replacement intuition must be assuming about personal identity or about what matters in survival. In particular, address whether the absence of a sharp boundary in the gradual case is doing genuine metaphysical work, or whether it is only an epistemic fact about our inability to pick a non-arbitrary cutoff for a vague predicate. Distinguish clearly between the claim that personal identity is vague and the claim that we are merely uncertain about where the boundary lies.
