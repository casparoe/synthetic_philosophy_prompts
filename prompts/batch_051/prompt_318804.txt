I have been trying to get a precise grip on something I see in disputes over contested events. A recording once functioned as a neutral referee: if someone produced footage of a public official making a remark, or a bystander's phone video of an altercation, that largely settled the factual question, because the recording predated the dispute and was not created to score a point in it. As synthetic media has improved, that role seems to be eroding in two distinct ways. First, fabrications can be passed off as genuine. Second, and more importantly, a genuine recording can now be dismissed as probably fake, so the party who wants to deny a real event gains a benefit merely because fakes have become plausible. I am not asking for a term for deepfakes themselves. I want to know whether the prior condition — the shared, strategically neutral baseline of records to which public argument could appeal — has an established name, and whether that second pattern, the advantage accruing to someone who denies a genuine record, has a name of its own. If the nearest labels only partly fit, say how the two concepts relate and where the boundary between them falls. A bare label will not settle it for me; I want the phenomenon carved at the joints. Keep your answer to at most 300 words.
