I keep seeing people defend the outside view as a kind of epistemic modesty: when you forecast a project you should mostly ignore the rich story about your specific plan and anchor instead to how similar projects actually went. Kahneman and Lovallo made that case forever ago, and it's become the default scold for optimistic planning. Now here's the thing I can't stop chewing on. Suppose you have a system that can assemble a reference class on the fly from millions of logged past projects, filtering on hundreds of features: roughly this size, this domain, this team structure, this funding model, these first-three-month signals, and so on, until the class is basically your project with the serial numbers filed off. It can even produce, for that hyper-narrow class, a distribution of outcomes and a narrrative version of the typical failure mode. Is that still the outside view, or has the outside view quietly become an inside view at higher resolution? Does the original argument for preferring the outside view survive this development, or did it depend on the reference class staying coarse enough that it couldn't just absorb the inside story? If it survives, say exactly which premise still does the work and why fine-grained classes don't break it; if it doesn't, say what the lesson of the planning fallacy should now be.
