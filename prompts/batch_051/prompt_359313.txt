I have been going back and forth about deflationism about truth, and I would like a negotiated verdict rather than a summary of the literature. On reflection, I find the view Ramsey gestures at in 'Facts and Propositions' (1927) — that calling something true does not ascribe a further property but only reasserts the proposition itself, and that there is no separate problem of truth, only a linguistic muddle about the word 'true' — almost irresistibly clean. It dissolves the correspondence problem, explains why truth seems transparent, and gives me a tidy decision procedure: whenever I am tempted to ask what truth consists in, I can translate 'p is true' back to 'p', and the supposed question disappears. The theory has the systematic virtues I usually trust: simplicity, unification, and a way of saying that the only hard problems are about belief and meaning, not about a further relation to the world.

And yet I cannot shake a firm intuition: when I say that a theory is true, I intend to say something about how things are, not merely to endorse or reassert the theory. There seems to be a real difference between being justified, or useful, or what inquirers would converge on, and being true — and that difference does practical and explanatory work. It lets me say that a claim can be fully justified and still false, that past theories were believed and useful but not true, and that truth is a standard of belief that outruns my evidence. If deflationism tells me that 'is true' is just a disquotational device, I feel it has subtracted exactly the thing I was trying to understand.

I do not want a survey of deflationist versus correspondence or primitivist positions. I want someone to walk me through what each move would cost. What would it take for the intuition to be debunked as an artifact of a misleading subject–predicate picture of language, rather than a datum to be respected? What would it take for deflationism to be amended or abandoned to accommodate the sense that truth is a standard and a way the world is? And is there a reading of 'saying how things are' on which both the theory and the intuition survive — or should I conclude that one of them has to go?
