I write about science and ideas for a living, and for a piece on why wordplay amuses us even when nobody comes off looking stupid, I need a case built to order rather than whatever joke I happen to remember. Heres the spec.

Construct a short joke — ideally a pun or a bit of pure wordplay — that a normal person could tell in a pub, engineered so that the incongruity account and the benign violation account both predict genuine amusement, while the superiority account predicts little or none. The joke should have no human or group as its butt: no one is humiliated, outsmarted, or exposed, and theres no status comparison anywhere. I'd also like the relief account to have to work for its money: don't build in any prior suspense, awkwardness, or taboo content for it to feed on. Keep the joke itself under about sixty words, and no famous or copyrighted jokes.

Once you've got the joke, run the checks for all four accounts — incongruity, relief, benign violation, and superiority. For each one, say whether it predicts the listener finds the thing funny and why. Then tell me which exact feature of the joke is doing the theoretical work, and what happens when you swap it out: if you retell the same joke so that it does target someone, does the superiority verdict flip and the case collapse back into the boring everyone-agrees kind? Finish with how a defender of the superiority theory would push back — what they'd say is really going on for the listener, and whether their defence succeeds.

The case should generate the verdicts from the theories rather than just stipulating the outcome. And do the answer in British English, please.
