I am putting together a sorting quiz for an upper-level undergraduate course in philosophy of law, to be used after students have worked through the problem of naked statistical evidence. I would like you to write the complete instrument: ten diagnostic questions, a scoring key runnable as a decision tree, and a one-paragraph description of each leaf. The quiz should sort a respondent into a position solely on the basis of how they answer concrete evidentiary cases, never by asking them which theoretical label they prefer.

The underlying question is: when, if ever, may bare statistical evidence — evidence that places the defendant inside a frequency or reference class but does not single the defendant out — satisfy a legal standard of proof?

The instrument must be able to place respondents into these five positions, and should use exactly these labels for the leaves:

1. Threshold probabilism: a standard of proof fixes only a probability threshold, so a sufficiently strong bare statistic satisfies preponderance and, if strong enough, beyond reasonable doubt.

2. Individualized-evidence requirement: bare statistics never suffice, at any strength, because proof must be about this particular defendant and this particular act.

3. Anti-luck account: bare statistics fail because they do not counterfactually track the defendant's conduct; the evidence would be unchanged in nearby worlds where the defendant is innocent.

4. Normic-support account: proof requires evidence on which the defendant's innocence would be an abnormality calling for special explanation, not merely a low-probability outcome.

5. Moral-expressivist account: the defect is not probabilistic but communicative and normative; a finding of liability or guilt on frequencies treats the person as an instance of a class rather than as an agent being blamed.

The ten questions should each present a short concrete case (two or three sentences) followed by three answer options phrased as case-specific judgments — for example, liable/not liable, convict/acquit, or "this evidence suffices only if it is combined with …" — never as endorsements of a theory.

Two of the ten questions must be the canonical blue bus case and the canonical gatecrasher case, presented in your own words rather than quoted. The remaining eight cases should be new variants you devise. Design them so that, taken together, the following come apart:

- whether the respondent is willing to allow any bare statistic to satisfy a civil preponderance;
- whether the respondent is willing to allow any bare statistic to satisfy the criminal standard, including when it is set at 99% rather than a bare preponderance;
- whether the respondent's objection survives when the statistic is built entirely from facts about this defendant (for example, a pattern in the defendant's own past conduct or a database cold hit as opposed to a population frequency);
- whether the respondent treats a verdict as an assertion of what happened, as a vehicle of blame, or as something with a public meaning of its own.

At least one case should be constructed specifically to test whether the two modal accounts — the anti-luck and normic-support views — actually diverge at the level of verdicts: a case where the statistic is exceptionally reliable but where an error, if it occurred, would be a bare coincidence. After writing that case, state in one sentence whether the two views genuinely come apart on it, and if they do not, flag the two leaves as inseparable by case judgments alone.

Before writing the questions, begin from the logical space rather than from the list of names: lay out the dimensions along which positions can differ (the civil/criminal line, the demand for case-specificity, the demand for counterfactual tracking, the demand for explanatory abnormality, and the role of verdict meaning), then locate the five positions in that space. After the key, list any coherent-looking answer patterns to which no standard position corresponds, and say whether each is empty because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Finally, write the scoring key as a decision-tree walkthrough: each internal node should be a question or answer pattern, and each leaf one of the five positions. Include one-sentence leaf descriptions stating the most awkward commitment of each position, written for a student who has just been sorted into it.
