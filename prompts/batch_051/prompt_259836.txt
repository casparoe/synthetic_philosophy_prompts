Two people in my deparment got into it over whether self-deception is actually possible, and I sat there unable to referee. Here's roughly how it went.

(1) Mina said it's conceptually impossible. Deceiving someone is getting them to believe something you know to be false. So deceiving yourself would mean getting yourself to believe p while also knowing not-p. A single mind can't hold both at once, so self-deception can't happen.

(2) Dev said that's only impossible if you insist the whole thing happens at a single instant. Real cases are drawn out: a man's marriage is failing, he has all the evidence, but he avoids the topic, reads his wife's messages charitably, remembers the good days, and over months he comes to genuinely believe things are fine. At no point does he hold p and not-p simultaneously, so the paradox never bites.

(3) Mina replied that this describes motivated reasoning, not deception. Deception needs an intention to produce a false belief. In Dev's story there's no moment where the man currently believes the marriage is failing and then deliberately hides that from himself. He only ever suspects, and suspicion isn't the same as knowing. So there's nothing being hidden.

(4) Dev said "suspicion" is doing a lot of work there. Often the person would, if pressed, say something like "I know it's over but I can't face it." That sounds like one part of the mind knowing, and another part arranging for that knowledge not to reach awareness. That's not an exotic picture — it's how ordinary self-deception is usually described.

(5) Mina came back: but if you split the mind like that, you've given up on self-deception. Now it's one subpersonal part deceiving another, which is just two people in one skull. The original problem recurs inside the parts: does the deceiving part believe not-p? If yes, it's a knowing homunculus and we've explained nothing. If no, the process isn't deception at all, just a causal system ending up with a false output.

(6) Dev said she was policing a word rather than engaging the phenomenon. If a person ends up sincerely believing p because some of their own cognitive machinery was arranged, by them, to favour p when the evidence favoured not-p, that's what we mean by self-deception. It doesn't matter whether you call it one mind, two subsystems, or a process stretched over time. It happens, so it's possible.
