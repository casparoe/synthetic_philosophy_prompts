The separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism says, roughly, that a person is allowed to trade Tuesday's pain against Wednesday's greater pleasure, but utilitarianism also sums gains and losses across distinct people as if they were time-slices of one big person—and the boundary between persons is not the same kind of thing as the boundary between days. Here are five moves people make in response, some defensive and some concessive. Please sort them into a tier list by how much argumentative work you think each genuinely does (top tier means it can carry the debate, bottom tier means it's mostly rhetorical), with one sentence per tier justifying it. The moves: (a) the time-slice analogy is fine—an individual life is already a composite of moments, so if aggregation across moments is allowed, aggregation across persons is allowed too; (b) the two-level move—a good utilitarian is perfectly alive to each person's distinctness in everyday deliberation, and does the summing only at the level of institutions or public justification; (c) the objection begs the question, because it simply assumes distinctness has the moral weight it is supposed to prove; (d) everyone aggregates somewhere—triage, war, redistribution, unavoidable risk—so the live question is not wether we aggregate but where and how; (e) the objection is right enough that we should go prioritarian: still sum, but weight changes in well-being toward the worse off.
