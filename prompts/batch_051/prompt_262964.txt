Suppose a person spends ten uninterrupted hours working through a difficult problem, keeping notes as they go. Their activity can be written out as a long sequence of individually small steps — read the last note, turn one consideration over, reject an idea, record a conclusion — and each step is small enough that a competent stranger, handed the notes, could perform it just as well. Now take that same sequence of steps and distribute it across ten people, each working for an hour and passing the notebook forward. Or make the case cleaner still: let the original person do the ten one-hour sessions themselves, but erase their memory of the earlier sessions each time, so that only the notebook carries anything forward. Setting aside practical concerns about who would in fact do better, most of us would still trust the uninterrupted thinker more than the relay, and we would call only the first result the product of genuine deliberation. I have been trying to work out whether that preference tracks anything real. If the final contributor has everything needed to take the last step, is the relay's conclusion less reliable in principle, or merely less comfortable? I want either an account of what is lost when cognition is factored this way, or an admission that the ordinary preference for a single continuous mind is a bias we should revise.
