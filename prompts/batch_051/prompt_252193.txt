I retired a few years back after a working life in field ecology and conservation — mostly wetlands, with some upland forest work. Since then I've been reading moral philosophy properly for the first time, and whilst I realise I'm an amateur (so be gentle), I've managed to get myself pinned between a theory I find hard to resist and an intuition I find hard to give up. I'm not looking to be told which side wins. I want the negotiated verdict: what would it honestly take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and is there a reading on which both can be kept?

The theory runs like this. Grant that what matters, morally, is the welfare of sentient creatures — not biodiversity for its own sake, not ecosystem services, not beauty. Now survey a healthy marsh the way I used to survey one. Most of the animals in it are r-strategists: they produce enormous numbers of offspring, and the overwhelming majority die early — starvation, predation, parasites, cold — long before anything like a flourishing adult life. An honest field ecologist's description of a thriving wetland is that it is, on aggregate, a machine for generating short and painful lives. If that's right, then the net welfare of a lot of wild habitat is negative. And then the conclusion is hard to dodge: destroying habitat doesn't add suffering, it prevents future suffering by preventing future lives from starting. Draining a marsh starts to look like preventing a great many miserable lives, not like ending a good thing. The theory is simple, it unifies a huge range of cases, and it follows from taking wild animal welfare as seriously as I once took my own census data. I respect it more than I'd like.

The intuition is this. I spent a good part of my career fighting to protect one particular bog, and I've stood in it before dawn in spring and watched the water move through the reed beds. The conviction that draining that bog would be a serious loss — not just to me, not just to the people who walk there, but full stop — is about as firm a belief as I have. I don't think it's mere aesthetic preference, and I don't think its a trick my brain is playing on me. Or rather: treating it as a trick would be cheating until someone actually shows me the trick.

So which yields? Walk me through the costs. If the intuition goes, what exactly is being debunked — the value of ecosystems, or only my habit of picturing the value as living in the place rather than in the individual lives? If the theory goes, what's the least revision that still lets me say the dragonflies and the wading birds matter — is it rejecting the net-negative-welfare arithmetic, rejecting the step from individual welfare to aggregate disvalue, or something narrower? And if both can be kept, what does that compatibility actually say about whether I should have fought for the bog?

One practical thing: please write your reply in American English. I read it happily either way, but I'm pulling these notes together for an American colleague.
