I have been trying to work out what I actually think about the unity of consciousness by recording my immediate verdicts on cases, and I want to know whether any single principle captures them.

In a classic commissurotomy case, different pictures are shown to the patient's two hemispheres, and the patient is asked to point to a related object with each hand. The right hand points to the object related to the picture shown to the speaking hemisphere, and the left hand points to the object related to the picture shown to the other hemisphere. When asked why the left hand chose as it did, the patient gives a plausible but false explanation. I think there are two streams of consciousness here, not one: the speaking subject is not the subject who guided the left hand's choice.

In an ordinary divided-field experiment with an intact brain, different words are flashed to the two visual fields and the person reports both, the word shown to the left visual field being reported slightly later. I think this is one subject throughout, even during the brief interval when one hemisphere has information the other has not yet received.

In blindsight, a person denies seeing anything in the blind field but can guess the location of a stimulus well above chance. I think this is one subject with a perceptual state that is not consciously accessible; the split is within the person's cognitive system, not between subjects.

In a posthypnotic suggestion case, a person opens a window and, when asked why, says the room felt stuffy, although the suggestion was the real cause. I think this is one subject acting on a motive they cannot fully report, not two subjects.

A person diagnosed with dissociative identity disorder finds a note in handwriting they recognize as their own, describing events they cannot remember. I am genuinely unsure whether this is one subject with severe fragmentation and amnesia or more than one subject.

In a thought experiment, two people are connected by a device that gives each direct, real-time access to the other's experiences. I think they remain two subjects, however complete the access becomes.

The pair I cannot reconcile is the intact-brain divided-field case and the commissurotomy case. Both involve one hemisphere's possessing information the other lacks, yet I count one subject in the first and two in the second. Is there a principle about what makes mental states belong to one subject that gets all of these verdicts, or nearly all of them? State it precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for cases I have not listed. In particular, what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a patient with a partial callosotomy, whose hemispheres sometimes share information and sometimes conflict, and who can sometimes report right-hemisphere contents but not always? And which of my verdicts, if any, does the principle force me to give up?
