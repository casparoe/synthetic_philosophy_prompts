I am giving a talk at my school's joint philosophy and computer science club, and I expect the question period to be the hard part. The working title is "The Problem of Induction Is the No Free Lunch Theorem." My thesis is that the No Free Lunch theorems in machine learning — the result that no learning algorithm beats random guessing when performance is averaged uniformly over all possible target functions — are best understood as a computational restatement of Hume's problem of induction, not merely a practical point about choosing algorithms. The connection I want to defend runs both ways. Hume argued that moving from observed regularities to unobserved cases requires the principle that the future resembles the past, and that neither reason nor experience can justify that principle without circularity. The No Free Lunch theorem reaches a matching conclusion from the opposite direction: unless a learner is already biased toward some class of hypotheses — a prior, a hypothesis space, a feature representation, a regularizer — there is no expectation of generalizing at all; every bit of apparent learning is paid for with an assumption the data cannot itself certify. What Hume called custom or habit, the machine learning tradition calls inductive bias, and the theorem's real force is that this bias is not an implementation detail but the entire source of generalization.

The audience matters because two groups will attack different halves of the claim. A few of the club members have worked through machine learning projects (logistic regression, decision trees, small neural networks) and already know the No Free Lunch result; they tend to dismiss it as a worst-case averaging fact about absurd target functions that has no bearing on real classification problems. The other group comes from the school's philosophy reading group and has read some Hume; they suspect the talk just repackages an eighteenth-century skeptical argument in equations and adds nothing. I need to be ready for both.

Give me a prep sheet for the question period, not a restatement of the talk. I want the objections in roughly the order they will actually come, phrased the way each group would actually phrase them, with what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, and how to reply in about a minute each. Tell me where the reply is weakest, and identify the one joint in my position that a sharp questioner could break open — then show me how to defend that joint rather than hope nobody pushes on it. Finally, tell me the objection I should most hope nobody asks, and my least-bad answer if it comes anyway.
