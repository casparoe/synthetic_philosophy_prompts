I have been working through Fearon's rationalist explanations for war — private information with incentives to misrepresent, credible commitment problems under shifting power, and issue indivisibilities — and trying to apply them to AI development races rather than to conventional interstate conflict. I keep coming back to something I think is a fourth mechanism, or at least a structurally distinct variant, and I want an honest assessment of whether it holds up.

I call it the *disclosure tax* on commitments in epistemic races. The core claim is this: in an AI race, the thing being contested is not territory or a political settlement but privately held knowledge about how to build capable systems. For either lab or state to make a credible commitment to slow down or to use only safe methods, it normally has to demonstrate the basis for that commitment — for example, by revealing enough about its current capabilities, its safety methods, or its internal progress to make the commitment verifiable. But the act of revealing that knowledge transfers some of the contested capability to the other side. So the bargaining instrument itself leaks value when used. I suspect this makes the bargaining space shrink as commitments are attempted: every verifiable promise costs exactly the private information advantage that would have made the promise meaningful.

My attempt at a formal statement: In conventional Fearon bargaining, a credible commitment is a move within a Pareto-improving contract; it is costly mostly in terms of reputation, enforcement, or foregone opportunities, but it does not directly hand the other side the underlying strategic asset. In an epistemic race, by contrast, many credible commitments require the disclosure of private technical knowledge, and that knowledge is itself the strategic asset. This creates a self-undermining commitment dynamic that is neither ordinary private information with incentives to misrepresent nor an ordinary commitment problem driven by shifting power, because the obstacle is not that the parties distrust each other or that the strong can later renegotiate; it is that the only way to credibly signal restraint is to make the other party stronger. I have a toy example: a lab that has discovered a highly efficient training algorithm can credibly promise not to scale it only by showing that it exists and showing enough of its structure for the promise to be monitored — but doing so is roughly equivalent to publishing the insight.

I know this may be hidden inside one of Fearon's three categories, or it may collapse into the observation that information has positive externalities. But I cannot tell whether the *self-undermining commitment* structure adds anything. I would like three things from you. First, reconstruct the strongest version of my idea and locate it relative to Fearon's framework: is it a genuinely distinct mechanism, a special case of commitment problems, or an instance of issue indivisibility? Second, tell me where it breaks — what exactly about AI development or about bargaining theory makes the disclosure tax the wrong way to frame the problem? Third, give me the clearest counterexample you can construct: a scenario where a racer could make a credible slowdown commitment without transferring capability, and explain why my framework either misclassifies it or wrongly treats it as impossible. Do not hold back on the diagnosis; I want to know if this is a real addition or just a metaphor that feels insightful because it uses the word *tax*.
