I am a PhD student in philosophy of law, and in preparing a section on the moral limits of the criminal law I have kept returning to an asymmetry in our judgments about threats that I cannot easily resolve. Suppose I am in a position to disclose some embarrassing but truthful information about you—an affair, a history of addiction, a professional failure you have kept private. Disclosing that information would, we can stipulate, be callous and cause real harm, but in itself it would not be illegal; depending on the details I might even be within my rights to publish it. And asking you for money is, by itself, perfectly legal as well. Yet if I link the two—if I offer to stay silent in return for payment, or threaten to disclose unless you pay—I have committed blackmail, which is treated as a serious crime nearly everywhere and as wrongful far beyond what the two component acts would be. Two acts that are each permissible on their own become seriously wrong merely in virtue of being combined into a conditional threat. I am aware this is often called the paradox of blackmail, but I have never seen the underlying asymmetry pressed quite the way I want to press it.

The puzzle, as I see it, is more precise than the familiar observation that two rights can make a wrong. In the ordinary case of a threat—do X or I will do Y—the wrongfulness of the threat seems to inherit from the wrongfulness of the threatened act: threatening to assault you is wrong because assaulting you is wrong, while threatening to contact the authorities is not wrong when contacting the authorities would be the appropriate thing for me to do anyway. Blackmail appears to be an exception to that principle, since the threatened act, truthful disclosure, is one I am otherwise permitted to perform. Our practice is therefore asymmetric: generally the moral status of a threat derives from the moral status of the act threatened, except when the threat is an offer to keep quiet, where we condemn it despite the threatened act being lawful. I can formulate candidate justifications—the secret is being used as a lever rather than disclosed for its own sake; the payment is extracted from a vulnerability rather than offered in a genuine exchange; the victim's consent is manufactured by the threat itself; the wrong lies in commodifying silence. But none of them has yet survived contact with nearby cases in my own thinking, including bluffing, threatening to reveal a secret I have a duty to report, transparently selling information to a third party while separately asking the person for money, threatening to disclose something false, and threatening to disclose something about myself.

I would like you to work through this with care. What, if anything, actually justifies the asymmetry? Give me your strongest candidate justification, test it against the sorts of nearby cases I have listed, and tell me whether the asymmetry is something we should keep or a local inconsistency we should resolve—and if the latter, whether you would resolve it by extending condemnation to one of the component acts, by relaxing our condemnation of the combination, or by revising the general principle about threats. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
