I have no legal training, but for several months I have been unable to stop thinking about a question that began as a family argument. My brother was cited under a city ordinance making it a misdemeanor to behave in a boisterous or disorderly way in a public park, after he played music at a volume the officer considered excessive even though no number is written anywhere in the law. I later learned that lawyers call this the void-for-vagueness problem, though the name states the consequence rather than the standard.

At first I assumed the problem was simply that the words are vague. But when I tried to imagine the rule made perfectly precise — a park code that assigned a permitted decibel to every possible sound, from a cough to a radio to a child's shout — the result was plainly absurd, and I realized that precision cannot be the only thing a law must be. My question is therefore not whether a law can be vague, but what a law has to be.

Before I read further, I want a specification of the standard itself. Please write out, for someone like me, the requirements that any adequate account of how precise a criminal law must be would have to satisfy — that is, a theory of when a prohibition is clear enough that it is fair to punish a person for crossing it. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or only the preference of a particular camp. I want the distinctions such a theory cannot collapse: vagueness as against ambiguity; a term with no sharp boundary as against a rule that is clear but reaches too much; a rule whose words are unclear as against one whose words are clear but hand unbounded discretion to an officer or judge. I want the cases it has to get right: a posted speed limit against a requirement to drive at a reasonable speed; a park rule banning vehicles that still has to say something about a bicycle; a statute that forbids annoying conduct. I want the values it protects, above all fair notice and protection against arbitrary enforcement, and I want to know whether those two can come apart, so that a law could satisfy one while violating the other. I also want the facts it has to accommodate, such as the fact that no ordinary word has perfectly sharp boundaries and that legal systems freely use open standards like reasonable care outside the criminal law. If any pair of requirements is in tension, so that the list itself cannot be fully met at once, that is the part I most need to see. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in separating an adequate account from a shallow one. A table is fine. Please leave case names and citations out; I am after the criteria themselves, not the case law.
