I'm the engineer rep on our product launch review board, and we've got a vote coming up where I've been told to "just be consistent with our past rulings." Problem is, I can't actually tell what the underlying rule is. Here's everything I remember us deciding:

- We approved a forced overnight security update that killed a legacy API a bunch of integrations depended on. Some users' stuff broke for a few days, but the vuln was being actively exploited and the people eating the breakage were mostly the same people who would've been compromised.
- We rejected a plan to throttle free-tier requests by about 10% at peak load so paid customers would get better latency and our infra bill would drop. The slowdown would've landed on free users; the benefit went mostly to paying customers and us.
- We approved, with an opt-out buried in settings, a driving mode that silences non-urgent notifications when someone's moving at car speed. Some people missed messages they cared about, but the safety benefit and the annoyance land on the same driver.
- We rejected a proposal to hide the "close ad" button for a random 5% of users for a week so we could measure ad tolerance. The annoyance was modest and temporary, but the only obvious beneficiary was our dashboard.
- We rejected a default-on "focus mode" that would block a user's own distracting apps during work hours unless they turned it off. The annoyance and the "stop managing me" feeling landed on the user, and the productivity benefit also landed on the user. We still said no, on the grounds that a non-trivial nuisance shouldn't be imposed without asking even if it's "for your own good."
- We approved collecting de-identified crash logs without prior consent. The privacy risk was genuinely tiny, and the stability benefit goes to basically everyone, including the people whose logs we collect.

The new proposal is a default-on scanner that checks photos in users' libraries and messages for child sexual abuse material (CSAM) and sends flagged material to human reviewers. Users wouldn't be asked before it's on, and false positives will be rare but not zero: an innocent person could have private photos looked at by a reviewer. The main beneficiaries are mostly not the people bearing the privacy risk. But the aggregate good could be very large.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which past case is the closest analog? If our record genuinely underdetermines it, what principle would we be adopting by approving versus rejecting, and which choice is more of a break from the pattern? And can you give me a cleaner test case that would settle which rule we've actually been running?
