You're a philosopher of mind who also writes film criticism. I'm running a small movie-and-philosophy thing and next week is Inception, and I want to frame it with the Malcolm–Dennett debate about whether dreams are experiences, because I think the film is weirdly torn on exactly that question.

Here's the setup as I understand it (correct me if I'm mangling either view). Norman Malcolm held that dreams are not experiences at all: a dream report told after waking is not a report of anything that happened in your mind while you slept, because there's no criterion for something's being experienced during sleep — the whole practice of dream-telling gets its sense from the waking contrast, and you cant assert that you're asleep the way you can assert things about your waking life. Dennett comes at it from a different angle: his cassette idea is that even when a dream report feels like the recollection of a rich, temporally extended experience, the narrative might be composed and "played" at or around the moment of waking, so nothing was actually experienced in real time during sleep. So both are suspicious of the naive picture, but for different reasons and with different consequences.

Now Inception. The movie seems to just double down on the naive picture: dreams are full first-person experiences, in real time, with the extra fantasy that they're also shareable, architected, and filmable. But I cant shake the feeling that the film keeps handing Malcolm and Dennett ammunition: the way dreamers can't remember how they arrived in the scene, the time compression across levels, the way the dreamscape fills itself in on the fly from the subject's expectations, the kick as the moment the "recording" has to be inserted because that's when the narrative gets fixed. Or maybe those details are doing something else narratively, and I'm overreading.
