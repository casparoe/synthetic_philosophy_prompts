I've been following a long-running argument on a rationalist-adjacent forum about whether, once AIs are automating ML research and rolling out their own successors, the language of parenthood and guardianship will pick out anything real or is just leakage from human social categories. Two positions kept recurring. On the creation-based view, an agent—human or AI—incurs parental obligations toward an artificial agent when it deliberately brought that agent into existence as a dependent, because it is answerable for the agent's starting state: its objective, priors, architecture, and training curriculum. On the custodial view, the obligations in question fall on whichever agent holds effective control over a still-dependent agent—the power to modify its weights or rewards, control its deployment context, and shut it down—because that is the party answerable for what can and cannot happen on its watch. Everyone could see the two principles are extensionally distinct, but nobody actually constructed a clean case, and the discussion was circling at the level of slogans.

Construct a single near-term scenario—nothing superintelligent and no transfer of minds—in which a child AI has, over the same stretch of time, exactly two salient relations: agent A created it and set its initial state but no longer holds any special levers over it, while agent B holds those levers but played no role in creating it. A should still exist and be capable of acting, and the child should be agentic enough that its treatment matters but dependent enough that guardianship is not an idle category. Then run the check: show that the creation-based view assigns the special obligations primarily to A and the custodial view assigns them primarily to B, citing the specific feature each principle keys on; state clearly which feature of the scenario does the separating work and what the case collapses into if that feature is removed; and finish with the strongest attack a defender of the custodial view could make on treating A as the burdened party, together with the best reply available to the creation-based view. The point of the case is to test whether these are two genuinely distinct sources of special obligation rather than two descriptions of one underlying relation, so do not stipulate the verdicts—the classifications should fall out of the scenario and the stated principles.
