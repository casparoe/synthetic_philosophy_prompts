You are a philosopher of language with a working background in lexical semantics, hired as a consultant by the editors of a mid-sized learner's dictionary of English. The dictionary's sense inventory also serves as the annotation guidelines for a word-sense-disambiguation project, so the decisions must be reproducible by junior lexicographers and annotators who have no formal training in semantics. The team reviews roughly a dozen contested entries each week, and inconsistency between entries is the thing the editor-in-chief most wants to eliminate. The current practice is, in the editors' own description, "by ear": a mix of precedent, intuition, and how loudly a senior editor argues. You have been asked to replace it with a single written procedure for deciding when a word receives one sense or several.

The question behind the assignment is the one the dictionary forces onto the page: when is a difference in use a difference in meaning? The editors oscillate between two positions they know are bad. One insists each word has a stable core meaning with everything else supplied by context; the other wants a new numbered sense for every distinguishable use. Your procedure must commit to a defensible working answer between those poles, and it must be usable by people who will never read the philosophy of language.

The procedure must be runnable in roughly twenty minutes per word, must rely on corpus-attestable sentences rather than private intuition, and must specify who has to sign off: for words in the dictionary's top 3,000 by frequency, any split or lump must be ratified by the chief editor and the NLP annotation lead; for everything else, a trained junior editor may apply the procedure alone.

Write the deliverable the editors will use. It should contain:

1. A plain-language account of each distinction the procedure rests on: ambiguity versus vagueness versus generality; homonymy versus polysemy; a coded sense versus a meaning merely made available by context; and denotation versus connotation. Define each so an editor can apply it without the theory.

2. A step-by-step decision procedure in the form of an ordered checklist or decision tree: the tests to run, the order to run them in, and the rule for when a difference counts as a split. Include at least these four tests, with a one-sentence statement of what a positive or negative result establishes: (a) a contradiction or truth-conditional test; (b) a zeugma or conjunction-reduction test, where one governing word applies to two complements that would require different readings (the standard pattern being something like "she opened the door and the discussion"); (c) an anaphora or "do so / do it too" ellipsis test using mixed predicates; and (d) a substitutability test using near-synonyms. State what the procedure should do when the tests conflict, and which test prevails for which word class.

3. Special rules for three recurring hard cases: (i) productive regular alternations — the animal/meat pattern (chicken, salmon, lamb), the container/contents pattern (a glass of water), the institution/building pattern (the university), the author/work pattern (I finished Proust) — where a general rule may be preferable to per-word entries; (ii) metaphor and metonymy at different degrees of conventionalization (warm in "warm welcome," bright in "bright student," mouth in "mouth of the river"); and (iii) coercion-like cases such as "start the book" or "enjoy the film," where a noun is interpreted as an event under verbs like start, finish, and enjoy. For each, specify when it is a separate sense, when it is one sense plus a productive operation, and who may override the default.

4. Worked applications to three entries the team has fought over, drawn from this list: book (physical object versus textual content), heavy (heavy bag versus heavy traffic versus heavy smoker), school, bank, love, or run. Apply every step of the procedure to each entry, including the actual test sentences you would use and the result they return. At least one application must yield a result the editors are known to resist — a split they dislike or a lump they call sloppy — and you must explain why the procedure commits them to it anyway.

5. A closing statement of what the procedure cannot do mechanically: the cases where editorial judgment survives, how those discretionary decisions should be marked in the entry so the NLP team knows not to treat them as settled, and where the procedure is likely to misfire even when followed correctly (for example, open-class verbs such as run, or heavily network-structured prepositions such as over).

Before writing the document, spend a paragraph making the central theoretical decision explicit: on your view, what exactly does a pattern of usage have to achieve to earn its own numbered sense, rather than being one sense put to varied work? Everything in the procedure should follow from that decision, so do not reach for the checklist until you have fixed it.
