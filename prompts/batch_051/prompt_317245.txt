I am a novelist rather than a professional philosopher. For the past two years I have been reading the philosophical literature on sweatshop labor as background for a novel set in a garment-exporting country, and during that time I was asked to help design and mark a short-answer question in a colleague's undergraduate course on the ethics of global production. The question and one student's answer are below. I would be grateful for a second opinion on how to mark the answer. Please assign a letter grade, and then give the specific, point-by-point comments that justify that grade; for each substantive step in the answer, say where it is right, where it goes wrong, and how much the mistake matters. In those comments, please separate what is settled among people who work on this topic from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Please also do not provide citations or a reading list. Please write in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write an email or a letter.

The question read as follows. Consider the thesis that mutually beneficial exploitation is an oxymoron: if a sweatshop job leaves a worker better off than her next-best alternative and she takes it voluntarily, then the transaction cannot be wrongfully exploitative, and outsiders have no business trying to prevent it. Assess this thesis. In your answer, separate the question whether the employer treats the worker wrongly from the question what consumers, activists, and governments should do, and say whether the first claim supports the second.

The student answered as follows. The thesis is wrong to deny that mutually beneficial exploitation is possible. A worker can be better off in absolute terms than she would have been without the job and still receive an unfairly small share of what her labor makes possible; exploitation concerns the fairness of the terms, not whether the worker's position improves relative to a miserable baseline. So the fact that the job beats the alternatives does not show that the employer is not taking unfair advantage of the worker's need. It follows, however, that the question that matters for moral assessment, including the assessments made by consumers and regulators, is simply whether the wage is fair relative to the value the worker creates; once we know that, we know everything we need in order to decide what outside parties may do. On the second part of the thesis, the surrounding facts do more work than the first claim itself: because the worker consents and prefers the job to her alternatives, the employer's conduct is at most unfair pricing rather than coercion, and outside parties therefore lack the permission to interfere with an exchange the worker herself has reason to accept.
