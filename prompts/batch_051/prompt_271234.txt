I'm putting together a unit on decision under ignorance and want a single worked example that forces students to see that Rawls-style maximin, Savage-style minimax regret, and Harsanyi-style equiprobability reasoning are genuinely different rules, not just different names for caution. The case should be a veil-of-ignorance choice: a representative chooser selects one of exactly three constitutional arrangements for a small society, knowing only that they will end up in one of three social positions, and the case itself supplies no objective probabilities over those positions. Use a 3×3 payoff matrix of non-negative well-being levels, with all entries between 0 and 10 (simple integers or one-decimal numbers are fine), and make the separation strict: maximin must strictly prefer one policy, minimax regret strictly prefer a second, and the equiprobability rule strictly prefer the third. Keep the scenario mundane and brief — a founding assembly choosing among ordinary policies — with no demons, no predictors, and no science-fictional content. The divergence has to come from the structure of the matrix, not from stipulating the verdicts. (For the handout I am using the simplified original-position version of Rawls, not the full apparatus of the two principles.)

Once you have the case, deliver it completely. State the scenario and the matrix, then work each rule's computation in full: the minimum of each row for maximin, the regret matrix and maximum regret per policy for minimax regret, and the row averages for equiprobability. Then identify the single structural feature of the matrix that makes the three verdicts peel apart, and show what happens when that feature is weakened or removed — which rules collapse together and why. Finish by taking a definite position: say which of the three rules behaves least plausibly in this case, give the strongest attack an advocate of that rule could make against the case itself — where they claim it is rigged, question-begging, or unrepresentative — and reply to that attack. Commit to one case and one judgment; do not offer several candidate matrices and leave the choice to me.
