Exam question: Thomas Nagel describes a tension between the condition of control, roughly the principle that an agent can be morally assessed only for what is within the agent's control, and our ordinary practice of judging one reckless driver far more harshly than another solely because the first driver happens to kill someone. Explain this tension, taking care to distinguish resultant moral luck from merely epistemic limitations in the agent's knowledge. Then assess the following response: the reckless driver who kills and the equally reckless driver who does not are equally blameworthy for what was within their control, namely the negligent driving; the difference in our reactions is only a legal or epistemic artifact, not a genuine difference in moral responsibility. Does this response dissolve the problem of moral luck, and if not, what exactly is left unexplained?

Student answer: The tension arises because the control condition seems to require that blame depend only on what the agent controlled, yet the case of two drivers who act identically and only one of whom kills shows that ordinary blame tracks the outcome, which is partly a matter of chance. This is resultant luck, and it should not be confused with epistemic luck: the lucky driver is not better justified in believing that he would get through the intersection safely, so the asymmetry between the two drivers is not explained by a difference in evidence or reasoning.

A tempting reply says that the two drivers are equally culpable for the same negligent act, and that harsher treatment of the unlucky driver is only a pragmatic convention of the criminal law, colored by grief for the victim. Nagel's own view is that this reply succeeds: once blame is located in the controlled act rather than the outcome, the control condition is satisfied and the puzzle disappears. But that cannot be right, because even apart from legal punishment we feel differently about the two drivers, and juries, families, and acquaintances would not regard them as morally equivalent people. The residual asymmetry in moral sentiment shows that outcome exerts a kind of control over moral assessment that is not merely epistemic.

A deeper issue is that the argument generalizes. The negligent act itself was made more likely by impatience, which was shaped by temperament and upbringing, both of which were unchosen. This regress suggests that if we apply the control condition strictly, no one is ever morally responsible for anything. Williams is therefore right that moral luck forces us to abandon the concept of moral responsibility altogether.
