ok, so here's roughly where I've landed after a summer of reading Rousseau for my political theory field exam, and I'd like a consistency check before I write it up as a paper. I keep feeling the package hangs together, but I also keep feeling like I'm smugling something in and I can't tell where. Here's the position I'd be defending:

1. A law only legitimately binds me if I am, in some real sense, its author. Self-legislation is what keeps obedience from being mere subjection; obeying a law I gave myself isn't a loss of freedom.
2. The general will is not a sum of private preferences. It's what citizens jointly will when each person asks what's good for all rather than only what's good for themselves. That's what the distinction between the general will and the will of all is supposed to mark.
3. The general will itself is always right, always aimed at the common good. But actual people deliberating can be mistaken about where that common good lies; their deliberations are often not enlightened.
4. When someone's private interest leads them to refuse to obey the general will, the whole body can compel them to obey, and this compulsion makes them free rather than merely forces them.
5. In a real republic, voting is how we determine the general will. So if my side loses, the right conclusion is that I was mistaken about what the general will was, rather than that the majority got it wrong.
6. But I also think votes can go wrong: under conditions of faction, serious inequality, or citizens deliberating from private interest, what gets registered is more like the will of all than the general will.
