ok, for a seminar on compromise and integrity i'm teaching this term I got talked into writing out my actual views on moral compromise so the students can do a consistency check on me before we read the usual stuff. I'd rather have you catch the problems first. So here's roughly where I've landed.

(1) I think there's such a thing as moral gains from trade. Two people with different but reasonable value systems can each make a concession they regard as a mild wrong, in exchange for the other making a concession that advances their values, and each can end up better off by their own lights. I think a trade like that can be permissable — and when each side prefers the package to no deal, there's nothing second-best about it.

(2) But I also hold a clean-hands constraint: a wrong you do yourself isn't cancelled by a good done by someone else, and you shouldn't deliberately do what you take to be wrong even to bring about a larger good.

(3) At the same time, I think refusing a compromise that would make things better by your own lights, just to keep your hands clean, is often a moral failing — a kind of vanity or self-indulgence.

(4) I'm also inclined to think some values are protected: there are commitments such that treating them as fungible — as things you'd trade away at a good rate — is itself the violation, not the magnitude of what you give up. I don't have a clean crieteria for which commitments are like this.

(5) I think integrity isn't another value on the ledger; it's a matter of acting from your own commitments in a reflectively stable way. So compromise per se doesn't threaten integrity, and never compromising isn't what integrity consists in.

(6) And I don't think the two traders' consent settles permissibility: the exchange also has to not make third parties — people who weren't at the table — worse off in their own terms.
