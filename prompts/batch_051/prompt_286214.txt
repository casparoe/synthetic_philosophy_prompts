Act as a philosopher who specializes in infinite ethics, i.e. how to morally evaluate worlds with infinitely many people. Give me a concrete example of a strange thing that happens there. Two worlds, A and B, are each an infinitely long list of utility numbers, one entry per person, with positions 1, 2, 3, and so on. B is just A with the entries permuted—so B involves the same wellbeing levels, just assigned to different people, and nobody is added or removed. Now construct a case where A Pareto-dominates B: the nth person in A is at least as well off as the nth person in B for every n, and at least one (idealy infinitely many) is strictly better off. So Pareto seems to say A is better, while an anonymity (impartiality) principle—swapping which person gets which utility level shouldn't change how good the world is—seems to say A and B are equally good. Build the actual two sequences; an alternating pattern of two numbers is enough. Then say in a few sentences why this is awkward for anyone trying to do infinite ethics. Start by brainstorming a naive attempt or two, like swapping every adjacent pair, before settling on the construction that works.
