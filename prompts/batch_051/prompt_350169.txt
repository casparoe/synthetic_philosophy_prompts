I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about tiny probabilities and enormous payoffs — the whole Pascal's-mugging family of cases — by looking at my own gut reactions. I'll list them, and I want you to tell me what principle, if any, captures them. I care less about a label than about getting the lines right: when should a tiny chance of a huge good (or bad) actually move me?

1. A stranger on the street asks for $10, saying if I don't pay, his magic computer will simulate 10^60 people being tortured for a billion years. I don't pay. I don't feel bad about it. And it's not just that I think he's lying — even when I try to imagine he's somehow telling the truth, I'd feel crazy paying. Expected-value talk says I should pay any small price for a tiny chance of preventing that much torture, but I just dont buy that.

2. A well-established malaria charity says $10 funds nets whose effect works out to something like a 1-in-50,000 chance of preventing a child's death (they don't put it exactly like that). I give, easily.

3. A newish organization works on reducing the chance of a nuclear war; they claim $10 buys on the order of a 1-in-10^8 chance of preventing a war that would kill a billion people. Their model is speculative, but it's built out of things I can at least check. I'd probably give, though I'd feel more uneasy than with the malaria case.

4. Someone sets up a quantum coin with a genuine 1-in-10^12 chance of landing heads, and a foundation has $10^12 in escrow. A ticket costs $1, and if heads, the money goes to my family. I'd buy it. It's weird, because the expected value is exactly $1. But buying feels fine; the coin and the escrow are real, and the boring parts don't fail.

5. Same setup, same verifiable escrow, but the prize is $10^30 — so, 1-in-10^30 chance of $10^30, ticket $1. I'd decline. I can't say why exactly; theres no amount of "dollars" like that in the world, the promise starts to feel like arithmetic rather than money.

6. Same quantum coin as in (4), but the person offering it has no escrow, no foundation, just his word he'll pay $10^12 if heads. The coin's probability is genuine, but the promise isnt. I decline, and I don't feel bad. Whereas with the escrow case I do buy. So for me the probability alone isn't what does the work.

7. Actually, wait — final case, because I keep going back and forth on it. A friend I trust completely shows me the same sort of quantum coin (1-in-10^15 chance) and says if heads, she'll make good on a promise to spend her life preventing human extinction; she's serious, I know her foundation, but the causal chain from her effort to "preventing extinction" is so long and fragile it might as well be a story. She wants $100 for the ticket. I genuinely don't know what to do. Half the time I think it's like the escrow case and I should pay; half the time I think it's a pretty version of the mugger.

What principle — if any — fits all of cases 1 through 6, and what does it say about case 7? Is it something like "maximize expected value only where the probabilities are real enough to survive scrutiny," or "round tiny probabilities to zero unless the mechanism is verifiable," or "utility is bounded, so huge payoffs flatten out"? Dont just name one: tell me which clauses are doing the work, which of my verdicts the principle would force me to drop, and how I should think about #7.
