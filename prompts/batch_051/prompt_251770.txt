Private Judy Benjamin is dropped at night into one of four sectors: Red Headquarters, Red Signal Station, Blue Headquarters, or Blue Signal Station. She regards the four sectors as equiprobable and knows the grid. Her radio operator, who knows the grid but cannot see her, tells her: "If you are in Red sector, you are at Headquarters." Judy reasons:

(1) The message tells her the proposition (in Red → at Headquarters), which is true in every sector except Red Signal Station.
(2) When you learn a proposition for certain, you should conditionalize on it: set its probability to 1 while leaving the ratios among the possibilities consistent with it unchanged.
(3) The possibilities consistent with the message — Red Headquarters, Blue Headquarters, and Blue Signal Station — were equiprobable, so they remain equiprobable.
(4) Therefore her posterior probabilities should be 1/3 for each of those three sectors and 0 for Red Signal Station.

I have a nagging sense that step (1) or step (2) assumes more than the radio message actually gives her, but I cannot make the objection precise. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and what it silently assumes. If the posterior is underdetermined, explain why and state what additional information would settle a unique one.
