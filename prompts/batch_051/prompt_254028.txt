I'm working on a piece about moral uncertainty, the puzzle of what you should do when you aren't sure which moral theory is right, and I need a thought experiment I can unpack for readers.

I want to contrast two approaches. Maximizing expected choiceworthiness (MEC): assign each option a score for how choiceworthy it is under each live moral theory, weight those scores by your confidence in that theory, and pick the option with the highest weighted total. My favorite theory (MFT): just do what the single theory you're most confident in tells you to do, and ignore the others. The section I'm writing argues that MFT mostly looks sensible because the standard examples quietly involve theories with roughly equal stakes in the decision. So I need a case that isolates the other direction: the lower-confidence theory has the much larger stake.

Build me a case where MEC says do it and MFT says don't, where the do-it option is strongly favored by the lower-confidence theory and moderately disfavored by the higher-confidence theory. Constraints: an ordinary professional decision (a journalist, nurse, teacher, small business owner, whatever), no trolleys, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics, one paragraph for the scenario, and payoffs a normal person could actually face. Also: no infinite numbers. If you use a deontological side, make it a finite, seriously weighty wrong rather than an absolute prohibition, so the divergence doesn't just come from infinity swamping everything.

After the scenario, walk me through it: state the live theories, assign confidence levels and round-number stakes under each, and show step by step what MEC says, what MFT says, and why they come apart. Then tell me which structural feature is doing the real work, and what happens if you remove it: if the two theories' stakes are set equal, does the case collapse back into MEC and MFT agreeing? Finally, say how a defender of MFT would attack the case. What, in their view, has the MEC calcualtion smuggled in?
