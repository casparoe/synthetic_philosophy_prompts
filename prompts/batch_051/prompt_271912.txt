there's this move I keep seeing when people argue about what counts as a biological individual: someone says an ant colony is basically an organism, with the queen as the germ line, the workers as somatic cells, the nest as a skin, and trophallaxis as circulation. Then they use that to argue the colony is the real individual and the level that selection acts on, and asking about individual ants is like asking about individual liver cells. I want you to audit that analogy properly, not just shrug at it. Give me the correspondence table first — what in the colony maps to what in a textbook organism, including the entries that don't map cleanly. Then the disanalogies. Then sort them: which ones matter for the work the analogy is doing and which are just cosmetic. I also want the features of the organism side that get smuggled in without anyone saying so — single genome, centralized control, shared fate, a single developmental bottleneck, common reproductive interest, whatever you think is doing the quiet work. Be concrete: in a body the somatic cells are clonal and don't reproduce independently, whereas ant workers are usually sisters, not clones, and in a lot of species they can still lay male eggs, so the germ-line boundary is leakier than in a mammal. Does that break the analogy or just make it more interesting? Is there a better analogy — a corporation, a slime mold, a body plus its symbionts — that would carry the unit-of-selection conclusion more honestly? Then the verdict: after the audit, does saying the colony is an organism survive as a substantive claim, or was the analogy doing the whole job? And briefly, does the same audit transfer to the holobiont version of this move, coral plus zooxanthellae as the real individual, or does that one need its own table?
