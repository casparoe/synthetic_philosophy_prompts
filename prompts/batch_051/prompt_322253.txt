Here is an asymmetry in ordinary judgment about play and leisure that I have been unable either to justify or to abandon.

Start from the autotelic tradition. Huizinga analyzes play as free activity standing outside ordinary life, bounded in its own space and time, ordered by its own rules, absorbing while it lasts, and done for no material interest. Pieper gives leisure a similar shape: true leisure is not time off in the service of work but a receptive, contemplative condition that carries its own justification.

Yet ordinary practice splits this by age. A child who spends an afternoon absorbed in an elaborate game of make-believe is praised, or at least left alone; almost no one asks whether the game develops a skill, improves health, or pays back the hours invested. It is treated as an end in itself, which the child is allowed for no further reason. An adult doing the analogue — an afternoon absorbed in an improvised rule-governed game, no spectators, no score kept, no fitness goal, no product — is judged to be wasting time, or at best is excused as self-care, which reinstates an instrumental justification under a more flattering name. The same internal activity is an end in itself at one age and a means at another, and this despite the fact that the autotelic tradition's own examples are not restricted to children: Huizinga's players and Pieper's person at leisure are, if anything, adults.

I want the strongest principle you can find that generates both of the ordinary judgments, the principle a thoughtful defender of common practice would actually offer. Test it against nearby cases before you commit: a retired adult whose afternoon game is structurally identical to a child's; a child whose play is from age six supervised, scored, and monetized; an adult artist whose studio work is indistinguishable from play; a despondent adult for whom aimless play is the only remaining activity done for no instrumental reason. If the principle survives, say what it is and why it survives those cases. If no such principle works, say so plainly.

Then give the bottom line: keep the asymmetry, reverse it (treat adult leisure as its own end while holding children's play to external standards), or give it up (drop the age split and apply the same standard at every age, naming that standard). End the response with exactly \answer{keep}, \answer{reverse}, or \answer{give up}.
