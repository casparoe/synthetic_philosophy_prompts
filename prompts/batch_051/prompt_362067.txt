I spend much of my working life reasoning about distributed protocols, where a piece of code that behaves correctly for a single process can fail when every process runs it at once. In my field this is treated as a problem of compositionality: local correctness and correctness under universal adoption are separate properties, and confusing them produces deadlocks, livelocks, and violated global invariants. Rereading Kant's discussion of the Formula of Universal Law, I was struck by an apparent structural parallel. Kant asks us to test a maxim not by asking whether the action works for me, but by asking whether I can will, at the same time, that the maxim become a universal law — exactly the move from one process to all processes. His two ways a maxim can fail also look like two engineering failure modes. A contradiction in conception, as with a maxim of false promising, resembles a specification that cannot even be rendered coherent when everyone adopts it, because the practice the maxim exploits is dissolved. A contradiction in will, as with a maxim of refusing aid to others, resembles a protocol that maintains a consistent global state but yields an outcome no rational participant could knowingly choose.

I would like to know whether this is a real identity of structure or only a loose analogy. Please state the mapping as precisely as you can; then identify what would have to be true of maxims — their semantics, their success conditions, their dependence on background practices — for results to transfer from one domain to the other. Could the distinction between safety and liveness in concurrent systems do genuine work in clarifying Kant's distinction between the two contradictions, or would it distort it? And, as a way of stress-testing the parallel, name at least one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false when checked against Kant's own examples.
