A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about imaginative resistance, and specifically about the Giselda case of a story that appears to ask its readers to accept, as true within the fiction, that a mother did the right thing in killing her newborn daughter because the child was a girl. We both accepted the datum: the same readers who cheerfully grant that a fictional mountain is made of gold, or that a fictional donkey talks, refuse to grant that Giselda's act is right in the story; they treat the narrator's moral judgment as a report of the narrator's own depravity rather than as one of the story's fixed facts. We disagreed about what explains it.

My friend's view is that the phenomenon has nothing specially to do with morality or fiction. His argument, as I reconstructed it this morning, goes like this:

(1) To imagine that P is to represent to oneself a scenario in which P, and a scenario just is a possible world.
(2) If no possible world makes P true, then P cannot be imagined; the round square is the paradigm.
(3) There is no possible world in which infanticide is right. If moral realism is true, wrongness attaches necessarily to infanticide, so there is no world in which an act of infanticide is right; if anti-realism is true, then nothing in any world is right or wrong, so a fortiori there is no such world.
(4) Therefore the reader cannot imagine the killing's being right, and the observed refusal of readers is exactly what step (2) predicts.
(5) A gold mountain and a talking donkey are merely physically impossible; they exist in some possible worlds, which is why we feel no resistance to them.
(6) So-called imaginative resistance is therefore not a distinct aesthetic or moral phenomenon; it is the ordinary inability to imagine a metaphysical impossibility, and the Giselda case belongs next to the round square.

I find that conclusion too strong, but I have not been able to locate the step that goes wrong; every objection I made ended up changing the subject, either into moral realism or into what readers would do rather than what they can do. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for a positive account of why imagination resists some stipulations and not others?
