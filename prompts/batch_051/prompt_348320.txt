I teach a third-year undergraduate metaphysics seminar with eighteen students, and we meet once a week for an eighty-minute block. Over the past term I have also been sitting as the faculty representative on our campus laboratory-safety review board, and I keep noticing that my colleagues there treat "it failed the standard test" as interchangeable with "it lacks the relevant disposition." In one case a gas detector failed to alarm during a bench test, and several people took the failed test as settling the matter; I wanted to argue that the detector could still be perfectly disposed to alarm while the particular test set-up was blocked from letting any alarm occur. I would like to build one session of my seminar around that experience, since I think the students will feel the problem more sharply than they understand it from a page.

The philosophical material is the problem of finks and masks for the simple conditional analysis of dispositions. The activity should be buildable from the following brief alone. The simple conditional analysis says that an object is disposed to manifest M under stimulus S just in case, if S were to occur, the object would M. A mask is a standing condition that stops a present disposition from manifesting: a water-soluble sugar cube that has been shellacked will not dissolve when lowered into water right now, though the solubility is still there. A fink is something whose disposition is removed by the very stimulus that would trigger it: a live wire connected to a safety cut-out so that the instant a conductor touches it the circuit goes dead, though it was live until the moment of contact. I want students to feel why both sorts of case matter, and to feel the contrary pressure too: once we start excusing failed tests by positing masks, what test could still settle whether the disposition was there at all?

Design a runnable in-class activity for this group. No lecture up front: the rules, roles, incentives, or materials should be arranged so that participants run into the distinction themselves and only afterwards have it named in the debrief. Please provide concrete rules and roles; a cheap-prop materials list, or, if a physical set-up would not survive an actual classroom, a scripted word-based version; a points scheme that needs no real money; a minute-by-minute timeline for the eighty-minute block; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the simple conditional analysis, then onto masks, then onto finks. The debrief should also say honestly which part of the distinction an in-class activity cannot reproduce. I suspect a genuine fink, in which the stimulus removes the disposition itself rather than merely blocking its manifestation, is difficult to stage with ordinary props; if so, the facilitator should introduce that part as a separate thought experiment and say plainly that the game did not demonstrate it. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run and how to handle it.

Participants should come away able to explain, in their own words, why "it did not M when I tested it" is not by itself a good argument for "it is not disposed to M," and able to state the difference between a mask and a fink.
