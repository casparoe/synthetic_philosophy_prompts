In a unit on cross-cultural communication, a classmate gave me the following argument, and I have not been able to find my way out of it.

(1) A white lie is a deliberate false statement offered for the hearer's benefit.
(2) What is supposed to make a white lie less bad than other lies is the speaker's kind motive.
(3) But a motive is a fact about the speaker, while the harm of a lie is a fact about the hearer: the hearer is left with, or encouraged to keep, a picture of the world the speaker knows to be false.
(4) So a kind motive does nothing to remove or reduce the harm that makes lying wrong; it only explains why the speaker was willing to lie.
(5) Therefore white lies are wrong in the same way and for the same reason as any other false statement with the same effect on the hearer.
(6) Now consider everyday politeness. It is largely a system of expected white lies: the required compliment for a meal one did not enjoy, the automatic reassurance that one is fine, and the indirect refusal that both parties are trained to decode.
(7) If white lies are wrong for the reason given in (3), then these polite formulas are wrong for the same reason, and we must conclude that ordinary courtesy is systematic wrongdoing, with blunt directness as the only honest style.

That conclusion seems too severe: it would turn a style built on indirectness, such as the politeness associated with Japanese communication, into a moral defect, and bluntness of the kind associated with Dutch communication into a duty rather than a preference. I most want to keep (3), because I do not see how a kind intention changes what the hearer ends up believing. Am I forced to accept (7)? If not, which step should I reject, and what would I give up by rejecting it? If you think the whole chain holds, then defend (7).
