I'm a software engineer at an edtech startup building an auto-grader for proof-based math courses. Right now the grader is basically a proof checker: it verifies that each step follows and the last line is the claim, and if so we pass the student. I just read William Thurston's essay "On proof and progress in mathematics" (1994) and this passage has been stuck in my head:

"For instance, when Appel and Haken completed a proof of the 4-color map theorem using a massive automatic computation, it evoked much controversy. I interpret the controversy as having little to do with doubt people had as to the veracity of the theorem or the correctness of the proof. Rather, it reflected a continuing desire for human understanding of a proof, in addition to knowledge that the theorem is true."

The way I read this, he's saying the thing people actually wanted was human understanding of the proof, not just knowledge that it's correct — and we're building the exact tool that only provides the second thing. But "understanding" is not a field I can grep for. Can you write me a reusable procedure, like a checklist or decision tree, that I or another engineer (no philosophy background) could run on a student submission to decide wether it shows understanding of the proof rather than just mechanically correct steps? It needs to work at scale, say 3–5 minutes per submission, and it should flag which steps are judgment calls or cant be automated. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a fully correct proof where the student can't explain why the key step works, (2) a proof with a small gap but strong signs the student has the main idea, and (3) a correct proof copied from a textbook. And tell me which of those three the procedure gives an answer I won't like.
