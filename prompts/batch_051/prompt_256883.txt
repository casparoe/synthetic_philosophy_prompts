Ive got a rule I use on exactly the kind of argument people make about insect suffering, and I want someone to tell me if its a guardrail or a coping mechanism.

The rule: when a recommendation leans on a product N × p, where N is a number so big I cannot actually hold it in my head and p is a probability nobody can estimate from the inside, I refuse to evaluate the product as stated. Instead I replace N with whichever is smaller: the number in the argument, or the biggest number I can genuinely visualize (call it 10^9). And I replace p with whichever is smaller: the argument's probability, or the biggest probability I would defend if someone offered to bet me real money on it (call it 1%). Then I ask whether the argument still goes through with those caps. If not, it goes in the "not actionable yet" pile.

The case that made me notice this is the scale argument for insect sentience. The standard form is roughly: there are ~10^18 insects, each with a nontrivial chance of being sentient, so even a 10% or 1% chance gives an enormous expected number of possibly-suffering individuals, and I should care about things like insect farming or honey/silk before I get around to most human-scale causes. Under my rule the claim gets knocked down to 10^9 × 1% = 10^7 possibly-sentient insects, which is still a lot, but now it has to compete with other things and I usually find myself unmoved and move on.

I honestly cant tell whether I've built a rational defense against Pascal-muggings or whether I've built a machine for shrinking numbers until unwelcome conclusions disappear. Is there a defensible version of this rule, and how would it differ from the one I described? Under what assumptions about how p should compound across all the steps in a sentience argument — that insects have valenced states, that the valence is net negative, that my action changes how many insects exist or suffer, etc. — does the capping actually do what I want? And is the fact that both caps are round numbers I feel comfortable with evidence that im doing aesthetics rather than epistemology?
