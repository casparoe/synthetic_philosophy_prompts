I underwrite multi-million-dollar specialty risks at an insurer — one-off events and weird corporate exposures, not standard car or home policies. I see maybe a dozen submissions a month: a company worried about a penalty after a first-of-its-kind court ruling, a festival worried about volcanic ash, a port authority worried about a cyber attack on its crane software. My manager wants a number on everything before he'll sign, because the pricing sheet has a box for "probablity of loss." I want a reusable procedure, built on Keynes's view of probability and uncertainty, for deciding which of three moves to make on a given risk: (a) assign a numeric probability; (b) say something only ordinal — this risk is more likely than that one, or less likely than some baseline, but I can't say by how much; or (c) refuse to put a probability on it and treat it as genuine uncertainty. I've half-absorbed the idea that Keynes thought most probabilities aren't numeric at all, and that there's a separate dimension — the weight of the argument — about how much relevant evidence sits behind whatever you do say, which isn't the same as how high the probability is. Give me a checklist or flow I can run in 20 minutes while reading a submission, written for an underwriter who has never read any philosophy. It should say which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and when I should push back on the manager instead of filling in the box. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a warehouse flood risk with 40 years of local river-gauge and claims data behind it; (2) a client seeking coverage for a fine that only materializes if a court applies a brand-new regulation in a way no court has yet; (3) a reinsurer asking us to take a slice of losses from a scenario that has never happened, like a major cyber attack on a central clearinghouse, where all the evidence is war games and expert opinion. I need the procedure to tell me, on at least one of these, that the box should stay blank or that I can only make an ordinal comparison even though someone above me wants a decimal. Finish by saying what the procedure cannot do — the places where Keynes's framework gives no mechanical answer and the call has to be owned by a person.
