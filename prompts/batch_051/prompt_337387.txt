I am a novelist writing a near-future climate novel in which a consortium of coastal states, after a lethal heat season, begins injecting sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere while the rest of the world is still negotiating emissions cuts. I can state the relevant ideas in the abstract — the moral hazard of a cheap technofix, the danger of termination shock if the intervention is ever interrupted, and the unresolved question of who decides — but I have never felt them as structural forces, and my characters keep coming out as either heroes or villains rather than agents inside a trap. I lead a twelve-person speculative-fiction MFA workshop, two hours a week, and I want one of our sessions to get the students inside that strategic logic before they try to write it.

Design a runnable classroom game or structured simulation for that setting: twelve participants, 120 minutes, ordinary classroom materials, no real money. The exercise should make them experience, through incentives and rules rather than through upfront explanation, at least three things: first, the pull of treating a cheap intervention as a substitute for costly long-term abatement; second, the vulnerability created when a low-cost intervention, once begun, becomes dangerous to stop; and third, the fact that a small coalition, perhaps even one actor, can take the decision away from everyone else. Give me the roles or positions, the rules, a point or payoff system that works without money, a timeline in minutes including the debrief, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the exercise onto those three forces, at which point you may finally name them. Tell me also what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and be honest about which part of the real dynamics the exercise cannot reproduce and must be supplied afterward.
