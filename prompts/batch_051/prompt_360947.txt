Ok there's this city ordinance where I live, passed in 1988, that's basically an anti-dumping law. The part that matters says: "No person shall deposit, cause to be deposited, or permit to be deposited any garbage, refuse, or other waste material upon any public sidewalk, street, or park." It's a strict liability infraction, $250 fine. The court cases over the years made clear no mens rea is required because its a public welfare thing; the textbook defendants were guys with pickup trucks dumping construction debris at 2am and landlords letting tenants pile junk on the curb.

Here's the case that's bugging me. I order dinner through an app most nights. The app told the courier to leave it at my building's front door, but he left the bag at the curb, and overnight an animal tore into it and spread food, containers, and napkins all over the sidewalk. A code enforcement officer cited me under the ordinance: my food ended up on public property, the order led to it, so I "caused or permitted" the deposit. But I never touched the bag, I didn't know where he'd left it, and the address on the order clearly said front door. The drafters in 1988 had never heard of a food delivery app, and they surely weren't thinking of a raccoon as the thing that does the actual depositing.

So my question is whether the ordinance actually reaches me. I want the different interpretive angles: the plain words (especially what "deposit" and "permit" can stretch to), the purpose of an anti-dumping strict liability law, what the council would have said in 1988 if you'd asked them about this exact case, and what a reasonable person reading the ordinance today would think it covers. If those diverge, which should decide? And one more thing: does the animal matter to the legal analysis, or is that a red herring because strict liability only eliminates mens rea, not the requirement that I actually did something — or does "caused" do more work here than people give it credit for?
