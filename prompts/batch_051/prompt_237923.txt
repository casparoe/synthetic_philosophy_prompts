I've spent way too long on rationalist-adjacent forums watching an argument that never seems to move, and I want you to diagnose the disagreement rather than settle the object-level question. The debate is whether the no-free-lunch theorems (the original Wolpert–Macready optimization version, and the supervised-learning descendants) tell us something deep about induction. One side treats them as devastating: averaged over all possible problems, every learner is exactly as good as every other, so the fact that our methods work on "real" problems can't be a fact about the world — it has to be a fact about us and the inductive biases we happened to install; there is no learning without an arbitrary prior. The other side says this is a formal curiosity with no epistemological bite: the uniform average over all possible target functions is itself a prior, and a wildly unnatural one, so the theorem just says you can't beat a universe that is adversarially uniform in a way our universe plainly isn't. Everyone agrees on the formal statement, more or less. And yet neither side has budged in, like, a decade. My own guess, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the two sides use "prior" in two different senses — one as an algorithm's inductive bias, the other as a coherent Bayesian probability distribution — and that the rest of the fight is downstream of that, but I'm not confident.
