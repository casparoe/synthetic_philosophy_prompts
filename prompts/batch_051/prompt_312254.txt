I recently began working as a junior researcher for my country's transitional-justice commission, and I took the job in part because my uncle was among the people who disappeared during the dictatorship the commission is investigating. The law establishing the commission offers individuals implicated in those disappearances amnesty — a binding, lifelong guarantee against prosecution — on one condition: they must give the commission a full, independently verified account of their own involvement, including dates, places, and the roles of others, and they must answer victims' relatives' questions in a closed hearing. The same confession, given to a journalist or directly to a family, earns no protection; only testimony to the commission counts.

What puzzles me is the bargain underneath this arrangement. The state is exchanging prosecution for memory, as though the wrongdoer can settle the debt by recounting the truth once, on the record. The testimony is then archived, but the law imposes no continuing obligation on the wrongdoer to remember, to teach, to commemorate, or to speak again; the wrongdoer's duty to the past seems to end when the transcript is signed. The families, by contrast, are given the truth but no say over whether it is published or withheld, and they receive no punishment in return.

I want to understand what this practice presupposes about the value of memory and the legitimacy of forgetting. Does it presuppose that telling the truth can stand in as a substitute for justice, that a community is obligated to remember only what it has first extracted from the wrongdoer, or that forgetting becomes permissible once an official record exists? Which of these commitments does the practice strictly require, and where is it compatible with several rival views? And what would have to change in the commission's design if we instead held either that the wrongdoer has an ongoing duty to remember, or that some things ought deliberately to lapse from public memory once they have been formally acknowledged?
