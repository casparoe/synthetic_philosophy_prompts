I'm auditing a philosophy of language course and got into an argument with a friend after we read Ernest W. Adams's article "Subjunctive and Indicative Conditionals" (1970). The paper opens with the familiar Kennedy/Oswald contrast: Adams asks us to compare an indicative sentence that says roughly, if Lee Harvey Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy then somebody else did, with a counterfactual sentence that says roughly, if Oswald had not shot Kennedy then somebody else would have. The first is naturally accepted by speakers who know that Kennedy was shot, while the second is naturally rejected because it seems to assert some impossible-to-defeat inclination toward Kennedy's death. (I'm paraphrasing the examples, but the structure is what matters.)

My friend says Adams's contrast actually proves more than Adams claimed. Here's the argument he gave, numbered:

(1) The two Kennedy conditionals have the same antecedent content and the same consequent content.

(2) Yet competent speakers stably assign them different verdicts.

(3) So the difference between them must be located in the indicative/subjunctive marking itself.

(4) But if that marking contributes truth-conditional content, then two possible worlds could differ only in virtue of whether a sentence is in the indicative or subjunctive mood. That seems absurd, because mood is a feature of the linguistic vehicle, not of the world described.

(5) Therefore, indicative conditionals are not truth-apt at all — or, if they are, they must differ in content from the corresponding subjunctives in some way we haven't identified.

(6) But we embed indicative conditionals under negation, belief reports, and probability operators all the time. If they aren't truth-apt, those embeddings need to be paraphrased away, which seems desperate.

I don't think I can accept (6), and I'd like to keep a truth-conditional semantics for conditionals. But I can't tell whether the bad move is in (3) or (4), or whether some premise needs to be qualified rather than rejected. Which step should I give up, and what does rejecting it cost? Does Adams's own account — where indicatives are not exactly propositions but something like conditional assertions — actually avoid this argument, or does it just relocate the problem?
