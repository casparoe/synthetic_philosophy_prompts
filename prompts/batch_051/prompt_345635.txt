Hi! I teach a second-year course called Reason, Evidence and Decision — roughly 25 students, 80-minute sessions. We're about to do a unit on information cascades and rational herding: the idea that when people act in sequence on a mix of private information and what they've watched earlier people do, a whole group can converge on the wrong answer while each individual is reasoning sensibly from what's in front of them. I'd like them to hit this themselves before I formalize anything, so that by the time I define a cascade they've already felt the exact moment where their private evidence pointed one way but the smart move was to ignore it and follow the crowd. No lecture up front.

Can you design a classroom game for that session? I'm imagining two covered boxes (one majority-red, one majority-blue), tokens drawn privately one at a time, and public guesses in a fixed order for points — candy or coursework credit, not real money — but change the setup if something works better. I need actual rules, a full materials list with quantities, a payoff scheme, a timeline for the 80 minutes, and a debrief that ties specific moments in the game to the parts of the idea: how one or two public guesses can outweigh a person's private signal, why following isn't mere conformism, and how the group can end up almost sure of something false while everyone is being perfectly rational.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students blurting out their token draw instead of a clean guess, the cascade starting earlier or later than it should, someone "helpfully" announcing the boxes are rigged — and how to handle each one. And be honest about what the game can't reproduce: my suspicion is it makes the informational content of each public guess too legible, and it fixes the order of turns and makes everyone's rationality common knowledge in a way real panics, fads, and markets never do. Finally, I want a short written exercise at the end: each student writes what their token told them, what they would have guessed if no one before them had spoken, and what they actually guessed, then we compare columns in the debrief. Does that wreck the game if they only learn about it at the end, or does it work as a retrospective nudge?
