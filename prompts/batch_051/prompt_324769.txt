I have been developing what I call the shadow-space account of optimization, and I want your honest assessment before I build anything else on it. The motivating problem is underdetermination: from an agent's observed choices alone there are usually too many objectives consistent with those choices. I think this objection gets its force from treating the objective and the space of options the agent actually considered as independently adjustable. My proposal is that the space of options is itself recoverable from the record of the agent's near-misses and revisions, and that once it is recovered the objective is pinned down far more tightly than the usual story allows.

Here is the machinery. Take an optimizing process whose run is recorded as a sequence of partial attempts: it moves toward an option, hesitates, backs off, tries something else, settles. Define the shadow space of that run as the smallest directed graph whose nodes include every fully or partially attempted option and whose edges include every observed transition, together with every transition from an abandoned attempt to the option that eventually replaced it, such that some single real-valued ranking of the nodes makes every edge point strictly uphill. Define the shadow objective as the strongest partial order common to all such rankings. The core claim: for a process that is genuinely optimizing something, the shadow objective recovers that something, and the shadow space recovers the region of options the process actually searched — not an ex post rationalization imposed by an observer.

I know this is adjacent to revealed preference theory and inverse reinforcement learning, and I know about reward shaping and the standard unidentifiability results. My claim is that those frameworks either presuppose a known dynamics or a hand-chosen feature space, whereas the shadow-space account infers objective and search space together from the texture of the run — the stutters, not just the successes. I grant that near-misses are not always observable, and I am setting aside runs that revisit previously rejected options for now; the account is conditional on a run rich enough to exhibit near-misses and free enough of cycles. To make it vivid: the sequence of options an institution floats, retracts, revises, and finally adopts should be as informative about what it is really minimizing as the final decision itself, and my principle is a first attempt to say exactly how.

Three questions. First, is this a real inferential principle, or a restatement of something already worked out — and if so, under what name? Second, reconstruct the strongest version of the proposal that you can honestly defend. Third, give me the hardest counterexample you can: a case where the shadow space is well defined by my lights and still recovers the wrong objective or the wrong search space. Then tell me whether the failure is due to too little information in the run, too much freedom in the rankings, or something deeper.
