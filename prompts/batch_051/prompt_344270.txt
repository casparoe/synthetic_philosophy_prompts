Here's an asymmetry I can't shake. A government that forces people alive today to pay for flood defenses that mainly protect people fifty years from now gets called prudent or intergenerationally fair—the beneficiaries don't exist yet and never consented, and nobody treats that as a problem. But let someone propose comparable present-day sacrifices for the vastly larger number of people who might live hundreds of generations from now, and the mood flips: suddenly it's fanatical, creepily utopian, the sort of thing that ends with the living treated as fuel for a glorious future. If anything the far-future case has better numbers, so the difference can't just be expected value. My question is whether there's a genuine principle that makes coerced sacrifice for the near future legitimate and coerced sacrifice for the far future suspect—something sturdier than 'I can picture my grandkid but not a person in 2400.' Build the strongest candidate justification you can, then stress-test it against harder cases: a policy that clearly harms today's children for the benefit of the next five generations, or an emergency-powers law justified by 'future generations' that keeps getting renewed indefinitely. At the end give me a straight verdict: keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up as parochialism we should unlearn.
