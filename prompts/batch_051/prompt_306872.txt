I have spent most of my working life inside large companies, and I keep noticing an asymmetry in the way people think about organizations and economies that I cannot justify to myself. When a national government proposes to direct resources the way a corporate head office does — deciding which industries should grow, which factories should close, and what prices should be assigned to inputs — the standard objection is that this cannot work even in principle, because no central authority can gather and process enough information to calculate what should be produced. Prices, people say, have to emerge from trade, or no one is really calculating anything. Yet the same people will praise a multinational firm as efficiently managed when a handful of executives in a head office make exactly those decisions for hundreds of thousands of employees: moving resources between divisions, setting internal prices, expanding one unit and closing another. A firm of that size is a planned economy in miniature, and it often works well enough that nobody thinks to call the arrangement impossible.

I want to know whether the asymmetry is defensible. Is there a principled difference between the two cases — for instance, that the firm is always disciplined by an outside market it cannot control, whereas a planned national economy would have no outside to supply real prices — or does that just push the question one step back? If there is a real difference, where exactly along the scale from a two-person partnership to a large firm to a whole economy does the logic change, and what does the change consist in? If there is no real difference, then why should the usual objections to central planning be thought to stop at the company gate?
