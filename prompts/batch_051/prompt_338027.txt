I help run a two-hour evening workshop for around 18 working adults — mostly engineers and a few policy folks, none of them philosophy types. We want to run a session on why well-intentioned groups and companies keep ending up in races to the bottom, and I'd rather make them live through it than talk at them. So design a game where the group splits into teams (or plays as individuals, your call) and plays through rounds with real but small cash payoffs on the line. The thing I want them to come away seeing, without being told up front, is that the multipolar trap doesn't need selfishness or stupidity or a failure to communicate. They should be allowed to talk freely between rounds, make public promises, even write nonbinding agreements with each other — and the bad equilibrium should still pull them back in. At some point I'd like a mechanic that looks like it should fix it (a pledge ledger, a reputation score, a bonus pool for restraint, a penalty on the worst offender, whatever you dream up) and that either gets gamed or just changes the surface without touching the underlying incentive, and afterwards participants should be able to say exactly why it didn't work. I also want the round winner to finish ahead of everyone else but behind where they'd have been under full cooperation, so winning feels a little hollow. Give me the complete rules, exact payoffs and round structure, what happens on ties, phase timings, and then a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the parts of the idea. In the debrief, say what predictably goes wrong when faciliators run this — e.g. someone turns it into a martyrdom contest, or the group finds a loophole that makes cooperation individually rational after all — and how to handle it without leaning on the lecture they skipped. Also be honest about the one feature of real multipolar traps the exercise can't reproduce and the facilitator has to supply. The name "Moloch" shouldn't come up until the very end.
