I have spent the past few months advising a small public-sector body on whether it can responsibly use a large language model to help triage applications, and the question has recently become acute. The project lead has proposed that we manage the safety concerns by breaking each decision into small, human-checkable subtasks and allowing the model to act only within those subtasks, on the grounds that each subtask is easier to supervise than the final decision. That proposal evidently descends from the programme of iterated amplification and distillation: the idea that a system can be kept aligned while it becomes capable by repeatedly decomposing difficult tasks into simpler ones that a human can oversee, training a faster model to imitate the resulting human-supervised process, and then iterating. The load-bearing thought is that decomposition is what preserves both alignment and competence, because it is what keeps every increment of capability under human supervision.

But something about the setting seems to have changed since that argument was formulated. The models we are actually considering arrive already broadly competent, having been trained end-to-end on an enormous corpus of human text. Their ability to handle a case does not appear to come from anyone's having decomposed the task into checkable subtasks, and much of what they do between input and output is opaque even to their own builders. The capability is present before the decomposition, and the decomposition is imposed afterwards as an auditing device rather than as the engine of competence.

Does the iterated amplification and distillation argument survive this development? Which specific premise is under pressure: the claim about competence, namely that decomposition is how one produces a capable system, or the claim about alignment, namely that decomposition is how one keeps a system overseen? Once the competence claim is given up because capability now arrives as a by-product of generative pre-training, can the alignment claim still stand on its own, or does it silently depend on the competence claim it was originally paired with? I am interested in what happens to the argument's structure, not in a policy recommendation, and I would rather not receive a reading list; I want to know whether the argument is dead, in need of repair, or still sound with one of its premises reinterpreted, and what the strongest surviving version of it says.
