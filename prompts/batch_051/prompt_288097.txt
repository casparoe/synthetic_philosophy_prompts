Lately I've been applying a personal rule whenever I read or hear someone explain why something already happened: if the explanation leans on the outcome being inevitable, overdetermined, or obvious in retrospect, I discount it almost to zero. I don't check the evidence first; I treat the form of the explanation as the red flag. So if someone says the 2008 crash was bound to happen, or a startup was always going to fail, or the war had to break out eventually, I basically stop taking the account seriously. My thinking is that hindsight bias is strong enough that an after-the-fact story will make the outcome look more probable than it actually was, and the narrative fallacy will always smooth over the places where things couldhave gone differently. My friend says I've just built a bias in the opposite direction: I'm so allergic to fake inevitability that I can't credit real overdetermination, and I'm smuggling in the assumption that history is more contingent than it is. Who's right? I'm not really asking whether hindsight bias exists; I'm asking whether my rule is a defensible response to knowing it exists. Under what conditions does a backwards-looking "it had to happen" explanation actually earn belief, and when would my policy systematically mislead me? Is there a principled discount rate, or a better version of the rule that keeps the protection without the cost? And if the way I've set this up is itself confused — maybe I'm running together predictable-in-advance and intelligible-after-the-fact — tell me that plainly instead of answering it as posed.
