Every fall, the small town where I grew up holds an apple-pressing festival, and within an hour of the main road closing to cars an unprompted order appears: pedestrians settle on the left side even though we ordinarily keep to the right, and by the second day people tut and step aside pointedly when someone breaks the pattern. Nobody announced the rule, and nobody can remember it starting. I have no training in philosophy, but I have spent a long time thinking about how a pattern like that turns into something people feel they ought to obey, and I have agreed to lead a forty-five-minute discussion about it at my local library for a dozen retired people with no academic background — curious, widely read autodidacts who are impatient with jargon. The idea I want them to grasp is that some of our most binding social rules are conventions: they solve a recurring problem with more than one possible solution, and they stand or fall on mutual expectations and precedent rather than on command or punishment.

Could you plan how I should teach that idea rather than give me the explanation itself? I need the order of ideas: what has to be introduced first because the later moves depend on it, and why. I need the single example or scenario that should carry the whole session, including the features it must have and the features it must not have, so it does not smuggle in an authority or a threat. I need the misconceptions to expect — I would guess 'it is just habit' and 'someone must have started it as an official rule' will come up — and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off before it hardens. I need one question to ask at the end that would tell me whether they understand how a pattern becomes felt as a rule rather than merely observed as a regularity. And I need to know what to set aside explicitly as too advanced for a single session.
