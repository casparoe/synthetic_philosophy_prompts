hey — I'm putting together a "which position is actually yours?" decision tree for a small population-ethics reading group, on the procreation asymmetry. The setup everyone will know: having a child you know will be miserable seems clearly bad, but failing to have a child who would be very happy doesn't seem bad at all, or at least nowhere near as clearly bad. I want a tree that sorts people by the answers that actually drive the disagreement, not by which label they walk in liking.

Please build the tree around these three questions, in this order, each phrased in plain language and with a short concrete case attached:
Q1: whether a possible child's prospective happiness counts as a reason to bring that child about at all — yes, no, or if anything it counts the other way (because you think starting any life is a net harm to the child).
Q2: whether the reason not to start a miserable life is grounded in what would be bad for the child who lives it, or in the bare fact that the world would contain more misery.
Q3: in a same-number choice between two not-yet-existing children, one with a clearly better life than the other, whether there is any welfare-based reason to choose the better-off child, given that neither exists yet.

Leaves your tree has to actually reach — don't leave any of these out:
- totalism (the symmetrical view: more happy lives is good and more miserable lives is bad, full stop)
- Narveson-style narrow person-affecting view (we have reasons to make existing people happy, but no reasons to make happy people; creating the miserable child is wrong only because it harms the actual child)
- the deprivation-based explanation (never-existing people aren't deprived of goods, but an existing person can be harmed, and that is the whole source of the asymmetry)
- Benatar-style anti-natalism (coming into existence is always a net harm, so even a happy child's creation isn't permitted either)
- the brute-asymmetry view (the two cases just differ, and no deeper story is needed or available)

A few rules. Work from the logical space, not from that list of names: say which combinations of answers your questions allow, then locate the actual positions inside that space. Mark any region of the space that nobody seems to occupy and say, for each one, whether it's unoccupied because it's incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. And if two leaves end up impossible to separate with your questions — I already suspect the Narveson-style view and the deprivation-based view are going to fight — say so plainly and give the extra question that would split them, even if you wouldn't put it in the tree on a first pass.

Audience is smart undergrads, not specialists: no jargon without a one-line gloss. Keep it to around five leaves and no more than three or four questions.

Format the response like this:
1. "Leaf inventory" (4–6 sentences total): one plain-language sentence on each leaf and its most awkward commitment.
2. "The questions" (150–250 words): your questions in plain English, plus a one-line case for each.
3. "The tree" (the main section; write it as an actual branching instrument, indentation or arrows fine, with the leaf name and path at each terminal).
4. "Empty regions" (2–4 sentences each on any genuinely unoccupied combinations).
5. "Cannot-seperate pairs" (2–4 sentences per pair, plus the extra question that would split them).

Write it casually; it's a handout, not a paper.
