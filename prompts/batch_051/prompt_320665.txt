I've been trying to think through a way of reasoning I keep seeing people gesture at: rather than arguing about the details of, say, American decline or China's trajectory, you ask how long things of that broad kind have tended to last, and you use that as your starting forecast. You assemble a reference class of past cases — empires, great powers, hegemonic orders — compute the base rate of collapse or loss of dominance within some window, and apply it to the present case. The appeal, as I understand it, is that this is the outside view, and it keeps you from telling yourself a flattering just-so story about why your own society is uniquely durable.

But when I actually attempt this, the conclusion seems to be determined almost entirely by which reference class I pick. Treating "all historical empires" as the class gives one picture; treating "European land empires" as the class gives another; "naval hegemons" gives another; "nuclear-armed great powers since 1945" gives almost no usable data; "Chinese dynasties" gives another still. I had thought the entire point of the outside view was to stop me from choosing a reference class that flatters my prior. But now it looks like the choice of reference class is exactly where all the motivated reasoning goes. Is there a principled way to choose the right one? And if there isn't, does the outside view actually constrain anything?
