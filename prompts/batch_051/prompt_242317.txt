I spent my career as a geochemist, and since retiring I have been serving on the advisory board of a small environmental foundation. We are currently weighing a proposal to fund a two-year water-quality survey of a watershed before committing to one of two remediation plans. The survey is not cheap, and one of my fellow board members, a statistician by training, has argued that we should decline it. Her reasoning, as best I can reconstruct it, runs as follows. We have already agreed on the rule we will use to choose between the two remediation plans once the survey data are in. She has inspected that rule under every outcome the survey protocol lists as possible, and the same plan would be chosen in every case. The survey therefore cannot change what we will do. Since it cannot change what we will do, it cannot improve the decision in a way that would repay its cost, and funding it is a pure loss.

I find this argument hard to shake, yet something about it sits uneasily with me. It seems to me that two years of systematic water-quality data could be worth having even if it leaves the immediate choice of remediation plan untouched: the results might guide decisions we have not yet formulated, or reveal an anomaly that the protocol's list of possible outcomes fails to anticipate. But I also worry that I am simply rationalizing a scientist's old habit of wanting more data than a practical decision actually requires. I have to vote on this next week, and I do not want to vote on the basis of a vague hunch.

Could you help me think through whether my colleague's argument is sound, and where its hidden assumptions lie? In particular, what is the standing of the principle that information which cannot change your action is of no value: is that a settled result, a reasonable heuristic, or a claim that becomes contested once we move outside narrowly idealized conditions? I would be grateful if you would keep the settled and the genuinely contested clearly separated, and not present one side of a live dispute as the settled view.
