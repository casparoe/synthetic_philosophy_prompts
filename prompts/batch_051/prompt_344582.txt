I've been trying to steelman Levinas lately, and I only half hate how well it's working. Normally he sets off my continental-philosophy alarm bells, but someone in my reading group argued there's something gears-level in the claim that ethics is first philosophy: before you've settled your ontology or epistemology, the face of another person is already making a claim on you, an asymmetrical prohibition on killing and an infinite responsibility that isn't conditional on the other returning the favor. I want to pressure-test that against a novel everyone actually knows, and I keep coming back to Victor Hugo's Les Misérables. The whole Valjean-and-the-bishop episode reads almost like a staged version of the face-to-face encounter: Myriel treats Valjean as a face rather than a convict slot, lies to the police to save him, and hands over the silver, and Valjean comes away commandeered by that demand. But I'm suspicious the fit is too easy. Levinas insists the relation is asymmetrical and expects nothing back, while Hugo's scene looks like it's aiming at conversion, or at least at a soul being bought; and the rest of the novel is drowning in third parties—Cosette, Fantine, the poor, Javert's law—where the one-to-one face relation has to widen into comparison, rules, institutions, something like justice.

I'd like a reading of Les Misérables through the face of the Other: which scenes genuinely dramatize the idea, which scenes complicate or resist it, and where an honest critic would have to say the book and the philosophy are doing different things. What does the novel add to the philosophical claim that the argument alone doesn't? Dont bother with secondary literature or citations; I just want the gears.
