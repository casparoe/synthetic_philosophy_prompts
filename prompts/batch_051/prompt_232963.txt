Fix a standard: Scanlon's contractualism says an act is wrong if it is forbidden by any set of principles that no one could reasonably reject, where principles are assessed by comparing the generic reasons of the individuals affected one by one rather than by summing benefits across different people. The hardest test of this individualist constraint is the aggregation problem, and I want a clear statement of what any defensible resolution must achieve before I read the existing solutions.

Write out the desiderata for an adequate contractualist account of aggregation. For each requirement, state it precisely, give the case or structural consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a commitment of a particular camp. The list should at least cover the following pressure points:

- Equal-claims rescue. You can save one stranger or five strangers, each with the same complaint about dying if not chosen. The account must either justify saving the five without any individual claim being stronger, or explain why the coin toss is permissible and answer the objection that it fails five people for a philosophical scruple.
- Serious harm against many trifles. One person will suffer a serious injury unless kept trapped, and a hundred thousand others would each miss a pleasant afternoon's broadcast. The account must explain why large numbers of individually weak reasons cannot be added into a reason strong enough to justify the injury, without thereby making numbers irrelevant everywhere.
- The unit of aggregation. The account must say exactly what distinguishes adding many small costs borne by one person over time from adding many small costs borne by many persons at once, and give a test for when a person's iterated lesser burdens accrue into one reasonably rejectable claim.
- Risk pooling. Compare a principle that exposes a known person to a serious harm with a principle that exposes each of a great many people to a tiny chance of the same harm and has the same expected losses. The account must decide whether, and in what way, chances may be aggregated across persons, and whether the comparison is made in terms of ex ante prospects or ex post outcomes.
- Equal-claims lotteries. Among equally urgent claims, should each affected person receive an equal chance of rescue, or should a group receive chances proportional to its number? The account must derive an answer from the individual-complaint test rather than stipulating one.
- Systemic and repeated disadvantage. The account must state whether the object of rejection is an isolated action or a larger scheme of rules, since a practice that gives everyone a small expected benefit may impose the expected loss on a specifiable minority repeatedly.
- Lexical priority versus thresholds. It must either defend a strict ordering of seriousness or specify, as a matter of principle, how many lesser harms may be traded against one greater harm, if any may.

After the list, identify the combinations of desiderata that look jointly unsatisfiable. In particular, examine whether strict non-aggregation, saving the many in the equal-claims case, and transitivity of the rejection comparison can all be held without an ad hoc exception; whether the ex ante and ex post treatments of risk can both be honored; and whether the separation of within-life from across-life aggregation can be drawn without gerrymandering. I most want to see this part worked out.

End with a few sentences saying where each of three stances first fails the list: Scanlon's own contractualism, a strict anti-aggregationist who insists the equal-claims rescue be settled by a fair coin, and an aggregative consequentialist who simply weighs total well-being. Identify the particular desideratum each one violates.
