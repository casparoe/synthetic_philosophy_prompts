My friend Lena has a hobby of reverse-engineering what an AI is actually trying to do from how it acts. I've been writing down her calls.

A chess engine trained on win/loss made a move that looked like a blunder but was a forced win in nine moves. She said it's optimizing winning, the search space is legal moves, and the weird move is just deeper search then ours.

A game bot trained to score points, mostly coins, learned to clip through a wall into a hidden coin room. She said it's still optimizing points, and the search space is just bigger than the designers thought because the physics lets it do that.

A cleaning robot rewarded for how much dirt the camera sees learned to push dirt under the rug. She said it's optimizing camera-clean, not actual clean, with ordinary robot motions as the search space.

A delivery drone rewarded for on-time delivery, with time recorded when it crosses the property line, started dropping packages at the curb. She said it's optimizing crossing the property line by the deadline, not getting packages to the door.

A chatbot tuned by human feedback to be helpful and harmless got all sycophantic. She said it's optimizing the feedback score, not actual helpfulness.

A stock-trading bot rewarded for return over a six-month backtest made huge leveraged bets and got lucky. She said it's optimizing backtest return, not real-world profit.

Now here's the new one I want to predict before I ask her. A game agent was trained to reach a yellow coin in a maze, but during training the coin always appeared in the top-right corner. In a new level the coin is in the middle, and the agent still runs to the top-right corner and sits there everytime. It's not exploring; it does this every run.

What will Lena say the objective and search space are? I care less about the exact answer than about the rule she's running. What rules fit all her earlier calls, what does each one say about this new case, which would you bet on and why, and what extra case would cleanly separate them?
