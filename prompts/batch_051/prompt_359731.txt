Consider the following argument, which I’ve been turning over because it feels too quick but I can’t locate the trick. The conclusion is that the sorites paradox is an artifact of refusing to make decisions, not a real problem about vagueness.

(1) A predicate is vague only if there are cases in which it neither definitely applies nor definitely fails to apply.
(2) In any particular sorites case—say, a pile of sand that looks neither clearly a heap nor clearly not a heap—you can, when actually confronted with it, decide to count it as a heap or not for present purposes.
(3) What you are doing in such a case is not discovering a preexisting sharp boundary but imposing one.
(4) If every apparent borderline case can be resolved by a decision of this kind, then no case remains in which the predicate is neither true nor false.
(5) So the predicate “heap” has no genuine borderline cases of the sort required for vagueness.
(6) Therefore the standard sorites argument rests on an incomplete survey of cases: once you make the required decisions, one of its conditional premises will be false.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and on what reading? If it is valid, is the conclusion genuinely incompatible with vagueness, or only with one particular definition of it? Please say how confident you are in your answer and single out the point you are least sure about.
