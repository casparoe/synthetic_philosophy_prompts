I spent a career in a lab where a unit was something you pointed too or locked a laser to — a standard cell, a reference electrode, a cesium clock. So when I started reading philosophy of mathematics after retiring, I got genuinely stuck on the way Euclid opens Book VII of the Elements. He does not start with numbers. He first defines a unit — "A unit is that by virtue of which each of the things that exist is called one" — and only then defines a number as "a multitude composed of units."

I'd like someone to walk me through what that definition is actually doing, and whether it has anything in common with the units I used to calibrate instruments against. In particular: when I count seven apples, do I first have to take each apple as a unit in Euclid's sense, or is that backward — is the apple a unit because I've already decided to count apples rather than apple-halves or fruit? Is being a unit a property of the thing, of the kind it falls under, or of the act of counting? And the thing that really nags me is this: we've spent the last hundred-odd years turning our scientific units into fixed numerical constants — the metre by fixing the speed of light, the second by fixing a cesium transition, the kilogram by fixing Planck's constant. A unit under that regime looks suspiciously like a number wearing a unit's clothes. Does Euclid's definition shed any light on that, or is modern metrology just using the word for something else entirely?

I'm after a careful conceptual explanation, not a history lesson, and there's no need for citations.
