Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications, within the domain of the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism.

Define the two positions as follows. Act-utilitarianism: an act is right just in case it maximizes total expected welfare, with gains and losses to different people summed without regard to whose they are. The separateness-of-persons anti-aggregationist: welfare may be summed within a single life, but gains and losses of distinct persons may not be summed to outweigh one another, because there is no single subject who experiences the combined total; each person's claim is weighed individually, and a greater number of lesser claims does not, merely in virtue of being more numerous, outweigh one stronger claim.

Engineer a realistic two-option allocation case — a hospital, clinic, or public-health budget decision, not a trolley case — with the following structure.

First, the interpersonal case. A scarce resource can either avert one severe harm to a single person or avert five lesser harms to five different people. Set the severities so that the act-utilitarian and the anti-aggregationist give the same verdict: both use the resource to avert the one severe harm, leaving the five lesser harms in place. The agreement must be generated by the two principles, not stipulated — the utilitarian's sum must favor the one severe harm, while the anti-aggregationist must reach the same verdict by comparing individual claims and refusing to let the five lesser harms add up against the one severe harm.

Second, the intrapersonal mirror. Present the structurally identical choice with all harms falling within a single person's future: one severe harm at one time versus five lesser harms at five other times. This time set the severities so that the five lesser harms are collectively worse than the one severe harm, and the person whose future it is plainly prefers to avert the five. Show that the anti-aggregationist, if they apply their own rationale consistently across the distinct temporal parts of that one life, must instead avert the one severe harm — a verdict the person themselves rejects — while the act-utilitarian, summing within the life, sides with the person.

Then provide the demonstration:

(a) The interpersonal scenario, in one paragraph.

(b) For each position, the verdict in the interpersonal case and the reasoning that produces it.

(c) The intrapersonal mirror, with each position's verdict and reasoning there.

(d) The structural feature that does the work: the suppressed premise by which the anti-aggregationist treats the boundary between persons as different in kind from the boundary between temporal parts of a single person, even though the stated rationale applies to both.

(e) What happens if that feature is removed, or if the anti-aggregationist declines to supply it.

(f) The strongest reply a defender of the anti-aggregationist position can give, and whether it succeeds on its own terms rather than by assuming what it needs to show.

Do not make the anti-aggregationist a strawman. Their position should be presented as a serious attempt to capture why one person may not be used as a mere means to another's good, and their permission of ordinary intrapersonal tradeoffs should be treated as a commitment they genuinely hold and are entitled to try to justify. A case that stipulates the verdicts instead of deriving them from the principles fails the assignment. The case is for a problem set on whether the separateness objection can bear the weight it is usually asked to carry.
