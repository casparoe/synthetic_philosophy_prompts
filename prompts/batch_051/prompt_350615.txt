I'm part of a reading group that's spent the past few months on sacrifice and scapegoating, and the group keeps talking past each other because half of us mean by "guilt" an objective stain that can actually be handed off, and the other half mean a felt pollution that just gets expressed. I want to settle some of this by making a "which theory of the scapegoat do you actually hold?" sorting quiz for the group — one of those quizzes where people answer concrete case questions and get sorted into a position without ever seeing the labels. The point is to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which thinker they vibe with, so no question should mention Girard, Durkheim, substitutionary atonement, catharsis, or any other label. Each question should be a concrete scenario — a village loading its sins onto a goat and driving it out, a company firing one manager after a decision everyone had a hand in, a priest telling a woman her guilt has been transferred to a slaughtered lamb, a frat hazing one pledge so the rest of them bond, a crowd that turns on a single scapegoat and visibly relaxes afterwards — with two or three answer options that differ along the axes that actually matter: whether something real is transferred or only expressed, whether the victim has to actually be guilty or only believed guilty, whether the mechanism happens in the community, in the victim, or in each person's feelings, and whether the ritual's working and its rightness are separate questions.

I'd like eight to ten questions with a scoring key. The positions that need to show up as leaves are: a Girardian view that scapegoating actually works by discharging mimetic violence, whether or not we should keep doing it; a Durkheimian functionalist view that the real work of the ritual is producing solidarity and the language of transfer is just the idiom for it; a theological substitution view that guilt really is borne by the victim and that's what makes the ritual effective; a psychological catharsis view that the only thing transferred is the participants' felt relief; and an eliminativist view that talk of transferred guilt is confused and nothing actually moves. If the logical space has natural positions that nobody ends up occupying, flag them and say whether they're empty because they're incoherent, repulsive, or just unexplored. And if two of these leaves can't be separated by any concrete question — I suspect the Durkheimian and the catharsis view might collapse into each other — say so rather than inventing a question that only tests whether someone knows the vocabulary.

The audience is a mixed group of theology nerds and anthropology nerds, so the answer options should be plain language and shouldn't tip which answer feeds which theory. One diagnostic split I care about especially and havent seen handled well elsewhere: whether the victim's innocence matters to the mechanism. Try to build the tree so that split comes in early if the answers allow it.
