Within economics, a long-running argument from revealed preference holds that utility need not be interpreted as an unobservable hedonic state or an introspectable quantity of satisfaction; it can be treated as a numerical index constructed from an agent's choices, with the only requirement being that the choices satisfy consistency conditions such as the weak axiom of revealed preference. On this view, to say that one option has greater utility than another is, at bottom, a compact way of summarising what the agent does.

Now consider a specific change in the choice environment. Many everyday choices are no longer made from a menu fixed independently of the chooser; they are made inside interfaces in which the menu is generated in real time by an algorithm that maintains a continuously updated model of the individual's past behaviour, inferred tastes, and known behavioural vulnerabilities, and that is optimised for the platform's objectives—engagement, retention, or revenue—rather than for the chooser's own ends. The system runs randomised experiments, learns which presentations produce which responses, and can offer different menus to different people, or to the same person in different contexts. Choices are therefore not simply revealed; they are in part produced by an optimisation process whose objective function may diverge from the chooser's. The original argument took the option set as given and treated the chooser as the only relevant optimiser in the picture; the new environment makes the option set endogenous and inserts a second optimiser between the chooser and the observed choice data.

Does the revealed-preference argument for the interpretation of utility as a construct from choice survive this development? Identify precisely which premise or assumption of the argument is put under pressure. Then assess whether the argument can be repaired—for instance, by restricting the class of admissible choice data to relatively 'unengineered' choice situations—or whether the pressure is deep enough that a different account of utility, and of how it is measured, is needed. If you think a repair is possible, state the repair and its costs; in particular, say whether it must tacitly rely on a notion of 'authentic' or 'unmanipulated' preference, and whether that notion can itself be given a purely behavioural characterisation. If you think the argument cannot be repaired in any form, say what concept of utility remains, and what observational basis, if any, it still has.
