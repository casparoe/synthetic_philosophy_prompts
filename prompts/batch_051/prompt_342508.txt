I’ve spent a fair amount of time outside formal study thinking about mysticism—Meister Eckhart, The Cloud of Unknowing, some of the Upanishads, and similar sources—and I keep coming back to the same idea. I’m not religious in any orthodox way, but I’ve become convinced that there is a kind of knowledge that cannot be put into words. I don’t just mean the usual examples like knowing what a color looks like or how to ride a bike. I mean something stronger: that the deepest truths about reality are in principle unsayable, and that language necessarily distorts them when it tries to capture them. The mystics seem to agree on this while disagreeing on almost everything else. They use paradox, negation, and poetry, and then they say even those fail. I’ve come to hold this view myself: that some people come to know something real—something more than a feeling or a mood—through meditation, contemplation, or certain kinds of experience, and that this knowledge cannot be stated in propositions.

I was arguing about this with a friend, and he said the whole idea is either empty or self-contradictory. He asked me what would change my mind, and I didn’t have a good answer. So I want to know: what would count against this position, really? Not just the obvious criticism that if it can’t be said, then no one can assert it, but the deeper ones. What observations, arguments, or developments should move me? Which are fatal and which merely awkward? Are there things this view predicts that rival views don’t? I’d also like an honest assessment of whether the position has become unfalsifiable—or whether it started out that way—because its defenders can always say the critic just hasn’t had the experience. If it is insulated, is that a problem for a claim about knowledge, or is it exactly what we should expect? Please rank the defeaters from decisive to minor, and please give the answer in American English.
