I’m a retired biologist who has recently gotten into these arguments about whether the Neolithic was a wrong turn. My friend Dana, who’s a historian, has very specific verdicts on cases, and I’ve started keeping a list. So far she says: mobile, low-density foragers like the Kalahari San were not a mistake—they had enough to eat, modest work hours, few wants, and no permanent bosses. Early cereal farmers in the Fertile Crescent were a mistake—worse teeth and bones, more infectious disease, longer work, and the first durable elites. Nineteenth-century industrial Machester was a mistake—falling stature, child labour, smoke, and enormous inequality. But she is torn about a hypothetical coastal population we’ve been discussing: dense and sedentary, but non-farming, living off stored salmon and acorns, with very good nutrition and health, moderate work hours, and plenty of leisure, yet with hereditary chiefs, sharply ranked lineages, and a permanently subordinate class that is not enslaved but is unfree in important ways.

I want to predict what she would say if forced to be consistent with her earlier rulings, and which earlier case is the closest precedent. I can see at least two plausible rules behind her record—one about health, leisure, and nutrition, and one about hierarchy and inequality—but maybe there are others. Tell me what each candidate rule predicts for the coastal case, which one fits her record best, and what further case would cleanly separate them. And please be explicit about which parts of this are actually settled among people who study the shift to farming and which are genuinely contested; don’t present a contested view as the consensus.
