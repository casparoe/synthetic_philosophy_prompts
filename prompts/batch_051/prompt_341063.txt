Readings of Hume on causation have been deadlocked since the 1980s, and I want a diagnosis of the deadlock rather than another verdict on Hume. On the older, still standard reading, Hume is a regularity theorist: causation in the objects is nothing over and above constant conjunction, and the idea of necessary connection is something the mind contributes, through habit, on the basis of experienced regularity. On the New Hume reading, he is a sceptical realist: he allows that genuine causal powers may exist in nature, while denying that we can know or even clearly conceive them; regularities are our evidence for such powers, not their whole content. Both camps quote the same passages, both agree on the text, and neither moves. My working hypothesis is that this is not, at bottom, a factual dispute about what Hume believed, but a dispute over interpretative policy: over how to handle an author who says things that pull in two directions, over what charity or fidelity requires, and over how far it is legitimate to import distinctions—between what exists and what we can know, for instance—that Hume never drew in so many words. Is that right, or is there a genuine hidden question here that could in principle be settled by closer reading, a new distinction, or something else? Diagnose the impasse as precisely as you can, and say what would count as progress under your diagnosis—or whether none should be expected. Assume a reader who knows the Treatise and the first Enquiry; spend the word count on the diagnosis rather than on explaining Hume. Keep the response under 900 words.
