I've ended up committed to a lexical-threshold version of negative utilitarianism and want to know how exposed it actually is. The view: below some level of suffering, pleasure and pain trade off in the ordinary way, but above a threshold—the kind of pain you'd call agony or torture—no amount of pleasure, spread across however many people, can make one instance of it permissible or not-worth-preventing. So one person tortured for a day isn't counterbalanced by a billion people each getting a nice afternoon. What would have to be true, or which cases would I have to find compelling, for this to be false? Give me the strongest objections sorted by how decisive they'd be, and flag which are genuinely decisive and which are merely awkward costs. Include the objections a defender of the view should themselves concede, not only what critics say. Aim especially at the threshold: why draw the line there rather than somewhere else, and does any attempt to justify it collapse the view into either ordinary commensurability or the much stronger claim that any pain trumps any pleasure? Finally, is this position just insulated from falsification by the vagueness of its threshold—and if so, is that a serious problem for a view of this kind, or just the normal state of normative ethics?
