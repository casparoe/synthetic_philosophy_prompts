Some people in my research group have started saying, as if it were settled, that "religion" is not a coherent category: at best it is a family-resemblance term, and at worst it is a modern Western construct that lumps together institutions and practices with no shared essence. Buddhism, Confucianism, and so-called secular religions like nationalism or organized fandom all get called religions, and on their view the label imports a Christian-shaped template and obscures more than it reveals. I find this plausible, but I notice the claim is usually stated in a form that could never be wrong. I want you to treat the constructivist claim as a hypothesis rather than a slogan, and to suppose there is a defender of the category who thinks "religion" does pick out a recurring cross-cultural kind, one whose instances share something beyond a label and whose classification predicts further features. Design the empirical program that would arbitrate, or at least substantially narrow, the dispute.

Concretely: first, say what "coherent category" would have to mean operationally—for example, within-category similarity exceeding between-category similarity, inter-rater reliability in classifying cases, predictive validity of the category label, and stability of its extension across cultural vantage points—and be explicit about which of these criteria each side could accept without stacking the deck. Then propose one or more concrete tests or comparative studies, using among your cases Buddhism, Confucianism, and putatively secular religions (and any other borderline cases you need, such as ancestor veneration), and specify for each test what pattern of results would count for the constructivist and what pattern would count for the category defender. Finally, and this is the part I care most about: state exactly what a positive result would and would not settle. Would high consensus among scholars show the category is real, or only that scholars share training? Would predictive utility show a natural kind, or only a pragmatically useful grouping? Are there parts of the dispute that no test can reach, because they concern how we should use the word rather than what the world is like? Push the empirical component as far as it can go, and then be precise about where the residue lies.
