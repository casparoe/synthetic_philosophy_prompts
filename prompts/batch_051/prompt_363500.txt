I am a philosophy professor, and next month I am giving a single ninety-minute session on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism to a reading group of advanced undergraduates and faculty from other departments. The participants will arrive having read a short introductory piece presenting hedonism as the view that well-being consists in pleasure and the absence of pain, but they will not have encountered the objection that the various states hedonism must classify together as pleasures look radically unlike one another. I do not want a lecture on hedonism; I want a plan for the session.

In what order must the components be introduced so that the heterogeneity objection registers as a genuine threat to the theory rather than as a terminological quibble — in particular, what must be secured first about what hedonism is a theory of, and why does the objection only bite once that is in place? Which example should carry the whole session? My sense is that the familiar low-versus-high contrast of a trivial bodily pleasure with an elevated intellectual one is the wrong choice, because it imports a question about ranking pleasures and invites the group to argue about whether some pleasures are nobler instead of about what all of them share; if I am right, what list of three or four apparently different pleasures should replace it, and what features must that list have and what features must it avoid? What misconceptions should I expect the audience to bring or form — for instance, running together prudential hedonism and psychological hedonism, or reducing pleasure to a single felt sensation, or thinking that the distinction between higher and lower pleasures already dissolves the problem — and at what precise point in the sequence should each be headed off? What question or task should I put to them at the end to check that they have understood the problem itself rather than merely learned the label 'heterogeneity'? Finally, what should I deliberately leave out at this level, and how should I say that I am leaving it out?
