Next month I'm giving a paper to an analytic metaphysics seminar on why Black's two-sphere universe is a worse objection to Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles than people usually think. The room is mostly Leibniz-friendly metaphysicians who take the principle as a structural claim about possible worlds, plus two foundations-of-physics people who keep wanting to talk about indistinguishable electrons. My talk fixes on a specific passage in Leibniz's fourth letter to Clarke: the bit where he argues, roughly, that if two things were perfectly indiscernible, God would have no sufficient reason to place one here and the other there, so a choice between the two arrangements would be arbitrary, and therefore such a pair cannot exist. I'm reading that passage as making a point about the intelligibility of God's spatial choices, not about intrinsic qualitative duplicates, and my thesis is that Black's spheres only refute a flattened version of the principle that Leibniz didn't hold.
