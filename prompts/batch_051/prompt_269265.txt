I'm taking a comparative seminar on equality, discrimination, and group-based selection, and we have been moving between American university admissions, Indian reserved legislative seats, and the Scandinavian boardroom quotas. Each week the instructor introduces a hypothetical selection policy and tells us how she would rule on it if she sat on a constitutional equality tribunal. I have been logging her decisions, and they feel as though they are being generated by a single principle rather than ad hoc preferences, but I cannot quite extract the principle.

Her track record so far: she upheld a public medical school's individualized, whole-file review that treats membership in a minority group as one favorable consideration among many, where the school had formally excluded that group until the 1980s; she struck down an undergraduate college's scheme that automatically awards a fixed number of points to minority applicants, where the college had no history of excluding them; she struck down a Norwegian-style statute requiring at least forty percent of corporate board seats to be held by women, defended only by women's statistical underrepresentation on boards and unaccompanied by any legislative finding of specific exclusion; and she struck down a city's set-aside reserving thirty percent of public contracts to firms owned by an ethnic minority, defended only by the goal of making the business community mirror the city's diversity, with no finding of past exclusion.

Next week's case is the one I have to predict: a constitutional amendment reserves a substantial block of seats in the national legislature for members of a low-caste group whose systematic exclusion from political office until recently is established by detailed legislative findings, and the reservation sunsets after twenty years.

Treat me as a colleague in the seminar who is trying to reverse-engineer the instructor's implicit theory. Start by brainstorming candidate principles that fit all four earlier rulings — an individualized-review rule that forbids quotas and numerical devices; a remedial-history rule that permits group-conscious measures only when there is a documented finding of past exclusion; an anti-subordination rule; strict color-blindness; anything else — and discard any that the record rules out. For each surviving principle, work out what it predicts about next week's reservation case. Then give your best prediction of her verdict, with your reason for preferring one principle over the alternatives, and state which earlier case is the closest analogy to the new statute and what principle the instructor would be committing herself to either way. Finally, propose one further hypothetical — not simply a rerun of next week's case — that would cleanly separate the two strongest remaining candidate principles, and say what each principle predicts for that further case.
