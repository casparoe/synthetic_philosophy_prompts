I recently got into an argument with a friend who defends utilitarianism, and he kept returning to an analogy that he treated as close to decisive. Society, he said, is simply a single person spread out across many bodies. Just as a prudent individual will accept a painful procedure now for the sake of a larger benefit later, so a society may require some of its members to absorb losses whenever the total gain is large enough to compensate for them. On his view, someone who objects to being sacrificed for the greater total is making the same mistake as a patient who refuses a necessary injection because it hurts: they are failing to see themselves as parts of the larger whole rather than as separate units whose losses cannot be outweighed. He concluded that the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism is therefore a piece of small-scale sentimentality, not a principled limit on aggregation.

I want an audit of this analogy, not just a verdict. Work out exactly what maps onto what when the utilitarian treats interpersonal aggregation on the model of intrapersonal prudence; identify where the mapping breaks down; separate the disanalogies that actually matter for his conclusion from the ones that are merely superficial; and say whether the conclusion—that the separateness-of-persons objection is defused—survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If there is a better analogy for what utilitarianism is trying to defend, present it and say what it gets right. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
