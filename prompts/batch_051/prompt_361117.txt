People I talk to who do ML keep saying natural selection is just stochastic gradient descent on a fitness landscape, and then they draw opposite morals. Some say gradient descent must be basically fine because it's what produced cognition; others say evolution is a stupid optimizer — no gradient, no credit assignment, just random perturbations and selection — so gradient descent is going to get stuck in the same local junk. I want to know how far this actually goes. Give me the full correspondence table: in evolution, what plays the role of the loss, the gradient, the learning rate, the batch, the parameter vector, the train/test split? Where I get confused is that quantitative genetics literally writes one generation of selection as a gradient step: change in mean trait = covariance matrix × selection gradient, with the breeder's equation as the one-trait cousin. If that's right, the analogy isn't just loose talk, its a real approximation in some regime. So the real question is what regime it's valid in and what gets quietly imported. Go through where the mapping breaks — no backprop through the genotype-phenotype map, an objective that's nonstationary because the environment includes other evolving organisms, sex and recombination breaking the one-parameter-vector picture, drift when populations are small, frequency dependance, niche construction — and tell me which of those actually break the two ML morals above and which are just fixable amendments. Then the verdict: is evolution a stupid optimizer, and what specifically should gradient descent take, and refuse to take, from natural selection?
