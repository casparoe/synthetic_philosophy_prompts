For a seminar I attend on early modern metaphysics, I have been asked to contribute a poem in place of a short reflection paper. The assigned text is the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence, and I keep returning to the passage in which Leibniz argues that no two distinct substances can be perfectly alike, supporting this by asking his reader to go into a garden and try to find two leaves that match exactly; if two things had all their properties in common, they would be one thing, not two. I want a Petrarchan sonnet on that argument and on Max Black's later counterexample of a symmetric universe containing nothing but two iron spheres. The octave should build Leibniz's case—qualitative sameness entails numerical identity, so the imagined pair of indiscernible leaves collapses into a single leaf—and the volta should carry the reader into Black's world, where the two spheres are exactly similar by construction and every relation each bears to the other is mirrored, leaving nothing for a property to do. The sestet should not settle the dispute; it should leave Leibniz and the two spheres each standing. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher who knows the dispute would recognize the argument rather than wince, and it should still work as a poem for someone encountering the problem for the first time. As someone who came to philosophy late from experimental physics, I am especially interested in what the poem does with the idea that the symmetry of the two-sphere world is supposed to be invisible from inside it. Please give the sonnet first, then one titled section, "Commentary," of about 150–200 words, explaining the structural choices—especially where the volta falls and what the closing lines leave unresolved—and noting any philosophical points the poem compresses or deliberately leaves open.
