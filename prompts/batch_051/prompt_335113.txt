In Chapter I of The Subjection of Women (1869), Mill writes: “In all other cases, the burthen of proof is supposed to lie with the affirmative.” He offers this remark while arguing that opponents of women’s emancipation are allowed to evade the evidentiary standards applied in other disputes; later in the essay he develops the related charge that appeals to “nature” in debates about women’s roles frequently confuse what is customary with what is natural. As a PhD student in political philosophy, I would like you to work out the most important implications of Mill’s procedural remark for contemporary naturalistic arguments about social arrangements. Specifically: if the burden of proof lies with those who assert a natural basis for unequal treatment, what must such an assertion establish before it may shift the burden, and how does Mill’s later critique of custom-as-nature bear on what could count as adequate evidence? Please also address whether Mill’s procedural point commits him to a substantive default of equality, and whether that default can be justified independently of his broader utilitarian commitments.
