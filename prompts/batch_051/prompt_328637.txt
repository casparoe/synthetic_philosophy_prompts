(1) A rule, unlike a mere pattern or habit, is exceptionless; a generalization that admits exceptions is only a summary of what usually happens.
(2) The rules of English grammar that people actually learn all admit exceptions. The "add -ed" rule for the past tense is broken by sing -> sang, go -> went, and hit -> hit; the "add -s" rule for plurals is broken by mouse -> mice, child -> children, and sheep -> sheep.
(3) So by (1), none of these are really rules.
(4) The same is true across the grammar: for any rule of English there are irregular forms or listed exceptions.
(5) Therefore English has no grammatical rules at all, only stored forms, patterns, and habits.
(6) But that conclusion cannot be right. Children produce forms such as "goed" and "mouses," which they have never heard and which look like applications of a rule rather than imitation. New verbs regularly take the -ed past tense ("tweeted," "Googled"), even when a similar-sounding older verb is irregular. And cases like go/went, where the past tense is a completely unrelated word (suppletion), stand out precisely because there is a regular pattern they do not follow. If (5) were true, none of this would make sense.

Which step or inference should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
