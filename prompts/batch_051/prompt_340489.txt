Question: State Russell's theory of definite descriptions as presented in "On Denoting," and explain how it resolves the puzzle of negative existentials such as "The present King of France is bald" and "The round square does not exist." In particular, address whether a definite description is a singular term with a meaning or denotation of its own, and explain the role of scope in Russell's solution.

Candidate's answer: Russell analyses a sentence of the form "The F is G" as a quantified statement rather than a subject–predicate one: there is exactly one F, and it is G; in symbols, ∃x(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y = x) ∧ Gx). The definite description is an "incomplete symbol": it has no meaning in isolation and drops out under analysis, contributing only the existential and uniqueness conditions. Its meaning is, in effect, "the unique F," which the rest of the sentence then says something about (for example, that it is bald). On this basis, "The present King of France is bald" is false, since France has no king, not truth-valueless as on Frege's account of empty designations; the negation, "The present King of France is not bald," is therefore true. The same analysis resolves the negative existential: in "The round square does not exist," the description takes narrow scope, so the sentence is analysed as "there is no unique round square," not as the assertion of a subject, the round square, that it lacks existence. Russell thereby avoids both Meinong's subsistent non-existent objects and Frege's truth-value gaps.
