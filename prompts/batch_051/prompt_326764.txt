I am a novelist, and an argument that began in my writing group has reached a point where I cannot referee it; I would welcome the verdict of a professional philosopher of language specialising in the semantics and pragmatics of loaded terms.

In my current draft, a sympathetic first-person narrator, recalling a bigoted character's outburst, uses a well-known racial slur once in her own narrative voice within the sentence reporting what the man said; call the slur S and its neutral counterpart N. My own concern had been merely stylistic, but two friends, Naomi and Julian, fell into a genuinely difficult dispute about what S itself contributes to the sentence.

Naomi argued that S carries its derogatory force as part of its standing meaning — something like a conventional implicature or an expressive component — and that this explains why the force survives embedding. She observed that S offends under negation, in questions, in conditionals, and inside attitude reports in a way that merely descriptive classifications do not: "He is not N" and "Is he N?" cause no particular difficulty, yet "He is not S" and "Is he S?" still trouble readers. Because the force projects through those embeddings, she concluded, it cannot be merely what a speaker does with the word on an occasion. That, she said, is why the slur in my narrator's sentence stays live: the word contributes the derogation itself, independently of the narrator's sympathetic attitude.

Julian replied that S and N pick out the same group and predicate the same classification, so they do not differ in what is said; what differs is that S is a word whose use is prohibited. The offence inside negations and questions is better explained, he argued, by a ban than by encoded meaning, because content can be denied, discussed, reported, or quoted at arm's length, whereas the sting of S mostly cannot. For fiction, he added, this matters: my narrator is depicting the man's act of speech rather than performing it, and a prohibition view leaves room to see that depiction as distinct from the slurring itself.

Naomi answered that the prohibition view makes S's force too independent of S's meaning. Severity, she insisted, tracks the history of the group targeted: some slurs are more poisonous than others, and a bare account of rule-breaking cannot explain that gradation. She also thought reclaimed in-group uses supported her, not Julian: when in-group speakers use S among themselves, they do something metalinguistic — flouting, revaluing, or riffing on a word whose derogatory charge is still in the shared lexicon. If S were not already charged in its standing meaning, reclamation would have nothing to reclaim.

Julian countered that reclamation shows exactly the opposite: one and the same expression can be abusive from an out-group mouth and affiliative from an in-group one, which is a difference in use rather than in encoded meaning. If derogation were a conventional implicature, he argued, it should still fire in reclaimed uses and produce offence where plainly there is none. As for severity, he said, a prohibition can be more or less entrenched, and its weight can reflect the history that made the word radioactive, so the ban view is not flat.

At that point both turned to me. They agreed the chapter would read differently if I printed S, N, or a paraphrase, but each drew the opposite lesson. Naomi thought the standing force of S would stain the narrator's voice regardless of her attitude, so only N or a paraphrase could keep the narrative voice clean. Julian thought a sufficiently careful, non-endorsing context could mark the narrator's sentence as report rather than use. I honestly cannot see which of their specific claims survives.

Please act as a professional philosopher of language and a careful close reader. Begin by brainstorming the main ways one might theorise the force of a slur, keeping the distinction between what a word encodes and what a speaker does with it firmly in view. Then adjudicate point by point: which of Naomi's and Julian's claims are correct, which are overstated or in tension with one another, and which distinctions they are both missing — for example, the difference between a word being offensive and its being derogatory, and the difference between a narrator reporting a character's speech and a speaker using the word herself. Conclude with a reasoned judgement about who is more right overall, and spell out what the best account would imply about whether that one sentence in my narrator's voice implicates the narrator in the slur. Please think carefully and answer in British English.
