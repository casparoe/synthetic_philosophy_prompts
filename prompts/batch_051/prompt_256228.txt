A friend and I have been arguing—in the way one does after reading too many rationalist-blog posts about taboo tradeoffs and moral intuitions—about how to separate the distinct objections one might have to a market transaction, and we keep stalling on one case. In 2015, Turing Pharmaceuticals acquired Daraprim (pyrimethamine), an old, off-patent drug used to treat toxoplasmosis, especially in immunocompromised patients, and raised its list price roughly from $13.50 to around $750 per tablet. The move provoked near-universal revulsion, congressional hearings, and extensive commentary, but no substantial change in American drug-pricing law; the executive responsible was later imprisoned for unrelated securities fraud. The easy Econ-101 reading says this is just a monopolist extracting rent from an inelastic demand curve, and the easy "repugnance" reading says the public reaction is itself evidence that something sacred was being commodified. I suspect both readings are too fast, and I would like a more disciplined analysis.

I want to run the episode through the lens of the repugnant-markets and commodification tradition associated with figures like Michael Sandel, Alvin Roth, and Elizabeth Anderson. Sandel, in particular, distinguishes fairness-based objections to markets from corruption or commodification-based objections—the worry that certain goods are degraded or their meaning changed when they are bought and sold—while Roth treats repugnance as a real constraint on which markets societies permit. The task is to map this case in both directions: what the theory says about what the relevant actors faced and should have done, and what the case does to the theory.

So please give me a careful case analysis rather than an op-ed. Specifically: Is the moral harm here best described as commodification at all, or is it better described as price gouging, monopoly pricing, or distributive unfairness—and does that distinction collapse under scrutiny? What exactly is being commodified in the Daraprim case—the drug, access to treatment, health itself, or something else—and under whose account does that matter? What would Sandel's corruption objection say the firm, the public, and regulators faced and should have done, and what features of the episode does it have to strain to explain? Does the public reaction look like Roth's repugnance operating as a genuine constraint on markets, or like something noisier and more downstream of distributive anger, and what would that imply about treating repugnance as moral evidence? Finally, what should we conclude from the fact that the episode produced revulsion but almost no legal or institutional change—does that tell us that commodification-based complaints are politically weak when pitted against standard economic arguments, or that the theory was never quite the right tool for this case? I am not especially interested in whether the executive was a bad person; I want to know whether the episode confirms, qualifies, or undermines the idea that repugnance and commodification are doing distinctive moral work here.
