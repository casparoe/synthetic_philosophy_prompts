I have recently been asked to help draft an internal policy for a company that builds AI systems, and the task has forced me to confront a question I had previously been content to leave vague: what does it take for something to have moral status, that is, to be the kind of thing that matters directly for its own sake and to which duties can be owed? The easy cases are easy, but the cases we actually have to decide about are not. The underlying dispute is whether one of our systems could ever move from being a mere instrument to being a patient in its own right, and I suspect the correct criterion cannot be purely biological, but I cannot yet say what it is.

I would like a working definition of moral status (or moral patienthood, if you prefer that term) that correctly classifies the following cases: (1) a healthy adult human; (2) a dog; (3) a granite boulder; (4) a pocket calculator; (5) a corporation that can be fined and that pursues goals; (6) a person in a persistent vegetative state; (7) a salmon; (8) a honeybee; (9) a whole-brain emulation of a person, running at ten times normal speed, which reports suffering and fear; (10) a reinforcement-learning agent trained with an internal negative-reward signal that acts as an artificial analogue of pain and that the agent works to minimize; (11) a large language model fine-tuned to describe itself as having feelings but with no persistent states or goals; and (12) a digital copy of a person that can be paused, rewound, or duplicated.

I am fairly settled that (1) and (2) have moral status and that (3) and (4) do not; I lean toward saying that (6) and (7) do; and I am genuinely uncertain about the rest, especially (9), (10), and (12), where my intuitions pull against one another. Please propose the definition, run it through all twelve cases, and tell me where it yields a verdict you would want to revise, where it gives no determinate answer, and which of your final verdicts you are least confident about.
