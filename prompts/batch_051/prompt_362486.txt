After a career in ecology and population genetics, I have been spending my retirement reading philosophy and the older human sciences. I keep returning to a passage near the beginning of Edward Sapir's *Language* (1921), where he distinguishes speaking from walking. Sapir writes: "Speech is a human activity that varies without assignable limit as we pass from social group to social group, because it is a purely historical heritage of the group, the product of long-continued social usage." He then compares this variation to the variation found in religions, beliefs, customs, and the arts, insisting that speech is an acquired, cultural function rather than a biological one. I want you to restate this position in the vocabulary of conservation biology and evolutionary genetics, which I know well. That means making explicit what would correspond, in Sapir's picture, to species, population, gene pool, heritable variation, adaptive radiation, ecosystem function, and extinction. Where the translation is clean, say so; where it forces his claim into a shape it does not naturally have, identify the distortion; and where his claim has no expression at all in that vocabulary, lay out the residue. Then assess the residue: does it show that Sapir's account is missing something a Darwinian framework would catch, or does it show that my biological vocabulary quietly presupposes that the objects worth conserving are reproducing biological lineages and therefore cannot, without amendment, express the value of a purely historical and cultural form? I am especially interested in what this implies for the claim that languages have value beyond the people who currently speak them.
