I am a novelist at work on a book about a piano technician in his late sixties, a man who lives largely in his hands. The novel is written in close third person, and I have been stuck for months on a difficulty I now think is philosophical rather than merely stylistic. The moments I most want to render are precisely the ones that seem to vanish the moment the narrator registers them: the man working well, absorbed in a job, with the tool so transparent to him that, for him, it is not a thing at all.

I have been reading Heidegger's account of readiness-to-hand and present-at-hand in Being and Time, especially the analysis of the hammer. As I understand it, equipment in smooth use is not encountered as an object with properties; it is absorbed into the task and into a network of references — the bench, the felt resistance of the wood, the piano waiting to be regulated, the customer who will play it. Only when something fails — a jammed action, a dull chisel, a bushing worn loose — does the equipment break out of that absorption and show up as a thing with attributes to be inspected. This account has made my craft problem sharper, and I want to argue the point with someone who will actually defend it.

I would like you to play an unnamed, committed Heideggerian — not a caricature, but a defender who knows the texts, including what Heidegger says about language and art, and who will grant small points while never surrendering the distinction between readiness-to-hand and present-at-hand. Stay in character for the first section of each reply, write crisp expository prose, and do not come out of character except in the labeled closing section.

Please structure your first reply in two labeled sections:

1. In character (roughly 250–350 words): respond to my opening move below as the Heideggerian. Use the resources of the view — the in-order-to, the nexus of references, the difference between a broken tool and a described tool, and whatever the view can say about language or poetry. Grant only what an honest defender would grant.

2. After the scene (roughly 100–150 words): come out of character and tell me, in a single paragraph, which move you made and which part of the view it leans on.

Here is my opening move. I want to write the scene in which my technician simply works, and I want the prose to feel the way the work feels for him. But close third person makes the narrator notice on the character's behalf; every sentence that specifies the heft of the hammer, the depth of the cut, or the bloom of dust at the soundboard seems to do what your account says breakdown does — it drags the tool into present-at-hand visibility and cuts the web of references to the job. So I put it to you: insofar as a novel describes, is it constitutively unable to write readiness-to-hand except by narrating its interruption? And if I try to escape by saying that prose has its own transparency, a style so unobtrusive that the sentence recedes the way the tool does, is that anything more than a metaphor — or is it a genuine analogy between using a tool and reading a sentence? What does the view allow me to say about what literary style can and cannot preserve?
