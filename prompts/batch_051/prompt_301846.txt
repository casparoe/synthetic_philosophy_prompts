Suppose that in 2035, new methods come into routine use that allow biologists to reconstruct, for any heritable trait in any extant population, the complete history of selection acting on the alleles underlying the trait since the trait first arose, at a resolution fine enough to distinguish positive selection, stabilizing selection, drift, and linked hitchhiking (spread due to genetic linkage to a selected site). The methods are cheap, automated, and regarded as reliable by essentially everyone in the field, and within a few years thousands of traits have been profiled.

Predict how the debate between etiological (selected-effects) accounts of biological function and causal-role accounts plays out over the following two decades. On the etiological view, a trait's function is what past selection favored it for; on the causal-role view, a trait's function is what it contributes to the current organized working of the organism. Be specific about which side, if either, gains or loses ground, and about how the standard cases and problem cases shift: vestigial organs, exaptations, traits in their first generation before selection has acted, traits maintained largely by drift, and traits that are by-products of selection elsewhere. Which parts of the old dispute are empirical, in the sense that the new historical data could plausibly settle them, and which parts are conceptual and would survive any amount of historical information? Distinguish the predictions you hold with high confidence from those that depend on sociological assumptions about how biologists and philosophers respond to new tools, and say what would have to hold for the weaker predictions to be wrong.
