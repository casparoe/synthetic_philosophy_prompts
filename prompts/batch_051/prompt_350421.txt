I recently moved from a molecular biology degree into a philosophy of science programme, and the transition has made a familiar piece of laboratory shorthand suddenly contentious. In the lab I would say without hesitation that DNA stores and transmits genetic information, that the genetic code is a code, that genes are expressed and read out, and that a point mutation is a copying error. In my philosophy seminar, however, a group of students and one of the tutors maintain that this entire vocabulary is a metaphor, imported into biology in the mid-twentieth century from communication engineering, and that it has been quietly distorting biological thinking ever since. What makes the argument strange is that the two sides agree on all the molecular facts: base pairing, transcription, the codon table, and the way transfer RNAs match codons to amino acids. The disagreement is about what follows from those facts. The seminar side says a DNA sequence carries information only in the way a thermometer carries information about temperature—by correlation—not in the way a sentence carries information about the world. On their view there is no sender, no receiver, no convention, and no one reading the message; codons do not mean amino acids, they merely precede them causally. The laboratory side replies that this is exactly what a code is in the relevant sense, and that the information vocabulary is not decoration but the framework that lets biologists formulate and test hypotheses. After a term of this, neither side has moved.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is partly verbal: the two sides mean different things by code and information, and once those definitions are made explicit they may no longer contradict each other. But I worry that this diagnosis is too quick, because a residual dispute seems to remain about whether information talk earns its keep in biological explanation or systematically misleads researchers and students into treating DNA as an intentional agent.

I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, grounded in what each side says, rather than a verdict on who is right. Is it verbal, substantive, or are the two sides answering different questions under a single heading? If it is partly verbal, what dispute remains after the definitions are clarified? Is there a hidden empirical question here, for instance about whether coding descriptions generate predictions that pure correlation descriptions do not? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a distinction, a formal characterisation of coding, an empirical result, a change of question—or should I expect the impasse to persist, and why? I am not asking for a literature survey or bibliography; I want the structure of the disagreement. Please answer in British English.
