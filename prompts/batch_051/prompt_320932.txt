I've been turning the following argument over for a week and I can't find a step I'm willing to let go of, even though the conclusion feels like far too much. It goes:

(1) When people say a country is undergoing democratic backsliding — the usual cases being Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and lately the US in some tellings — they point to acts like court-packing, changing the electoral rules to favour the incumbent, turning the public broadcaster into a government mouthpiece, purging the civil service, refusing to accept unfavourable results, and so on.

(2) But every one of these acts is a lawful, often constitutionally lawful, exercise of power, and in that respect none of them differ from ordinary democratic governance. Each new majority reshapes the courts somewhat; every set of electoral rules advantages some players over others; every government replaces officials.

(3) So the difference between backsliding and routine democratic reform can't be located in the form or legality of the acts themselves.

(4) The difference must therefore be located in the substance: in whether you approve of the interests and values being entrenched.

(5) And substance is exactly where partisanship lives. So for any proposed case of backsliding, the government's defenders can offer a coherent redescription of it as legitimate reform, and for any proposed reform, its opponents can offer a coherent redescription of it as backsliding.

(6) But a concept whose application can always be flipped according to who is speaking isn't tracking a difference in the world; it's laundered political hostility.

(7) So "democratic backsliding" isn't a real analytic category — it's a partisan label with an academic veneer.

I want very much to keep (1): the gap between, say, a normal change of government in Denmark and a government that rewrites the rules so it can't lose does not feel like something I projected onto the world. But (7) seems to prove too much, and every attempt I make to break the chain ends up looking like me just insisting that the distinction is real. Which premise or step do you reject, or if the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to give up? And what exactly does rejecting it cost?
