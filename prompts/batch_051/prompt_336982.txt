I am taking a seminar on freedom and well-being, and for my paper I want to read Ivan's poem of the Grand Inquisitor in The Brothers Karamazov (Book V, Chapter 5) through the problem of freedom versus happiness. The reading I am testing is that the Inquisitor's argument turns on something like adaptive preference formation: he does not merely claim that most people find freedom painful, but that under the burden of choosing, they will come to prefer having their choices made for them and will be happy — perhaps genuinely happy — in that condition. I would like a close reading that tests how far the chapter supports this. Does Dostoevsky show the happiness of the Inquisitor's flock as authentic, or as manufactured by the very authority that claims to relieve them? Is the Inquisitor a diagnostician of how preferences deform under coercion, or is he rationalizing his own power? And does the chapter answer his empirical claim about what people would choose, or does it sidestep it — for instance, through the ending with Christ's kiss and Alyosha's kiss? I need the essay to take both the philosophical argument and the literary framing seriously, including the places where the text resists my adaptive-preference reading.
