For a module on the ethics of algorithmic decision-making, I have been working through the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — the formal results showing that, when the base rate of some outcome differs between groups, a classifier generally cannot simultaneously satisfy calibration (equal positive predictive value across groups at each score level) and error-rate balance (equal false-positive and false-negative rates across groups). I should like to test my understanding of these results against a real deployment, and the case I keep returning to is the public dispute over the COMPAS recidivism-risk instrument, used in pre-trial decisions in Broward County, Florida. My understanding of the salient facts is as follows. An investigative analysis published in 2016 reported that, among defendants who did not subsequently reoffend, Black defendants were substantially more likely to have been assigned a high-risk score than White defendants, whereas among defendants who did subsequently reoffend, White defendants were substantially more likely to have been assigned a low-risk score. In response, the developer of the instrument maintained that the scores were nevertheless well calibrated: at each risk band, the observed reoffending rate was roughly equal for Black and White defendants. The score was presented to judges as a risk assessment, but judges retained discretion to depart from it, and the outcome variable in the analysis was a proxy for reoffending rather than the legally relevant constructs of pre-trial dangerousness or flight risk.

Please analyse this episode through the lens of the impossibility results, with the discipline of a careful mapping rather than a summary. Specifically: (i) set out precisely the two fairness criteria in play and identify which criterion each side of the dispute was implicitly treating as non-negotiable; (ii) explain how both empirical findings could be true simultaneously, and why, given differing base rates of reoffending, no reconstruction of the risk score could have satisfied both criteria at once; and (iii) then turn the lens in the other direction: identify what the impossibility results genuinely illuminate about the episode, what they are silent or potentially misleading about (for example, judicial override, the choice and validity of the outcome proxy, and the distinction between the statistical properties of a score and the policy decision to use it at all), and what the episode, in turn, reveals about the limits of impossibility results when one moves from formal properties of classifiers to the normative questions of what to predict, who decides, and what equal treatment demands.
