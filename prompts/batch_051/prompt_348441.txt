I'm taking a grad seminar on Bayesian epistemology and we keep being told that the convergence theorems show priors "wash out" with enough data, and I think I've built one of those little arguments that seems to prove too much. I'd like someone to tell me which step actually breaks and what it costs to break it.

(1) Rational agents update by conditionalising on a shared stream of evidence.
(2) The Bayesian convergence results say that when two agents with different priors condition on the same data, the influence of the priors dies out and their posterior probabilities for any fixed factual proposition get arbitrarily close, at least in the long run. (I know I'm brushing over the measure-zero and support conditions; flag them only if they're the thing that matters, not for completeness.)
(3) So if two rational agents have seen the same evidence and still disagree about a factual claim, then either they simply haven't had enough data yet, or at least one of them has a dogmatic prior that puts probability zero on some hypothesis the other leaves open.
(4) But in the disputes I actually care about -- say, over the causes of the business cycle or how much of the post-2008 slowdown was weak demand rather than supply-side damage -- the two sides have had decades of the same public data. If that isn't "enough", then the wash-out theorem starts to look like it can't do any of the work in real disagreements that we wanted it for.
(5) Therefore either Bayesian convergence is practically irrelevant to real disputes, or in each of those disputes at least one side is irrational in a way we can't identify.

I want to keep (1) and (2), and I'm sympathetic to (4), but I can't accept that half the economics profession is secretly irrational. So which step do I give up, and what do I pay for giving it up? (And please, if you could write the answer in British English, I'd appreciate it.)
