Suppose that, beginning in 2030, a consortium of archives, publishers, and investigative organizations succeeds in creating a publicly accessible and independently verified registry of the moral conduct of every artist—living or dead—whose work is commercially published, exhibited, streamed, or performed. The registry is not a legal judgment and carries no direct penalties; it simply makes accurate, well-sourced information about cruelty, exploitation, deceptiveness, and comparable wrongdoing, as well as notable acts of decency, effectively impossible to ignore at the moment a person encounters a work. Assume the facts are no longer contested in any serious way and that the registry persists indefinitely.

Give me your best reasoned prediction of how the debate over whether immoral art can be good art is transformed over the following two generations. I am less interested in a yes-or-no verdict on separating the work from the artist than in the mechanisms: how audiences, critics, curators, publishers, and platforms adapt their practices and language; whether separationism becomes easier to maintain because the facts are settled or harder to maintain because the facts are always salient; which parts of the art world change first and which resist longest; and what happens to the retrospective canon as a result. For each major link in the prediction, indicate how confident you are and what assumption it depends on. Which outcomes are robust across reasonable assumptions, and which reverse if ordinary audiences turn out to be more or less retributive than you expect?
