In my semantics seminar we keep treating conversation as a game with a score: the common ground is the scoreboard, assertions update the score, and presuppositions are moves that are only legal if the score already registers the right thing. Accommodation then comes out as the odd case where the scoreboard gets quietly edited after the fact so a move counts as legal that wasnt strictly legal at the time. I want to know how much weight that analogy can actually bear. Lay out what corresponds to what: the score, the board, the players, the umpire, the rulebook, the win condition. Then tell me where the picture breaks. In an actual game the score is public and updates happen by rule, with someone empowered to adjudicate; in conversation none of that obviously holds, and the retroactive revision that the analogy treats as an exception might be the core phenomenon rather than a repair mechanism. So once the disanalogies are explicit, is the standard claim that presuppositions impose requirements on the prior common ground, and that accommodation is a kind of fix, a real model or just the metaphor's shadow? And is a better picture a shared ledger with an accountant who backdates the entries after you've already spent the money, or does that just relocate the mystery? Give me the audit, not a bibliography.
