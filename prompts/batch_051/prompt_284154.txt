I've been trying to get clear on when an infinite regress is vicious rather than harmless, and the argument below is my attempt to show the epistemic regress falls on the harmless side. But the whole thing feels shifty, and I can't tell wich step is doing the work.

(1) A regress is vicious only if the items in the chain are ordered as tasks: each step has to be completed before the next one can begin, so the process never gets off the ground.
(2) A regress is harmless when the chain is an order of dependence that obtains all at once. No one has to run through the steps; the dependence structure is just there.
(3) In the epistemic regress, whichever belief you pick, there is a further belief that serves as its reason.
(4) That is an order of dependence, not performance. For a belief to be justified, an agent doesn't have to work through the chain from bottom to top; it's enough that for every belief in the chain there's a further belief standing in the reason-giving relation.
(5) So, by (1) and (2), the epistemic regress is harmless rather than vicious.
(6) Therefore a belief can be justified by an actually infinite chain of reasons, and no foundational beliefs are needed.

My suspicion keeps drifting between (1) and (4): either (1) defines viciousness too narrowly, or (4) slides from 'for every belief there is a further belief' to 'there is a completed infinite chain doing the justifying'. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails? Give the strongest reading on which the offending premise is true and say whether the argument still reaches (6).
