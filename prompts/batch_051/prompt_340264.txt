We're staging a community production of The Crucible, and I've been asked to write the program essay. The play is set in seventeenth-century Salem but was written in the early 1950s, and it is commonly read as a commentary on McCarthyism. I've been trying to think about that connection through the historians' debate over presentism: whether any account of the past can escape the standards and concerns of its own time. I'd like the essay to treat the play as a test of that idea rather than as a simple allegory. Is Miller's use of Salem to speak to his own moment a distortion of the historical record, or a demonstration of how the past becomes legible to a later age? Work through particular scenes and choices — the court's self-sealing logic, the accusers' motives, Elizabeth Proctor's lie, John Proctor's refusal to sign — and show where the play resists both a flat presentist reading and a flatly antiquarian one. End with what the play does as drama that a scrupulous non-presentist history could not.
