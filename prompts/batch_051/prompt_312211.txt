I have been trying to understand the boundary between medical treatment and human enhancement through a concrete policy rather than as a slogan, and I want you to analyze a proposal in that spirit. A national health service announces that it will reimburse only interventions classifiable as treatment: an intervention is covered when it restores, corrects, or prevents a departure from statistically normal functioning for the patient's age and sex, where the threshold for normal is fixed by population reference data. An intervention that raises functioning above that norm is classified as enhancement and is excluded, even when the underlying state causes genuine distress. A standing Therapy/Enhancement Board decides contested cases using a published rubric: it asks whether there is an identifiable pathology, whether the intervention moves the patient toward the population norm rather than beyond it, and whether the request would plausibly have arisen absent social pressure or competitive demand. Doctors must attach a diagnostic code to every reimbursable intervention, and each clinic's funding is adjusted by how often the board upholds its coding decisions on audit.

The line gets strained quickly: stimulants for a student whose attention sits at the twentieth percentile with no diagnosis; growth hormone for a child predicted to be far below average height; beta-blockers for someone with no anxiety disorder who wants them before interviews; IVF for a woman past the ordinary reproductive window; laser eye surgery that yields above-average acuity. At the same time, a legal private market remains for any intervention, so the well-off can buy whatever the insurer calls an enhancement.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a question about whether enhancement should be covered in principle. Walk through how the boundary between the normal and the pathological moves over time as interventions spread and population norms shift; where the system gets gamed first — by patients, doctors, manufacturers, or the board's auditors — and how that gaming shows up in the diagnostic codes; what second-order effects follow from allowing a private enhancement market alongside the restricted public system; and whether the scheme operationalizes a defensible therapy/enhancement distinction or instead manufactures a distinction of its own. Say which failure modes could be fixed by changing the rules and which are inherent to any system that must draw a line at normal functioning.
