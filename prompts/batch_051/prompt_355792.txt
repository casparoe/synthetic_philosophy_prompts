I'm trying to get my gut reactions on low-probability, high-stakes decisions into some kind of order, and I want to know if there's one principle behind them.

A stranger online says he'll run a simulation of a huge number of people being tortured unless I venmo him $50. I wouldn't pay, even though the number he names is so large that the expected suffering avoided is astronomical.

I also wouldn't pay more than about $25 to play the St. Petersburg game (flip a fair coin until it comes up heads; you get $2 on the first flip, doubling for each earlier tail), even though the expected payout is infinite.

But I do think you should fund a charity that saves lives with near-certainty over one with a one-in-a-billion shot at something astronomically good, and I wear a seatbelt even though the chance it saves my life on any given trip is tiny. So I'm not ignoring small probabilities wholesale.

Here's the part that bothers me: I also think that if a cheap intervention had, say, a one-in-ten-thousand chance of preventing something enormous like the collapse of civilization, it would be a mistake not to fund it. That looks structurally a lot like the mugging case — small cost, tiny probability, enormous stake — but I want to call one foolish and the other prudent. Is there a coherent principle that gets all of these verdicts, or nearly all of them, and what does it say about cases I haven't considered? Please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present one side as the consensus.
