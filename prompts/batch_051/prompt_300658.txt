I’ve been poking around in the philosophy of language lately—retired from physics, so I have the luxury of reading things I always meant to get to but never had time for. The stuff on empty names and fiction has really grabbed me, but I keep getting stuck on a particular issue and I’d like a fresh set of eyes.

Here’s the passage that’s been bugging me, from Bertrand Russell’s 1905 paper *On Denoting*. It comes near the end, where he’s discussing the consequences of his theory for “non-entities”:

> The whole realm of non-entities, such as ‘the round square’, ‘the even prime other than 2’, ‘Apollo’, ‘Hamlet’, etc., can now be satisfactorily dealt with. All these are denoting phrases which do not denote anything. A proposition about Apollo means what we get by substituting what the classical dictionary tells us is meant by Apollo, say ‘the sun-god’. All propositions in which Apollo occurs are to be interpreted by the above rules for denoting phrases. If ‘Apollo’ has a primary occurrence, the proposition containing the occurrence is false; if the occurrence is secondary, the proposition may be true.

Russell says here that “Apollo” and “Hamlet” are *denoting phrases*—on the same footing as “the round square” and “the present King of France”—rather than genuine proper names in the logical sense. But he treats them differently from one another without quite saying how. “Apollo” gets paraphrased via the dictionary as “the sun-god,” which looks like a disguised definite description. But then he adds that if “Apollo” has a *primary* occurrence, the proposition is false, whereas if the occurrence is *secondary*, it may be true. That is his standard machinery for descriptions. What I don’t see is how that machinery is supposed to apply to “Hamlet.” Hamlet isn’t a dictionary abbreviation for a unique individual description; he’s a character introduced by a particular work of fiction. When we say “Hamlet is a prince of Denmark,” we aren’t making a claim about the actual world that happens to be false, and we aren’t making a false existence claim. We seem to be making a claim *within* the fiction. Russell’s passage doesn’t mention fiction as such, but lumping “Hamlet” in with “Apollo” suggests he thinks the same analysis covers both.

So here’s what I’d like. Please write me an essay of around 1,000 words in British English that does the following. First, unpack what Russell means by primary versus secondary occurrences and explain, concretely, how he would analyse an ordinary false statement like “Apollo called pestilence upon the Greeks” as against a more cautious statement like “Homer believed that Apollo called pestilence upon the Greeks.” Then turn to the hard case: take a sentence from literary criticism or ordinary talk about *Hamlet*—something like “In the play, Hamlet is a prince of Denmark” or “Hamlet is more indecisive than Laertes”—and ask whether Russell’s description-theoretic apparatus can handle it without turning it into a claim about the actual world. I want you to argue a clear, considered position, but I also want you to be honest about the weakest point in that position. The aim isn’t to defend Russell at all costs; it’s to test whether this one paragraph of *On Denoting* can do the work it pretends to do. End with a one-sentence bottom line that a non-philosopher could understand.
