I'm taking a formal epistemology course this term and I'm trying to pull together my term paper on the problem of logical omniscience, and I'd love some comments on the core idea before I commit to it.

The way the problem is usually introduced in class is as one big headache: possible-worlds semantics makes agents know every logical consequence of what they know, and real people obviously don't. But I want to argue that "logical omniscience" is actually a bundle of at least three seperable failures, and treating them as one problem messes up the debate.

My rough notes for the paper:

1. Closure failure: not knowing the remote logical consequences of your beliefs, like never having worked through a long deduction. This seems like a resource problem.

2. Concept mastery failure: a competent speaker who knows something is a bachelor but doesn't seem to know it entails unmarried (or someone who uses "vixen" fine but balks at "female fox"). Not a matter of missing proof steps, but of how we model concepts.

3. Self-knowledge failure: an agent who knows P but can't report or act on the fact that they know P. Positive introspection fails, not closure.

My claim is that these violate different ideals, so they probably need different formal fixes: impossible worlds or awareness models might patch closure, but they don't automatically give you concept mastery or introspection, and sometimes they make self-knowledge worse. So the standard "solution to the problem of logical omniscience" framing is asking one question where there are really three, or more.

Before I start writing, could you give me exactly five comments on this? I'm definately wondering whether the three-way split holds up, whether I'm missing an obvious fourth category, whether the point about fixes not transferring overstates things, and whether a skeptic could say the second one is just closure plus a definition. Please make each comment a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion, and just number them 1 through 5.
