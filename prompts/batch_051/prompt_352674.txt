I teach a graduate seminar in population ethics, and we have been working through the old utilitarian fork that Sidgwick lays out in *The Methods of Ethics*: if one aims at total happiness, one may be led to prefer a more numerous population with lower average well-being, whereas if one aims at average happiness, the same population may be judged worse. My students tend to say this is merely a verbal dispute about the word better, or merely a difference in evaluative temperament, and then they stop arguing. I would like to force them to get clearer about which parts of this dispute, if any, could be settled by evidence about ordinary judgment and which parts cannot.

Act as a professional philosopher with expertise in both moral psychology and experimental philosophy. Design a concrete study, or a small battery of studies, that would test whether ordinary judgments about population cases track total well-being, average well-being, some threshold of individual well-being, or something else. Start by brainstorming several possible operationalizations before settling on the one you recommend. Then specify the vignettes, the comparison cases, the dependent measure, and the exact result pattern that would favor each interpretation. Be careful to include cases where total and average come apart, including cases with lives barely worth living, and say how you would distinguish a sensitivity to average well-being from a sensitivity to the quality of the worst-off life.

After presenting the design, say exactly what a positive result would and would not settle. In particular, state which claims about the ordinary concept of better, or about the psychology of moral judgment, could be confirmed or disconfirmed, and why the results would not by themselves settle the normative question of which population policy or axiology one ought to adopt. If you conclude that some part of the disagreement is not empirical at all, identify that residue precisely and say whether it is best understood as a genuine evaluative disagreement, a difference in temperament, or purely verbal.
