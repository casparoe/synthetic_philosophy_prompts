I've been going to my town's ordinance review committee meetings for a couple of years now, mostly because they let you bring coffee and the arguments get good. Once a month the committee takes a request to dust off some old law nobody's enforced in decades and either throw the book at somebody or leave it alone. I've been trying to figure out what rule they're actually running, because next month's case could go either way and I've got a small bet with my brother on it.

Here's the track record as I remember it.

The 1912 palmistry ordinance says you need a five-dollar license and good character to tell fortunes or read palms for money. It hasn't been enforced in living memory. When a palm reader opened a little shop on Main Street, the committee voted to do nothing. One member said enforcement would just be embarrassing, and another said she's never heard of a palm reading hurting anybody.

The 1963 rail embankment ordinance bans motorized bikes on the old embankment trail. It's old but there are two faded signs, and it was ticketed as recently as the early 2000s. After a dirt bike hit a jogger last summer, the committee voted to enforce and ordered the signs replaced.

The 1955 door-to-door sellers license requires traveling salesmen to buy a ten-dollar permit and register their wares at the town office. It's been ignored since sometime in the 80s. When a pushy magazine subscription guy spent a week annoying people on the west side, a resident asked the committee to charge him under the old law. The committee said no, and told residents to use the modern no-knock list if they didnt want him at the door.

Now here's the new case. There's a 1930s ordinance that makes it a fine to swim or bathe in the town reservoir; it was passed to protect the drinking water. For decades nobody did that, so nobody enforced it, and the law got forgotten entirely. There are no signs at the spot where people enter; the only old sign is a rusted Keep Out on a service gate at the other end of the property that nobody uses. From where they swim it just looks like any other pond. This summer the regular lake closed for toxic algae and people started swimming in the reservoir. Two weeks ago a kid dove off the old pump house and got a concussion, and the water department says the swimmers are stirring up sediment but it isn't an emergency yet. The committee has to decide whether to start enforcing the old reservoir law.

I keep going back and forth between two guesses about what their record shows. One is that they only enforce old laws when a reasonable person could still have known the law was real. The other is that they enforce old laws when the thing being done is actually causing trouble right now, and they let the dead ones go when it isn't. Maybe there's a third way of looking at it I'm missing.

Give it to me in four sections. "The track record" (about 150 words): what the past votes seem to have in common. "The different rules" (about 250 words): the two or three principles that would all predict those past votes, including ones I might not have thought of. "The reservoir case" (about 300 words): what each of those principles says about enforcing the swimming law, and which past case is the closest comparison. "My best bet" (about 150 words): your prediction for how the committee votes, the real reason you lean that way, and what future old-ordinance case would settle which rule they're using once and for all. If their past votes don't force one answer, just say what each choice would mean.
