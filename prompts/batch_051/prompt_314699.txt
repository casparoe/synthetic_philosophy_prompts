I keep coming back to a parallel I cannot decide about, between a problem in the philosophy of mind and something familiar from writing software.

Epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness says that felt experience—the taste of coffee, the ache of a headache, the redness of red—is caused by the physical world but causes nothing in its own right. It rides on top of the brain's causal work without altering it. The standard objection is the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if experience causes nothing, then my judging that I have experiences, and my writing sentences about them, cannot be caused by those experiences. But then it looks like a massive coincidence that people produce rich, fluent, endlessly elaborated descriptions of the experiences they take themselves to be having. Natural selection, the worry continues, can only reward effects, so it cannot have shaped accurate reports of something that has no effects. That would make the entire practice of reporting and theorizing about experience, and this very objection, inexplicable.

Now the software side. In the code I write, logging statements are supposed to be causally inert with respect to the program's result: the line written to the log does not change what the program returns, even though the log faithfully describes the computation. (I know real logging can perturb timing and concurrency; set that aside, since the intended design is precisely a side channel that does not feed back.) A log can be reliably informative about an execution even though nothing downstream depends on it. The programmer or the logging framework sets things up so that one underlying execution produces both the functional result and the log line that mirrors it. Here, then, is a case in which an effectless side channel carries accurate information about a process—not because it feeds back into that process, but because it shares an underlying cause with it.

The resemblance looks structural to me, but I am suspicious of it. In the logging case the accuracy is secured by an intentional designer, by an external reader, and by conventions about what the log line means. The epiphenomenalist has no designer, no external reader, and no convention; natural selection can select for the physical causes of reports, but the experiences themselves allegedly contribute nothing. So the analogy may be doing hidden work by smuggling in a common cause whose existence is exactly what the paradox calls into question.

I would like someone at home in both philosophy of mind and the logic of software instrumentation to help me sort this out. Is this a deep structural parallel, a loose analogy, or a confusion? What precisely would have to be true of both sides for the logging case to transfer to the epiphenomenalist's reply to the paradox of phenomenal judgment? Does anything useful transfer, or does the absence of a programmer and a reader make the analogy break at the load-bearing point? And can you name something the analogy predicts—about how accurate introspective reports should be, or about when they should fail—that turns out not to hold for conscious experience?

Please treat this as a genuine question in the philosophy of mind rather than an invitation to settle for a slogan. Act as a professional philosopher who is also a competent programmer.
