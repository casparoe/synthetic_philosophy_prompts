Ok so I've been going round in circles on the Born rule problem in many-worlds for ages, and I want a consistency check before I write something up for the local rationalist meetup. here's the current state of it:

(1) I think unitary-only quantum mechanics basically forces many-worlds. Once you treat the measurement apparatus as a quantum system, the terms decohere and you get branches; adding collapse as an extra postulate is the ad hoc move, since nobody can say when or why it happens without bolting something on.

(2) I think probability in many-worlds has to be about self-locating uncertainty. Before I look at the detector I should genuinely be unsure which branch I'm in, and that's the kind of uncertainty the Born rule quantifies. 'Every outcome happens so probability is meaningless' feels like it misses the indexical point.

(3) I think naive branch counting is a non-starter. Not just because branch number is ill-defined (decoherence gives no sharp count, and you can always fine-grain further), but because counting branches equally gives the wrong weights when amplitudes are unequal.

(4) The solution I find most defensible is the decision-theoretic one, the Deutsch–Wallace sort of thing: don't add a probability measure to the formalism at all; instead show that a rational agent who knows they're in an Everettian universe, and whose preferences satisfy the usual coherance constraints, is forced to bet as if outcomes had probability |amplitude|^2. No deep metaphysics of chance, just constraints on rational preference.

(5) But I don't want the Born rule to come out merely as a rule for rational betting. I want it to be a story about what a pre-observation agent should actually believe about their branch — genuine epistemic probability, not just a clever way of pricing games. If only the betting rule is forced, that feels like someone has changed the subject.

(6) For equal amplitudes I'm happy to say the symmetry forces equal credence — the envariance point in Zurek's vocabulary: swap two equal-amplitude branches and nothing physical changes, so equal credence. For unequal amplitudes I lean towards splitting the state, with the environment's help, into equal-amplitude components; then the symmetry applies piecewise and the squared-amplitude weights fall out of the number of equal pieces. Not fully settled, but it's my current fix.

(7) I'm also sceptical that 'typicality' arguments do any real work here, the ones that say most branches show Born statistics relative to some measure. Before you have the measure you can't say 'most', and once you have the Born measure you don't need typicality, so I'd drop that whole family of moves.

That's roughly the package. Could you go through it and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like they are, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each say what the cheapest revision is and what it costs. I'm not sure myself where the load-bearing cracks are, so be blunt.
