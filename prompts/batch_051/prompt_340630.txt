I am a doctoral student working on the ethics of historical judgment, and I keep returning to the man-of-his-time defence as applied to Thomas Jefferson's slaveholding. On its strongest formulation, the defence runs: a historical figure can fairly be blamed only for failing a standard that was accessible in that figure's own context; the moral condemnation of slavery we now take for granted was not accessible to Jefferson; therefore judging him by that standard is anachronistic and unfair. The second premise depends on a picture of the period as having a fairly unified moral horizon. Several decades of historiography have replaced that picture with a contested moral field: articulate condemnations of slavery that anticipate later abolitionism were voiced in Jefferson's own lifetime by free Black writers, Quaker critics, and European correspondents he knew, and were also pressed by enslaved people themselves; some of these criticisms reached him directly. Assume for the sake of argument that this historical picture is broadly correct. Does the man-of-his-time defence survive? What is the most defensible repaired version, and what does it concede — for instance, one that indexes blame to the dominant norms of the day, or to the agent's best actual judgment? Or does the original argument collapse for Jefferson and figures like him? I want the structure of the argument, not a survey of sources.

Please structure your response as follows:
1. The argument reconstructed (one paragraph). State the strongest, most charitable version of the defence and mark its premises.
2. The premise under pressure (one paragraph). Identify exactly which premise the historiographical development bears on and why.
3. The case for survival and the case against (two paragraphs, one each). Give the best repair of the defence, then the strongest reason to think it still fails.
4. Verdict (one paragraph). Say whether the original defence is dead, merely qualified, or survives for some figures, and distinguish the generic principle from its application to Jefferson.
