I've been rereading Sidgwick's *Methods of Ethics*, and there's a claim in Book IV I cant get out of my head. He argues, roughly, that from a utilitarian point of view, population should be encouraged to increase not up to the point where average happiness is greatest, but up to the point where total happiness—the number of people multiplied by average happiness—is greatest. That seems to imply that if you can add people with any positive happiness, even very little, you should, until the product stops rising.

So I built this argument, and now I'm suspicious of it because the conclusion is absurd.

(1) If total happiness is what matters morally, then it is good to add a person whenever their net contribution to total happiness is positive.

(2) A possible person could have a life that is barely worth livng, with a net happiness of +1, say.

(3) Therefore, total happiness alone would recommend adding enormous numbers of such people until the marginal person's happiness is zero, because each addition increases the total.

(4) A world with, say, a trillion people living lives just barely worth living is worse than a world with five billion people living much happier lives.

(5) So total happiness is not what matters morally, or at least not the sole thing.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep the idea that adding a happy person can make the world better than give that up. But I don't want the trillion barely happy people. Is the flaw in (1), in the move from total to adding, or is (4) just an intuition I should drop? And if I drop total happiness, what do I replace it with—average happiness? But average happiness seems to imply that a world with one extremely happy person is better than a world with billions of quite happy people, which also feels wrong.
