I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy background, so I expect some of my vocabulary is loose. For years I've clicked through terms of service without reading them, and I've been trying to work out a theory of what actually happens when someone clicks "I agree." I'd like it stress-tested.

The theory, which I call consent by exception, has two parts. First, clicking "I agree" is not acceptance of the terms on the screen. It is better modeled as asserting: "I have no exceptions to file against this transaction at this time." The document is a large block of unread default rules; the click is the only line of user-supplied code. If my software completed an operation successfully, you would not infer that it had inspected every branch it executed; you would infer that no exception was thrown. I think clickwrap works the same way.

Second, I claim that obligations can still be generated by this exception system without any term-level meeting of the minds — not promissorily, but the way a simple handshake establishes a connection even when neither side yet knows what payloads will follow. The vendor relies on the user's no-exceptions signal to deliver the service, and that reliance does the obligating, not the user's comprehension of section 14.3. For example, if a user clicks through an installer and is later held to an arbitration clause buried in the terms, my theory says the real source of any obligation is that signal plus the vendor's reliance on it, and the analysis should proceed from there rather than pretending the user agreed to arbitration in particular.

So here is what I want: an honest assessment. Is the distinction coherent, or am I polishing a confusion? Have I reinvented an existing position, and if so, what is it called and how does my version differ? Where is the theory strongest, and where does it break? I can already see one vulnerability: maybe ordinary pen-and-ink agreement is also largely "by exception," since almost no one reads or tracks every term. And please tell me what would change your verdict — which argument or observation would most plausibly flip it, and where your assessment is most likely to fail.
