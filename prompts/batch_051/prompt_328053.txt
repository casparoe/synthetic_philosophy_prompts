I'm a backend engineer working on a tool that recommends how to split a fixed wellbeing stipend across teams, and each option gets scored by the predicted wellbeing of the people affected. I've got a bunch of gut calls about which distributions are better but I cant figure out the rule underneath them.

Here are my calls, using wellbeing scores from 0 to 100:

1. If Ann is at 30 and Bob is at 70, giving Ann +10 feels clearly better than giving Bob +10.

2. Same starting point, giving Ann +10 also feels better than giving Bob +20. I know that's a bigger total gain, but it doesn't move me.

3. Going from (30, 70) to (31, 65) — Ann +1, Bob -5 — feels like an improvement, or at worst a wash.

4. Going from (30, 70) to (30, 30) — Bob loses 40, Ann gains nothing — feels clearly worse, even though they end up equal.

5. Going from (30, 70) to (31, 20) — Ann +1, Bob -50 — feels clearly worse, even though Ann ends up ahead.

6. The one I flip on weekly: (30, 70) to (31, 45) — Ann +1, Bob -25. The gap shrinks a lot and Ann gets something, but the total drops a lot too. I can't settle on a verdict.

My working theory was something like "inequality is what's bad," but case 4 seems to break that: forcing equality by dragging Bob down feels worse, not better. The best repair I can come up with is: a gain to a worse-off person counts several times more than an equal gain to a better-off person, but making someone worse off without making anyone better off is never an improvement. But that feels like two rules glued together, and it still doesn't tell me what to say about case 6.

Is there a single principle that gets all my settled calls? If not, which should I give up? And what does the principle you'd defend say about case 6, plus maybe one further case I should use to stress-test it?
