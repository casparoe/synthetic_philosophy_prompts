I am a retired natural scientist who has recently taken up philosophy, and I would like a conceptual explanation rather than a formal proof. There is a family of results in economics and formal epistemology — the no-trade theorems — whose upshot is roughly this: among fully rational agents with common priors, purely speculative trade should unravel, because each party's willingness to trade at a given price is itself information that should lead the other party to revise, and so on, until trade based on mere differences of information disappears. Yet real markets show vast trading volume even where no hedging or liquidity motive is obvious. I would like you to explain, as clearly as you can, what the no-trade argument really requires; which of its assumptions bear the most weight (common priors, common knowledge of rationality, the distinction between speculative and non-speculative trade, the idea that the act of trading reveals information); and what each candidate resolution implies for the familiar idea that a market can aggregate dispersed information that no participant individually possesses. In particular, I want to understand whether the no-trade theorems refute the notion that a market can in some sense know things no trader knows, or merely force us to qualify that notion.
