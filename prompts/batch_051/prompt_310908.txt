I've been going back and forth on panpsychism for weeks now, and I keep ending up in the same deadlock. The pull is that it seems like the only account that doesn't cheat somewhere. If the very basic constituents of the world have some experiential or proto-experiential character, then my consciousness doesn't have to be produced out of wholly non-conscious parts, which is the step I can never make sense of. It gives you a clean, unified story about mind and matter without strong emergence, and without positing some extra non-physical substance halfway through. By my lights that's a genuinely big theoretical virtue, the kind of thing I normally say should settle a debate.

The problem is a very stubborn intuition. When I have a headache while looking at a red wall, there is one subject that has both the pain and the colour at the same time; the experiences don't just float seperately near each other, they belong to a single point of view. Panpsychism, as far as I can see, asks me to believe that a vast number of micro-subjects (or carriers of proto-experience) get arranged in my skull and somehow yield that single point of view. But subjects don't look like the sort of thing that can be summed. However tightly you integrate a billion tiny perspectives, you seem to get a billion tiny perspectives in very close contact, not one new perspective. If that's right, then panpsychism hasn't solved the hard problem, it's just shrunk it and multiplied it. But if I drop the intuition, I worry I'm debunking something I have better evidence for than I have for any theory. So tell me: what does each concession actually cost? What would justify deciding that the unity intuition is an artefact, or abandoning the constitutive project, or adopting some third reading on which both survive? I don't want a verdict so much as a map of what I'd be commiting myself to.
