A national planning authority is considering a forecasting rule designed to respect a distinction often associated with Keynes. In the background are two contrasts: first, between risk, where a numerical probability can be assigned, and uncertainty, where there is no firm basis for assigning one; and second, between how favourable the evidence is and how much independent, stable evidence there is — the balance of an argument versus its weight. The proposed rule addresses the second contrast directly.

Under the rule, every agency or private sponsor that submits a numerical projection — cost, demand, revenue, credit losses, climate-related losses — must attach to each central estimate a weight-of-evidence score on a 1–5 scale. The score is assessed by a small independent review board, not self-reported, and it is not a confidence interval and not a second probability. It measures the amount, independence, and stability of the evidence behind the probability assignment. An estimate of 0.8 resting on one overfitted model would score low; an estimate of 0.8 resting on many independent sources would score high.

Projects are then approved under a dual threshold. To pass, a projection must have an acceptable-outcome probability above 0.70 and a weight score of at least 3. If the probability is high but the weight is low, the proposal moves to a separate register: the projection must be labelled a convention, meaning an estimate held largely because others hold it, and the decision-maker may approve it only by arguing for robustness across scenarios rather than by leaning on the central estimate. Forecasters are rewarded for calibration within each weight tier, so a wrong high-weight forecast carries a larger penalty than a wrong low-weight forecast, and a lucky low-weight forecast earns little credit.
