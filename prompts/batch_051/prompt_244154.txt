A large social media platform is designing a content moderation policy built around the distinction between using a slur and merely mentioning it. Under the proposed rule, a post containing a slur is removed only when the slur is used — that is, when it is directed at a person or group or otherwise functions in the sentence as a derogatory term. Posts that mention the slur, quote it in a metalinguistic discussion, or embed it in an example are left up. The policy then adds two exceptions: a use is permitted if the speaker is a member of the targeted group and the use is plausibly an act of reclamation, and a use is removed regardless of speaker identity if it is directed at a member of the targeted group. Enforcing the rule therefore requires the platform to classify each occurrence along three dimensions: mention versus use, in-group versus out-group speaker, and derogatory versus reclaimed function.

Treat this policy as a mechanism whose behavior you are asked to predict, not as a moral position to evaluate. What does it incentivize among users, among those who report posts, and among the moderators and automated classifiers who must apply the three distinctions? How would it be gamed at the boundaries — sarcastic or baiting uses, reclaimed slurs aimed at outsiders, deceptive self-presentation, mixed audiences in which some readers cannot tell that a use is in-group, and posts that deliberately engineer ambiguity so that a token is simultaneously mention and use? What second-order effects does it have on the surrounding vocabulary of abuse and on the platform's incentive to make the classifications cheap rather than accurate? Where does the policy break down first, and are the failure modes fixable by adding finer rules, or do they reveal something about where the derogatory force of a slur is located — in the word, in the speaker, or in the audience's uptake?
