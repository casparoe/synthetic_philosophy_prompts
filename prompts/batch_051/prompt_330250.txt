In my philosophy of emotion class we keep circling around this question of whether sadness, grief, and other negative emotions are actually worth having — not just instrumentally, like grief signaling to other people that you need support, but in some stronger sense, like a life that never had them would be worse off, not just emptier but somehow less truthful. My friend in the class says this is just unfalsifiable hand waving, but I think at least part of the claim could be given some emperical pressure. The part I'm thinking of is the idea that negative moods make you a more careful thinker: less likely to jump to conclusions, less swayed by first impressions, better at noticing when the details don't add up. My profesor mentioned some old studies suggesting that mildly depressed people see the world more accurately than cheerful people do, but I don't remember the specifics.

So I want to work out how you'd actually test that carefulness claim in a convincing way. Say I pick one small, concrete form of carefulness — like resistance to a misleading first impression on some task — and compare people after a sadness induction to people after a neutral or happy mood induction. What exactly should I measure, and what pattern of results would count as evidence for the claim that negative affect improves judgment in that domain, versus against it? And then, the part I keep getting stuck on: even if sadness made people more careful on a bunch of lab tasks, would that ever get me to the conclusion that a life without sadness would be worse overall, or is there a jump there that no experiment can make for you? I want to be able to say clearly which part of the idea that negative emotions are valuable a test can touch and which part it simply can't.
