Please give an intuitive explanation of the following puzzle about the transitivity of 'better than.'

Consider a very long sequence of possible experiences, E1, E2, ..., En, differing along two dimensions: how long a pain lasts and how intense it is. E1 consists of a full day of very mild, barely noticeable discomfort. Each later experience is somewhat shorter but only slightly more intense; the changes are calibrated so that, at every adjacent step, the later experience seems better than the earlier one, because the reduction in duration appears worth the small increase in intensity. After sufficiently many steps, En consists of a split second of excruciating pain, which many people would judge worse than E1: a day of barely noticeable discomfort is preferable to even a split second of excruciating pain.

These judgments together appear to generate a cycle: E2 is better than E1, E3 is better than E2, and so on through En, yet E1 is better than En. Explain the conceptual conflict this creates for the assumption that 'better than' is transitive, and set out the main ways of resolving it. In doing so, distinguish carefully between the claim that the strict relation 'better than' is transitive and the claim that the weaker relation 'not worse than' (or 'at least as good as') is transitive. Explain how the force of the argument depends on which of these relations is taken to hold between adjacent items, and what a defender of the transitivity of strict 'better than' would need to say about the adjacent judgments. Finally, briefly comment on how this puzzle resembles, and differs from, a sorites paradox.
