I am giving a workshop talk next month to a joint audience of applied Bayesian statisticians and formal epistemologists, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a written defense of the talk. The thesis I am defending is this: the slogan that priors wash out with enough data is not licensed by the standard convergence results, because two different results have been run together. Blackwell–Dubins-style merging-of-opinions results show that, when two agents' probability measures over the data sequence are mutually absolutely continuous, their predictive distributions for future observations given the shared history merge with one another almost surely; they do not show that either agent is converging on the true data-generating process. Truth-tracking requires further identification, support, and regularity conditions of the kind that appear in Bernstein–von Mises-style results, and it is those conditions, not the washing-out slogan, that do the epistemological work. Moreover, even where posterior concentration toward the truth holds, it is asymptotic, and the standard results place no practical bound on how much data a given prior needs before its influence becomes small. The finite-sample claim that practitioners actually rely on is therefore left unsupported.

The audience: mostly applied statisticians who use weakly informative and shrinkage priors, treat prior choice as a sensitivity-analysis problem, and tell students that with enough data the prior stops mattering. There are also three or four formal epistemologists who know the agreement-and-consensus literature and care about the distinction between consensus among agents and convergence to the truth. They are not hostile to Bayesian methods. They are mildly hostile to anyone claiming the standard results fail to underwrite the washing-out slogan, because several of them teach it or have built practical advice on it.

Produce the prep sheet. Give me seven or eight objections this exact audience will actually raise, not the objections of an audience outside Bayesian statistics and not the objections a radical subjectivist would raise. Order them from most to least dangerous, where danger is likelihood multiplied by damage. For each objection, include: the likely asker and the commitment that drives the worry; the objection phrased in their vocabulary, the way they would actually put it aloud; a reply of roughly two minutes; and one sentence on where that reply is softest and what the questioner will say next. Also include, separately if it is not already among the seven or eight, the objection I should most hope nobody asks, and mark it clearly. Do not include citations or a bibliography. End the response with a final line exactly in the form \answer{<short label for the single most dangerous objection>}.
