I’m preparing a set of notes on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, focused on the question whether a person can rationally believe that P while also believing that her total evidence does not support P. I want to collect the strongest reasons for answering yes, and I already have the standard ones. Please do not restate, reword, or combine any of the following:

1. The level-splitting or evidentialist argument: first-order evidence and higher-order evidence answer different questions, and if each level of doxastic response is measured against its own evidence, nothing in evidentialist rationality requires the two levels to align; in a mismatch case, the akratic pair is what each level supports.
2. Christensen-style disagreement and self-doubt cases, where conciliatory or independence-based reasoning pushes a subject to lose confidence in her own first-order reasoning while that reasoning still rationally moves her, leaving her first-order and higher-order attitudes out of alignment.
3. Worsnip-style conflict cases, in which the evidence can support both P and the proposition that one’s evidence does not support P; evidentialism then recommends the akratic combination while the enkratic requirement forbids it, so rejecting rational akrasia would make rationality issue inconsistent demands.
4. Lasonen-Aarnio-style considerations about the limits of defeat, on which misleading higher-order evidence need not defeat a first-order belief, so a subject can rationally retain the first-order attitude while acknowledging the higher-order evidence rather than ignoring it.

I take these to exhaust the standard pro-akrasia toolbox, so I am looking for something else. Give me one more consideration in favor of the rationality of epistemic akrasia that differs in structure from all four: not a special case or application of level-splitting, not another disagreement or self-doubt case, not an evidentialism-versus-coherence conflict, and not a point about the defeasibility of higher-order evidence. It may be less than standard, but it should be philosophically serious. State the consideration as precisely as you can, lay out the dialectical work it does that the four listed moves do not, and then assess it honestly: where is its weakest point, and how would a defender of an enkratic requirement most naturally resist it?
