For an upper-level undergraduate seminar on formal social choice (sixteen students, 75 minutes, no pre-reading), design a structured classroom activity in which participants discover Sen's liberal paradox themselves, before it is named: the result that a social ranking can satisfy the Pareto principle and give each person a minimal sphere of individual decisiveness over one pair of alternatives, and yet be intransitive, leaving no best alternative. Begin your response with a one-sentence statement of the core mechanism of your activity (the design's bottom line), then give the full activity and debrief; no preamble, no restatement of the question.

The activity should be built on the two-person, three-alternative skeleton, with roles and stakes arranged so that participants produce or endorse the cycle-inducing preference profile themselves rather than receiving it. Set up alternatives x and y as alternatives that touch each person's private sphere and z as the alternative in which neither occurs; assign person 1 decisiveness over the pair {x, z} and person 2 over {y, z}. Have participants apply two social rules pairwise: unanimity (if both prefer a to b, society prefers a to b) and minimal liberalism (each person's preference over the assigned personal pair is adopted as the social preference). Design the case so the natural profile is person 1: z ≻ x ≻ y; person 2: x ≻ y ≻ z, which forces the cycle z ≻ x ≻ y ≻ z. The crucial performance constraint: students should notice the cycle emerging from their own rankings and their own applications of the two rules, not be told it exists.

Include rules and roles, a timeline, materials that need no real money, and a debrief that maps what happened in the activity onto the formal conditions: unrestricted domain, Pareto, minimal liberalism, and the demand that social preference be transitive. Tell the facilitator what will predictably go wrong in the first run — for example, groups whose elicited preferences do not produce the cycle, and students who spontaneously try to repair the intransitivity by silently editing someone's ranking — and how to handle it.
