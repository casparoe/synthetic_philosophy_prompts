I am a political philosopher who is sometimes retained by local governments to help design participatory processes. A city of about 250,000 residents has asked me to supply a reusable procedure for its Office of Public Engagement, and I want your help working out the procedure before I present it to the city. The office must decide, several times a month, who should be included in which stage of a pending local decision. The decisions include closing a branch library, redrawing school attendance boundaries, siting a low-barrier homeless shelter, and setting the spending menu for a participatory budgeting cycle. The senior coordinator who will run the procedure has no formal training in philosophy or political theory, will typically have half a working day per decision, and must produce a written rationale that the city attorney can review. The city manager has made two constraints explicit. The office cannot simply include everyone whose interests might be affected anywhere, because that is unworkable and invites legal challenge. It also cannot default to the city's registered voters plus anyone the office happens to have heard from, because that default is precisely what community groups have been contesting.

The underlying issue is what political philosophers call the boundary problem: for any given decision, who constitutes the demos to whom the decision is answerable? I want you to turn the main competing answers in the literature into an operational procedure. The answers I have in mind are these. Under the all-affected-interests principle, anyone whose interests a decision meaningfully affects should have some form of say in it. Under the all-subjected principle, binding decision rights belong to those who will be subject to the decision's coercive force, while mere affectedness may justify voice rather than a vote. Under a stakeholder or membership principle, participation rights should track how far a person's life prospects depend on remaining and functioning as a member of the community, not merely the fact that a decision touches them.

The procedure you produce should be usable without the theory: questions in ordinary professional language, asked in order, with a stopping rule for each decision. It should distinguish three inclusion roles rather than collapsing them into one: who must be informed, who should have deliberative voice, and who should have a decision right or veto. It should also handle the familiar circularity problem honestly: applying an affectedness test requires someone first to decide whose interests count, at what threshold, and how far along a causal chain one looks. Make that preliminary decision an explicit, documented, revisable step of the procedure rather than a hidden assumption. For diffuse, uncertain, and cumulative effects, tell the coordinator how to make a defensible call in half a day and how to record it.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases: the library closure, the site selection for the shelter, and the participatory budgeting menu. At least one application should produce a recommendation the city manager will not like, and you should say why the procedure nonetheless requires it. End with a short statement of what the procedure cannot do: which boundary questions it settles, which it leaves to political judgment, and where it is most likely to misfire.
