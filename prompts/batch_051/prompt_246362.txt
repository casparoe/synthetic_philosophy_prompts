I sit on the Campus Mobility and Safety Committee at a large urban university, and we must decide by Friday whether to permit a private vendor to operate forty small autonomous delivery robots on the university's pedestrian paths. I regard this as a concrete instance of a question the committee rarely faces head-on: when, if ever, may an institution impose small risks of serious harm on people who have not consented and who may not benefit? I need a written decision memo from you that I can circulate before the vote.

The proposal: the vendor would deploy up to forty six-wheeled robots, speed-limited to 4 mph and weighing about 45 pounds, to deliver meals and groceries from campus dining halls to students and staff who pay a delivery fee. The robots would share crowded walkways with roughly 35,000 people a day, most of them students and employees who have chosen to be on campus, but also visitors, children attending summer day camps, and neighbors who cut through campus. The vendor's pilot data from two other campuses and one municipality show no serious injuries in about four million miles, but the vendor's own safety model gives a wide range for the per-mile risk of serious pedestrian injury; scaled to our expected 40,000 robot-miles per year, the annual probability of at least one serious injury is estimated at somewhere between 1 in 200 and 1 in 20, depending on modeling choices the vendor itself flags as uncertain. Minor collisions are more common and have produced skinned knees, bruised shins, and one sprained ankle. The vendor offers indemnification, a $5 million insurance policy, daylight-only operation, and remote human supervisors who can take over.

The committee is split. One faction argues that the risks are tiny compared with ordinary background risks from bicycles, scooters, and delivery vans, that students and staff are choosing a convenience for themselves, and that rejecting the proposal on these numbers would commit us to a zero-risk standard we apply to nothing else. The other faction argues that the central issue is not the size of the risk alone but its imposition on people who never consented and who receive little or no benefit: the summer-camp child on a field trip, the wheelchair user whose curb cut is blocked, the neighbor using a shortcut. They also worry that approving this program normalizes trading small risks to third parties for the convenience of institutional members, and that many such small approvals could accumulate into a meaningfully less safe environment.

Write the committee's decision memo. It should recommend one of three courses of action — approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions — with reasons both factions could engage. Do not merely split the difference; if you recommend conditions, say which ones, why they answer the objection, and why they are enough. Take the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously, and make the recommendation concrete enough that the committee could vote on it. At the end, add a short note saying how confident you are in the recommendation and identifying the single point in your reasoning you are least sure of.
