In my political philosophy module an upperclassman explained Hobbes as basically being a prisoner's dilemma. Everyone prefers the everyone-keeps-the-peace outcome to the everyone-at-war outcome, but each individual rationally prefers to defect — arm up, strike first, grab things before they get grabbed — so mutual defection is the only rational equilibrium, and that's why we need the sovereign, like an enforcer outside the interrogation room who changes the payoffs so cooperating becomes rational. I started reading Leviathan and now I'm not sure it fits: Hobbes gives three causes of quarrel, competition, diffidence, and glory, and glory in particular sounds like some people just want to win, not merely be secure. Also the sovereign gets created by a covenant among the players themselves, not imposed by an outside enforcer. So how far does the prisoner's dilemma reading actually hold? I'd like the correspondence table — what maps to the two prisoners, the two options, the payoffs, the enforcer — then the places the mapping breaks, and an honest verdict on whether the conclusion (a sovereign with overwhelming power is the rational way out) survives once the disanalogies are in view. If the state of nature is really a stag hunt or some other game, name it and say what that changes.
