I run a neighborhood civics discussion group (14 or so adults, we meet for two hours) and I'm on the hook to design next month's session. I picked the ethics of voting because it got personal: a friend of mine is running for city council in a district were the last race was decided by a couple dozen votes, and I've been canvassing for her while genuinely unsure whether I should be telling people their vote matters.

The group is split in a way that makes the discussion go nowhere. Half keep saying an individual vote is never decisive, so nothing about voting is really a duty; the other half keep saying that's beside the point because voting is how you express your values and stand with the people you vote for. We go in circles, and I don't want to lecture them out of it.

Can you design a runnable 90-minute activity for this group that makes the instrumental-versus-expressive distinction show up in the mechanics rather than in anything I say afterwards? I want actual rules, roles, and small real stakes (a pizza or a few bucks a head), with at least three rounds where everyone knows the probability of being pivotal is different, and where the expressive payoff attached to each candidate is private info written on cards. Build in one round where the candidate someone most wants to express support for is a lost cause by the numbers and a compromise candidate is genuinely close, so the lesser-evil choice is concrete. Also build in a moment where the if-everyone-thought-that-way or free-rider worry happens to them as a situation, not as a slogan.

Then give me the debrief: which moments in the game map onto which parts of the idea — pivotalness and expected-utility reasoning, expressive reasons, duty, the generalization/free-rider problem, lesser-evil voting. Tell me what will predictably go wrong when I actually run it (people gaming the stakes, refusing to treat a few bucks as real, the loudest person steamrolling) and how to handle each. And be honest about what a room like this can't reproduce — I'd guess the secret ballot, the fact that real electorates are millions of correlated voters, and what it actually feels like to lose — so I know what I'll still have to supply myself.
