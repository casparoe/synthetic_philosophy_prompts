In my philosophy class we read a short excerpt from Leibniz's preface to his New Essays on Human Understanding, in which he answers Locke's claim that the mind begins as a blank tablet. Leibniz says the mind is not a blank tablet but more like a block of marble whose veins already run in certain directions: the sculptor's chisel has to do the actual carving, and experience has to do the actual triggering, but the shape that emerges is partly set in advance by the veins. I want to know whether any version of that idea is scientifically testable, not whether Leibniz's exact wording is right.

The claim I want to examine is this: human beings are born with some domain-specific innate cognitive structures — for example, a readiness to acquire language, or to notice certain causal patterns — that are not merely the result of general learning from sensory experience. In other words, the mind has something like Leibniz's veins: biases or constraints that shape what we learn and how easily we learn it.

Design the strongest empirical test you can for that claim. It could draw on psychology, linguistics, artificial intelligence, or developmental studies. For each possible outcome, tell me exactly what it would show and what it would not show. Then be honest about the residue: even if the test comes out one way, what part of the debate between rationalists and empiricists would remain untouched?
