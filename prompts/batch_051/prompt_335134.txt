A therapist I used to see would often cut off an anxious or self-deprecating report with the line "feelings aren't facts" — when I said I felt worthless, or that I could feel that a colleague was annoyed with me, this was meant to show that the feeling gave me no reason to believe the thing. I have since seen the phrase used much more broadly, and it seems to me that it shifts between several different claims. Philosophers of emotion standardly distinguish at least three pictures — emotions as judgments, emotions as perceptions, and emotions as non-cognitive feelings or bodily states — and I suspect different readings of the slogan align with different pictures.

Lay out the distinct precise theses "feelings aren't facts" could be used to assert: for example, that emotions lack propositional content, that emotions cannot be true or false, that emotions cannot even be accurate or inaccurate, that feeling that p is never evidence that p, that the occurrence of an emotion is not a fact "about the world" but only a fact about the subject, or that emotions have no justificatory force in practical or theoretical reasoning. For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; indicate which philosophical picture of emotion it presupposes; and say which reading, if any, would actually license the therapeutic move of treating a feeling of worthlessness or a felt certainty that someone is angry as no reason to believe it. I especially want to know where the plausible readings end and the overreach begins — the point at which the slogan stops being a useful corrective and starts implying that emotions carry no information at all. Please close by saying how confident you are in this taxonomy and singling out the point you are least sure of.
