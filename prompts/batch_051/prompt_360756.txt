I'm a doctoral student working on nineteenth-century moral psychology, and I've been trying to get clear on the exact structure of the disagreement between Schopenhauer's defense of compassion and Nietzsche's attack on pity. Schopenhauer takes compassion — Mitleid — to be the one genuine moral motive, grounded in the recognition that the boundary between self and other is an artifact of the principium individuationis and ultimately illusory; when I see another's suffering as wholly theirs, I misperceive, and compassion is the beginning of seeing through that illusion. Nietzsche, by contrast, treats pity as a hazardous and partly self-deceived affect: it neither simply relieves suffering nor leaves the sufferer's dignity intact, it spreads pain by making the observer suffer as well, and it often serves hidden impulses of power, superiority, or ressentiment even while wearing the mask of benevolence. The puzzle for me is that both seem to be looking at the same phenomenon and drawing opposite evaluations, so I want to force the two positions into direct contact rather than letting them talk past each other.

Please write the dialogue that might take place if a sophisticated Schopenhauerian and a sophisticated Nietzschean were left alone in a seminar room after a talk on the ethics of compassion and had to argue the matter out. Give the two characters equal competence, and have them answer each other's actual objections rather than deliver set speeches. I'd like the dialogue to begin from one concrete case — the two of them watching a stranger injured in the street — and then move from that case into the deeper dispute about what pity does to the person who feels it, what it does to the person who receives it, and whether either of those effects should count as a reason for or against the emotion. Partway through, the Nietzschean should press the charge that pity is shot through with hidden self-regard and that, far from reducing the world's suffering, it multiplies it; the Schopenhauerian should concede whatever is psychologically plausible in that charge and then ask whether the hidden ancestry of a motive settles its moral worth, and should press in turn on the normative standard that allows Nietzsche to treat strengthening as good and weakening as bad without having defended that standard.

The dialogue should end not with a declared winner but with the disputants themselves locating the point at which their disagreement becomes fundamental: is the crux a psychological-empirical question about what pity characteristically does, a normative question about what an ethic should aim at, or a metaphysical question about whether the separation between selves is real? Have them reach that diagnosis in their final exchange rather than appending it as an announced moral.
