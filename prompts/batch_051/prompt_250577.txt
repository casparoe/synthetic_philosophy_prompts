I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am writing a long essay about how languages are individuated: what makes a language one thing rather than two, and what it means to say a language dies or is born. The case I keep returning to is the change from Latin to Italian, and I have come to hold a position I would like you to test.

The position is this: there was no point at which Latin became Italian. Across the Italian peninsula, spoken Latin changed continuously from generation to generation, and at no moment did parents and children fail to understand one another because one spoke Latin and the other Italian. The appearance of a sharp break is an artifact of two things: the survival of a fixed written standard, Classical Latin, while speech kept moving; and the later, partly deliberate construction of a literary vernacular that came to be called Italian. On this view Latin and Italian are not two successive languages but two labels for different registers and stages of a single continuous tradition, and the question "when did Latin become Italian?" is not well posed in the way it first appears.

I want you to tell me what would count against this position. Do not just give the obvious objection, that by some date the spoken language had changed enough that a speaker of Classical Latin and a speaker of the vernacular could not converse. I want the fuller map: which historical, linguistic, sociological, or conceptual facts would have to obtain for the position to be false, and which of those are live possibilities rather than merely logical ones. Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which of them a defender of the position would concede as genuinely counting against it and which they would dismiss as question-begging or as resting on a confusion about what a language is. Tell me also what the position predicts that its rivals, the rupture views, do not, and whether any of those predictions have already been tested by the historical record. Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that is a problem for a claim of this kind or simply the correct consequence of treating languages as social practices rather than natural kinds. The evidence I would personally find most persuasive against it would be evidence that speakers, before the standardization of Italian, experienced their everyday speech as something other than Latin and said so; please be explicit about whether that test is fair.
