I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about who should get a vote, so I wrote out a bunch of cases and my gut verdicts on them. I'd like you to find the principle behind them, if there is one.

- A city is deciding whether to allow a big factory just inside its border; the fumes will mostly drift over the neighboring town downwind. I think the neighboring town's residents should get a real say in the decision, not just a chance to comment at a hearing.
- A country is deciding whether to adopt a carbon tax; a lot of the effects will land on people in low-lying countries halfway around the world. But I dont think Bangladeshi citizens should vote in our elections, even though they may be more affected than many of our own voters.
- A town is deciding its budget. Tourists there for a week are subject to its noise ordinance and its sales tax, and the budget changes both. I don't think they should vote.
- Permanent residents who aren't citizens but have lived in the town for a decade, work here, and pay taxes: yes, they should vote in local elections. Actually, I think they should vote in national ones too.
- Nonresident citizens who moved away twenty years ago and have no property or family here anymore: no, they shouldn't keep voting, even though it's "their" country on paper. Maybe a grace period of one election, then cut it off.
- Convicted prisoners: yes, they should vote. The point of a sentence is loss of liberty, not loss of a say over the laws that still govern them.
- Sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds who work and pay some taxes: I keep going back and forth, but mostly yes, at least in local elections.
- Future generations: obviously you can't poll them, but some of the biggest decisions (climate, nuclear, debt) are mostly decisions about them. I think we need some institution whose only job is to represent their interests, with actual power, not just an ombudsman.
- A city is deciding whether to build a subway line. The city's own residents decide; people in the broader metro area who commute in and will use it don't get a vote, even though they're affected.

I keep reaching for "all and only the people affected should get a say," but my own verdicts don't obey it: the Bangladesh case and the tourist case come out different, and so do the downwind-neighbor case and the commuter case. Is there one principle that gets all or most of these, or one that forces me to revise some of them? What does your principle say about cases I haven't listed—like a city deciding rent control that will push rents up in neighboring towns, or a country deciding military intervention abroad? And which of my verdicts does it force me to give up?
