Adopt the role of a philosopher who works at the boundary between virtue ethics and the critique of ideology, and think through the problem below carefully. Before committing to an answer, brainstorm at least three candidate principles, test them against my cases, and say explicitly where each breaks.

For the past several years I have been reporting on the science and public debate over grit and resilience: the psychological research that treats perseverance as a character trait, and the critical response that treats "resilience" language as a way of converting structural failures into tests of individual character. Reporting on this has left me with a set of verdicts that each feel right, but I cannot state the principle behind them, and I suspect some of them conflict. I would like you to tell me whether there is a coherent view here, and if so what it is.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. A doctoral student spends three years on an experiment that repeatedly fails, diagnoses each failure, and eventually gets the result published. I call this grit, with no reservations.

2. A start-up founder burns through savings and investors' money for years on a product no one wants; staff burn out; he insists that his refusal to quit is grit. I think this is self-deception, not virtue, and that praising it is wrong.

3. A hospital coping with chronic understaffing publicly profiles a nurse who keeps taking extra shifts without complaint as "resilient." I find the praise ideological: it turns a staffing failure into a test of individual character.

4. A single mother works two jobs to keep her child in school against long odds. I admire her persistence, and I feel uneasy when someone tells me this is just resilience ideology.

5. A political prisoner holds to her convictions through years of solitary confinement. I want to call this courage rather than ideology or pathology, but it does not look like a case of "achievement" at all.

6. A gambler keeps returning to the table convinced that the next bet will recover his losses. I deny that this is grit; it is compulsion. Yet structurally it resembles the founder's case.

I want three things from you. First, state precisely the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that best captures my verdicts, and acknowledge any tension between covering all the cases and keeping the principle simple. Second, apply your principle to further cases of your own construction that would discriminate between rival principles I might be unconsciously using. Third, say honestly which of my verdicts, if any, your principle cannot accommodate, and whether you think I should revise that verdict rather than complicate the principle.
