A friend of mine is hung up on multiple discovery—Newton and Leibniz, Darwin and Wallace, Gray and Bell. He says it shows discoveries are basically inevitable once a field is ripe, and the individuals are interchangeable delivery mechanisms, so priority disputes are silly. I think the claim is either too vague to test or secretly counterfactual (what would have happened without Newton?), but I suspect there's a testable core in there and I want you to pull it out. Design the strongest empirical program you can for the claim that discoveries happen when a field is "ripe." That might mean operationalizing ripeness somehow, collecting near-simultaneous discoveries and comparing their lag times, or using cases where a result was published but ignored and then rediscovered much later—Mendel is the obvious one—as a kind of natural experiment. Tell me what pattern of results would count for inevitability and what would count against it, and how you'd guard against just finding whatever you went looking for. Then be precise about the residue: what part of the original claim, especially the bit about credit and whether a particular person mattered, would remain untouched even by a perfect dataset?
