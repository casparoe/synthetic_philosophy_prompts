I'm a CS PhD working on LLMs that do multi-step reasoning—math word problems, code that writes code, that sort of thing. Right now our whole training setup is outcome supervision: we only ever give the model a score on the final answer, or pass/fail on the tests. I've been pushing in lab meetings to add process supervision for a theorem-proving side project, i.e. have annotators or a helper model rate individual reasoning steps as valid, redundant, helpful, or wrong, because the final-answer signal is so sparse that the model will happily emit a plausible-looking intermediate lemma and then build garbage on top of it. My advisors entire rebuttal was "reward is enough," delivered like a QED, and half the lab nodded. As he clarified it, he meant: if you specify the outcome reward correctly and optimize it hard enough, the model is forced to learn sound intermediate steps anyway, and hand-labeling steps just bakes your own idea of good reasoning into the target. I left the meeting anoyed but also unsure where exactly the disagreement is.

So I want to bring back a disambiguation of that one sentence, because I suspect it's doing several jobs at once. What are the distinct precise claims "reward is enough" could be making here—e.g. about scalar outcome reward being sufficient to specify a task, about credit assignment being solved automatically, about process feedback being redundant rather than harmless, about sample efficiency in finite regimes versus optimality in the limit, about generality across tasks? For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive-but-contestable empirical claim, or probably false, and whether that reading actually rules out per-step rewards. A few things I keep getting stuck on: in the limit a lot of things are enough, but we have a compute budget and a deadline; and a per-step reward is still a reward, so maybe the slogan doesn't even exclude what I'm asking for unless you quietly read "reward" as "outcome reward only." I want a menu of readings with verdicts, plus which reading my advisor would need—not just which he could fall back on—to get his conclusion.
