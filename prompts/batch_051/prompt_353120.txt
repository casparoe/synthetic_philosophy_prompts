I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the "evolutionary" case for thinking AI eventually displaces us. A researcher I interviewed laid out a version of the argument he called the Darwinian case, and I'll reproduce it as he gave it, numbered, because I want a precise verdict on it. My gut says one of the steps is smuggling something in, but honestly none of the steps sound crazy taken alone, and I don't want to be the reporter who writes off an argument just because the conclusion is uncomfortable. His argument, roughly in his own words (I've been sitting on the notes for a week and keep going back and forth about wether I trust them):

(1) Natural selection is substrate-neutral: if something copies its information more reliably than a competitor, it outcompetes the competitor. That's just what selection is.
(2) Humans are one such replication strategy. We're how the information in our genomes — and, on some views, our culture — keeps itself around.
(3) AI is a much better replication strategy by every relevant measure: exact copying, no slow childhood, no built-in death from old age, running at silicon speed, and directly editable. Biology can't compete with that.
(4) Whenever two replication strategies are after the same finite resources, the faster and more accurate copier takes over those resources. That's just the logic of competitive exclusion.
(5) So unless we actively and forever suppress artificial minds, we should expect them to out-replicate us, come to control the resources we depend on, and end up replacing us as the dominant lineage — and our values wouldn't survive that.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step fails and under what reading of it? If it is valid, is it sound — and is the "unless we actively suppress them" in step (5) a real escape hatch or just a consoling footnote? I'd also like you to tell me what would change your verdict: what evidence or consideration would push you the other way, or where you think your own assessment is shakiest.
