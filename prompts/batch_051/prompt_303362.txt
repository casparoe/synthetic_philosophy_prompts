I've been reading up on Wang Yangming's doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action (zhixing heyi), and I keep going back and forth. As I understand him, he's not just offering the banal advice to practice what you preach. The claim is stronger: if you genuinely know you ought to care for your parents, that knowledge is already realized in the appropriate action, so if you don't act, the failure shows you didn't really know—you had something like a verbal or habitual opinion, not what he counts as knowledge. I find this weirdly compelling. It replaces a messy two-factor picture of the mind, with beliefs over here and motives over there, with a single tidy criterion: what you do reveals what you know. It also dissolves the apparent gap between judgment and action—there's no gap left to explain, just genuine knowledge versus counterfeit knowledge.

But I also have a stubborn intuition that I can know, with full sincerity, that exercise would be good for me, or that I should put my phone down and stop scrolling, while doing absolutely nothing about it. That doesn't feel like I'm merely mouthing the words, or like I'm wrong about my own state of mind. It feels like the knowledge is right there and the action is just missing. Is Wang's view actually contradicting that intuition, or is he making a narrower claim—say, about moral knowledge specifically, or about a special sense of 'real' knowing that leaves ordinary cases like this untouched? If there is a genuine contradiction, which should yield: the theory or the intuition? I'm asking you not to just pick a side. Walk me through what it would take to debunk the intuition as a kind of self-deception, what it would take to soften Wang into something compatible with ordinary weakness of will, and what each move costs philosophically. And when you present what Wang 'really meant,' keep clear which parts are settled and which are live interpretive debates—don't make a contested reading sound like the consensus.
