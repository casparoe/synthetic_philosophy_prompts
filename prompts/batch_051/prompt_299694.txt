I teach an undergraduate aesthetics course with about 18 students, and I want a 75-minute in-class activity for the unit on forgeries and the value of originals. The conceptual target is the puzzle that two objects can be perceptually indistinguishable — even physically identical in every feature a viewer could check — and yet differ in value because of how they came into existence and what has happened to them since. I want the students to run into this themselves rather than have it explained afterward: at some point in the exercise, their own bids, rankings, or decisions should shift in response to a change in provenance alone, with nothing visible about the object changing.

Please make it more specific than the usual original-versus-copy contrast. Build the mechanics around at least three cases: (a) an object accepted as an original; (b) a visually perfect copy whose status is initially unknown; and (c) a fake or copy that has by now acquired a historical standing of its own — a famous forgery, or a replica made by the artist's own hand and long displayed in a well-known collection. The point of decision should not be spotting fakes (participants should not be able to win by inspection), but what, if anything, a label or certificate changes. A two-round token auction, a museum acquisition committee role-play, or a relabeling hearing would all be plausible shells, but choose whatever mechanics make the valuation shift happen under the rules rather than through the facilitator's commentary. Include a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the distinction between aesthetic value, historical or provenance value, and market price, and be explicit about one feature of the phenomenon the exercise cannot reproduce in a classroom and that I will need to supply in discussion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance, students reducing the game to investment strategy, or flatly denying that a hard-to-verify fact about an object's past could matter to its value — and how to handle that without arguing them into the conclusion. Use no real money and require no art-historical background knowledge; all materials should be printable or improvised.
