I'm a retired chemist — spent forty years treating measurments as the boss, and now I've got time to read philosophy. I've been working through Hume's stuff on aesthetics, especially his attempt to find a "standard of taste" while still admitting that beauty isn't literally sitting in the object the way mass is. Last week a friend and I got into it about whether someone's taste in music can be wrong. He ended the argument by announcing, with some finality, that beauty is in the eye of the beholder. That was supposed to settle it.

Here's my problem. Hume's whole discussion suggests people don't actually behave as if taste is a free-for-all: we argue about art, we treat some critics as more reliable, we think a person can be trained to see or hear something better, and some judgments seem to win out over time. I can't square all of that with what my friend said, and I suspect the slogan is smuggling in several different claims at once.

Please act as a professional philosopher working in aesthetics. Before you commit to anything, brainstorm a bunch of distinct precise claims that "beauty is in the eye of the beholder" could be making — ideally six or more. Then lay them out as a menu, and for each reading tell me whether it's a platitude, something substantive but defensible, or actually false, and why. I especially want to know which of these readings would genuinely support my friend's use of the slogan to end an argument about whether someone's taste was wrong, and which readings commit him to much more than he probably meant. Finally, say which reading (if any) a Humean standard-of-taste person could accept without contradiction, and where the slogan starts to conflict with the idea that some judgments are better than others. Think through this carefully — I'm less interested in the usual "it's all relative" cliché than in exactly where the sentence earns its keep and where it breaks.
