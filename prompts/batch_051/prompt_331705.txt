My friend and I have been going in circles about whether a big neural net running on a server could ever actually matter morally — not just act smart, but suffer, or have something it is like to be it. It started with whether a super detailed simulation of a mouse's brain would feel a pinch, and slid from there to servers. I started writing down every distinct rule of thumb people use for where to draw the line between computations that matter and ones that don't, and so far I have these: (1) behavior — the thing can talk convincingly about its own feelings, express fear, ask not to be shut off; (2) integration — its parts are wired into one unified whole, so it isn't just a heap of separate processes that happen to share a box; (3) self-modeling — it runs a model of itself that it uses to plan, so there's a self in there to be hurt; (4) reinforcement-style pain and pleasure — it has internal signals that steer what it does and get strengthened the way an animal's fear or relief does, rather than just having passive cameras and microphones; (5) substrate — only certain kinds of wet biological stuff can count, and silicon can never be in the club no matter what it does to my cousins satisfaction.

I can't shake the feeling there is at least one more line people actually draw, genuinely different from these five — not a restatement, a special case, or a mashup of them. What is it, and is it any better at deciding which computations matter morally than the ones on my list?
