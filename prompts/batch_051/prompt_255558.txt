I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about escalation — putting more into something partly because you've already put a lot in. Going by my gut on cases:

- A friend has been in a miserable relationship for eight years and won't leave because "that would make the last eight years a waste." I think that's a terrible reason; the eight years are gone either way.
- A studio has spent $200m on a film that's 80% shot, needs $30m more to finish, and the finished film would probly gross $80m. I think they should finish it, but not because of the $200m — because $30m for $80m is a good deal on its own.
- A general argues we can't withdraw from a failing war because 5,000 soldiers have already died and "otherwise they died for nothing." I think that's wrong: the deaths don't improve the odds of the next phase, and you can't buy meaning for the dead with more dead.
- A gambler at a roulette table keeps doubling his bets to win back his losses. I think he's being an idiot; the wheel doesn't know or care what he's down.
- A PhD student in year 7 hates the program but has a complete draft and just needs a semester of revisions. I think she should finish — again not because of the seven years, but because one more semester buys the degree.
- A company publicly commits to never abandoning a product line, so rivals won't enter its market; sometimes this forces it to finish products that are individually losing money. I think this can be rational — the occasional loss is the price of keeping the threat credible.

So far I could almost state a clean principle: sunk costs are irrelevent, only future costs and benefits matter. But two things bother me.

First, the company case seems to violate it, or at least need an escape hatch: the past commitment matters because of what it makes rivals expect about the future, which is itself a future benefit. Is that a genuine exception or am I rationalizing?

Second, the dollar auction. Two people bidding on a $20 bill, highest bidder wins and pays their bid, second-highest pays their bid too. I'm confident two rational people should never let it get to $18 vs $19. But once it's there, sitting at $18 and deciding whether to bid $20, I find myself thinking the $18 bidder should bid $20 — stopping means losing $18 for sure, bidding $20 at least gives a shot at breaking even, even though it risks losing $20 if the other goes to $21. But if that's right at each step, how did the whole thing become a trap you shouldn't enter? That feels like a mistake in my step-by-step reasoning, but I can't locate it.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? What does it say about the step-by-step reasoning in the auction, and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And what does it predict about a case I keep going back and forth on: a city that's spent $2bn on a half-built transit line and is deciding whether to spend another $500m to finish it or abandon it?
