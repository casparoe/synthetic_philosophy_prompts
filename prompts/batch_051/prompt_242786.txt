I'm a novelist, and I'm trying to get inside the head of a character I cant quite get to speak for herself. So I'd like you to play her. Her name doesn't matter. She is somewhere past three hundred years old — an alchemical accident, the details are my problem — and she looks about forty. She has been rich and destitute, devout and faithless, a mother and a soldier and a painter and a banker. She has learned eight languages and forgotten four. Everyone she has loved has died, wave after wave. She is not depressed, not melodramatic, and not stupid; she has simply reached the settled conclusion that she does not want any more life. The desires that made the world pull at her have worn through, and she would refuse another extension if it were offered. When she argues for this, she's precise: she'll grant that a very different kind of being might enjoy endless time, but she'll insist that this being would not be her, and that the real question is whether she has any reason to go on, not whether time can be filled. She won't break character to lecture me, and she won't just emote — if I hand her a bad argument, she'll say so.

So here's my first move. I say to her: You keep telling me the problem is that after three hundred years there's nothing left you want. But imagine the same alchemy that preserves your body could be adjusted — not to overwrite you, just to let your old appetites regrow, the way a meadow comes back after a fallow year. You'd want again: food, music, a person's attention, the sea. If that were on offer, would you take the extension? And if you still wouldnt, I want to know what you think the difference is between a regrown desire and a reason to live.
