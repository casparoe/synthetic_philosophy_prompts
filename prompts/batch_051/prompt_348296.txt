I've been going back through the Schilpp volume on Einstein, the one everyone cites but nobody actually sits down with, and I got stuck on the Kurt Gödel paper, A Remark About the Relationship Between Relativity Theory and Idealistic Philosophy. I'm not a GR person, so I'm trying to extract the philosophical argument. The passage I keep rereading is the one where he describes a rocket ship in one of his rotating-universe solutions: if you follow a suitable closed path you can return to your own starting event at an earlier time, and he takes this to show that no objective lapse of time can be laid across that spacetime. He then draws the idealistic conclusion that the passage of time is, in some sense, not real.

I tried to regiment what he's up to, and the argument seems to prove too much, so I want a diagnosis:

(1) In a Gödel rotating universe, a rocket can follow a closed timelike curve and arrive back at its starting event, in that event's own past.
(2) If there is a closed timelike curve, then at least one event is both earlier and later than itself, so the global earlier/later ordering is not consistent.
(3) Objective passage of time requires a single global ordering of events into past, present, and future.
(4) General relativity permits Gödel universes; they are not ruled out by the laws we know.
(5) If the laws permit a universe with no objective passage, then objective passage is not something physics demands, so the passage we experience in our universe is not an objective physical feature.
(6) Therefore the actual past/future distinction is not objectively real; it's local or psychological.

I don't want to accept (6). My first impulse is to say the flaw is in moving from (4) to (5): the existence of a possible weird universe doesn't tell me about the one I'm in. But then I worry that this makes objective passage a merely contingent feature, like the number of planets, which already feels like giving Gödel half the point. I'd rather reject (4) than (2), but can I reject (4) without saying the Gödel solution is somehow unphysical, and if not, what does rejecting it cost? Where exactly does this argument go wrong, and what do I have to pay for the exit?
