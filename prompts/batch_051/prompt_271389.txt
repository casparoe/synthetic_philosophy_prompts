I have a standing rule for my own studying, and I want a genuine audit of it rather than confirmation. Before I allow myself to say that I understand a new idea — in mathematics, history, biology, wherever — I require myself to construct at least one analogy that maps the new idea onto something I already understand well. If I cannot build such an analogy, I refuse to say I understand the idea, even when I can recite the definition and work the standard problems. I apply the rule to other people's explanations too: an explanation that remains formal or abstract earns only provisional trust until I can translate it into a comparison with a structure I already know. I adopted the rule because I have come to suspect that analogy is not one study technique among many but the underlying operation of thought itself, so an idea for which I can find no analogy is probably an idea I have not yet grasped. Lately, though, I can see that the rule does two distinguishable things, and only one of them is legitimate. One thing it might test is whether I have located the deep structure of an idea; but it might instead only test whether the new idea resembles something I already happen to know. In that case it would make me overconfident about familiar-seeming ideas and push me to dismiss genuinely novel ones as unintelligible. A second, opposite worry is that the test is too weak, because I can always manufacture some analogy if I want the answer to come out a certain way. I use this rule in ordinary high-school studying, not in research. Is the rule defensible as a standing policy? Which of my two worries is the serious one, under what conditions does the rule mislead, and is there a modified version that keeps whatever the rule genuinely buys me?
