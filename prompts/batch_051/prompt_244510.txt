I recently read an explainer on formal epistemology that summarizes van Fraassen's Reflection principle as follows:

"Reflection says that, for any proposition, your confidence now should defer to the confidence you expect to have later: conditional on your future self assigning probability r to a claim, your present self should assign probability r to it as well. So if you now think there is a 20 percent chance that, after reviewing the evidence over the next year, you will be 95 percent confident in some claim, then you are rationally committed to the conditional judgment that, if next-year-you really has reached 95 percent, now-you should already be at 95 percent. The motivation is that your future self deserves your trust because it will know everything you know now plus whatever you learn in the meantime, which makes it strictly better informed. On van Fraassen's picture, then, a rational person cannot anticipate changing their mind: to foresee a future opinion and decline to adopt it at once is to treat your future self as an expert in general while refusing to take its advice in this particular case."

Something about the final two sentences strikes me as off, but I don't have the technical background to locate the problem. Is this a fair summary of the Reflection principle? I would like an accuracy audit: which parts are correct, which are common simplifications or overstatements, and whether the conclusion about anticipated mind-changing follows from the parts that are accurate.
