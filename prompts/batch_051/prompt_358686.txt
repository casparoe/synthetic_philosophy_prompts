Suppose an author has carefully checked each of the 1,000 claims in her book and, on the strength of her own fallibility, writes in the preface that at least one is false. Now consider an argument that her situation and the lottery paradox stand or fall together. (1) In the lottery, the evidence gives each ticket a very high chance of losing while also making it certain that some ticket wins; in the preface, the evidence supports each claim individually while also supporting the proposition that some claim is false. (2) Any rule of rational belief adequate to one case must yield the same verdict in the other, because the two cases have this structure in common. (3) Therefore, no solution to one paradox can leave the other untouched. (4) Hence the only available solutions are uniform: reject the rule that high probability warrants belief, reject closure of rational belief under conjunction, or reject the consistency requirement on rational belief. I believe the argument is valid but suspect the two cases differ in an epistemically important way. Is the argument valid? If not, state exactly which step fails and on what reading. If it is valid, say whether (4) follows, and if you think the cases differ, identify the difference precisely and explain what it does to the argument.
