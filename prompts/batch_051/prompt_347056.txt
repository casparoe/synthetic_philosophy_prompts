I'm a PhD student working on early modern political economy, and I keep going back and forth on something in Hume's essay Of Money (from the Political Discourses of 1752, later collected into the Essays Moral, Political, and Literary). It seems like two claims in the same essay pull in opposite directions, and I'd like a careful take on wether they can be reconciled.

Here's the first claim, closely paraphrased: money is not, properly speaking, one of the subjects of commerce. It is only the instrument people have agreed on to facilitate exchange. Hume calls it none of the wheels of trade: it is just the oil that makes the wheels move more smoothly and easily. He then says that the greater or lesser quantity of money in a country is, in itself, a matter of no consequence, because prices always settle in proportion to the quantity of money.

Here's the second claim, also closely paraphrased from later in the same essay: in the interval between an increase in the quantity of money and the resulting rise in prices, the increase of gold and silver is favorable to industry. New money must pass through many hands before prices adjust, and in passing it quickens the diligence of people, since they feel themselves richer and spend more. Hume even says a wise magistrate should try to keep money in a state of gradual increase, because that keeps alive the spirit of industry in the nation.

So on one page the quantity of money is said to be of no consequence, and a few pages later an increasing money supply is said to be good for industry and something policy should aim at. Is this just the standard short-run versus long-run distinction, or is there something more subtle going on? Are there readings on which one of these claims is doing more work than it looks like, or on which the second actually follows from the first? Which reading requires the least special pleading?

Honestly, semester brain has me half-convinced it's just the usual short-run/long-run move, but I want to know whether that undercuts the oil-of-commerce point or actually follows from it. Also, since I'm trying to get this precise: say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
