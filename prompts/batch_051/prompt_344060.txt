Something about asylum practice has been bugging me and I want help pulling out the theory behind it. Here's the puzzle. As the rules stand, a person outside their own country who has a well-founded fear of being singled out for harm on account of race, religion, nationality, politics, or membership in a particular social group gets a strong legal guarantee: they can't be sent back to where they'd face that harm, which is called non-refoulement. But a person who fled because drought made their region unlivable, or because the economy collapsed and there kids were starving, or because a war flattened their city without anyone targeting them in particular, has no comparable protection on those grounds alone. They're usually classed as "economic" or "environmental" migrants, and the receiving state treats itself as free to send them back, however bad the conditions. Meanwhile states are also really reluctant to admit any obligation to take people in from abroad; they insist that real obligations, where they exist, kick in only once a person reaches there territory or is otherwise within their jurisdiction. So protection at the border is treated as much more optional than protection from return.

What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit moral or political theory this practice presupposes: about what makes some harms count as persecution and other, equally severe harms not; and about why borders matter differently for sending back versus taking in. In particular, tell me whether the practice secretly presupposes that only harms inflicted by an agent ground a protection claim, while harms suffered (famine, disaster, diffuse economic forces) generate at most humanitarian discretion; whether it presupposes that the duty not to return someone is stronger or more basic than the duty to admit; and whether those two suppositions add up to a coherent view or push against each other — for example, is the person refused because they're fleeing starvation being penalized for the source of their threat rather than its severity? Please be careful to mark which parts of this are settled law or settled interpretation and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this; don't present some contested view as if it's the consensus. And keep it under 900 words.
