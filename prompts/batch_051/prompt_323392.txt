I'm giving the toast at my brother's wedding. I'm a formal epistemologist, and about half the room know it; the rest don't. I hold a view that will constrain what I can honestly say: I think we almost never update on evidence we are certain of. Most of what observation actually gives us is uncertain evidence — a remembered glance, a tone, two people's small habits with each other — and it shifts the probabilities we assign to various claims without letting us conditionalize on some exact proposition we have now learned for sure. That is what I mean by Jeffrey conditionalization, also called probability kinematics. It follows that I cannot stand up and call the marriage guaranteed, or use phrases like 'I know,' 'without a doubt,' or 'there's no question.' But I can say what I have observed and how far it has moved me, and I can be precise about what my confidence rests on.

Write the toast, about five minutes when spoken, grounded in one concrete memory of the couple. Let that memory work as the uncertain evidence on which the speech performs a Jeffrey update — perhaps on a partition like 'they are right for each other' versus 'they merely look right for each other' — without ever naming the machinery. The epistemologists in the room should recognize exactly where the probability kinematics happens; the family should simply hear a warm, funny, precise account of why I believe in the marriage without my claiming more than I think the evidence supports. Avoid all explicit explanation of the philosophy; the constraint should do its work invisibly, in what the toast can and cannot say.
