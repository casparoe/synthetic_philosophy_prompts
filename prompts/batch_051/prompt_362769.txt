I keep going back and forth on this argument that a report of a miracle can never rationally convince you. It's meant as a cleaned-up version of the sort of line Hume takes about miracles, but every version I see skips over a step, and I can't find the exact one where it goes wrong. It runs:

(1) A miracle is an event that would violate a law of nature.
(2) A law of nature is a regularity that has been observed to hold without exception, so our evidence for it is about as strong as evidence can be.
(3) When two bodies of evidence conflict, you should believe whichever is supported by the stronger evidence overall.
(4) Testimony is always weaker evidence than the direct, exceptionless evidence behind a law of nature, because people misperceive, confabulate and lie all the time.
(5) So however good the testimony for a miracle looks, the rational response is always to prefer some non-miraculous explanation — fraud, mistake, coincidence — over the claim that the law of nature was actually violated.

I half-suspect the flaw is in (4), that testimony isn't a single weak piece of evidence but a package of many independant pieces, and enough independent pieces could in principle outweigh the law-evidence. But then I worry that just pushes the problem back: each individual piece is still only human testimony, while the regularity has never failed once. Is the argument valid? If it's invalid, which step should go, and on what reading does it break? If it is valid, is the conclusion weaker than it looks?
