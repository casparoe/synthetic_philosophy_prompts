A sentence from last week's seminar has been rattling around in my head. I teach a philosophy of games course, and we were discussing first-person shooters set in real, recent conflicts. One student argued that a game like that can't help but turn a war into an optimization problem—clearing sightlines, timing reloads, managing ammo, repositioning—and that the pleasure of doing any of that well is, in some small way, the pleasure of directing an actual engagement. Another student cut her off with "oh come on, it's just a game," and most of the room nodded. I let it go because class was ending, but I should have pressed it.

The phrase seems to be doing a lot of work at once. It could be claiming that nothing in the game really refers to actual events, or that pretending isn't endorsing, or that no one is harmed so there's no moral question in the first place, or that whatever habits and attitudes get trained in the game stay in the game, or that a game is walled off from ordinary life in a way that ordinary moral categories simply don't apply to it. Some of those strike me as trivially true, some as very likely false, and some as substantive but unproven.

I want to go in next week with a proper disambiguation. What are the distinct precise claims someone might be making when they say "it's just a game" in that context? For each, tell me whether it's trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and—this is what I care about most—which reading would actually have to be true for the slogan to defuse my student's objection about optimization, seriousness, and war. I'd rather not settle for "whichever reading is most charitable"; I want the menu laid out with verdicts.
