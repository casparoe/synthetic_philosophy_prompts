I am a novelist, and the book I am drafting turns on a small community's response to a reported miracle: my real subject is not whether the event happened but what a reasonable person should do with an account of it. I have been reading the first part of David Hume's "Of Miracles" (An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, Section X), and the passage I keep returning to is this:

"It being a general maxim, that no objects have any discoverable connexion together, and that all the inferences, which we can draw from one to another, are founded merely on our experience of their constant and regular conjunction; it is evident, that we ought not to make an exception to this maxim in favour of human testimony, whose connexion with any event seems, in itself, as little necessary as any other."

The position I currently hold, largely on the strength of this passage and what surrounds it, is this: testimony has no evidential standing of its own; it is just one more empirical channel, and its deliverances must be balanced against uniform experience exactly as any other inference from constant conjunction would be. It follows that a report of a miracle is never entitled to a special evidential dispensation, and is credible only if the falsehood of the testimony would itself be a larger departure from uniform experience than the event reported. I want to know what would count against this position — not in the sense of a rival theory of miracles, but in the sense of genuine exposure. Which observations, cases, arguments, or developments would a defender of this reading have to admit as counting against it, and which alleged objections are only apparent counterexamples the position can absorb? Rank the real defeaters from most to least damaging, because I want to give the book's skeptical challenger the strongest version of the case, not a strawman.

Give me exactly five numbered items, ranked from most damaging to least damaging. Write each item in one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. In the final sentence of each item, state either what would have to be true for that consideration to overturn the position outright rather than merely force a qualification, or where that consideration is most likely to fail.
