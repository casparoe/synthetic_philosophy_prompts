So me and my friends have been going in circles about the yellow vests, and I keep thinking the whole argument turns on a distinction Rawls draws in A Theory of Justice that nobody in the argument actually uses carefully. In his discussion of envy, he tries to keep three things apart: envy proper, which he treats as a vice—sort of a spiteful impulse to tear down someone else's advantage even though their being better off isn't what's actually hurting you; resentment, which for him is a moral feeling, a response to what you take to be an injustice done through institutions or other people's conduct; and "excusable envy," where someone's response gets mixed up with envy but the background conditions are so unequal, and have so damaged people's self-respect, that Rawls thinks we shouldn't be too quick to condemn the envier—the institutions are the real problem. The case I want to run through this grid: the French fuel tax hike in 2018 and the gilets jaunes protests. The salient facts, in case they matter for the mapping: Macron's government raised taxes on petrol and diesel partly on climate grounds; the increases fell hardest on people in peri-urban and rural areas with long car commutes and no serious transit alternative, while the urban professional class with metro lines and short commutes was comparatively insulated; the same government had scrapped a big chunk of the wealth tax not long before; and the movement mostly talked about dignity, fiscal injustice, and being treated as disposable, even as alot of commentators described the whole thing as envy of the winners of globalization.

What I'd like is a careful two-direction case analysis. First map the episode onto Rawls's three categories: which grievances look like resentment proper, which look like envy proper, and where "excusable envy" is doing the work—and then tell me where that mapping strains or breaks. I'm especially interested in whether the case makes the resentment/envy distinction look principled or just rhetorically convenient: what's the actual test for whether a yellow vest is reacting to injustice or just envying somebody's nicer life? And what does a Rawlsian prescribe here—condemn the envy, bracket the feeling and fix only the tax design, or treat the envy itself as evidence that the background structure is unjust? Does the episode end up supporting the idea that resentment is a signal of injustice and envy a vice, or does it show that "excusable envy" is just an unstable halfway house between the two?
