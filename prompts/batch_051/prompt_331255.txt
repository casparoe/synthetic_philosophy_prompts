For an epistemology reading group, I have been asked to write down my considered position on higher-order evidence so that the others can audit it. I currently hold all of the following, and I cannot see where the fault line is.

(1) Higher-order evidence is real evidence. If I acquire good evidence that my reasoning about some question is unreliable — for example, evidence of impairment, bias, or fatigue — this should normally move my first-order confidence in the relevant proposition.

(2) Epistemic akrasia is always irrational. An agent is irrational if she believes p while also believing that her total evidence does not support p. (Mutatis mutandis for graded belief: a rational agent's credence should match her judgment of what her total evidence supports.)

(3) Calibrationism about credence: a rational agent's credence in p should match her considered judgment about how likely it is that her reasoning about p has gotten things right. If I rationally judge that such reasoning is 70% likely to be correct, my credence in p should be around 0.7.

(4) Permissivism: at least some bodies of evidence rationally permit more than one doxastic attitude toward the same proposition; two rational agents with identical evidence can rationally differ.

(5) About disagreement specifically: when I discover that someone I regard as my epistemic peer disagrees with me about p after we have fully shared our evidence and reasoning, I should move substantially toward their view — even if my own first-order reasoning still strikes me as correct.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in tension, which pairs merely appear to be in tension, and how severe each genuine tension is. For each genuine tension, say which commitment would have to give and at what cost. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
