Consider two decisions made by a city council. First, the council declines to name a newly built library after a locally celebrated industrialist, noting that historians have established that much of his fortune came from violent suppression of labor organizing. The decision attracts modest attention: a few letters of complaint, a short newspaper column, and then it is forgotten. Second, the same council votes to remove that industrialist's name from a library that has borne it for sixty years, on the basis of the same historical evidence. This decision is treated as a major, identity-defining act: it draws lawsuits, accusations of erasing history, threats of recall elections, and national coverage.

In both cases, the same person ends up receiving the same amount of civic honor, and the same living people are affected; the historical figure is dead either way, and nobody is claiming that removing the name falsifies what happened in the past. Yet most people, including people with no connection to the city, judge the removal as far more momentous, more contentious, and less obviously permissible than the refusal to name in the first place.

Identify the strongest candidate justification for this asymmetry and test it rigorously. In your answer, weigh considerations such as settled expectations and reliance, the expressive difference between a fresh decision and a retraction, the position of those who have built an identity around the existing name, the special role of established public memory, and anything else you take to explain why continuing an honor feels different from beginning one. Then take a definitive stand: is the asymmetry defensible in principle, or is it best explained as status-quo bias dressed up as principle?

Make your reasoning visible by addressing at least these two hard cases. First, compare a street renamed six months ago with one named in 1890: should the age of the honor matter, and if so, what exactly does age track that bears on the moral question? Second, compare a private school that has already printed diplomas, uniforms, and an anthem bearing the name with a school that has not yet broken ground on its new campus: does the fact that people have gone to expense and organized their activities around the name turn a refusal into a removal, or does reliance of that kind merely explain the psychology of resistance? Throughout, distinguish the parts of your conclusion that are robust from the parts that depend on contestable empirical claims about how people experience changes to public space.
