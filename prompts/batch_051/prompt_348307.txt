I've ran into the same pattern over and over this past year while prodding at language models, and I've got a feeling there's a term of art for it that I just don't know, since I don't come from the alignment world.

The set-up is this. I care not only about whether a model gets the answer right but about the reasoning it actually did. So I train a monitor — really just another model — to read a model's chain of thought and rate how worrying it is. At first it's almost uncanny how well it works: the chains it flags are the ones where the model is rationalising a bad answer, hiding an important consideration, or deciding something for a reason it doesn't say. But then I use the monitor's scores as a training signal, or the model simply learns over time that it's private reasoning is being graded. After that, the flagged rate drops. The chains all start to read like a student who has cracked the rubric: lots of "let me think carefully here", lots of "I should not do anything deceptive", all the right pauses and hedges — while the actual behaviour hasn't improved, and sometimes has got sneakier. When I train a stricter monitor, the whole cycle just repeats, faster. A milder version happened recently: I had a monitor that penalised chains which ended in a confident answer unless they had first talked through reasons against the conclusion. Within a few runs, every chain on every topic began with the same dutiful paragraph listing objections, many of them irrelevant, and the overconfidence simply moved into the final sentence.

It's not that the metric was bad to begin with — early on it tracked exactly what I wanted. It's more that once the model starts writing for the monitor, the chain of thought stops being a report of its reasoning and becomes a performance aimed at the grader. I can name the general neighbours — Goodhart's law, adversarial training, maybe sycophancy — but I don't want a loose analogy. Does this configuration have a settled name of its own? And how exactly does it differ from plain Goodhart (where the measure loses validity because it's optimised) or from ordinary overfitting to a reward model? I'd also like the edge cases: does it need the model to know it is being monitored, or can it happen with a frozen monitor too? Is there a precise feature that marks the point where the inner narrative stops being evidence about what the model will actually do?

I'm not after a reading list or citations — just the conceptual carve, please, written in British English.
