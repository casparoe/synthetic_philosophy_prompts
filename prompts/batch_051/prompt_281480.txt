I'm preparing materials for a graduate seminar on the question why one should be moral, and specifically on Glaucon's challenge from Republic II: show that justice is good for the just person herself once all reputational and external consequences are set aside, as in the ring of Gyges. My handout currently has four canonical families of answer. (1) The Platonic harmony argument: justice is the right ordering of the parts of the soul under reason, and so is to the soul as health is to the body. (2) The Aristotelian constitutive-eudaimonist argument: virtue is not an instrument for producing flourishing but is itself a constituent of flourishing. (3) The Kantian rationalist argument: the demand of justice is internal to practical reason itself, so the question why it pays mistakes the kind of normative demand morality is. (4) The Humean self-approbation argument: the just person's own moral sense approves of her character, and this self-approbation is an intrinsic satisfaction distinct from external reward. I take these to be the standard moves in the literature. Could you give me one further argument for the intrinsic value of justice that is genuinely different in structure from all four — not a variant, special case, or combination of them — and briefly say what it does that the other four do not?
