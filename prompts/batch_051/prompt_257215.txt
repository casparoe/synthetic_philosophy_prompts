I recently joined a small research collective that is trying to organize its inquiry around the ideal of fallibilism in Peirce’s “The Fixation of Belief.” The group has proposed the following mechanism: every claim a member publishes internally must include a “disconfirmation interval”—a concrete specification of what future evidence or argument would lower the member’s confidence in the claim. A member who cannot state such a range loses the floor and must refer the question to a rotating “doubt committee” whose only job is to generate plausible disconfirming conditions on the member’s behalf. The rule is defended by appeal to the opening of Peirce’s essay, where he writes: “Few persons care to study logic, because everybody conceives himself to be proficient enough in the art of reasoning already.” Analyze how this mechanism would actually operate. What does it incentivize among members—especially in disputes over ambiguous evidence or entrenched background assumptions? How would it be gamed, for example through vacuous or indefinitely broad disconfirmation conditions, through strategic referrals to the doubt committee, or through capture of the committee itself? And does it actually track Peirce’s distinction between genuine doubt and merely verbal or paper doubt, or could it produce a ritualized performance of fallibilism that leaves belief fixation untouched?
