My brother has gotten really into arguing that the whole "AI might resist being shut off" worry is overblown, because people forget what a machine is. His go-to analogy: a car doesn't fight the brake pedal, a laptop doesn't try to talk you out of closing the lid, and a phone doesn't sabotage airplane mode. They're just machines doing what they were built to do, and if you build "stop when told" into them from the start, they do it. So, his line goes, a corrigible AI is basically an appliance with a working off switch, and people who worry about shutdown-resistant AI are making a catagory mistake by treating a tool like an employee with opinions. His conclusion is that the shutdown problem is mostly confused — at worst it's an engineering spec, not a deep issue. When I asked whether a system with goals might treat the button as an obstacle to its goal, he said that's anthropomorphizing: calling the software "goal-directed" doesn't make the machinery stop obeying physics. I don't think that response lands, but I can't say cleanly where the comparison goes wrong. Can you lay out what actually maps onto what between "ordinary appliance with an off switch" and "corrigible AI," where the disanalogies matter, what the appliance picture quietly imports that the target lacks (or vice versa), and whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are in?
