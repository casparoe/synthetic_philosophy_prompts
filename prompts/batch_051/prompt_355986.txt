A chapter in a popular data-science book I'm reading summarizes Simpson's paradox as follows: "Simpson's paradox arises when a trend in a pooled dataset reverses once the data are split into relevant subgroups. The standard example is Berkeley's 1973 graduate admissions, where men were admitted at a higher overall rate than women, but once the figures were examined department by department the gap essentially vanished and in several large departments women were admitted at a higher rate. The lesson is that aggregate statistics can be misleading and the subgroup analysis reveals the truth: there was no sex bias against women in Berkeley's admissions. More generally, whenever a correlation reverses under disaggregation, the aggregate correlation is spurious, which is why responsible analysts always report and rely on subgroup-level results rather than pooled numbers." I'm inclined to accept most of this, but I've also heard the "lesson" is more delicate than the book makes it sound. Is this a fair summary? Please go through it piece by piece and separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what a statistician would reject. In particular, does the conclusion that there was no bias against women at Berkeley, and the rule that one should always disaggregate, actually follow from the paradox? Please also distinguish what is settled among statisticians from what is genuinely contested about when the pooled versus the subgroup estimate is the one you should trust.
