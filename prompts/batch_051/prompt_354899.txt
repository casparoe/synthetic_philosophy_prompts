For some time I have been struck by an analogy that people around me treat as nearly self-evident whenever language preservation is discussed: a language, they tell me, is like a species; the loss of a language is like the extinction of a species; and the world's living languages are a kind of biodiversity. This comparison is made to do real work. It is used to argue that small and endangered languages deserve the same urgent protection as endangered animals, that pushing a language out of schooling and public life is akin to destroying a habitat, and that reviving a language with few remaining speakers is like saving a species from a tiny remnant population.

I would like an audit of how far this analogy actually holds.

Begin by setting out the correspondence explicitly. What on the linguistic side is being made to play the role of the species, the individual organism, the breeding population, the genus or family, the habitat, the niche, descent with modification, and extinction? Then identify the places where the mapping strains or breaks. I am especially interested in disanalogies such as these: a language is not a self-reproducing organism but something transmitted by learning and changed by human choices; languages mix and give rise to pidgins and creoles in ways biological species mostly do not; a language can be preserved in writing or recordings and reconstructed or revived from partial materials, whereas an extinct species cannot be brought back from a stuffed specimen; and speakers can leave a language behind without dying out, which means the death of a language is not literally a death at all. Tell me which of these disanalogies matter for the preservationist conclusion and which are merely decorative.

Finally, address the conclusion itself. Once the disanalogies are on the table, does the claim that endangered languages should be protected still stand, or was the biodiversity analogy carrying more weight than it can bear? If a better analogy exists, for example from the conservation of craft traditions, local knowledge, or cultural practices, sketch it and say where it gets things right and where it misleads. Please write the answer in British English.
