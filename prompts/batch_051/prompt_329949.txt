Consider the thought experiment known as Thomson's lamp. The lamp is off at t = 0. At t = 1/2 minute it is switched on, at t = 3/4 it is switched off, at t = 7/8 it is switched on, and so on: the nth switching operation occurs at time t = 1 − 1/2^n, so that infinitely many operations are scheduled before t = 1. The standard diagnosis is that the lamp has no determinate on/off state at t = 1, because the sequence of states following the operations (on, off, on, off, ...) does not converge, and because the completed supertask described would require infinitely many faultless operations accumulated within a finite time.

Now vary the operation of the switch. Suppose each individual switching operation has an independent probability p, with 0 < p < 1, of jamming. If the switch jams on an operation, it fails to make that operation's intended change and remains permanently in the state it was immediately in before that operation; once jammed, it can never be moved again, and all later scheduled operations do nothing. The schedule of intended operations is otherwise exactly as before.

Analyse this variation in detail. At t = 1, is the lamp in a determinate on/off state? If it is, with what probability is it on, and with what probability off? Some might say this variation dissolves the original puzzle, while others might say it merely relocates its oddity; say which you think, and explain what your view commits you to about the original assumption of an infallible switch.

Then contrast a second failure model. Here the switch does not jam as a result of being operated. Instead it has a continuous tendency to seize over time: in any brief interval of length dt, with t measured in minutes, it has probability λ dt of seizing in whatever state it is then in, with λ > 0, after which it can no longer change state, independently of how many operations have occurred. In this model, is there a positive probability that the entire supertask is executed faultlessly? If so, what should be said about t = 1 in that event, and what should be said when a seizure occurs instead? Use the contrast between the two failure models to identify which feature of the original set-up — the infinite number of operations, the finite total time, or the exact infallibility of the switch — carries the philosophical weight.

Finally, comment briefly on the limit p → 0 in the first model. Does the analysis tend back toward the original Thomson's-lamp verdict, or toward something more like a lamp whose final state is settled by a fair coin? Taking the two models together, say whether the original paradox is robust or fragile under small departures from idealisation.
