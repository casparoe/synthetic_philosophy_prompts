You are an expert in population ethics and procreative ethics, writing for a philosopher who teaches a graduate seminar on the ethics of procreation. I hold the procreation asymmetry, in roughly this form: the presence of pain is bad and the presence of pleasure is good; but the absence of pain is good even when no one exists to enjoy that absence, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is someone who is thereby deprived. I take this asymmetry to support the stronger claim that coming into existence is always a net harm. I want a map of what would count against the asymmetry itself, as distinct from defeaters that only tell against the anti-natalist conclusion. Then rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, note which ones a committed defender should concede genuinely count against the position and which ones should be dismissed as changing the subject, and end with an honest judgment about whether the asymmetry has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that is a defect in a principle of this kind. Do not offer citations or a reading list; I want the reasoning, not the literature review.
