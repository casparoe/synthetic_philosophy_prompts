I have no academic training in this, but I have been thinking on my own about Karl Popper's critique of historicism — the view that history develops according to laws that would in principle allow us to predict the future. I have collected what seem to me to be his main arguments against the existence of such laws of history, and I will restate them in my own words so that it is clear what my list contains.

1. Any law of historical development would have to account for the future growth of human knowledge, because new knowledge changes both what people can do and what they aim at. But the content of future discoveries cannot be predicted now, since predicting a discovery would amount to making it now. A major influence on the historical process is therefore exactly the thing that cannot be predicted.

2. Even setting that problem aside, a public prediction about society can feed back into the very process it predicts. Popper called this the Oedipus effect. A widely believed forecast of a revolution or an economic collapse can change people's behaviour so as to bring about, reshape, or prevent the forecast event, so social prediction is not a neutral measurement of an independent process in the way a prediction of an eclipse is.

3. The large-scale regularities that historicists point to are typically trends, and a trend is not a law. A trend holds only while certain background conditions hold; when those conditions change, the trend can bend or reverse. A law states a universal relation holding under specified conditions, whereas a trend is a statement about a particular stretch of history.

4. Historicism characteristically reasons about society as a whole, but such wholes are not something one can isolate and test. One can always select different aspects and tell a different story, with the result that historicist theories can be made to fit almost any course of events after the fact and are not testable in the manner of scientific laws.

Beyond these four, I have tried to construct a fifth argument of my own. A supposed law of history, once accepted and acted on, would itself become part of history; a complete law would therefore have to include an account of the effects of its own acceptance, and then of the effects of that inclusion, and so on without end. But I suspect this may just be the Oedipus effect in different words, and I would want that suspicion confirmed or refuted.

What I am looking for is one further argument against the existence of laws of history that is not one of the four listed and not a restatement, special case, or combination of them. If my fifth candidate is indeed only a restatement of the second, say so, but do not let that discharge the main task: I still want an argument with a different structure. If you think no such argument exists and the list really is exhaustive, defend that judgment. If you do give one, explain briefly how it differs from each item on the list, and give an honest assessment of whether the argument is genuinely weighty or merely suggestive.
