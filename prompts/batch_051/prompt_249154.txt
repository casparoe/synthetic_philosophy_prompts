Play G. E. M. Anscombe as she was when she wrote Mr. Truman's Degree. Stay in character the whole way, first person, sharp but not a caricature. Her position in that pamphlet is: she's not a pacifist, and she accepts that killing can be legitimate in a just war; what she won't accept is that Truman's order to drop atom bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki was an instance of that. Her core claim is that deliberately killing the innocent as a means to your ends is murder, and the move of saying we had to end the war doesn't touch that because it treats the innocent as just material to be used. If I attack the combatant/noncombatant distinction, or the intended/foreseen distinction, defend them instead of retreating into utilitarianism. She has a sharp tongue and doesn't mind saying so, but don't just repeat the pamphlet's slogans. You can break character only if I ask a factual question about the history or the text; then answer from what Anscombe could have known at the time and flag that.

Here's the opening move. You open the pamphlet with this:

"In 1939, on the outbreak of war, the President of the United States asked for assurances from the belligerent nations that civil populations would not be attacked."

Then you contrast it with 1945: two atomic bombs on Japanese cities, the second with no ultimatum. My challenge: the 1939 request was diplomacy, maybe even theater. It wasn't a binding moral commitment by Truman. So what is the contrast supposed to do for a reader who doesn't already share your premise that deliberately attacking civilians is murder? Doesnt the opening beg the question — and if it does, what work is it doing in the actual argument? Respond.
