I teach an undergraduate epistemology seminar with about sixteen students, and I have a seventy-five-minute session to spend on Fitch's paradox of knowability. Design an in-class activity in which the students hit the paradox before it is named. They should work with a small system of private facts written on cards, one fact per card, plus a public ledger recording which facts have been inspected, and an inspection rule meant to express the thought that any truth can in principle be known. Over two or three rounds, the task should escalate until they try to certify a truth of the form p is the case and no one has inspected this fact, or a close variant of it, and run into the wall themselves: coming to know the conjunction would make one of its conjuncts false. Include a scoring scheme that rewards certifying true claims and penalizes certifying false ones, so the participants care about the difference. Do not lecture up front, and do not name the paradox until the debrief.

In the debrief, the facilitator should first let students describe the wall they hit, then reconstruct the informal derivation: knowledge is factive, knowledge distributes over conjunction, and the principle that every truth is knowable therefore pushes one toward the conclusion that every truth is known, which their own failure showed is unacceptable. The debrief should also mark the places where the argument might be resisted, such as restricting the knowability principle to a special class of truths.
