Since I joined my city's planning commission and, around the same time, began discussing with my partner whether to have a third child, I have had to make explicit what I actually think about whether a larger population—more children, more residents, more future people—is a better thing. A colleague asked me to write down the commitments I rely on in those two settings so she could check whether they hang together. I would like the same check done here. The set, as it stands, is this:

1. Comparing communities. When I compare two towns or neighborhoods, I compare how well the residents are doing, not how many residents there are. I do not think a city of a million is a better city than a city of a hundred thousand simply in virtue of being larger; average quality of life, and how the worst-off fare, are what move me.

2. Extinction. When I imagine a species—human or otherwise—going extinct, however, the difference between a future containing many worthwhile lives and no future at all seems enormous, and it does not reduce to anyone's average going up. I count the loss of all possible future lives as a catastrophe even though no currently living individual is killed by it.

3. Extra children. I think a couple who want another child and could care for it well choose well, but I do not think there is any obligation to have another child merely because the child would have a life worth living; nor do I think a family of six is morally better than a family of five just because it contains one more happy member.

4. Density tradeoffs. I believe a city can be improved by adding many residents and becoming somewhat more crowded and less comfortable, if the newcomers go from bad lives to clearly good lives and the current residents' losses are small, even though average well-being falls. I do not know where the boundary is, but the fall in the average does not by itself decide the question for me.

5. Aggregation in charity. When I choose among charities, I routinely add up individual benefits and select the intervention with the largest total, without discounting benefits according to recipients' average. I do not think this is merely a practical shortcut; I think the total is what ultimately matters when asking which policy does the most good.

6. Municipal decisions. Yet when the planning commission votes, I treat "it will add five thousand happy residents without reducing anyone else's welfare" as no reason at all to approve a project, while "it will make current residents' lives better" is a real reason. The explanation I give myself is that the commission feels like stewardship of an existing community rather than an enterprise for manufacturing a larger one.

I do not know whether these six commitments hang together, and I would rather not be told simply that one of them is false. I want to understand exactly which parts pull against which other parts. Please audit the set. For each pair or larger cluster in tension, say how severe the tension is and what would have to be revised to resolve it, and identify any apparent tensions that dissolve on inspection and why they dissolve. Then identify the largest unresolved tension and say which single revision of one commitment would do the most to make the set coherent. Please keep clear what is settled in this area and what is genuinely contested among people who know it, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. At the very end, write \answer{...} containing the number of the single commitment whose revision you would recommend first, or the word none if you judge that no revision is needed.
