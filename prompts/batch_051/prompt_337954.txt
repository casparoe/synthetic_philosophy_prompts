I've spent the past few weeks reporting on a corporate manslaughter prosecution for a long-form piece, and the case keeps reducing to the same impasse. The defense argues that a company cannot have a guilty mind: there is no corporate skull to point to, no single brain that formed the intent, so talk of a company "deciding" to cut corners is metaphor, not moral fact. The prosecution argues that the firm's budget allocations, its safety procedures, its promotion incentives, and its audit failures together amounted to a decision to let the risk fall where it did, even though no individual ever sat down and said the incriminating thing out loud. I do not want to adjudicate this one case. I want the prior thing: the specification that any adequate theory of corporate moral agency would have to meet before one can even argue about whether a company, as distinct from its managers, can be blamed.

Write me that specification. What must a theory of corporate moral agency—a theory about whether a company can be a proper object of moral blame, resentment, or punishment, and not merely a legal defendant or a locus of causal responsibility—get right? Give the list of requirements with the motivating case or consideration behind each. Your list should at least handle the following: the difference between a corporation and a crowd, a market, or a collection of coincidentally cooperating individuals; the problem of distributed knowledge and intention, where no single employee knows the whole plan but the organization behaves as if it does; the relation between blaming the company and blaming individual employees, and whether one crowds out the other; whether blame requires capacities such as guilt, remorse, or the ability to understand a norm, and what that implies for a fictional person; whether the entity survives turnover, restructuring, or acquisition in a way that makes punishing "the same" agent intelligible; and the gap between being causally implicated, legally liable, and morally blameworthy. I would also like you to mark each requirement as negotiable or non-negotiable, and to say which single requirement would do the most to unseat the most popular positions. Spend your effort on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on a scorecard of named theories.

End with a one-sentence summary as the very last line, beginning with "Summary:" and stating the single most load-bearing requirement, with nothing after it.
