I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science and the graduate-student member of the methods review panel of a small private foundation that funds theoretical research. The panel is revising its review criteria, and we are stuck on one question: how much weight should be given to a proposal whose central promise is to explain a body of already-known results or a long-standing anomaly, as opposed to a proposal that commits to deriving novel, testable predictions? One faction says post-hoc explanation should be heavily discounted because Bayesian confirmation theory cannot even represent already-known evidence as confirming a new hypothesis: the evidence has probability one, so the likelihoods are trivially one and the hypothesis's probability cannot shift. The other faction replies that this would penalize the way some major advances were initially defended, and that standard moves (subtracting the evidence from the background before evaluating the hypothesis, or treating the confirmatory event as the discovery that the hypothesis entails the evidence) recover old evidence as evidence. The compromise under consideration is to permit such projects only under a condition: within an initial phase the investigators must derive at least one distinctive prediction that the leading rival hypothesis would not also entail, with failure to produce that prediction treated as a go/no-go checkpoint. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend a concrete policy, explain why that policy follows without pretending the Bayesian problem of old evidence dissolves, give reviewers criteria they can actually apply, and represent the strongest consideration on the losing side fairly. I do not need citations or a bibliography; referring to positions and arguments by name is fine, and I will add references myself. Please write in American English.
