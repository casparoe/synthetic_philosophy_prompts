I am a retired geochemist, and after a career spent sorting minerals into categories, I have lately been reading the American pragmatists, particularly Peirce and James on truth. I have been trying to use a case I followed when it was in the news as a test of their views: the 2006 reclassification of Pluto by the International Astronomical Union. My recollection of the episode is as follows. Pluto had been treated as the ninth planet from its discovery in 1930, but by the early 2000s astronomers had found a number of other sizable bodies in the outer solar system, Eris among them, and in 2006 the International Astronomical Union adopted a definition of planet requiring a body to orbit the Sun, to be massive enough for its own gravity to pull it into a roughly spherical shape, and to have cleared the neighborhood around its orbit. Pluto meets the first two conditions but not the third, so it was placed in the new category of dwarf planet. The decision was taken by a vote of the members present at the meeting, and it remains contentious: many planetary scientists favor a geophysical definition keyed to roundness and internal geology, many dynamical astronomers emphasize orbit-clearing, and a fair amount of the public simply rejects the demotion.

As I understand him, Peirce held that a true belief is one that the community of inquirers would agree upon in the long run if inquiry were carried far enough; truth is not settled by any present vote, but neither is it independent of what inquiry would eventually converge on. James, by contrast, treated truth as something that proves itself in experience by working, by being useful or expedient to believe, and he held that a new truth has to be fitted onto the old stock of beliefs with the least possible disturbance.

I would like you to analyze the Pluto episode through these two views. What would each view have said the astronomers were faced with, and how should each have proceeded? Which features of the episode does each explain well — the discovery of the trans-Neptunian bodies, the vote itself, the split between dynamical and geophysical definitions, the public backlash — and where does each misdescribe the case or fall silent? Finally, what does the episode show about the two theories: does it confirm one, expose a weakness in each, or present a kind of case neither was designed to handle? I want a disciplined mapping rather than a general essay — who the relevant parties and options were, what counted as evidence, and where each view has nothing to say. I am not after a bibliography or a retelling of the astronomy; I want the two lenses aimed at one episode, and the episode aimed back at the lenses.
