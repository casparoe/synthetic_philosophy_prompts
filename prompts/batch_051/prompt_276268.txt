Since my father died last spring I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about whether death is bad for the person who dies, and I would be grateful for a consistency check on the position I have written down for myself. The commitments, as I have formulated them, are these:

1. The deprivation account is correct: death is bad for the person who dies exactly when, and because, it deprives them of a life they would otherwise have had and that would have been good for them.
2. A harm must be had by someone at some time. If something is bad for a person, there has to be a time at which it is bad for them, and a subject who exists at that time.
3. There is nothing it is like to be dead. Being dead is not an unpleasant experience or a negative state, and I do not ascribe any badness to nonexistence itself.
4. It can be rational to fear or dread my own death, and not merely the process of dying or the impact on other people.
5. My not having existed before I was born is not bad for me, and I do not think this is in conflict with the deprivation account.
6. Nothing that happens after I am dead can harm me. Whatever is bad about my death must be located in the life I lose, not in any posthumous situation.

I cannot see where this set pulls apart, if it does, and I do not want to be told simply which of these claims the Epicurean or the anti-Epicurean side would reject. I want an audit: which commitments are genuinely in tension with one another, which only appear to be, and how severe each genuine tension is. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost, and then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the set hang together.
