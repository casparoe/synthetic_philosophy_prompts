For a government class, I am trying to work out when it is fair to hold someone to a rule they did not know existed. My reactions to particular cases pull in different directions, and I would like help finding the principle behind them, if there is one. Here are four cases. (1) A tourist is fined for drinking from a can on a train platform; the ban is posted only in the local language, which she cannot read, and there was no other way for her to learn it. I think the fine is unfair. (2) A homeowner builds a fence two inches over the height limit without checking the local code, though the code is on the town website and at the municipal office. I think fining him is fair. (3) A man is prosecuted under a criminal statute that, at the time of his action, had been passed but not yet published or announced anywhere. I think that is also unfair. (4) A driver misses a temporary sign changing the right of way at a confusing interchange and violates the new rule; the sign was visible, but the rain was heavy and the traffic was dense. I lean toward saying the ticket is fair, but I am not confident. My tentative principle is that enforcement against someone who did not know a rule is fair only when that person could have learned the rule with an amount of effort it was reasonable to expect in their situation. But I do not know how to apply "reasonable effort" so that it explains my verdicts, and I especially do not know whether (2) and (4) should come apart from (3). What principle best captures my verdicts? If no single principle captures all four, which verdict should I revise, and why? Please state the principle precisely, test it on these cases, and tell me how it would handle a fifth case: a small-business owner who violates an obscure labeling requirement after relying in good faith on an outdated compliance guide.
