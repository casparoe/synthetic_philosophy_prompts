I’m a PhD student in political theory doing work on drug policy, and I keep getting stuck on an asymmetry I don’t think people notice clearly enough. In On Liberty, chapter V, Mill runs through two examples that seem to pull in opposite directions: the sale of poisons, like arsenic, and the British prohibition on importing opium into China. As I read him, he thinks the state may require a poison seller to keep a register of who buys arsenic, because that’s a precaution against future murder. But he treats the opium prohibition as the clearly bad case, because the point there is to stop the buyer from consuming the substance itself—to make the commodity hard to obtain for the buyer’s own good.

Now compare actual drug policy. A liquor store owner in my city is a respectable small-business person, even if half the neighbourhood knows a particular customer is drinking herself to death. A person who sells heroin or fentanyl is a criminal, often seen as morally responsible for the buyer’s overdose, even when the buyer is a willing, informed adult. Alcohol and tobacco are legal, with age checks, taxes, warning labels, and licences; heroin and fentanyl are banned outright. Cannabis sits somewhere in the middle, depending on jurisdiction. So the legal and moral asymmetry isn’t between “poison used to kill someone” and “drug used for pleasure”—it’s between intoxicants that are culturally embedded and intoxicants that aren’t.

I’m not asking for a policy proposal exactly. I’m asking whether there is a principled Millian or post-Millian justification for this asymmetry, or whether it’s just cultural inertia plus a guess about which harms count. Which features of a drug—acute toxicity, addictiveness, consent-undermining effects, crime externalities, health-system costs, the mere fact that one is more common than the other—could actually justify treating alcohol and heroin differently under something like the harm principle? And which current justifications collapse under pressure? I want the strongest defence you can give of the asymmetry, and then an honest verdict on whether it should be kept, reversed, or given up. If you think Mill’s distinction between preventing harm to others and protecting people from themselves can’t handle this case, say so, and tell me what principle is doing the real work in people’s judgments.
