I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I've been going around in circles about something that came up while arguing with friends about COVID border controls and lockdowns. In the first months of the pandemic, democratic governments closed borders almost overnight, told people to stay home, shut down restaurants and gyms, and imposed curfews, often with minimal public debate and on the basis of epidemiological models that everyone admitted were uncertain. Most people I know, including me, treated these as not just legal but legitimate: the state was right to do them, and we owed the measures a certain degree of cooperation and acceptance. Now imagine the same government, in a normal year, proposing to close restaurants and gyms for three months, require a permit to leave home, and keep out travelers from countries with high road-traffic death rates, all to save a comparable number of lives. We would call that authoritarian—legal only in a thin procedural sense, but obviously illegitimate, something we would resist even if the numbers worked out. The only clear difference is that one case is declared an emergency, with it's attendant urgency and uncertainty. So my question is whether "it's an emergency" can carry that much normative weight, or whether the difference between the two cases is mostly a psychological fact about how fear changes what we tolerate.

It gets messier when I look at how we evaluate these measures afterwards. A lockdown that plausibly saved many lives tends to be treated as having been the right call even when the government skipped normal procedures or leaned on models that later turned out to be questionable, while a lockdown that was too broad or poorly targeted is treated as a scandal. Ordinary state action doesn't work that way: we don't say an illegal police search was a legitimate act because it happened to find the criminal, and we don't call a wrong verdict right in hindsight because it worked out well. So the deeper asymmetry is between our usual ex ante, procedure-focused standard of legitimacy and the retrospective, outcome-focused standard we seem to reach for with emergency measures. Is that shift ever justified, or is it a rationalization?

I want you to act as a philosopher working on emergency powers and political legitimacy, and give me a committed answer. Don't lay out all the options—take a definite position on whether the asymmetry is justified, by what principle (if any), and whether we should keep it, reverse it, or give it up. Start by brainstorming the candidate justifications for treating emergencies differently, then defend the strongest one, and be explicit about its limits or where it gives out. And please don't tell me I need to distinguish legality from legitimacy as if that settles it—I'm asking whether the legitimacy judgment itself should track the emergency status or the outcomes, and why.
