I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about the spread of non-disclosure agreements in municipal settlements. In an interview, a county administrator gave me a rationale I keep hearing: a government is best understood as a large service provider and should be run like a business. A business that is sued settles quietly to protect its methods, its reputation, and its negotiating position, and the savings get passed on to customers; a government non-disclosure agreement in, say, a police-misconduct settlement or a toxic-exposure suit is the same instrument serving the same purpose. The public, on this view, is better off, and critics who demand disclosure are behaving like rival firms asking to see the books. The critics I interviewed call this a category error: the public is not a competitor, they say; it is the principal on whose behalf the government acts.

For the piece, I want to structure the analysis around an audit of that analogy rather than a tour of the case law. Take the analogy that a government is like a business and that an NDA in a government settlement is like a company's confidential settlement. Show me how far it holds by doing the following. First, build the correspondence table explicitly: what in the business domain plays the role of the owners, the managers, the customers, the competitors, the trade secrets, the brand, and the bottom line in the government domain, and where each cell gets strained. Second, separate the disanalogies that are merely decorative from the ones that do real work for this argument. Third, identify what the analogy quietly imports into government: for instance, that secrecy creates value rather than merely hides liability, or that a request for disclosure comes from an outsider rather than from a principal inspecting its agent. Fourth, say whether the administrator's conclusion survives the audit — namely, that settlement NDAs are justified because they are sound business practice that leaves taxpayers better off. If it does not survive, say what the analogy actually establishes once the disanalogies are on the table. Finally, if a better analogy would do more honest work, propose it and say what it would clarify.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — your overall verdict on how far the analogy holds — and then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of the question.
