Write a villanelle in the usual nineteen-line form, from the point of view of someone who has just been accused of deception after answering a question with a carefully chosen, literally true sentence. The poem should stage the question of whether misleading is morally different from lying, without ever naming that distinction. Give the speaker a concrete interlocutor and a single episode, so the intervening lines have something particular to hold onto. The two refrains should carry the philosophical weight: compose one refrain that insists on the literal truth of everything the speaker asserted, and another that gives voice to the false belief the hearer was led to hold, with the two refrains rhyming with each other as the villanelle requires. The form should not be decorative. Let the same true utterance recur in changing contexts across the tercets, gathering implications it never states, until the two refrains stand side by side in the final couplet as two descriptions of one episode that the poem refuses to adjudicate. Do not use the words lie, lying, mislead, misleading, implicate, assert, assertion, or any other term of art from the philosophy of language or the ethics of deception; the distinction should be enacted by what is said and what is left to be inferred. A specialist in the ethics of deception should recognize the poem as staging the debate between treating lying and misleading as morally equivalent and treating them as distinct wrongs, while a reader who has never encountered that debate should still find it a coherent and emotionally precise poem. Present only the poem, with no title, no epigraph, no commentary before or after, no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic type, and no tables; use plain text with line breaks and stanza breaks only, as one would send a poem in the body of an email.
