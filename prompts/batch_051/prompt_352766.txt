Simon Weil argues in the essay that we would translate as On the Abolition of All Political Parties that a political party is not a mere association of citizens who share a political outlook, but an organization constituted to exert collective pressure, and that this makes parties structurally incapable of serving truth or justice. She writes:

Nearly everywhere — often even where theory denies it — the party is in fact a machine for generating collective passions. And nearly everywhere — often even where theory denies it — the party brings pressure to bear on the minds of its members with a view to a collective form of thought.

Please work through whether her core claim here rests on an overly narrow definition of a political party, or whether it identifies something that any would-be party inevitably becomes. In particular: if a group explicitly organized around open inquiry and refusal of party discipline called itself a party, would Weil have to call it a party and condemn it, or does her argument show that such a group would either be a party in a different sense or would be institutionally unstable? Do not just report her views; say which reading of the argument does the most philosophical work and whether the conclusion survives the strongest version of that reading.
