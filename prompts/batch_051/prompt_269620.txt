Please explain a conceptual issue about how mathematical definitions are chosen, using topology as your main case. The textbook definition of a topological space takes open sets as primitive, subject only to closure under finite intersections and arbitrary unions, and calls a map continuous just when the preimage of every open set is open. But the same class of spaces can be axiomatized through a system of neighborhoods at each point, through a closure operator on subsets satisfying axioms like monotonicity and idempotence, or through convergence of filters or nets. Because these formulations are intertranslatable, the preference for open sets cannot be a matter of expressive power; whatever makes open sets the standard choice must be doing different work. I want a substantive account of what that work is. In particular: (i) which mathematical desiderata favor the open-set formulation — the economy of the two closure conditions, the fact that continuity is naturally a condition on inverse images rather than images, the way arbitrary unions let compactness arguments pass from an arbitrary open cover to a finite subcover, and how the axioms behave under products, subspaces, and quotients; (ii) the respects in which one of the rival formulations is actually the better tool, for instance because neighborhoods, closure, or convergence are closer to the original intuitions about nearness and limits, or because a definition or theorem becomes more natural in one of those languages; and (iii) what this shows about how mathematicians pick one of several equivalent definitions as the right notion when the choice cannot be justified extensionally. Is the standard tracking the category or structure one wants to study, the proofs one wants to make short, the concepts that generalize best when the equivalents come apart, or something else? Make the tradeoffs concrete rather than simply noting that the formulations are equivalent.
