Ok so I keep coming back to this thing about arbitration clauses and whether they're actually courts, and I'm not sure if my criteria are the problem or the arbitration is. My working criteria for something counting as a court doing justice, not just a dispute-resolution vending machine: (1) the decision-maker is genuinely independent — not paid by one side, not picked from a list in a way that repeat players can game, and not dependent on one side for future work. (2) The proceedings are public at least in the sense that the reasons get written down and the law gets clarified over time; opinions are published or at least citable, and the public can see how the rules are being applied. (3) There's real review for legal error — if the judge gets the law wrong, someone above can fix it. (4) The forum doesn't systematically tilt against the weaker party: discovery, evidence rules, and class procedures don't let the stronger side hide documents or peel off claims one by one.

Now the case. In the U.S. today, a huge share of consumer and employment contracts have a pre-dispute arbitration clause: you can't have the job or the phone unless you agree that every dispute goes to binding arbitration, and class actions are waived. The arbitrator is chosen from a roster, with each side striking names; the company is a repeat player and you're a one-shot. The hearing is private. There's no transcript unless somebody pays, the award can be a sentence or two with no findings or reasoning, and courts set it aside only for the narrow stuff — fraud, partiality, the arbitrator exceeding their powers — not for legal error. But once issued, the award is binding and the state will enforce it. It still looks adverserial on the surface: both sides present evidence, call witnesses, cross-examine, and a neutral-ish third party decides. Defenders say it's faster, cheaper, and closer to what most people can actually access than a two-year lawsuit, and that the anger is really about losing the class action, not about unfairness inside the hearing.

So: is this a court doing justice, or a privitized, stripped-down imitation? Work through my four criteria one by one and tell me where it passes, where it fails, and where the criteria just don't bite (maybe privacy matters to the public's interest in law but not to the parties' dispute). Then give a verdict: does the case qualify, or do my criteria need changing — maybe adding something like genuine consent or a real exit option, or dropping publicity on the grounds that I was smuggling in a theory of lawmaking, not justice? And whatever you conclude, say what would change your mind and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
