I'm drafting a one-page teaching statement for my department's seminar on undergraduate mathematics, and the organizer has asked each participant to set out the commitments behind their position and then submit them to a consistency audit before presenting. The seminar has spent the term on the place of motivation and rigor in the standard sequence, with one colleague arguing that a student never really knows a theorem until they can prove it in the cleanest possible way, and another insisting that a definition or proof handed down before the problem that gives it a point teaches compliance rather than understanding. I find I agree with parts of both, and after several rounds of revision I have ended up with the following set of commitments:

1. Proof is the sole warrant: nothing except a valid derivation justifies a mathematical claim; intuition, diagrams, and worked examples may suggest or illuminate, but they never establish.
2. Certification and explanation are not the same thing: two proofs can both establish a theorem while only one shows why it holds, and an explanatory proof is a distinct good that a student should learn to recognize and produce.
3. Explanatoriness is an objective, formal feature of a proof. If one proof explains better than another, the difference must be stateable in mathematical terms — shared structure, fruitfulness, unity with neighboring results — not in terms of a reader's feeling of illumination.
4. The correct pedagogical order is motivation first. A definition or theorem introduced before the problem that makes it feel necessary leaves most students unable to see why anyone would draw that line or give that name, and that failure is a real epistemic loss, not a matter of taste.
5. For advanced courses, the logical order — definition, theorem, proof — is nevertheless the right one; motivational or historical narrative is a concession that often misleads because it imports ideas the formal development has not yet licensed.

Please audit the set rather than lecture me on it. Go through it and say which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension and which only look as though they are, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each tension say what would have to give and at what cost. End with the single revision that would clean up the most. Keep the response to at most 800 words.
