Okay, here's something I've been stuck on, and I'd like your help with it. You can think of yourself as a political philosopher who knows Mill's On Liberty well and who has some experience running adversarial collaborations between people who talk past each other.

I lead a reading group on Mill, and two of the regulars have been arguing for about a year about whether his argument for "experiments in living" actually goes through. One of them is basically a Millian individualist: she thinks society should default strongly toward letting people live differently, partly so we can all learn from the results. The other is much more sympathetic to the idea that inherited customs and settled social practices are where the real wisdom is: for him, a custom is itself a kind of stored experiment that has already survived a lot of testing, and letting everyone re-run it badly has real costs. They've finally agreed to try an adversarial collaboration, and I want you to draft the document they should have produced.

Here is the passage they keep coming back to, from ch. 3, "Of Individuality, as One of the Elements of Well-Being":

"That mankind are not infallible; that their truths, for the most part, are only half-truths; that unity of opinion, unless resulting from the fullest and freest comparison of opposite opinions, is not desirable, and diversity not an evil, but a good, until mankind are much more capable than at present of recognising all sides of the truth, are principles applicable to men’s modes of action, not less than to their opinions."

I picked this one because both of them say they accept it, and then they immediately split on what follows. The Millian reads it as licensing a strong presumption in favor of letting people live differently so that the worth of different modes of life gets tested in practice. The custom-friendly one reads it as much weaker: opinions should be free because that's how false belief gets corrected, but action is different, because a surviving custom already embodies evidence, and the harms of a failed experiment in living don't always stay on the experimenter. They also go back and forth about wether "diversity not an evil" is an epistemic claim or a claim about well-being. I suspect some of the fight is verbal—they mean different things by "experiment"—but not all of it.

So here's what I'd like. Before you draft the final statement, think carefully and brainstorm what the genuine cruxes might be, and sort out which disagreements are merely about words. Then write the actual joint statement they'd both sign:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps accept, about the passage and about experimentation and disagreement in modes of life, phrased so neither side would wince.
2. The exact residual disagreements, each put as a neutral stem plus a version of each camp's view that that camp would accept word-for-word.
3. Three or four cruxes—sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree would bear on it, and state what each side predicts about it in advance.

I suspect one crux is whether a settled custom is itself evidence (like a surviving experiment) or just a frozen arrangement that survived for other reasons; another is how far failed experiments in living actually stay on the experimenter. But don't let me pre-judge it.

Don't adjudicate or tell me who is right—the value is in the document itself. And don't waste space on secondary literature or citations; just work from the passage and the two positions as I've described them. Aim for something around 1200 words for the joint statement, not counting the quoted passage.
