I've lost count of how many times I've seen the prescriptivist/descriptivist fight break out in a comment section over something like singular 'they' or 'could care less'. So here's a prompt in that spirit: suppose a hard-line descriptivist linguist and a genuine prescriptivist (not a strawman — someone who thinks there are real norms of grammar and some usages are simply wrong) agree to sit down and produce an adversarial-collaboration summary on one question: is there such a thing as bad grammar? Draft the document they'd produce.

Use the standard format: a numbered list of claims both can sign, then a table of the points where they geniunely part ways, with each side's view written in wording that side would actually endorse rather than a caricature. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that, if you settled them, at least one side would have to change its mind. For each crux, record what each side predicts in advance, and what each side would count as evidence against its own view.

Press hard on the verbal-disagreement question, because I suspect a lot of this fight is two people using 'grammar' and 'bad' to mean diferent things. Where exactly does the disagreement stop being about words and start being about facts or values? Is the prescriptivist claim better read as 'non-standard forms carry real social costs, and gatekeepers are justified in enforcing a standard' — a claim the descriptivist might roughly accept? And does the descriptivist's 'there's no such thing as bad grammar' shrink, once you taboo 'correct', into something nobody disagrees with? I'd like the document to finish by stating, in precise language, the smallest residual claim each side insists on and refuses to give up.

Don't resolve the debate or declare a winner; just produce the document a good adversarial collaboration would produce.
