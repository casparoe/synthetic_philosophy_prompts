A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about who, exactly, is the "we" in democratic self-government — the problem of how a demos gets its boundaries. By the end he had laid out an argument that he says proves too much for any of the standard ways of handling the question. I find the conclusion unacceptable, but every step looked plausible as he moved through it, and I cannot locate the slide. I would like you to work through the argument and tell me which step should be rejected, and what rejecting it costs. The argument, as I reconstruct it:

(1) A political decision is legitimate only if it is made in a way that includes, or is at least answerable to, all the people over whom it exercises authority.

(2) Decisions about a political community's boundaries — who is a citizen, who may enter, who may be excluded, who has a vote — are political decisions that exercise authority over more than the current insiders: they determine the life prospects of would-be entrants, the standing of noncitizens already inside the territory, and the future composition of the community itself.

(3) Therefore, by (1), a decision about boundaries is legitimate only if all those over whom it exercises authority can participate in making it, or can at least hold the makers to account.

(4) But the class of people over whom a boundary decision exercises authority cannot be specified without already drawing a boundary: whether a person falls within that class depends on whether they are inside or outside the very distinction the decision is supposed to establish.

(5) So a boundary decision presupposes exactly what it must settle. Any procedure for constituting the demos already requires an answer to the question of who belongs to the demos, so the justification is circular.

(6) Since no existing state has constituted its boundaries through a procedure that includes everyone over whom those boundaries exercise authority, it follows that no existing state has legitimate boundaries — at least not under (1).

(7) Yet (1), or something close to it, is also the principle I reach for when I say that it is wrong to gerrymander people out of the franchise, or to make laws that govern people while denying them any say. If giving up (1) means accepting that a state may draw any line it pleases and that the excluded have no standing to complain, then I would rather keep (1).

(8) But if (1) stands, then the argument seems to force the conclusion that no demos can have a legitimate boundary: either the boundary is drawn without the participation of everyone it determines, or it includes everyone it determines — at which point there is no boundary left to draw.

My friend says the weak point is probably (1), and that I should abandon it entirely. I am not willing to do that without knowing exactly what I would be paying. Please tell me where the argument goes wrong, if it does, or which premise I must surrender and what the least costly version of that surrender looks like. If a premise has to go, explain why it looked so natural and what the main alternatives would have to give up in its place.
