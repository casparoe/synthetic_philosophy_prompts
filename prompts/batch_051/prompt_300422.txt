I sit on my county's land-use and conservation commission. I have no academic background, but for a few years I have been thinking on my own about a question that keeps surfacing in our decisions: whether destroying wild habitat could ever reduce the total amount of suffering in the world, and what that does to the ordinary conservation reflex that treats an abundance of wild lives as an obvious good.

The case before us is a developer's application to drain and fill a roughly forty-acre seasonal wetland and build a light-industrial site on it. The staff report says no protected species depend on the wetland, the hydrology is ordinary, and the plan meets code. It also says the site supports a dense population of frogs, small fish, rodents, and marsh-nesting birds with extremely high early mortality: most of the young die within days or weeks, from starvation, exposure, disease, or predation, and only a small fraction reach adulthood.

One commissioner has been pressing an argument I cannot dismiss. She reasons that habitat like this produces enormous numbers of short, painful lives, and that preserving it does not obviously make the world better because most of the lives it creates go badly almost immediately. On her account, draining the wetland would stop the creation of those lives, and that loss should count as a reduction in suffering — a point in favor of the application, not against it. Another commissioner says this is an abuse of our role: a planning commission should protect habitat, not do welfare arithmetic on wild animals.

I need you to write the short formal opinion I will circulate to the other commissioners before we meet on Thursday. It should recommend that we approve the application, reject it, or approve it with conditions, and it should engage the wild-animal-welfare argument on its own terms rather than merely setting it aside. If you propose conditions, make them concrete and include any time frame. The document should be something the full commission could act on, and it should state honestly the strongest consideration on the side you recommend against. Then, in a brief note to me that is not part of the opinion, tell me how confident you are in your recommendation and which specific point in your reasoning you are least sure about.
