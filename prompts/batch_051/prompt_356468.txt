I've been going in circles on the old Letter Spirit puzzle about what makes every 'a' an 'a'. Consider a Helvetica 'a', a rushed handwritten 'a' with the loop left open, and one of those blackletter 'a's that looks to me more like a two-headed fish than a letter. There's no shared geometric feature across all of them: sometimes two stories, sometimes one, sometimes a closed counter, sometimes not, sometimes a terminal, sometimes not. Yet people effortlessly sort them all as a's. A designer friend keeps telling me this is just learned exemplars plus a bit of family resemblance and that I'm being mystical; I find myself half-believing the stronger claim, that a letter's identity doesn't live in a list of features at all but in something like a generative style or an abstract 'spirit' — the way a whole family of shapes hangs together, so a new shape can be judged as more or less 'a-like' even if it shares no measured feature with any existing a.

I'm trying to figure out whether that's a testable claim or just a nice way of talking. If someone insisted "letter identity is a matter of generative style, not a feature list," how would you test it? I want a concrete experiment or measurement, not a vibe: say what you'd vary, what you'd collect, and what pattern of results would count for the style view versus the feature-list view. Then be honest about the residue — what would a positive result actually establish, and what part of the claim would stay untested no matter how clean the data come out? I'm less interested in the obvious move (run yet another font classification study) than in a design that could actually seperate those two pictures.
