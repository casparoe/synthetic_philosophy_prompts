In 2024, the geological body responsible for reviewing the proposal voted not to recognize the Anthropocene as a formal epoch of the geological time scale. The proposal had rested on the standard case for formalization: that the mid-twentieth-century Great Acceleration left a durable, roughly isochronous signature in sediments, ice, and biota, including plutonium fallout, fly ash, concrete, plastics, and the fossil record of industrial livestock, and that a boundary placed around the middle of the twentieth century could satisfy the stratigraphers' own criteria. Some geologists have responded to the rejection by arguing that the Anthropocene is better treated not as an epoch with a sharp lower boundary but as a drawn-out event or ongoing transformation. Assume for the purposes of this question that the rejection stands.

Does the broader case for treating the Anthropocene as a genuinely new historical period survive this development, or does the rejection of its geological formalization undercut the idea that it is a coherent period at all? Identify which premise of the original argument the rejection actually bears on: that a boundary can be drawn, that the mid-twentieth century is the right boundary, or that geology is the tribunal with standing to decide the question.
