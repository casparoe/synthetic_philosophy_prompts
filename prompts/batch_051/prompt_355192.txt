I'm trying to get a handle on Berkeley's master argument and I suspect my reconstruction is subtly wrong. In A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge, he writes: "Prejudices and errors of sense do from all parts discover themselves to our view; and, endeavouring to correct these by reason, we are insensibly drawn into uncouth paradoxes, difficulties, and inconsistencies, which multiply and grow upon us as we advance in speculation, till at length, having wandered through many intricate mazes, we find ourselves just where we were, or, which is worse, sit down in a forlorn Scepticism." So heres my reconstruction of the master argument that he uses later:

(1) Suppose there is a tree that exists completely unperceived by any mind.
(2) In entertaining this supposition, you are conceiving that very tree.
(3) So the tree is present to your mind, i.e., it is perceived by you.
(4) Therefore the tree is not unperceived.
(5) Hence the supposition of an unperceived tree is contradictory.
(6) So nothing can exist unperceived.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step fails? I have a feeling the trick is in (2) or (3), but I cant pinpoint it. Give the strongest reading of the argument on which it's plausible, then say exactly where it goes wrong. At the very end, put your final verdict in \answer{step number or "valid"}.
