I build systems that infer what people want from what they do. The technique is inverse reinforcement learning: rather than hand-coding a reward function, I give the system examples of a person's behavior and it finds the reward function that best explains those examples. The system then plans by maximizing that learned reward. Members of my team routinely say such systems have preferences, and I want to know whether that is literally true.

Here are the criteria I have been using for saying that an entity has a preference for X over Y:

1. Availability. When X and Y are both available and the choice is otherwise unforced, the entity tends to take X.

2. Cost-sensitivity. The entity keeps taking X when X is made moderately more costly than Y, but eventually switches if the cost grows too large. I treat a preference as a rate of exchange, not just a ranking.

3. Consistency. The X-over-Y ordering shows up across many contexts, not only in the specific situations where it was first observed.

4. Ownership. The entity can reveal the preference in some mode that goes beyond the choices you inferred it from: by stating it, by resisting when it is denied the preferred option, or by giving the preference as its own reason.

Now the case. I trained a route planner with inverse reinforcement learning on several years of one driver's GPS traces. The recovered reward assigns a large positive weight to minimizing travel time, a smaller positive weight to avoiding complex intersections, and a near-zero weight to scenic quality. The weights were estimated from the driver, so it is tempting to say they are hers; but the deployed planner is what now acts on them, in cities she has never visited. In a new city, the planner systematically selects fast, left-turn-heavy routes over slower, simpler, more scenic ones. When I add synthetic delays to the fast routes in simulation, it accepts up to an extra 15 percent of travel time before it begins switching to the scenic routes. It reproduces the same trade-off across different neighborhoods and times of day. But inspecting the deployed system, I find only a vector of route-feature weights and a graph-search routine. Nothing in the system states that it wants to get to the destination quickly; it cannot answer a direct question about which routes it prefers; and when I force it to take a slow route in simulation, it shows no sign of dissatisfaction: it simply continues.

Does this route planner literally have a preference for short travel time, in the sense of my four criteria? Work criterion by criterion, and say clearly where the criteria settle the question and where they underdetermine it. Then tell me whether the case shows that the criteria themselves need changing: in particular, whether criterion 4 is doing necessary work, or whether it smuggles in a subjective, first-person notion of wanting that criteria 1 through 3 never required. I am more interested in whether the criteria are right than in the bare yes or no.
