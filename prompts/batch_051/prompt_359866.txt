You are a professional philosopher working in formal epistemology and the theory of rational credence, experienced at translating philosophical distinctions into decision procedures for practitioners without philosophical training. Think carefully about the distinctions you draw before writing the procedure, and mark every place where the procedure must hand off to human judgment.

I direct the underwriting analytics unit at a mid-sized mutual life insurer. Our mortality model assigns each applicant a numeric five-year mortality probability, built from a large population dataset and refreshed quarterly. Underwriters are expected to treat the model's number as authoritative, but they may adjust it when they have additional information. Each underwriter makes dozens of these decisions a day; small, biased overrides compound across a large book of policies, while individual decisions are rarely catastrophic. Every adjustment must be documented, and our auditors increasingly penalize inconsistent departures from the model. The current guidance is effectively "use your judgment," so different underwriters weight the same information in incompatible ways. I want a reusable procedure — a checklist with stop conditions — that an underwriter can run in a few minutes per application.

Ground the procedure in the Principal Principle. I understand it this way: when a rational agent knows that the objective chance of a proposition is x, the agent's credence in that proposition should be x, provided the agent has no "inadmissible" information — where admissible information is, roughly, information whose bearing on the proposition comes entirely through its bearing on what the chance is. The clearest example of inadmissible information is something like a crystal ball or an insider tip that predicts the outcome directly without teaching you anything about the chance. The hard work is operationalizing that distinction, and I want the procedure built around a workable three-way map:

1. Admissible discriminating information — additional facts the chance setup could in principle condition on; these should move the underwriter away from the model's number by way of a conditional probability, not by fiat.
2. Inadmissible or indistinguishable-from-inadmissible information — bare intuition, unauditable tips, subjective impressions, and anything that looks directly at the outcome rather than entering through the chance setup.
3. Undermining information — evidence that the model's probability itself is miscalibrated, stale, or built on the wrong reference class for this applicant.

If this three-way map blurs a distinction the principle actually enforces, say so and fold the repair into the procedure.

Before writing the final checklist, brainstorm three or four edge cases that stress the boundary between those categories, and use them to test the procedure as you design it.

The deliverable should include: the questions an underwriter must ask in order before touching the model's number; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points at which the procedure says "defer to the model exactly," "adjust," or "set the model aside and escalate"; and explicit flags for the cases the procedure cannot settle, where the decision is one of judgment or values rather than facts.

Run the procedure on these three cases, with enough detail that we can use them as training examples:
(a) An applicant with a chronic condition whose medication adherence has improved substantially, where the model was trained before the current treatment protocol became standard.
(b) An applicant for whom a new genetic risk score partially overlaps with variables the model already uses.
(c) An underwriter's strong, sincere conviction — after reading the application file — that this applicant will outlive the model's estimate by a wide margin, with no further stateable evidence.

Be willing to give an answer we will not like where the procedure demands it. Finally, say explicitly what the procedure assumes about the model's outputs — in particular, how close they must be to genuine objective chances for the Principal Principle to apply — and what we would need to change if that assumption fails. I do not need references or a reading list; I need the procedure and the reasoning behind it.
