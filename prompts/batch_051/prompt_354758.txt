As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I sometimes run workshops that introduce a philosophical problem through an exercise rather than a lecture. I am now preparing a 75-minute session for 24 graduate students and early-career journalists at a small ideas festival. I want them to feel the force of a puzzle first posed by Gregory Kavka in his 1983 Analysis article *The Toxin Puzzle*. The case, roughly, is this: a person is offered a very large sum of money if, at midnight tonight, she can genuinely intend to drink a mildly unpleasant but harmless toxin tomorrow afternoon. The money is delivered before she has to act, and the payout depends only on her having the intention at the specified time, not on actually drinking. Most people find that they cannot produce the required intention, because once the money is already secured, they know they will have no reason to drink the toxin. The intended lesson is that an agent can want to intend to do something, and can even believe it would be rational to have the intention, without being able to form the intention itself.

Please design a runnable classroom activity that makes participants encounter this gap between wanting to intend, deciding to intend, and actually intending. The session has 24 people and 75 minutes. The exercise should not rely on real money, but should use tokens, points, or role-based incentives so that participants are rewarded for forming a certain intention or for being credibly disposed to do something while the action itself is optional, postponed, or made unattractive after the reward is secured. The idea should emerge from what the participants try and fail to do, before it is named. Provide the rules, materials, payoff structure, any roles, and a debrief that maps concrete moments in the exercise to Kavka's puzzle. Also explain what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle those failures, and be explicit about which aspect of the original puzzle a group exercise cannot capture and must be supplied by the facilitator.
