Write a dialogue between a defender of the Enlightenment and a critic of it, both historians of philosophy, about the following passage from Immanuel Kant’s “An Answer to the Question: What is Enlightenment?” (1784), in Mary J. Gregor’s translation:

It is because of laziness and cowardice that so great a part of humankind, after nature has long since emancipated them from other people’s direction (naturaliter maiorennes), nevertheless gladly remains minors for life, and that it becomes so easy for others to set themselves up as their guardians. It is so comfortable to be a minor! If I have a book that understands for me, a spiritual advisor who has a conscience for me, a doctor who decides upon a regimen for me, and so forth, I need not trouble myself at all.

The defender argues that this passage shows why the Enlightenment is a source of progress: emancipation is available to everyone, and the main obstacle is a self-incurred failure to think for oneself. The critic argues that the passage exposes the Enlightenment’s dark side: by treating minority as “self-incurred” and blaming “laziness and cowardice,” Kant blames the dominated for their own domination and ignores the material, educational, and institutional conditions that keep people dependent. Let them press this disagreement concretely: Is the critic’s reading fair to the passage? Can Kant’s diagnosis absorb the objection without collapsing into victim-blaming? Does the passage contain any resources for answering the charge that Enlightenment reason serves domination rather than emancipation? Both characters should be equally competent, grant the other’s strongest points where appropriate, and respond to each other’s actual arguments rather than talking past each other.
