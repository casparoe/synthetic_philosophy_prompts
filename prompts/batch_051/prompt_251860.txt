I am taking an upper-level course in comparative social philosophy, and we have spent several weeks on how different traditions respond to conspicuous success: the American Dream, the Scandinavian Jantelagen (the Law of Jante), and the tall poppy syndrome familiar from Australia and New Zealand. The course readings tend to treat Jantelagen and tall poppy syndrome as two names for the same leveling impulse, but I have come to think they are importantly different, and I have been building a framework for a term paper. I would like an honest assessment of it before I commit to the argument.

The framework starts from two permissions that I claim are independent. The first I call aspiration licence: how far a culture permits a person to form and openly pursue the thought that they might become exceptional. The second I call status licence: how far a culture permits a person who has actually become prominent to keep and enjoy that prominence without being taken down. On this reading, Jantelagen is primarily a low aspiration licence. Its rules are addressed to how a person should think of themselves before any distinction has been achieved—roughly, that one should not think oneself special, better than others, or above the common run. Tall poppy syndrome, by contrast, is primarily a low status licence. Ambition is not especially policed and may even be encouraged, but the person who visibly rises above the rest triggers a corrective impulse; the poppy is cut down only after it has grown. The American Dream then scores high on both: everyone is permitted to aspire, and success is celebrated rather than resented, provided it can be told as earned.

This yields a fourth cell, low on both, which I have been calling success-indifference: a culture that neither encourages aspiration nor much resents the successful. My central claim is that the familiar one-dimensional contrast between cultures that celebrate the successful and cultures that cut them down is too crude, because it runs together low aspiration licence and low status licence and thereby predicts that a Jantelagen culture should also be a tall poppy culture. I suspect the opposite: a strong Jantelagen culture should be comparatively gentle toward the person who has become successful quietly, because the relevant violation is the earlier one, the aspiration itself, and it has already been policed.

An example that seems to discriminate between the views: a junior colleague announces, without irony, that they intend to become the best in the field. On a one-dimensional picture this is simply evidence that some leveling norm will activate. On my framework it should draw a Jante-style correction at the moment of the announcement, long before any tall-poppy response becomes available, and the two should look very different in practice.

Is this distinction already in the literature under another name? Are aspiration licence and status licence genuinely two axes, or are they one axis with different timing, or one axis plus some further variable such as how much inequality a culture will tolerate before it reacts? And where does the framework break? I can already see cases that strain it: a person resented as a tall poppy whose actual offense was earlier visible ambition, or a community that speaks the American Dream aloud while enforcing a tall-poppy response in practice. I would like to know which parts of this framework survive those cases and which parts I should abandon.
