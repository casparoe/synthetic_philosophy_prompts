I'm a PhD student in economics preparing for a field exam in decision theory, and I want to work through a variation on a now-standard repeated gamble rather than just rehearse the canonical version. The canonical game runs as follows. A player begins with positive wealth. In each round a fair coin is flipped: on heads the player's entire wealth is multiplied by 1.5, and on tails by 0.6. Because the player is forced to stake everything, wealth after N rounds is the product of N independent draws from {1.5, 0.6}. The expected value of wealth therefore grows by a factor of 1.05 per round, since the arithmetic mean multiplier is 0.5(1.5) + 0.5(0.6) = 1.05. The typical trajectory, by contrast, shrinks: the geometric mean multiplier per round is sqrt(1.5 × 0.6) = sqrt(0.9) ≈ 0.949, so the median wealth after N rounds is about (0.949)^N. The standard lesson drawn is that the ensemble average and the time-average growth of a single realized trajectory can diverge sharply, and that a player facing repeated multiplicative gambles should care about time-average growth. This is usually presented as a fixed all-in position, which bypasses the central question in Kelly betting, namely what fraction of wealth one should commit at all.

Now vary the game in one respect. Before each flip, the player may set aside any fraction of current wealth as cash that the coin leaves untouched; only the remaining fraction f is staked and is multiplied by the same coin outcome as before, where 0 ≤ f ≤ 1 and no borrowing is allowed. A round therefore multiplies wealth by (1 − f) + 1.5f = 1 + 0.5f on heads and by (1 − f) + 0.6f = 1 − 0.4f on tails. Setting f = 1 recovers the canonical forced all-in game, and f = 0 means the player has fully exited the gamble. To keep the problem manageable, assume the player chooses a single fraction f at the outset and holds it fixed for all N rounds.

Please work through how the analysis changes under this variation, in four parts. First, write the expected-wealth growth rate per round and the time-average geometric growth rate per round as explicit functions of f. Second, determine which fraction each criterion selects, and describe what happens at the endpoints f = 0 and f = 1 as well as at the interior optimum. Third, say which of the two lessons of the canonical game survive the variation and which were largely artifacts of the forced all-in stake — in particular, whether the contrast between rising expected wealth and falling typical wealth is a feature of the underlying gamble or of the full-stake assumption, and whether the geometric criterion now changes its verdict on the gamble itself. Fourth, explain whether the time-average growth criterion and expected-utility maximization with logarithmic utility still coincide once the staked fraction is a choice, and how they stand relative to maximizing expected terminal wealth. As you go, please separate the parts that are mathematically settled from the parts that are genuinely contested among people working on this material, and do not present a contested normative claim as if it were the consensus.
