On reflection I keep coming back to the view that the endowment effect is just a bias. The argument seems airtight: in the mug experiments, you hand one person a coffee mug and ask her, ten minutes later, what she'd sell it for; you show the same mug to someone else and ask what he'd pay to own it. Their final holdings are identical in the two cases, so the prices ought to line up, and when they don't, the preference is tracking a description ('mine', 'not mine') instead of a state of the world. Loss aversion looks like the same mistake: in terms of final wealth, losing $100 and failing to gain $100 are the same fact wearing different framing, so why should the loss loom larger?

But I also have an intuition I can't shake. I would genuinely pay more to keep a scuffed-up jacket I've owned for ten years than to acquire the identical jacket online, and that doesn't feel like an error; it feels like the ownership changed what the thing is for me. Same with the family house: the identical house next door is not the same good to me, and I don't just mean that I'd be predictably irrational about it.

I don't want a book report on behavioral economics. I want a negotiated verdict. Tell me what would have to be true for the intuition to be dropped as status quo bias, what would have to be true for the theory to be amended or abandoned, and whether there's a reading on which both survive—say, the mug cases reveal a bias while the jacket and house reveal a legitimate preference. Is there a clean line between those cases, or is 'legitimate preference' just what I call the bias when its mine? Hard cap: 450 words.
