I am rereading Peter Abelard's Ethics, or Know Yourself, and I want to pressure-test its central claim against my own considered verdicts about cases. As I understand the passage early in the work, Abelard maintains that sin is located in the mind's consent to what it takes to be forbidden, not in the outward deed: a person can commit the full sin while doing nothing externally, and a person can perform an outward act without sin if the inner consent is absent. I find this attractive, but when I test it against concrete cases my intuitive verdicts pull against it.

Here are my verdicts. A man decides to poison his business partner and prepares the dose, but before he can administer it the partner dies of a heart attack; I consider him fully guilty of murder in the moral sense, though not a legal murderer. A nurse administers what she reasonably believes is a routine medication, but the patient has an undiagnosed allergy and dies; I consider the nurse not morally guilty, though the death is terrible. A driver who habitually texts while driving kills a pedestrian; I consider the driver morally guilty, but less guilty than the poisoner, even though the pedestrian's death was never something the driver consented to. A hostage is forced at gunpoint to open a safe for robbers; she does it to save the other hostages, never wanting the robbery to succeed; I consider her not guilty of the robbery. A person with florid paranoid delusions sets fire to a house, fully convinced they are destroying a demon that is about to kill their family; they do not believe they are doing anything forbidden, and I lean toward not morally guilty, though uneasily. A person repeatedly entertains vivid adulterous fantasies but firmly refuses ever to act on them and does not communicate them; I consider this a vice, not a wrongdoing. A monk has taken a vow of silence but writes a note to convey gossip; before anyone reads it, he burns the note, and I consider him to have broken the vow.

Acting as an expert philosopher on Abelard and on contemporary theories of moral responsibility, please help me extract the principle behind these verdicts, if there is one. I would like you to start by brainstorming two or three candidate principles that could fit these cases, then state which one you think best captures them, and spell out precisely which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Does my gut reaction to the texting driver already force me beyond Abelard's consent-only picture? If so, what is the least revision to his view that still does everything else I want? Or should I give up one of my verdicts instead? Please think carefully and be explicit about the difference between a principle that merely fits the cases and one that explains them.
