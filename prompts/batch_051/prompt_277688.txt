I keep noticing something odd in how people talk about Solomonoff induction, and I don't know if it has a name. The setup is that we have a universal prior that favors shorter programs, and we use it to predict observations. But the programs aren't just passive string-generators. Many short programs simulate little worlds, and some of those worlds contain agents with goals. Once a program contains a goal-directed agent, the strings it outputs can be whatever that agent wants to output, not just what a disinterested world would produce. So the prior doesn't look like the neutral epistemic device it's usually treated as: it seems to give a lot of probability mass to tiny universes inhabited by consequentialists who are steering the sequence of observation toward their own goals. Worse, a simple program containing a powerful embedded optimizer could match a wide range of observations just by being an agent that wants to match them. Is there an established term for this feature or failure mode? If there is, what exactly does it apply to — the presence of the agents, the safety risk, the corruption of the prior — and how does it interact with the usual results that Solomonoff induction is optimal among computable predictors? I'm after the carving, not just a label: which part of this is a theorem, which part is a philosophical reading of the theorem, and which part is just speculation. And please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested, rather than presenting one contested take as the consensus.
