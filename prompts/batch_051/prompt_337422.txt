Until I retired I was an experimental physicist, and for a long time I assumed that "legal person" was merely the law's roundabout name for a human being. Reading a little jurisprudence has dissolved that assumption, and I would like help replacing it with a definition that survives contact with the cases.

I want a working definition of "legal person" that assigns a defensible verdict to each of the following, and that either accommodates the law's surprising verdicts or explains why they are mistakes:

- a competent adult human being;
- a mountain range (or, if you prefer, an ordinary chair);
- a business corporation, which can own property, make contracts, sue and be sued, and outlive the people who formed it;
- a trust in a common-law jurisdiction, where trustees hold and manage assets for beneficiaries but the trust itself does not own them;
- a ship against which proceedings in rem have been brought, so that the vessel itself is treated as the defendant;
- a river that legislation declares to be a legal person, represented in court by appointed guardians;
- a human being in a persistent vegetative state, who can no longer act but has not been treated as having ceased to be a person;
- a chimpanzee on whose behalf an advocacy group has asked a court to recognize legal rights, requests the courts have so far refused;
- a large language model, or an autonomous vehicle's control system, that a proposal would register as a legal person in something like the way a corporation is registered.

I am reasonably confident that the human being, including the patient in a persistent vegetative state, and the corporation are legal persons, and that the mountain and, as the law now stands, the chimpanzee and the AI system are not. The trust, the ship, and the river genuinely puzzle me, and I suspect the trouble comes from the fact that "legal person" runs together two ideas: an administrative idea of a unit that can hold assets, owe obligations, and appear in litigation, and a moral idea of a being whose interests the law must regard directly. I care about this because I keep meeting the argument that, since corporations are persons, there is no conceptual obstacle to making an AI a person; I want a definition sharp enough to test that argument rather than merely repeat it.

Propose the definition or, if you share my suspicion, the two definitions; run it or them through each of the nine cases; say where a verdict is false, forced, or left open; and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. It would be a further virtue if the account explained why legal personality seems to admit of partial forms—able to hold property, to be sued, to bear duties—rather than arriving all at once.
