In a running argument about how we should respond to disagreement, a colleague has been defending conciliationism with an instrument analogy. She reasons: suppose two scales of the same make, recently calibrated against the same standard, give different readings for the same object. No sensible person would retain full confidence in the first scale and simply dismiss the second; you would revise toward a weighted average, or at least become substantially less confident in either reading. Finding that an epistemic peer — someone you regard as roughly as competent and as well informed as you are on the question — disagrees with you is, she insists, the same situation: you have learned that an equally well-calibrated instrument produced a different output, so the only rational response is large revision toward the peer's view. Audit this analogy. Provide a correspondence table (the scale's mechanism, calibration, the object being measured, measurement error, averaging the readings), then identify where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that threaten the conciliationist conclusion from those that are merely superficial. Finally, say whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some better analogy could carry any part of it.
