Retired geologist here, recently got into philosophy and I keep finding myself stuck on an argument for the Kelly criterion that a quant friend sent me. It feels like there's a shell game somewhere, but I can't locate the pea. Here it is. Suppose you can make repeated even-money bets on a coin that comes up heads 60% of the time, and each round you bet a fixed fraction f of your current wealth. (1) After n rounds your wealth is some constant times (1+f) to the number of heads and (1-f) to the number of tails, so by the law of large numbers the per-round growth rate of your actual wealth converges almost surely to 0.6 log(1+f) + 0.4 log(1-f). (2) That expression is maximized at f = 0.2. (3) So a bettor using f = 0.2 will almost surely end up arbitrarily richer than a bettor using any other fixed fraction; the ratio of their wealths goes to infinity. (4) A rational person cannot knowingly choose a strategy that will almost surely leave her poorer in the long run than an available alternative. (5) Therefore betting any fraction other than Kelly is irrational, full stop—whatever your risk preferences. I can't tell wether it's airtight or doing something sneaky with 'long run' and 'almost sure.' Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and on what reading of that step? And if the argument is valid as far as it goes, what does it actually show about the rational sizing of bets?
