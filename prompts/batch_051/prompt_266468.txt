I have spent more time than I care to admit on rationalist-adjacent forums reading arguments that legal proof is probability under another name, and that the law's refusal to decide cases on bare base rates is a failure of nerve rather than a principled position. The standard hypotheticals are familiar: a plaintiff struck by a bus can show only that the defendant's company operates eighty percent of the buses on the route; a promoter can prove how many people entered a venue without paying but cannot identify which attendees they were. The problem has an even sharper criminal analogue: one might be certain, at the aggregate level, that six hundred of the thousand prisoners in a yard participated in a riot, while being unable to say which six hundred, so that convicting every prisoner on that statistic would make each defendant very likely guilty by the numbers while offending proof beyond a reasonable doubt for any given individual.

I would like you to write a sustained, deadpan satire on the probabilistic-reductionist position in this debate, in the form of a mock law review note of roughly 1,000 words. The piece should adopt the voice of a self-serious reformer from "quantitative jurisprudence," complete with a short abstract and formal section headings, and should carry a title of its own invention in the same register. The satirical target is the view that because all evidence is ultimately probabilistic, the distinction between naked statistical evidence and particularized evidence is a superstitious holdover, and courts should simply impose liability or convict on whichever reference class yields the highest posterior probability. The note should follow that logic, without winking, to conclusions a reader recoils from: reference classes narrowed until they contain exactly one solvent defendant; penalties distributed across a population because we are confident how many of its members offended but not which ones; and due process redescribed as an inefficient taboo on risk aggregation. The piece must find its humor in the content rather than in asides. The familiar objections—reference-class gerrymandering, the difference between "a randomly selected member of this class is likely guilty" and "this particular defendant is likely guilty," and the coexistence of aggregate certainty with reasonable doubt about each accused—should be made vivid by the proposal itself, but never named or argued directly.
