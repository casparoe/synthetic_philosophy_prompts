Take the standard Chesterton's fence scenario: a reformer proposes to remove an old institution, and a conservative replies that the reformer may not remove it until he can state why it was put there in the first place. Now alter one feature of the case. The institution is an old rule that all sides agree imposes a small, continuing cost on a large number of people, but no one can remember its original justification. The only way to recover the justification, if one exists, is an archival search. The search takes a year and has known reliability: if the rule still serves some forgotten function, each search will discover that function with probability 0.8, independently, and if the rule serves no such function, the search always returns no finding. Before any search, a reformer assigns probability 0.6 to the claim that the rule still serves a forgotten function. After three searches in a row return no finding, he proposes repeal, pointing out that the probability that the rule still serves a forgotten function has fallen to barely more than one percent. No one on either side disputes these numbers or the reliability estimates; the only ground offered for keeping the rule is the Chesterton principle itself. The conservative answers that the posterior probability is beside the point: the reformer may not clear away what he does not understand, and a blank archive is not the same as understanding. Assess this standoff. Does the principle, read as a principle, require the reformer to keep searching indefinitely, or does it allow the burden of proof to shift at some point? If it shifts, formulate the principle that determines when; if it does not, explain what the conservative's position leaves a reformer facing an unresolvable archive to do.
