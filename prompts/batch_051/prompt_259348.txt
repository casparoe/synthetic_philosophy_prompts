Putnam's "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" has two moves that people usually run together: the Twin Earth thought experiment, and the later bit about the division of linguistic labor, where he points out that most speakers can't tell an elm from a beech but their word "elm" still refers to elms because they defer to experts. I know that's often treated as a conceptual claim, but it also seems to predict something about actual speakers: that for at least some natural kind terms, people's willingness to keep applying the term is governed less by surface similarity than by what they take relevant experts to say.

How would you test whether ordinary speakers' use of a natural kind term actually works in that deferential way, rather than just tracking appearances? Give me a concrete study design (vignettes, labeling tasks, whatever), say what pattern of results would support the deferential picture versus a similarity-based one, and be explicit about what even a clean result would leave undecided about Putnam's larger externalist claim.
