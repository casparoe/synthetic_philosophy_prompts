I am a senior taking a seminar this semester on democratic legitimacy and the ethics of public office, and in three weeks I have to give a research presentation followed by a question period. My paper argues that the practice of keeping one Cabinet member away from the State of the Union address as a designated survivor is not, as its defenders say, a merely prudent administrative precaution. I argue that the practice is best understood as an unannounced alteration of presidential succession: it gives one unelected person, confirmed for a subordinate office and never vetted for the presidency as such, a realistic path to the presidency, selected by criteria — low public profile, political expendability, and physical survival, rather than governing ability or public authorization — that would be rejected as a basis for choosing a chief executive in almost any other context. The argument, in outline, is that if we assess the practice by the questions we would ask about any other route to executive power — where did the authorization come from, who deliberated, what relationship to citizens does the person have, and did they consent and were they prepared for the office — the practice fares poorly; and that continuity of government, while a real value, does not by itself justify a succession mechanism whose occupant is chosen essentially by the criterion of whose absence will be least noticed. My constructive claim, kept short in the paper, is that continuity could be better secured through a small, publicly disclosed panel of successors selected and prepared in advance for governing, rather than through a single survivor chosen largely in private. The talk is twenty minutes, with twenty minutes of questions afterward.

The audience is small and not easy. The instructor is a democratic theorist who has written critically about executive emergency powers; she is most concerned with authorization, accountability, and any arrangement that replaces public reason with administrative discretion. Two students are in a dual J.D. program. One is a conservative constitutionalist who believes institutional regularity matters more than policy outcomes and tends to defend existing procedures unless the constitutional text clearly rules them out; the other is a left-leaning political economist who thinks the deeper problem is the unrepresentative structure of the executive branch and is likely to call my constructive proposal too modest. There is also an emergency-management practitioner doing an M.A., who has worked on continuity-of-government planning and is likely to think my argument ignores what actual decapitation scenarios look like on the ground.

Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet. I want the six or so objections I am most likely to hear from this specific audience, ordered by likelihood and damage, phrased as those four people would actually put them rather than as a generic opponent would. For each objection, give me a reply that works in about two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front during the talk, which points I should refuse to give away, and the one question I should most dread and why. Finally, tell me the single strongest point in my paper, because I want to steer the discussion back to it if I start losing control of the room.
