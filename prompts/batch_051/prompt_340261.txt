I spent my working life as an experimental physicist, and since retiring I have been reading my way through population ethics in the way that comes naturally to me: I collect case judgments and test which principles survive them. A former colleague, an economist who sat for years on a policy committee on demographic questions, has a track record of verdicts about whether particular population-affecting policies make the world better. I have four of her verdicts and want to know what she should say, if she is to remain consistent with them, about a fifth proposal.

By total well-being I mean the unweighted sum of all persons' lifetime well-being; by average well-being I mean that sum divided by the number of persons. Treat the demographic projections in each case as accurate.

Case 1 — Voluntary child allowance. A fully voluntary monthly child allowance is projected to raise the birth rate by roughly a tenth over two decades. The additional people are expected to have lives about as good as the current average, and no one already alive is made materially worse off. She judged that the policy would leave the world better than it otherwise would be.

Case 2 — Child-survival program. A program to treat pneumonia and malaria in a high-mortality region saves many children who would otherwise die young, and therefore increases the future population. Because services are stretched, average well-being falls slightly over the next half century, but the saved lives are plainly good and total well-being is larger than under the status quo. She judged the program an improvement.

Case 3 — Coercive pronatalism. A housing program makes continued tenancy conditional on bearing a third child. Demographers project that it would add many lives worth living and raise total well-being, but it would do so by overriding many women's considered preferences about childbearing. She judged that the proposal would not make the world better, whatever its aggregate benefits.

Case 4 — Voluntary assisted reproduction in a low-fertility region. A fully voluntary expansion of assisted reproduction is expected to add many lives whose lifetime well-being is somewhat below the current average but still clearly worth having. Total well-being rises, and average well-being falls slightly. She judged the program an improvement.

The new proposal is a fully voluntary assisted-reproduction program in another region. The additional lives it creates are projected to be worth living, but only barely: expected lifetime well-being is clearly above zero, yet far below the level of the lives in Cases 1 and 4. No one already alive would be made worse off, and the program would markedly increase the number of lives, so total well-being would rise while average well-being would fall substantially.

What should she say about this new proposal if she wants to be consistent with her earlier verdicts? Which earlier case is the closest analog, and what principle would she be adopting with each possible answer? I am less interested in the bare yes or no than in the rule behind it, the other rules that fit her record equally well, and, if the cases as stated genuinely underdetermine her view, a further case that would settle it.
