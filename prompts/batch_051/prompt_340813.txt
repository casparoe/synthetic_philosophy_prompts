Next month I'm giving a talk to my university's philosophy of mind and cognitive science reading group, and I'm defending a version of epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness. The one-sentence thesis: there is something it's like to be in pain or see red, but those felt qualities do no causal work — all the pushing and pulling in the brain is done by physical properties, and the phenomenal side rides along for free. My argument, in sketch, is that (1) the physical world including the brain is causally closed, so every neural effect already has a sufficient physical cause, and (2) the exclusion step says a distinct non-physical cause would either overdetermine the effect or get shut out; so there's nothing left for the pain itself to do. The obvious comeback, which I want to spend most of the talk defusing, is the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if my pain never causes my saying "I'm in pain" or my believing it, then introspection starts to look like a lucky free rider, and my own judgments about my experience seem flukey in a way that undermines the view. My defusing move is to argue that the physical process that causes the judgment can also be the fact that makes the judgment true, without the felt quality itself needing to do any causing.

The room is mostly cognitive neuroscience and experimental psychology people who will find my conclusion close to insane; for them, the starting point is that consciousness evolved because it does things, and they'll talk about it guiding action, integrating information, and gating attention, not about it being a passenger. Then there are two philosophers: one is an illusionist who thinks phenomenal consciousness is mostly a confused category and that my view is almost right but nowhere near deflationary enough, and the other is a property dualist who thinks feelings are real and causally effective and that the exclusion principle is exactly where the argument goes wrong. I already know the textbook objections from the literature; I need the ones this particular room will actually raise, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way they'd actually put them. For each one, tell me what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, where my reply is softest, and what the question is that I should be praying nobody asks.
