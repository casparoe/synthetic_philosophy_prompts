I want to use the eradication of the screwworm fly from North America in the mid-20th century as a test case for a claim I keep hearing in pest-control debates: that calling something a "pest" changes what we owe it. Here's what I know: female screwworm flies lay eggs in open wounds, the larvae eat living flesh, and the fly cost the livestock industry a lot while also causing real suffering in wild animals. The US got rid of it without insecticides, by releasing radiation-sterilized males until the population collapsed, which means a species was deliberately eliminated from a whole continent. Skip the history and the biology; I want the ethics, run in both directions. Does the pest label do any independent moral work here, or does everything reduce to a welfare tradeoff between the fly and the animals it parasitized? Who had standing, and what were they owed? And does aiming at a whole species raise anything that a framework built around killing individual pests leaves out? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
