Modern natural-language systems are built around a particular representational practice. A model is trained on a large corpus, usually by predicting a word from its surrounding words or by predicting words masked out of a sentence, and the byproduct of that training is a mapping from words — or, in more recent systems, from word-tokens in context — to vectors in a high-dimensional space. These vectors are then treated as carriers of semantic information. The similarity between two expressions is measured by the cosine of the angle between their vectors; a search engine retrieves a passage by placing the query and the candidate passages in the same space and measuring their proximity; and the same spaces support apparently meaningful arithmetic, such as the classic result that the vector for king minus the vector for man plus the vector for woman lands near the vector for queen. The practice has an internal twist, however: with so-called static embeddings a word type has a single location in the space, whereas with contextual embeddings the same string — bank, for example — moves through the space depending on the sentence in which it occurs. And there is a quieter tension as well: cosine proximity is routinely used as a measure of sameness or closeness of meaning, but it is equally sensitive to topical association and shared context, so that two words opposed in meaning can sit close together precisely because they are about the same thing.

What theory of meaning does this practice presuppose? Charitably reconstructed, it seems to assume something like a distributional view: that a word's meaning is determined by, or at least fully recoverable from, the patterns of contexts in which it appears, and that semantic similarity is fundamentally a matter of location in a relational-geometric space. But I want to know whether the practice strictly presupposes that theory, or whether it is equally compatible with a referential, truth-conditional, or mentalist semantics that merely uses the vectors as a computational shortcut. Then consider the consistency of the practice's own parts. The fixed location of a word type is what makes operations like king minus man plus woman possible; the context-dependent location of a token seems to abandon the idea that a word has a standing meaning at all in favor of a meaning that is assigned only within a sentence. Do these two features presuppose different accounts of what a word's meaning is? Can the same system consistently hold both, and does the antonym case show that the practice's measure of similarity is not really a measure of semantic equivalence? Finally, explain what would have to change in the practice — the training objective, the similarity measure, the way ambiguity and composition are handled — if its implicit semantics were replaced by an explicit compositional, truth-conditional semantics. In answering, distinguish what the technology strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.
