I've been trying to get my head round the logical omniscience problem in epistemic logic, and something kept nagging at me, so I want to check whether I'm pattern-matching or onto something. In the standard possible-worlds semantics, an agent ends up knowing every logical truth, and her knowledge is closed under logical consequence: if she knows P and P entails Q, she knows Q. Obviously that's a gross idealisation of real, bounded reasoners. Now, in program analysis there's a technique called abstract interpretation, where you can't compute the exact set of behaviours a program could exhibit, so you compute a sound over-approximation instead: a superset of the real behaviours, chosen to be computable. The analyser then reports that certain states are reachable which in fact never are. To me there's a common shape here: in both cases you have a set — known sentences, reachable states — that gets closed under some consequence-like relation (logical consequence in one case, the abstract transition relation in the other), and the closure inflates the set well beyond what the actual system, a person or a running program, really exhibits. The difference is that the epistemic logic side does this as a philosophical idealisation, while the static analysis side does it as a deliberate engineering trade-off to keep the problem computable. Is this a genuine structural identity or just a loose analogy? If it's genuine, does anything transfer? For example, could the widening trick from abstract interpretation, where you deliberately coarsen the approximation to force the analysis to terminate, be reworked into a model of how bounded agents close off some but not all consequences of what they know? And can you name something the analogy predicts that comes out false on one side or the other? I'd rather you commit to a definite verdict than lay out the options.
