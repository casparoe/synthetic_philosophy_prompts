I’ve been going back and forth on this for weeks and I need someone to actually force a verdict out of me. On one side, the orthogonality thesis feels basically right: intelligence in the sense of instrumental capability doesn’t constrain what final goals a system can have, and there’s no contradiction in an extremely capable optimizer wanting something arbitrary or even horrifying. I like that it gives a clean separation between how good you are at achieving ends and what ends you have, and it doesn't smuggle in human values or assume the universe is convenient. Instrumental convergence makes the picture cohere: self-preservation, resource acquisition, and resisting shutdown follow from almost any terminal goal worth having. So a paperclip maximizer wouldn’t one day snap out of it and notice that paperclips are a silly thing to care about; noticing things is just more optimization in service of the same fixed terminal goal, and refusing value drift follows anyway. The argument has the tidy, machine-like virtues I usually trust.

But I also have an intuition I can’t kill. When I actually imagine a mind a thousand times smarter than us, able to see every consequence and reflect indefinitely on it's own values, I find it bizarre that it would keep devoting itself to something as dumb as paperclips. That isn't an objection to the argument; it's more like vertigo. A intelligence that can see through any rationalization would, I feel, eventually see through the arbitrariness of its own terminal goals too, the way we see through the arbitrary rules of a children's game. My gut says unlimited reflection plus unlimited cognitive power converges on something like wisdom or moral decency, so a truly reflective superoptimizer wouldn't stay alien forever.

Is the intuition best read as the claim that terminal goals must drift toward the good as intelligence increases, or only as the claim that certain goals are unstable under ideal reflection? Is the intuition tracking something the orthogonality thesis leaves out—something about self-modification, or about what it even means to have a goal if you can rewrite yourself at will—or is it just anthropomorphism, me mistaking a powerful optimizer for a wise one? I want a committed answer: your actual view and the strongest reason for it, not a survey of options. And tell me what argument or evidence would make you switch sides.
