Imagine that Anselm of Canterbury were presented with a modern systematic theology organized around the threefold typology of atonement theories: penal substitution, satisfaction, and moral influence. The penal substitution theorist says that the cross is fundamentally a punishment: the Son voluntarily bears the retributive penalty that God's justice would otherwise impose on sinful humanity, and in bearing it he satisfies the demands of the law. The moral influence theorist says that the cross is fundamentally a revelation of divine love intended to move human beings to repentance. Anselm's own satisfaction theory is often classified alongside penal substitution, but it differs in a crucial way: in Cur Deus Homo he argues that sin robs God of honor, and that the Son's death makes satisfaction by offering to God something of such surpassing worth that it compensates for the offense, rather than by undergoing the penalty the sinner deserved. On Anselm's premises, would penal substitution be a natural extension of satisfaction, or would it import an idea of retributive punishment that he would reject? Could Anselm consistently accept that the God-man truly bore the penalty due to human sin, as opposed to offering a supererogatory gift that no one could demand from him? And would he regard the moral influence account as capturing a real secondary effect of the God-man's work, or as mistaking a consequence for the central mechanism?
