Me and a friend have been arguing on and off for weeks about whether the sheer fact that you're the one who clicked, walked, searched, and bought gives you a property right in the behavioral data those acts generate. He thinks Locke cuts the other way: the company does the actual labor of logging, cleaning, and aggregating the data exhaust, so on a labor-mixing story the platform ends up owning what it "mixed its labor" with. I think that's backwards—my labor produced the trail, and the platform is just filming me as I make it. We both keep coming back to this bit of John Locke's Second Treatise of Government (ch. V, sec. 27):

"Though the earth, and all inferior creatures, be common to all men, yet every man has a property in his own person: this no body has any right to but himself. The labour of his body, and the work of his hands, we may say, are properly his. Whatsoever then he removes out of the state that nature hath provided, and left it in, he hath mixed his labour with, and joined to it something that is his own, and thereby makes it his property. It being by him removed from the common state nature hath placed it in, it hath by this labour something annexed to it, that excludes the common right of other men: for this labour being the unquestionable property of the labourer, no man but he can have a right to what that is once joined to, at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others."

We're not asking what current privacy or data law says; it mostly doesn't treat any of this as property, and that's exactly why we keep running in circles. For context, he's at roughly 0.7 that the collector would end up owning the data under this passage, and I'm at roughly 0.6 that I would. I'd like your own credence on the philsophical claim that if this Lockean account were the only source of property rights, a person's behavioral data (location traces, clickstreams, purchase histories) counts as her property rather than the property of the firm that records and aggregates it. Please decompose the credence rather than just giving a number: your probability that a labor-mixing theory is the right account of first appropriation for information at all; that the relevant labor belongs primarily to the person acting rather than to the collector; that the collector's recording and refining labor is enough to ground a rival property claim even if the user's labor comes first; that the proviso about leaving enough and as good in common matters for non-rival things like data; and any other term you think is doing real work. Give a number and a one-line justification for each, then show how they combine into your final credence. Then tell me which term you're least confident in, which term you'd guess our disagreement actually lives in, and what specific argument or evidence would move your final number the most.
