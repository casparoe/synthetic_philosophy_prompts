I've been trying to make sense of the idea that one person can have two natures — like Jesus being fully God and fully human at the same time. I don't have any training in this stuff, so I worked out my own way of pictureing it and I want someone to tell me honestly if it holds up.

I think of a nature as a full instruction set for being a certain kind of thing. Human nature is the complete set of rules for being human: getting hungry, growing, dying, learning things, being tempted. Divine nature is the complete set of rules for being God: not depending on anything, knowing everything, not being able to die. A person is the thing that runs an instruction set, not the instruction set itself — the runner, not the rules.

So on my picture, one person with two natures is just one runner executing two instruction sets at the same time. I call it parallel natures. The two natures don't have to merge into some third blended thing, and neither one has to be shut off for the other to run. It's like one computer running two operating systems side by side, except unlike a computer there's no user picking one over the other.

The part I keep getting stuck on: when Jesus is tired or hungry or doesn't know something, that's the human instruction set doing its thing. When he forgives sins or calms a storm, that's the divine one. But if it's always one or the other at any given moment, then isn't he just switching between two modes, not being both at once? And if it's genuinely both at once, don't the two instruction sets fight — like the divine rule says cannot die while the human rule says will die? I try to fix this by saying the person has a kind of scheduler that lets each nature do its own thing without contradiction, because the conflict is between rulebooks, not between actions. But then I've introduced this third thing, the scheduler, and I'm not sure if that means I've secretly made the person into a seperate third thing with its own nature.

So: is this picture coherent? Does it already have a name in the debates? And give me the hardest objections you can think of — not just the polite ones — and tell me which of them actually breaks the idea instead of just making it awkward.
