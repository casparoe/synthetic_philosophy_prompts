I have been developing a set of working criteria for when we may attribute phenomenal consciousness — an experiential point of view, however minimal — to a nonhuman animal, and I want to pressure-test the criteria against a case that has been troubling me. The four criteria I have been using are these: (i) pain-like states that go beyond reflexive nociception, in the sense that the behavior persists, is modulated by changing motivation, and is damped by analgesics; (ii) flexible learning that transfers beyond the training context rather than being a narrowly associative or prewired response; (iii) integration of information from distinct sensory channels into a single behavioral decision, the kind of thing a global-workspace account would look for; and (iv) a self-directed motivational economy, shown when an animal treats its own bodily condition as a common currency — protecting or grooming an injured site, trading one drive off against another, and adjusting effort after deprivation.

The case is the western honeybee. The capacities cited in its favor include the following. Workers learn to associate an odor with a sugar reward in the standard proboscis-extension paradigm, and some studies report that they can learn a same-versus-different rule and transfer it across sensory modalities. A returning forager uses the waggle dance to combine information about the direction of a food source relative to the sun with information about its distance and quality into a single communicative output. Bees also modify their behavior when the hive overheats, trading foraging effort against fanning to cool the colony, and after being vigorously shaken they become more likely to interpret an ambiguous odor as predicting punishment — a response some researchers read as a pessimistic, anxiety-like bias. On the other side, the nervous system of the honeybee contains on the order of a million neurons, with the mushroom bodies and central complex doing much of the integrative work, and it is organized more like an ensemble of specialized subsystems than like a single central integrator; moreover, whether insects possess nociception with the full motivational profile of pain remains genuinely contested.

Run the honeybee through the four criteria and tell me, for each, whether it clearly satisfies the criterion, clearly fails it, or leaves the criterion underdetermined. Then say whether the case should come out as genuinely borderline, and if so, whether that borderliness is a discovery about the bee or a defect in the criteria — for instance, that criterion (iii) or (iv) is too vertebrate-centered, or too demanding for a distributed nervous system, or that the four criteria as a set fail to track a gradable property. I am less interested in a final yes or no than in learning how much weight each criterion is carrying and whether the criteria themselves need revision.
