I'm a novelist, and I'm about a third of the way into a manuscript set in the late Bronze Age: a real woman, a queen who founds a city, slowly gets remembered as a goddess after her death. The novel's whole conceit is basically euhemerism — the idea that the gods were once people, and the divinity is something that accretes later. I'd like to pressure-test that conceit by working through the Odyssey with it, but I'm not a classicist and I don't entirely trust my own reading.

Could you read the Odyssey through a euhemerist lens and tell me where the poem invites that reading and where it actively resists it? Because on the one hand the Homeric gods feel weirdly like persons: Athena turns up as Mentor, Poseidon nurses a personal grudge, Calypso keeps Odysseus on her island out of something that looks like desire, and the Olympians bicker like a dynastic family. That all seems compatible with treating them as inflated memories of Bronze Age chieftains or remembered ancestors. On the other hand, they do things no remembered king does — Poseidon can raise storms, Zeus can thunder — and the poem seems to need them not to be just big people.

Where would a careful euhemerist point, and where would they have to squint? What do you make of Zeus's speech early on, where he complains that mortals blame the gods for the messes they make themselves — is that the poem arguing against the whole premise, or is it the kind of thing that gets remembered about a real ruler? And does the epic ever show the mechanism crossing the other way, living humans being addressed as godlike or offered something like apotheosis? I'd honestly like to know wether the conceit would survive contact with the poem, or whether I'm forcing Homer into my plot. Take both the text and the theory seriously, and don't be afraid to say the euhemerist reading is half right and half a distortion.
