I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and I want to make a handout for undergrads sorting the mess of positions on whether the golden rule can be salvaged or not. Could you draft a decision tree that starts from where versions actually come apart rather than from a list of names: which formulation someone takes as the core one (negative, positive, or full role-reversal), whether the rule is meant as a decision procedure or just a rough test, and whether the agent's actual desires set the standard or an idealised version does. The diagnostic questions should use concrete cases — a masochist, a criminal objecting to being punished by a judge, someone with false empirical beliefs about what's good for them, a self-hating person, someone who'd rather not be helped if it frees them from helping others — phrased so people can answer without knowing any labels. Each leaf should get a name if there's a real one (literal golden rule, copper rule, idealised role-reversal, Kantian replacement, virtue-ethical heuristic, no self-standing principle, that sort of thing) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Mark combinations that are logically open but nobody defends, and briefly say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Also flag positions your tree can't seperate, and say where it silently papers over a distinction. It might help to use Kant's complaint in the Groundwork that the rule can't ground strict duties to others or duties to oneself — no need to quote it, just have the tree test whether a version can handle those. Don't worry about exhaustive coverage of the literature; I care about the branching being right. British English, and keep it to maybe eight to twelve leaves.
