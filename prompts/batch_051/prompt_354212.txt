For a seminar on the ethics of markets, I have been developing a distinction that I would like checked before I build an essay around it. I call it source repugnance versus context repugnance. A transaction is source-repugnant when the objection attaches to the exchange itself—to the very fact that this kind of thing is offered for sale—and would remain even if the exchange were fully voluntary, mutually beneficial, and harmless to third parties. A transaction is context-repugnant when the objection attaches only to the conditions surrounding the exchange, such as background inequality, coercion, informational asymmetry, or desperation, so that the same kind of trade could in principle be unobjectionable under better conditions. My working view is that kidney sales and price gouging are mostly context-repugnant, whereas vote selling is the cleanest case of source repugnance; I also suspect that many disputes about markets are really disputes about which of the two kinds of repugnance is doing the work. Is this distinction coherent? Does it already exist in the literature under another name? And is my sorting of the three cases defensible, or does it break down under pressure?
