I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of language (sixteen students, one ninety-minute meeting), and we will be taking up the question whether a language could do without pronouns and indexicals — that is, whether expressions such as 'I', 'you', 'here', and 'now' can be systematically paraphrased away in favor of names, definite descriptions, coordinates, and clock times, or whether they play an ineliminable role. The philosophical backdrop is the familiar supermarket-style case in which a person has all the objective information that someone is making a mess and yet only gets into motion when he realizes that the someone is him, together with the thought that the missing element is self-locating rather than descriptive. I want the students to run into this themselves, preferably before any of the vocabulary ('indexical', 'token-reflexive rule', 'essential indexical') is put on the board.

Please design a structured classroom activity, with roles or rules, a timeline, and a debrief, in which participants attempt to carry out a short, time-sensitive coordination task while forbidden from using indexicals and must produce substitutes for them. The constraints should be arranged so that the obvious substitutions — proper names for 'I' and 'you', spatial coordinates for 'here', timestamps for 'now' — look as though they might work and then produce a distinctive kind of failure: a message that carries the same world-describing information but no longer guides the recipient's action at the right place and time, or a paraphrase that silently leans on a demonstrative such as 'this note' or 'the current time'. The debrief should map those failures onto the distinction between descriptive content and the contribution of an indexical, explain why token-reflexive rules are the standard repair and why they do not simply eliminate indexicality, and be candid about what the activity cannot establish — for instance, that no possible language could do without indexicals, as opposed to that the most natural elimination attempts succeed only by borrowing the very phenomenon they were meant to remove. Also say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it.
