I host a podcast on philosophical theology, and next week's guest is a systematic theologian who defends a modified penal substitutionary account of the atonement. Her standard move is to deflect objections by insisting that the courtroom picture of a penalty paid by a substitute is only an analogy: the cross is God's own action in Christ, not a transfer between third parties. Draft eight to ten interview questions that make that deflection progressively harder to use, while never lapsing into questions she could answer by restating the doctrine.

The sequence should include: a question on the objection that the cross has God punishing an innocent, with a follow-up that anticipates her likely reply that Christ's voluntary consent and identity as God incarnate defeat the analogy to an innocent third party being punished in someone else's place; a question pressing the tension between payment and forgiveness, since a penalty fully paid seems to leave nothing left to forgive, and asking whether Anselmian satisfaction faces the same difficulty or whether the penal framework makes it worse; a question on whether imputed righteousness makes the believer's actual moral transformation merely decorative, with a follow-up that blocks the anticipated answer that sanctification follows automatically from justification; a question on whether the atonement changes God, changes us, or only changes our perception of God, and whether her answer can avoid collapsing into a moral-influence account of the cross; and a question on whether Christ's bearing the Father's wrath splits the Trinity, with a follow-up keyed to her likely distinction between the Father's wrath toward sin and a rupture within the Godhead.

End with a question that asks what, in her own terms, would count as evidence against the account.
