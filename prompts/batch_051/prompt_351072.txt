I have come to accept a debunking view about a familiar contrastive question in economic history: why industrial capitalism arose in Britain rather than in China, including its sharper Yangtze Delta variant. On my view, the standard formulation is ill-posed. It compares a recently unified nation-state with a subcontinental empire; it treats a contingent, path-dependent outcome as the natural terminus that the more commercially dynamic economy should have reached first; and it lets hindsight do the work of selecting which features of each case count as causes. I would like to know what would have to be true for this debunking position to be false. Which historical findings, comparative methodologies, or constraints on well-formed contrastive explanation would count against it? Distinguish defeaters that would be decisive — for instance, showing that genuinely comparable units did face a shared problem with a non-anachronistic measure of success, and that a specified mechanism was present in one case and absent in the other — from defeaters that would merely complicate the debunking. Also say which objections a defender of my position should concede as genuine evidence against it rather than dismiss as changing the subject, and assess whether the position is insulated against every possible finding, and whether that insulation is a strength or a defect.
