A curious asymmetry runs through our judgments about contaminating environments, and I want to know whether it can be defended. On Earth, even people who are not especially sentimental about nature tend to treat the deliberate introduction of an organism into an ecosystem where it does not belong as a serious wrong. A researcher who released foreign bacteria into a pristine subglacial lake would be widely condemned as a vandal, and space agencies go to considerable expense to sterilize landers so that Earth microbes are not accidentally exported to other planets. Yet many of the very same people find the idea of deliberately seeding Mars with Earth organisms, or eventually terraforming it, exciting and morally acceptable—indeed a kind of achievement—even though that would be the most thorough introduction of foreign biology into an environment ever attempted. A single contaminated lake is an outrage; a remade planet is progress.

Is there a principle that generates both of those judgments, or is the asymmetry simply an inconsistency we live with? The obvious candidate distinctions are that Mars may be lifeless while the lake is not; that terraforming would create a biosphere rather than damage one; that existing life matters more than merely possible future life; or that Earth's environments are finished and must be preserved while Mars is empty and may be used. I want the strongest version of the best candidate, tested against nearby cases. Would the case for terraforming extend to releasing engineered microbes into a deliberately sterilized, lifeless region of Earth? Should the case against contamination extend to a lifeless but geochemically unique part of Mars, or to a lifeless moon? Does it matter that contaminating Mars might destroy evidence of past life, or that a local introduction on Earth might be reversible while terraforming would not be? If no principle survives these cases, say which side should move: should we extend something like planetary protection to Mars as a matter of principle, relax our protectiveness toward Earth environments, or accept that we simply value the old and familiar over the new? Give an honest verdict, not just a survey of considerations.
