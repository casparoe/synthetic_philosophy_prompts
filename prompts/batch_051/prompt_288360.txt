I'm a backend engineer, and I keep losing an argument with a coworker about whether a constitution is basically a Ulysses contract. His move goes like this: Odysseus knew he'd cave when he heard the sirens, so he made his crew tie him to the mast and ordered them not to untie him no matter what he said; a constitution is the same thing, citizens in their calmer moments binding their future selves so they can't wreck things in a panicked majority mood. He uses this to argue that it's legitimate for constitutional rules to be almost imposible to change even for people born after the thing was written — he says that's not a bug, it's the ropes doing their job. When I pushed back that at least Odysseus consented for himself, while a constitution binds people who never got a say, he said that's exactly the feature: the whole point is the bind has to outlive the people who tied it, otherwise it's just a New Year's resolution.

So how far does the constitution-as-Ulysses-contract analogy actually hold? I'd like the explicit mapping — who or what plays Odysseus, the sirens, the mast, the ropes, the crew, the song, the escape route — then the places where the mapping snaps. The consent one seems huge to me, and maybe also that Odysseus knew the exact threat and roughly how long it would last, whereas nobody knows what weird future the constitution will have to deal with. Then tell me whether my coworker's conclusion — because precommitment is legitimate for an individual, entrenched provisions binding later majorities are legitimate too — actually survives the disanalogies, or whether the analogy was carrying the argumeent the whole time. If it breaks, what would be a better analogy (software-flavored is fine) for what a constitution actually does?
