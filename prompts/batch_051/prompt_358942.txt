so i teach a course on time and motion, and next week we do zeno's dichotomy on its own. you know it: to get from the doorway to the blackboard you first have to get halfway, then halfway of what's left, and so on, so on one telling you never get started and on another you never arrive. i want to end the lecture with a villanelle, and i want the form to carry the argument rather than the vocabulary doing it. the two refrains should be the two facts the paradox keeps rubbing against each other: one roughly "there is always another half between me and the blackboard" and the other roughly "and yet here i am at the blackboard." word them so that in the final couplet the two refrains can be read as a single sentence made of both lines, so the students feel the endless sequence of halves and the arrival landing on top of each other rather than one refuting the other. the refrains coming back in the middle should feel less like a chorus and more like the same stuck thought reasserting itself after every attempt to move on. no naming zeno, no word "paradox," no line about calculus riding in to fix things. a person who works on the mathematics of the continuum should be able to tell the poem already understands that you don't finish an infinite sequence by taking one more step — you finish it the way the sum 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... is 1, or thats the reading i'd defend anyway. and the undergrads who havent had that yet should still get a real poem about nearly arriving. keep the specialist stuff in the form, not in a footnote.
