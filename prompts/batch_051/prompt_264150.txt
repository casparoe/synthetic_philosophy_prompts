I'm planning an hour-long seminar for first-year law students on what legal fictions reveal about the ontology of law. I want the anchor to be the prefatory note to the Everyman's Library edition of Henry Sumner Maine's Ancient Law, where the editor says that early law is, to use Maine's own phrase, 'a habit' and not a conscious exercise of the volition of a lawgiver or a legislature. Plan how to teach from that observation to the claim that a legal fiction is a practice of changing the habitual operation of a rule while keeping its verbal form intact. The students will arrive thinking a legal fiction is just a polite lie or a legal mistake. Give me the explanatory sequence: which distinction has to be in place before the puzzle of legal fictions even arises; which one example should carry the session and why it must show a rule's operation changing while its letter does not; which misconceptions to expect and at what point in the sequence each should be headed off; and a final question whose wrong answer would show they still think of law as a set of texts rather than a practice. Respond as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
