I find myself increasingly persuaded by a position I have heard described, usually dismissively, as the case against deathism. Its positive argument runs something like this: death is an evil whenever it deprives someone of life they would have had reason to value; ageing brings that evil about on an enormous scale; and there is no principled reason to seek a cure for cancer but not for the underlying deteriorations of ageing. The view has the attraction of a tidy, unified decision procedure: where an intervention can be expected to add life worth living, and does not wrong anyone, the presumption favours pursuing it. Its negative argument is that much of the resistance to life extension is not a rival theory but a predictable failure of reasoning — adaptive preferences formed because mortality cannot be avoided, status quo bias, fear of disappointment, and a moralistic impulse to dignify what we have no choice but to accept. All of this accuses the intuition before I have even stated it.

Yet the intuition persists. It is not that I think death is good; it is that a human life seems to be the sort of thing that needs a horizon. Urgency, irreversible commitment, the weight of choices made against limited time, and the way a life can fail to hang together if it merely accumulates — these seem to depend on there being an end, or at least an ending. An indefinitely extended life looks, from inside my more reflective moods, less like more of the goods of life and more like a different kind of thing altogether. I can already hear the reply: that this is precisely the rationalisation the theory predicts, dressed as wisdom. So I want to know exactly what each side must sacrifice before it yields. What would have to be true for the intuition to count as debunked rather than merely named? What would have to be true for the theory to be amended or abandoned, rather than the intuition suppressed? Is there a stable reconciliation — perhaps one in which moderate extension is desirable while indefinite extension is not — or does the underlying argument forbid that halfway house? And if you had to surrender one, which should it be: the decision procedure or the sense that a life needs an ending?
