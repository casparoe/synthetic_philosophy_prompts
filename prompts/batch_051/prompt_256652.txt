In my department the paradox of tolerance gets used as a conversation-ender: someone says we have to deplatform them, someone else says that's illiberal, and the first person says tolerance is self-defeating. I'm a PhD student in political theory and I've started to wonder what, if anything, that claim is actually saying about the world. As I understand it, the paradox is that a society which extends full tolerance to intolerant groups—letting them speak, organise, and stand for office—will eventually be destroyed by them, since they use the norms of tolerance as cover while working to abolish it; so tolerance has to refuse to tolerate the intolerant. My supervisor says the whole thing is unfalsifiable: either we define 'intolerant' by the fact that they won, in which case its true by stipulation, or we don't, and then it's just a story. I don't think that's quite right, but I can't point to the observation that would settle it. So: how would you test the causal claim that tolerating intolerant actors causes a society to lose its tolerance? Give me the strongest design you can—cross-national comparison of party bans and militant-democracy provisions, a natural experiment around platform moderation, Weimar, whatever you'd trust. What result would favour the paradox, what result would count against it, and what part of the claim no test could settle even in principle?
