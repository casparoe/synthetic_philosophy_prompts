I run trust and safety for a small subscription forum (rare houseplants, if you must know), and I have to rule on a case that's been eating at me all week. Our terms of use, drafted in 2014 and untouched since, say: "You may not access the Service by any automated program, script, bot, or other non-human means, except through the API we make available." That clause was aimed at scrapers and inventory-hoovering bots; nobody had LLM agents in mind. A member with a spotless record has started using one of those new browser-based AI agents on his own account. It logs in as him, reads his messages, searches listings, answers DMs, and sometimes buys plants, all through the normal web interface. It runs at roughly human speed, hesitates, goes back, and once failed to buy a plant because it didn't notice a little "sold" badge. It does not touch our API. I keep flipping on wether it's "automated." At the request-and-DOM level it's plainly a script driving a browser, and the pattern of automation is about as real as any pattern gets. At the design level it's a personal assistant built to do ordinary member things for one member. At the intentional level the member is just delegating tasks he would have done himself, at human speed. I've been told these are basically Dennett's three stances, and I think the wording is less helpfull than we all assumed.

So help me think it through. Go through the standard interpretation approaches — plain words, the purpose of the clause, what the drafters would have said if they'd been asked in 2014 about a human-speed agent that uses the site like a slow, error-prone human, and what a reasonable reader of the terms would understand. For each, say whether it treats this as "automated means" and which of the three stances it seems to be adopting. Then tell me which approach or stance should win here and why. I need a ruling, so make the very last line exactly one of: \answer{Violates} or \answer{Does not violate}.
