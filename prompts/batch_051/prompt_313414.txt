The decades-long dispute over logical omniscience in epistemic logic has always looked to me like a debate that cannot settle into a real conclusion. The formal situation is clear to everyone: on the standard possible-worlds semantics, knowledge is truth in every world the agent regards as possible, and from that alone it follows that the agent knows every logical truth, every logical consequence of what it already knows, and every necessary proposition whatsoever. As far as I can tell, the dispute is not about the mathematics, which all sides accept, but about whether this is a defect. Some regard the idealization as benign, because the point of the formalism is to trace the commitments of a rational agent, not the cognitive performance of a bounded one. Others treat it as a reductio, because the resulting idealized knower is so unlike any actual subject that the formalism cannot be a theory of knowledge at all. Various repairs have been proposed, and yet neither side has moved. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is really about what a logic of knowledge is for: a normative ideal, a descriptive model of cognition, or a theory of the content of ordinary knowledge ascriptions. I am asking for a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute, two literatures answering different questions under one heading, or a substantive disagreement about closure or idealization that could in principle be settled? What would count as progress given your diagnosis — a formal result, an empirical constraint, a distinction, or a frank change of question? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says, and be honest about whether anything could end it.
