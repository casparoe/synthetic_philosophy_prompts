I've been sitting in on a seminar on Confucian role ethics, and a pair of cases from the discussion has left me unable to find the joint in our ordinary judgments. A mother is on a beach when her child and a stranger's child are both caught in a current. She swims to her own child first, and the stranger's child drowns before anyone else can reach them. We do not merely excuse this; many of us would think it morally wrong for her to have saved the stranger's child first and let her own die. Now move the same woman behind a desk: she chairs the admissions committee of a selective school, and she quietly arranges for her own child, whose record falls below the cutoff, to take a place over a better-qualified stranger's child. Here the judgment reverses—nepotism, corruption, an abuse of position.

Both cases are exercises of the same impulse, partiality toward one's own. What principle separates them? One candidate is that the admissions case involves public office while the rescue does not; another is that lives are at stake in one case and scarce opportunities in the other; a third is that in one case she acts as a private person and in the other as a trustee for a community. But none of these, by itself, explains why the same underlying orientation toward her child should be a requirement in the water and a corruption in the committee room. The third candidate, in particular, runs into a further case: a private employer who hires her niece over a stranger with a stronger résumé. It is her own firm and her own money, yet many of us still find the choice objectionable.

The Confucian role ethicist, as I understand the view, treats the beach as the template rather than the exception: obligations are fundamentally role-obligations, persons are constituted by their relationships, and treating one's child as merely one beneficiary among others is already a moral failure. But if that picture is right, why doesn't role-based partiality extend to grading, hiring, and admissions? If it does extend, are we prepared to accept the result? If it doesn't, I want the principle that licenses a mother's partiality in the water while forbidding it at the admissions desk—and I want that same principle to give an answer in the private-employer case. Is there a principled line here, or are these ordinary judgments simply inconsistent with one another?
