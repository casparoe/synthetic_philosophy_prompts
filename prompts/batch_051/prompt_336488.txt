I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've agreed to run a 90-minute session at a local skeptics' meetup — twenty to thirty people, mixed ages, no philosophy background, and a room with chairs but no screens. The hook I advertised is "could arguing be a sport, and would that make us better at it?" I don't want to lecture at them; I want the structure of the thing to make them feel the tension between treating an argument as a contest to win and treating it as something to inspect and improve.

So: design me a two-phase in-person activity. Phase one should be a recognizable competitive debate format — real winners, a visible scoring system, maybe a bracket — so people actually feel the pull of winning. Phase two should keep the same rough format (pairs, timed speeches, an audience, scores) but change the scoring so it rewards the behaviors good reasoning actually requires: accurately restating the other side before attacking it, conceding a point when it's good, saying what evidence would change your mind. I want the difference in incentives to do the teaching, not me standing there saying "observe the motivated reasoning."

Practical constraints: I can print one-sided handouts and use index cards and name tents; no slides, no real money. Payoffs should be symbolic — ranking, tokens, bragging rights, a mildly ridiculous trophy I can make from cardstock. Give me the exact rules, the timing for each phase, what's on the handouts, and a scoring rubric simple enough that audience members can use without training.

Pick the debate motions to do a specific job: I want topics that are genuinely arguable and that people have preexisting views about, but not the culture-war stuff where team loyalty will swamp the lesson. Give me three candidate yes/no motions and say why each would or wouldn't work for this group.

Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments in the game — someone refusing to concede a true-but-unhelpful point in phase one, someone getting penalized in phase two for refuting a weakened version of the other side, a pair discovering halfway through that they've switched positions and didn't notice — onto the conceptual machinery, whether that's accountability vs. audience capture, adversarial collaboration (two people who disagree committing to a joint test instead of a fight), the argumentative theory of reasoning (the idea that reasoning evolved to win arguments and only secondarily tracks truth), or your own distinctions. Make sure the debrief returns to the advertised question: after phase two, is there a version of argument-as-sport that actually sharpens reasoning, or does the contest frame itself do the damage? Be frank about which parts of the experience the game can't produce on its own and where I'll have to supply the framing with plain talk.

And tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: who games the scoring, which round drags, what kind of participant turns the whole thing into a performance.

Last thing, because I have to defend this to my editor: seperate the design choices that have real empirical backing (the accountability literature, adversarial collaboration, that sort of thing) from the choices that are just your educated judgment, and don't present it as settled that competitive formats improve reasoning. It isn't, and I don't want my piece to imply otherwise.
