I have been thinking through a version of the Lewis triviality argument, and it seems to prove far too much, but I cannot tell which step should be rejected. The argument starts from the claim that the probability of an indicative conditional equals the conditional probability of its consequent on its antecedent. Let A and C be any two propositions such that all four combinations A∧C, A∧¬C, ¬A∧C, and ¬A∧¬C are possible, and let P be a probability function assigning positive probability to all four. Suppose the thesis T says: for every probability function Q in some class that includes P, P(·|C), and P(·|¬C), if Q(A)>0, then Q(A→C)=Q(C|A). Now reason as follows:

(1) Since P satisfies T, P(A→C)=P(C|A).

(2) Let P_C be P conditionalized on C. Since P_C also satisfies T, P_C(A→C)=P_C(C|A). But P_C(C)=1, and P_C(A)>0 because P(A∧C)>0, so P_C(C|A)=1. Therefore P(A→C|C)=1.

(3) Similarly, let P_¬C be P conditionalized on ¬C. Since P_¬C satisfies T, P_¬C(A→C)=P_¬C(C|A). But P_¬C(C)=0, and P_¬C(A)>0 because P(A∧¬C)>0, so P_¬C(C|A)=0. Therefore P(A→C|¬C)=0.

(4) By the law of total probability, P(A→C)=P(A→C|C)P(C)+P(A→C|¬C)P(¬C). Using (2) and (3), this gives P(A→C)=P(C).

(5) Combining (1) and (4), P(C|A)=P(C).

(6) But P was arbitrary subject only to the four cells having positive probability. Thus any two propositions A and C, whenever all four truth-value combinations are possible, must be probabilistically independent under every such probability assignment. That would make probabilistic dependence between distinct propositions impossible: a positive test result could never raise the probability of a disease, for example, since A and C would always have to be independent. That cannot be right.

So the argument appears valid, but its conclusion is unacceptable. Where does it go wrong? Which premise is the least costly to surrender? I would most like to keep something like the original conditional-probability thesis, perhaps restricted to assertibility rather than truth, but I suspect the closure assumption in (2) and (3) is doing the damage. Is the right response to restrict T so that it does not survive conditionalization on the consequent or its negation? Or must one instead deny that A→C expresses a single proposition whose probability can be evaluated under different probability functions in this way? What exactly does each choice cost?
