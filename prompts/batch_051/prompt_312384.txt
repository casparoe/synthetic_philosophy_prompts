I'm teaching Othello in a course on influence and autonomy, and I want to use the play to pressure the standard distinction between manipulation and legitimate persuasion rather than merely to illustrate it. One familiar view holds that rational persuasion works through considerations the target could in principle endorse, while manipulation operates through hidden or non-rational channels; another view locates the wrong of manipulation not in the route the influence travels but in the manipulator's intentions and in the concealment of those intentions. Now press both views against Iago's actual method. He seldom argues directly that Desdemona is unfaithful; he stages the Cassio–Bianca conversation for Othello to overhear, lets the handkerchief do its work, floats suggestions he pretends to regret, and leans on Othello's position as an outsider in Venice. Does Iago bypass Othello's rational capacities, or does he corrupt them from the inside by feeding an otherwise-reasoning agent selectively misleading evidence? Is the play best read as forcing a third category, manipulation as tampering with the deliberative context rather than bypassing it, and what does that reading imply about how much blame Othello's own reasoning bears in the end? Give me a reading that is faithful in both directions, including the places where Othello resists the philosophical categories brought to it.
