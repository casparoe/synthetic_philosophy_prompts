I run the "activities track" at a rationalist-ish meetup (mostly people who read the same blogs I do — you know the type), about 16 adults, and we get a two hour evening slot once a month. Next month is the attention economy. The thing I actually want people to leave with is the shift from "the algorithms are evil" to "the people running the algorithms are doing local gradient descent on an engagement metric, and the metric is a slowly corrupting proxy." I've tried to get this across by just explaining Goodhart and Moloch, and what happens is everyone nods and then somebody says "sure, but Zuckerberg still chose the thing," and we're back to looking for a villain.

So I want a game, not a talk. The core mechanic I have in mind, which you should steal or improve: split the room into designers and users. Designers are told their job is to maximize a single number — predicted minutes-on-app or clicks or something — and they only see that number, never the users' actual goals. Each round they pick from a menu of design changes (autoplay, infinite scroll, numeric badges, friction on the exit, whatever) and see how the metric moves. Users have private cards with something they actually came to do ("send a two sentence message to my sister," "read one article I'll actually remember") and a limited number of rounds to get it done. The catch is the metric is mostly-but-imperfectly correlated with users finishing their real goals, and the correlation is worse for some changes than others — you'll have to tune the numbers so the choices that juice the metric feel locally rational but the users end up spending all there rounds stuck. I want designers to never be told to be manipulative, and ideally to defend what they built before the debrief.

Can you turn that into something actually runnable? I need: exact setup and materials (cards, score sheets, a clean payoff scheme that dosen't need real money), a timeline that fits two hours including discussion, no lecture up front, and a debrief script that maps specific moments in the game onto the ideas — Goodhart's law, metric drift, revealed preferences vs reflected preferences (or whatever you want to call that gap), and the multipolar trap / Moloch point that no individual designer had to want the outcome. Naming everything only after the fact. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and one thing about the real attention economy that a two-hour game categorically cannot reproduce, so I know what I still owe them afterward.
