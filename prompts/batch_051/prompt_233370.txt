I am serving on the review committee of a foundation that funds forecasting research, and we have before us a proposal that strikes several of us as methodologically puzzling in a way worth thinking through carefully. The applicant, drawing on the Malthusian tradition, argues that a long-run forecast can be "right but early": the core mechanism may be sound even when the predicted catastrophe has failed to arrive on schedule, provided the mechanism has not been disconfirmed but merely deferred by identifiable countervailing factors. To make this more than an unfalsifiable excuse, the applicant proposes a formal schema: any such forecast must specify (i) the mechanism that generates the trend, (ii) the offsetting factors invoked to explain the delay, (iii) a measurable indicator that would distinguish "mechanism still operating, merely offset" from "mechanism absent or defeated," and (iv) a date by which some observable state of the world should differ depending on which of those two is true. The applicant then applies this schema to the canonical case of Malthus's own prediction of geometric population growth outstripping arithmetic food supply, arguing that the demographic transition and agricultural productivity gains count as identifiable offsetting factors, not as refutations of every possible Malthusian mechanism.

Our committee is divided. One faction holds that "right but early" is just unfalsifiability with a scheduling addendum, and that no amount of schema can rescue a failed prediction whose time horizon has already passed. Another faction believes the proposal is exactly the kind of sober work a forecasting foundation should fund, because long-run predictions are rarely wrong all at once and we need an account of how they can be wrong on timing while remaining informative about mechanisms.

Draft the short decision memo I will circulate before next week's meeting. It should recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions, and it should give the committee reasons the applicant could genuinely respond to. The memo should engage seriously with the strongest objection on the losing side, and it should say concretely what evidence, in the case of Malthusian demographics, would count as the mechanism being alive or dead under the applicant's own criteria.
