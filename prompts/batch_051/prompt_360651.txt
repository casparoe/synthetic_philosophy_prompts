I am drawn to a consequentialist reading of frugality, because it offers a unified and tractable criterion: the value of using resources carefully lies in its outcomes, and the right question is always which measure—habit, incentive, default, or technology—will most reliably reduce waste and depletion; whether people are themselves frugal matters only as an instrument to that end. But I have a firm intuition that there is also a distinct requirement to be frugal, and not merely to promote frugality: a society of profligate consumers kept within resource limits by defaults, price signals, and smart regulation seems to me ethically worse than a behaviourally indistinguishable society of genuinely frugal people, even if the two never differ in outcomes. Give the strongest version of both readings—the consequentialist case for treating frugality as an agent-neutral good to be promoted, and the deontological case for treating it as a virtue one is required to exercise in one's own choices—and then offer a negotiated verdict. Should the intuition be debunked as a lingering preference for character over consequences, should the consequentialist reading be amended or abandoned, or is there an interpretation on which the two are compatible without merely relabelling one another?
