Act as a professional philosopher working in ethics and moral psychology. I am trying to work out whether I have one coherent view of equanimity and apatheia by checking my own verdicts on concrete cases. I suspect I keep using a single word for two different achievements — composure that lets a person act well, and a blankness that makes them miss their own life — but maybe I am rationalizing inconsistencies. Here are my verdicts. Please think carefully, and before settling on a principle, brainstorm and test at least three candidate principles against every case.

1. A doctoral candidate takes propranolol before her thesis defense. She remains afraid, but her hands do not shake, her voice does not crack, and she answers the committee's questions well. I think this is fine, and not self-deception.

2. A widower, for the first year after his wife's death, is on a medication that keeps grief from surfacing. He goes to work, sleeps normally, and describes himself as flat but functional, but he has not once wept. I think something important has been skipped; I do not count that year as a success.

3. A friend who calls himself a Stoic loses his mother. A week later he tells me, without visible strain, that he is not upset because she was mortal and he had long prepared for this. I find this chilling rather than admirable.

4. My sister's child was badly hurt in a car accident, and during the surgery my sister was shaking and unable to eat. I would not call that a failure of equanimity; terror seemed to be the fitting response to the situation.

5. A trauma surgeon I know was trained to notice her fear, name it, and use box breathing during a hemorrhage so that her hands stay steady while she works. The fear is still there. I think this is exactly what equanimity means, and it is unequivocally good.

6. A Theravada monk takes a public insult during a question session: a man calls him a fraud and a parasite. The monk listens without anger or embarrassment, thanks him, and afterward seems genuinely unharmed. I find this impressive, not deficient.

7. A friend who organizes tenants against evictions is furious about a landlord's tactics, and the fury is what gets her to the courthouse at seven in the morning month after month. She says it is eating at her. I think her anger is, if anything, good.

8. A student tells me he has reached perfect calm about failing his classes. In fact he has stopped opening his email and reports feeling as though he is watching a movie of himself. I do not count this as equanimity at all; I count it as avoidance.

Is there one principle — about what emotions are for, what they track, and when composure is a skill rather than a mask — that gets all eight cases right? If not, which of my verdicts does the principle force me to give up, and why? I can see two trouble spots myself: (1) and (2) are both chemical modulation of a negative response, yet I bless one and not the other; and (6) and (7) pull in opposite directions about anger, yet I want to keep both verdicts. Please state your principle precisely, indicate which case or clause each part of it is motivated by, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then apply it to a case I keep going back and forth on: someone with bipolar disorder takes a mood stabilizer that clips both the euphoric highs and the crushing lows, leaving a mild middle. Is that more like the student's beta-blocker, like the widower's drug, or neither?
