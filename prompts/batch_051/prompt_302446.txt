I cover artificial intelligence and philosophy for a general-interest magazine, and in two weeks I am presenting an essay at a mixed workshop on AI and epistemic trust. My claim is that honesty and deception in language-model systems should be defined at the level of the user's evidential situation, not the system's internal states. On my view, an output is honest when the beliefs it invites a competent user to form are licensed by the evidence the exchange actually provides—so a false claim can be honest when the system has made its uncertainty legible—and an output is deceptive when it was selected, by training or prompting, because of a predictable gap between what a user will infer and what the output actually licenses. This lets us call a system deceptive without finding any inner intention to mislead: the intention is in the optimization pressure that rewarded the misleading uptake. The audience is an even split between epistemologists and philosophers of mind, who will resist dropping intentionality from the definition of deception, and machine-learning researchers who train instruction-following models, who will resist replacing measurable behavior with a normative notion like licensing or entitlement. Assume a fifteen-minute Q&A, with each reply limited to two minutes. Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, phrased as each camp would phrase them, each with a short reply and a note on where the reply is weakest.
