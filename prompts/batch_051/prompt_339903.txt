Last week at a dinner party, two friends got into an argument about whether competition builds character or wears it down, and it kept circling back to a passage from William James's "The Moral Equivalent of War." The passage is:

"Ask all our millions, north and south, whether they would vote now (were such a thing possible) to have our war for the Union expunged from history, and the record of a peaceful transition to the present time substituted for that of its marches and battles, and probably hardly a handful of eccentrics would say yes."

Dana said James's point there isn't that war or competition is good, but that the value people retrospectively find in it is collective: the "we" in "what we now own together" and the shared sacrifice. So when we talk about competition building character, we have to ask whether the competition actually creates a shared object that outlives the winning and losing. Most modern competitions—rankings, admissions, job markets, sports tournaments—dont do that; they just produce individual winners and a lot of people who feel like they lost. So they mostly corrode.

Marcus said Dana was reading a nationalist passage as if it proved something about all competition. James's larger argument, he said, is that the martial virtues are deeply rooted and need a substitute, not that only nation-scale collective projects count. A startup team, a lab group, a pickup basketball squad all have "marches and battles" in miniature; the members come to own those memories together. If the passage says anything, it's that retrospective meaning comes from facing something hard together, which is exactly what competition provides.

Dana replied that the "something hard" in James's example isn't chosen for its own sake; it's forced from outside, and the meaning comes from responding to that. Voluntary competition lets people opt in when they think they'll win and out when it gets rough, so it selects for people who already want the prize, not for the kind of sacrifice James is trying to capture. That's why James didn't just say "let everyone compete more" but flirts with conscription into civilian projects.

Marcus said that's too high a bar. James says the military feelings won't go away until "better substitutes are offered." A good substitute can be smaller and voluntary and still cultivate the relevant virtues: discipline, cohesion, the ability to subordinate your own interest to a shared task. If you insist that only a nation at war can do that, you've made the argument useless for anyone not living through a crisis.

Dana answered that the voluntary part cuts the other way: once competition is voluntary, the "virtues" it builds are exactly the ones that make you good at competing—strategic self-interest, status-seeking, willingness to take risks—and those are not the same as the civic virtues James wanted to preserve. The passage's "sacred spiritual possession" is not the winner's trophy; it's something even the losers in the country share. A startup that fails doesn't give its ex-employees a sacred possession.

Marcus said that's empirical, not conceptual; people who played on losing teams or worked at failed startups often do describe the shared struggle as meaningful, even sacred. The distinction Dana wants between collective and individual is doing too much work.

They went back and forth a few more times and then asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't untangle it. Could you go through their exchange step by step and say who's right about which point, where each one overreaches, and who's more right overall?
