If not, which exact step fails and what would the correct version of that step need to be? If it is valid, which premise am I actually forced to abandon? I half-suspect the trick is in step (2) or (3), where we apply the Equation to a conditionalized credence, but I can't make the worry precise.

Here's the setup. Let "A→C" be the indicative conditional "if A, then C," and let P be a rational credence function. The assumption is the Equation (E): for any rational credence function Q, and any propositions A and C with Q(A)>0, Q(A→C)=Q(C|A). Now pick any A and C with both P(A∧C)>0 and P(A∧¬C)>0. The derivation goes:

(1) By the law of total probability,
P(A→C) = P(A→C | C)·P(C) + P(A→C | ¬C)·P(¬C).

(2) Consider the credence function you get by conditioning P on C. Conditionalizing a rational credence function should still give a rational credence function, so E applies to it too. Under that conditionalized function, the probability of C given A is 1. So E gives us P(A→C | C) = 1.

(3) Do the same thing conditioning on ¬C. Under that conditionalized function, the probability of C given A is 0. So E gives us P(A→C | ¬C) = 0.

(4) Plug (2) and (3) into (1): P(A→C) = 1·P(C) + 0·P(¬C) = P(C).

(5) But E applied to the original P says P(A→C) = P(C|A). So P(C|A) = P(C).

That would mean that for any A and C as long as A∧C and A∧¬C are both possible, A tells you nothing about C. But that's nuts: take A = "the coin was flipped" and C = "it landed heads." Presumably P(C|A)=1/2 while P(C) is less than 1/2, so knowing it was flipped changes the probability. The derivation says they must be equal. So something's gone wrong. Is the argument valid? And if it's valid, does it succeed in showing that the universal version of the Equation is untenable, rather than just that I've made a technical error?
