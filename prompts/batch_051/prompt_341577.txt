I can't shake the feeling that Fearon-style rationalist explanations for war and the way AI labs race each other have the same three-part skeleton, even though they don't usually get compared.

On the war side, the puzzle is: rational states should prefer almost any bargain to a costly fight, so why shoot? The standard answers are private information plus incentives to misrepresent (both sides bluff about strength or resolve, so they can't find an overlapping bargain), commitment problems (one side can't promise not to use a future power shift to demand more later, so it fights now), and indivisibilities (some stakes just can't be divided for compromise).

On the AI side I think I see each one. Labs hold private information about how close they are and have every reason to hype to investors while hiding real progress from rivals and regulators; any agreement to pause is unenforceable because the lab that secretly keeps going gets a lead, and the others can't trust it not to dictate terms later; and being first to a decisive system seems indivisible, since theres no partial version of winning a race.

Am I looking at a real identity of structure, or just pattern-matching? I want a committed answer—tell me plainly whether the parallel is deep or superficial and where exactly it breaks. Do it as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
