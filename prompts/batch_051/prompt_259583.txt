I'm a grad student in epistemology and next week I have to present a paper at our department's epistemology workshop, and I'm already dreading the Q&A. My thesis is that the dogmatism paradox is best handled by splitting the phrase "can ignore": if you know p, you may rationally refuse to lower your confidence in p when apparent counterevidence shows up, but you can't ignore that counterevidence in your wider inquiries — you update on it as evidence about your own reliability and as a sign something in your evidence set needs rechecking, not as a direct reason against p. The audience is mostly epistemologists: two are knowledge-firsters who think all and only knowledge is evidence, one is a diehard Bayesian who thinks any counterevidence has to move your credence at least a little, and a couple think the paradox is just the price of fallibilism and we should stop looking for a fix. Draft me a prep sheet for the questions: the objections in roughly the order this specific crowd will raise them, phrased the way they'd put them, which ones actually damage me, what to concede up front, two-minute replies, and the question I'm praying nobody asks, with an honest note on where my reply is softest.
