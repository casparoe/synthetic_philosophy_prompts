Okay, here's a task I think rewards actually thinking rather than pattern matching. I want you to act as a philosopher of science who cares about Wigner's puzzle. I'm collecting examples of mathematical concepts or structures that were developed for purely mathematical reasons and then later turned out to be shockingly useful in some empirical or practical area. I already have the obvious ones: pi turning up in the Gaussian distribution and hence in population statistics and error theory; complex numbers in the foundations of quantum mechanics; Hilbert spaces and linear operators as the language of QM; non-Euclidean geometry in general relativity; and Borel sets/measure theory in probability. Don't repeat those or give me near-variants of them.

Wigner himself puts the worry this way in The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences (1960):

"We are in a position similar to that of a man who was provided with a bunch of keys and who, having to open several doors in succession, always hit on the right key on the first or second trial. He became skeptical concerning the uniqueness of the coordination between keys and doors."

I want the examples to be the kind of thing that would make someone feel like the guy with the keys. So first, brainstorm silently for a bit about fields outside physics—chemistry, biology, economics, cryptography, machine learning, whatever—because that's where the non-obvious examples usually live. Then give me exactly five new examples not on my list, each one or two sentences, stating the mathematical concept, where it came from, and where it unexpectedly shows up. No intro, no conclusion, just the five numbered items. At least two should be from outside physics.
