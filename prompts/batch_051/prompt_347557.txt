Here is an argument I have been trying to get straight. It is meant to show that the demand for transparency is not merely an institutional nicety but something participants in a market society owe one another. But I cannot tell whether the argument is tight or whether a slide is buried in it.

(1) To cooperate with another agent is to undertake to be answerable to her for the part you play. That is what distinguishes cooperation from merely doing the same thing, or acting in ways that happen to interlock.
(2) Being answerable to someone for your part in a joint activity requires keeping the reasons for it recoverable by her: you cannot answer for a move if you cannot reconstruct for her what you took yourself to be doing and why.
(3) So anyone who cooperates with others undertakes to keep her reasons recoverable by them.
(4) Keeping your reasons recoverable by others is just making yourself legible to them. An agent is legible when those who interact with her can read her reasons.
(5) Therefore, cooperation involves an undertaking to make oneself legible to one's fellow cooperators.
(6) A modern market society is cooperative in this sense: in exchanges, employment, credit, and platform-mediated dealings, strangers act on shared terms and hold one another answerable through reputation, contract, and regulation.
(7) Hence, insofar as we participate in market society, we undertake to make ourselves legible to the strangers with whom we thereby cooperate.
(8) But legibility, so understood, requires more than answering truthfully when someone asks. It requires presenting one's reasons, valuations, and intentions in advance, in a form others can act on without special inquiry.

The conclusion is that we are under a standing obligation to make ourselves proactively transparent to the strangers we transact with, and not merely answerable after the fact.

Is this argument valid? If it is not, say exactly which step fails, explain what distinction it collapses, and restate that step in its clearest defensible form. Then say whether the argument, once corrected, still supports the conclusion or supports only some weaker claim.
