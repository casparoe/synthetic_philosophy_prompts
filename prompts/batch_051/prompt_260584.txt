Here's an argument about whether buying factory-farmed chicken is wrong that I've been going back and forth on, and I'd like to know whether it's valid and, if not, exactly which step breaks.

(1) If buying this pack of chicken has no expected effect on how much suffering the chicken industry produces, then buying it isn't wrong in virtue of the animals.
(2) One person's purchase doesn't by itself cause the industry to raise or kill any additional chickens: the supply chain responds to aggregate demand over time, and no single purchase among millions is the one that tips the system into a bigger production run.
(3) So buying this chicken has no expected effect on how much suffering the industry produces.
(4) So buying the chicken isn't wrong in virtue of the animals.

My friend insists the argument is sound and that people who still call it wrong are just squeamish. I have a nagging feeling that (2) and (3) slide between "doesn't by itself make the actual difference" and "has no expected effect" — like there's a tiny probability my purchase is the one that triggers the next shipment, and a tiny probability times a huge harm might still count as an expected effect. But my friend says expected effects are just chances, not actual suffering, and morality cares about what actually happens, not about chances. Wether that's right is what I'm unsure about. If the argument fails, say which step is the weak one and what the strongest repair of that step would need to look like.
