I recently sat in on an argument between two colleagues — both software engineers, neither a lawyer — about a licensing problem, and I have been unable to settle it in my own head since. They are designing a product in which a proprietary analysis tool drives a simulation engine that is distributed under the GNU GPL. The tool never links against the engine's code: the two run as separate processes, and the tool generates input files in the engine's format, invokes the engine, and parses its output. The tool has no function apart from the engine; it was built purely as a front end to it.

Nadia argued that the arrangement is unproblematic and that the tool is a distinct, original work. It shares no code with the engine, communicates only through a documented interface, and is no more a derivative of the engine than a novel drafted in a word processor is a derivative of the word processor. If functional dependence made something a derivative work, she said, then no one could write proprietary software for a free operating system without the kernel's license swallowing every application.

Daniel replied that this is precisely the dodge copyleft exists to prevent, and that Nadia's test would let anyone circumvent the GPL by wrapping a GPL core behind a separate process or a local socket. The relevant unit, he said, is the designed system the user actually receives. The tool is an adaptation of the engine's functionality — an extension written to exploit it — and the fact that it talks across a process boundary rather than through linked object code is only a packaging detail. He claimed the license's own talk of works based on the program is not tied to how the bytes are arranged on disk.

Nadia answered that Daniel's functional-dependence principle proves too much, because it would make every client of a GPL-licensed web service a derivative work simply by being the counterpart the service was built for — a conclusion Daniel himself does not accept. She also insisted on the need for an objective line, and said the only objective line available is the technical one: either you distribute the protected expression itself, or you distribute something you wrote that merely interoperates with it. If that line is too easy to game, she said, the right remedy is to change the license or the law, not to stretch the concept of a derivative work until it covers whatever feels parasitic.

Daniel came back that derivative works have never required the copying of literal text — a translation or an adaptation is derivative without a single identical sentence — and that nothing in the purpose of copyleft allows the boundary of a modified version to be redrawn by process isolation. The tool, he said, passes every test for being derivative except the mechanical one: it has no independent market, no intelligible function apart from the engine, and its authors have effectively rebuilt the interface as an extension rather than used it as a consumer.

They went around once more on whether authorial intent matters, whether economic substitution matters, and how much weight the distinction between a mere aggregate and two parts of a single program can actually bear. Neither moved. I came away unsure whether the dispute is ultimately about the law, the concept of a derivative work, or the purpose of copyleft — and which of those questions decides the others.

Please adjudicate this exchange as a careful philosopher of law with real technical fluency in how software is built. First, work through the strongest version of each side's position rather than the caricature of it. Then go move by move: for each specific claim in the exchange, say who is right or wrong about that claim, what each side concedes or assumes without argument, and what both of them are missing. Finally, give a considered verdict on whether there is a principled boundary here, where the most defensible version of it lies, and which of the two is more right overall — or whether their question is badly framed in a way that explains the impasse. Think carefully, and do not collapse the legal, interpretive, and normative questions into one another.
