Okay here's the thing. My friend Mara has this hobbyhorse about idolatry—she thinks it's an ordinary, secular category, not just golden calves and graven images. Over the years I've been keeping a running list of the cases she calls idolatry and the ones she doesn't, and I still can't find the rule. You're a professional philosopher who works on value theory and the philosophy of love; take your time, think it through slowly, and maybe start by brainstorming candidate rules before you settle on one.

Cases she says ARE idolatry (or misplaced worship—she uses the terms interchangably):
1. A finance guy who doesn't need or spend the money; growing the number is his scoreboard. He once said he'd feel like a failure at a billion because someone else has two, and he clearly meant it.
2. A writer who admits she no longer believes what she publishes, but keeps writing so she'll be cited after she's gone; she says her legacy is the only part of her that will survive.
3. A widower who treats his late wife's coats, keys, and saved voicemails as sacred objects, greets them each morning, and has let every living friendship lapse because, he says, she's still there in the things.

Cases she says are NOT idolatry:
4. A gardener who spends nearly all her spare time and money on her garden, talks to her plants though she knows they can't hear her, and genuinely mourned an elm that had to come down.
5. A mother who left her career to care full-time for a disabled son; it's a big part of who she is, but she keeps up her friendships, does a little volunteering, and treats the devotion as one thread in a larger life.
6. A monk who gave away everything and lives in a contemplative order, mostly silent and at prayer.

The case I actually care about: a retired actuary in our town spends every day on his stamp collection. It isn't about money, status, or killing time; he loves the stamps themselves—the watermarks, cancelations, tiny printing flaws. He has no family, no close friends, and says he doesn't want any; the collection is the center of his days.

What's Mara going to say about the stamp collector? More importantly, what rule or theory of idolatry is she running, what other rules fit her track record just as well, and which of them diverge on this case? If you could present her one more case to settle which rule she actually holds, what would it be, and how would each rule handle it? I'd love an actual best guess, not just a menue.
