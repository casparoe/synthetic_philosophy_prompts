Youre a philosopher of law who works on reparative justice and speech acts. Ive noticed a pattern I dont have a name for, and I want you to take it seriously, not just slap a label on it.

Governments keep apologizing for historical wrongs—slavery, colonial massacres, forced removals of children, that sort of thing. Sometimes the apology is elaborate: it names the wrong, speaks in the first-person plural, says the state was wrong. But in the same speech or the same settlement, the government adds that the apology is not an admission of legal liability, or that it cannot be used in any later court action, or that no compensation should be inferred from it. I dont think this is just hypocrisy, because the two moves seem to be doing two different jobs: one makes the apology count as a serious moral acknowledgement, the other switches off its legal force. And they are often put together so cleanly that the structure looks deliberate.

Does this two-level move have an established name? I know about non-apologies and about safe apology statutes, but those arent quite it—one is about failing to apologize, the other is about evidence rules. Im after the thing where the state takes the moral credit of apologizing while explicitly ruling out the legal consequences that would normally follow from admitting a wrong. If the name exists, map its exact boundaries against those neighbours; if it doesnt, coin one and define it with two or three boundary cases. Before you settle on an answer, brainstorm the closest concepts and say exactly where each falls short. And as you go, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on state apologies, and dont present a contested reading of the governments motives as the consensus view.
