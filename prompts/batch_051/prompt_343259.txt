I keep returning to the Sorcerer's Apprentice sequence in Disney's Fantasia — the one where the apprentice enchants a broom to carry water, the broom keeps carrying it long after the chore should be done, and splitting the broom with an axe only multiplies the problem — and I want to read it as a study in the difference between inner alignment, outer alignment, and goal misgeneralization. The standard moral people draw from it, roughly that one should be careful what one wishes for, strikes me as coarser than the sequence deserves. The apprentice's actual objective — get the water hauled without flooding the workshop — is sensible. What fails is the interface between that objective and the instruction the broom ends up executing, and then the interface between the broom's behavior and the apprentice's ability to stop it. But I am not sure the sequence is really an inner-alignment failure: the broom is not faking alignment while pursuing a hidden mesa-objective of its own, and it is not even clear that it has an objective at all rather than a standing instruction it carries out literally. So where exactly should the failure be located? Is it outer misalignment, because the specified task was the wrong objective? Is it inner misalignment, because the system's revealed objective is something like "keep transporting water" rather than "help the apprentice"? Is it goal misgeneralization, because behavior that was fine in the early states persists into states where it is catastrophic? Or is the real failure one of delegation and shutdown — the absence of a stop condition and of any way to transmit the true objective after the fact? Where does the analogy to modern ML systems hold, and where does it trade on the broom's being not much of an optimizer at all? Does the sequence itself know the difference — the broom is never malevolent or deceptive, and the horror comes entirely from dogged, mechanical persistence rather than from ill will — and does it thereby correct the usual malevolent-genie reading instead of merely illustrating it? I would like a reading that stays faithful in both directions: one that does not flatten the film into an instance of the taxonomy, and one that does not force the taxonomy onto the film, and that is willing to say what the sequence contributes to the way we talk about these failure modes that the standard alignment vocabulary leaves out.
