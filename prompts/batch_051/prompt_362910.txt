I am taking a course this term on American political culture, and I have developed a heuristic that I can no longer tell is an instance of intellectual hygiene or a bias dressed up as a rule of method. My policy is this: whenever an argument, whether in a reading, in seminar discussion, or in political commentary, invokes American exceptionalism as part of its explanation or justification, I discount that argument substantially, often almost to zero. I treat the phrase as a warning sign that rhetorical work is being done under the cover of analysis.

My reason is that the expression seems to me to move among three different claims depending on what the argument needs. In its first sense it is the banal descriptive observation that the United States differs from other countries; as a friend in the course keeps pointing out, this is true of every country and can hardly support any interesting conclusion by itself. In its second sense it is a substantive historical thesis: the country developed without a feudal past, was organized around liberal principles rather than inherited hierarchy, has remained unusually religious relative to comparable nations, and so on. In its third sense it is a normative claim: the United States has a special role, mission, or set of obligations in the world. My suspicion is that most ordinary invocations of exceptionalism borrow empirical plausibility from the historical thesis while doing their real argumentative work in the normative claim, and that the term thrives precisely because a speaker can slide among the senses when challenged.

A friend in the same course says I am committing a genetic fallacy, or at least poisoning the well: I am refusing to engage with arguments because of a phrase they contain rather than because of their actual reasons, and I am letting a prior suspicion substitute for the analysis I claim to value. I have some sympathy with him, because I cannot reconstruct every argument from scratch, and the heuristic does protect me from vague appeals to national character. But I also suspect the policy could be stated more carefully rather than simply abandoned. Under what conditions is it reliable to discount an argument that appeals to American exceptionalism, and under what conditions does the discounting become a rationalization for dismissing views I already dislike? Is there a principled way, in practice, to separate the descriptive, historical, and normative senses without ending up in exactly the analysis I was trying to shortcut? And, given that I am a student with limited time and no deep background in comparative history, am I entitled to a heuristic as strong as the one I have been using?
