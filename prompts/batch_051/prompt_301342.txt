I'm a software engineer on a civic-tech team, not a philosopher, and I could use help making sense of a system I'm building. Our city runs citizens' assemblies on contentious local issues, and my team owns the sortition platform that selects the panel. We draw from the full municipal registry using a cryptographically seeded random number generator, then stratify the draw so the panel roughly matches the city's profile on age, gender, district, and housing tenure. Over the past year I've had to implement features that feel like small departures from a pure lottery: oversampling groups too small to appear at all under a straightforward draw, letting the assembly set part of its own agenda, excluding people with direct conflicts of interest, and compensating participants so that who stays isn't just a matter of who can afford the time. Each change felt obviously right in isolation, but together they seem to pull against the idea that the lottery itself is what makes the body legitimate: the more we engineer the panel, the less it is a random microcosm of the public, and the more our own design choices seem to carry normative weight. I can code the constraints, but I don't know what theory is driving them.
