During a meeting of the animal care and use committee I serve on, two other members got into an argument while we were revising our policy for assigning protection levels to species used in research, and I was asked to referee it. (1) One proposed using brain size to set default protections — bigger brain, more protection — since brain size correlates with cognitive sophistication and is quantitative and already in our records. (2) The other replied that moral status should track capacities such as the ability to suffer, not anatomy that merely correlates with them; using brain size, he said, is like using skull shape to assign rights. (3) The first conceded the principle but said we need a workable proxy: we cannot test every species' cognition before approving a protocol, and a measurable, comparable criterion beats intuitive guesses. (4) The second answered that the proxy is unreliable exactly where it matters: bird brains are small but densely packed with neurons, some small-brained species outperform larger-brained ones, so raw size would underprotect corvids and parrots and overprotect large-bodied mammals. (5) The first said this is why we would use brain size relative to body size — an encephalization quotient — rather than raw grams. (6) The second said the quotient is a statistical construct that says nothing about what an animal can actually do; better to assess capacities directly — pain, flexible behavior, learning. (7) The first replied that capacity judgments are exactly what cannot be standardized across species, and when a proxy correlates with the trait we care about, using it is no more illicit than using a thermometer to track a fever. (8) The second said a thermometer measures the thing itself, while brain size measures a weak statistical correlate; useful as one piece of evidence among many, but not as the thing that sets protection levels. (9) The first then moderated his position: brain size would be only a rebuttable presumption, so a small-brained species could be upgraded by direct evidence. (10) The second replied that a rebuttable presumption still puts the burden on advocates of small-brained species, and that the practical argument is doing unacknowledged moral work. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say who is right about what, and identify what both parties are missing.
