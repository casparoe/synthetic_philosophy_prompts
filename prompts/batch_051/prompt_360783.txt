A standard way of setting up Kripke's rule-following problem runs as follows. A subject has so far computed sums only for numbers smaller than 57. A skeptic points out that the subject's entire past behavior is equally compatible with her meaning addition and with her meaning quus, where quus is the function that agrees with addition for pairs of numbers both smaller than 57 and yields 5 for every other pair. When the subject now answers 125 to 58 and 67, that answer is ordinarily treated not as an observation that settles what she meant but as something that itself needs justification.

Now consider the following claim, made concrete by choosing a particular system—a person, a neural network trained on arithmetic, or a program that carries out an algorithm: there is a fact of the matter about whether that system has internalized addition, as opposed to one of the infinitely many gerrymandered functions that agree with addition on every input the system has so far encountered. Design the strongest empirical test, or battery of tests, you can for that claim. Specify what result would count as evidence that the system has addition rather than a deviant extension, what result would favor the skeptical reading that nothing has yet distinguished addition from the deviant functions, and then say exactly what a favorable result would fail to settle. Does adding further novel cases ever close the gap, or is the skeptical challenge aimed at the gap itself rather than at any shortage of data? If no behavioral evidence can close the gap, say whether you would draw the skeptical conclusion that there is no fact of the matter here, or resist that conclusion, and on what grounds.
