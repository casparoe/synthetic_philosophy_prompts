I write fiction for a living, and I serve on the board of a midsized private foundation whose endowment has long supported literary translation and small-press fiction. I am more practiced at inventing moral dilemmas than at settling real ones, but I have been asked to draft the written opinion that will be circulated before our next meeting, and I want the reasoning in it to be clean. The founder of the foundation died three years ago, and her daughter, a software engineer in her early forties, inherited the seat. She has now proposed that the foundation stop making grants to writers and move its annual disbursement of roughly nine million dollars to a consortium of global health charities working on malaria prevention and maternal and neonatal care in low-income countries. Her argument, though she would not put it this way, is essentially Peter Singer's drowning-child case: a child who could be saved at modest cost is not made less urgent by being far away, and the choice to keep funding literature while preventable deaths continue is made comfortable only by distance, custom, and the impression that the pond is elsewhere. The rest of the board is divided. Some reply that the foundation exists to carry out a specific charitable purpose, that its grantees have built multi-year budgets on promised support, and that abandoning them now would turn our own institution's neglect into a slow drowning of a different kind. Others suspect that several of us are looking for a principled way to keep the money in a world we like.

The proposal has been placed on the agenda as a formal motion, and I am to circulate a short decision memo ahead of the vote. Draft that memo for me. It should recommend a concrete course of action: approve the shift outright, reject it, preserve the literary mission while redirecting a defined portion of the annual disbursement, or adopt a phased plan with explicit conditions and a review date. It should engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than caricaturing it, and it should address the question quietly animating the board: whether a charitable foundation's duty tracks an individual's duty of rescue, or whether institutions may weigh purposes and relationships in ways individuals may not. The board will not be moved by citations or reading lists, so make the argument stand on its own reasoning, and write it in continuous prose, as one would write a letter or an email — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
