Suppose Charles Darwin could be given a working knowledge of modern evolutionary biology and of gradient-based machine learning, and were then asked to referee a disagreement between two researchers. Both accept that natural selection is some kind of optimizer; they disagree about what kind, and about whether gradient descent is a better model of learning than natural selection is.

The first researcher argues that natural selection is, by the standards of numerical optimization, a poor optimizer. Gradient descent exploits the local geometry of a loss surface: at each step it follows the direction of steepest descent, so in a high-dimensional and reasonably well-behaved problem it converges quickly toward a low-error region. Natural selection, by contrast, is a derivative-free stochastic search. Mutations are proposed without any estimate of the local slope of the fitness landscape; most are deleterious and are simply discarded. Selection can only compare the realized reproductive success of the variants that happen to arise, so a population climbs whatever small hill it happens to be on and easily gets trapped on local peaks. The process has no way to see a higher peak across a valley of lower fitness, and it cannot plan a route through temporarily deleterious intermediate genotypes. What counts as fitness is also constantly changing, because the environment changes and because the presence of one variant alters the selective value of others. The first researcher concludes that for a well-defined learning task, gradient descent is the more serious optimizer, while natural selection is mostly a cautionary tale about path-dependence and short-sightedness.

The second researcher replies that this comparison is rigged, because it assumes exactly what is in question: that there is a single fixed objective that a rational optimizer ought to climb toward. Natural selection has no gradient to compute because, in most biologically relevant settings, the mapping from genotype to reproductive success is nonsmooth, stochastic, and nonstationary. Gradient descent adjusts parameters with respect to one loss function, whereas an evolving population is simultaneously adjusting millions of correlated variables with respect to a moving and partly self-created objective. The very properties the first researcher calls defects, such as localization and myopia, may be what allow evolution to retain variation, cross adaptive valleys by drift and recombination, and respond to novel conditions without collapsing onto a brittle optimum. This researcher adds that gradient descent has at least as much to learn from natural selection as natural selection has to learn from smooth optimization, especially if the goal is open-ended learning rather than static task-fitting.

Given Darwin's own mature commitments about variation, heredity, the struggle for existence, the gradual formation of complex organs, and the analogy with domestic breeding, which elements of each position would he most plausibly accept, and which would he resist? In particular, do the following. First, assess whether Darwin would accept the premise that natural selection has, or should have, a predefined optimum toward which it climbs, and explain how his answer bears on the rest of the dispute. Second, explain how Darwin's insistence on slow, stepwise, imperceptible change interacts with the local-optimum objection: would he regard a valley of low fitness as an insuperable barrier, and what resources in his own account of variation and domestication could he plausibly extend to answer it? Third, address the role of artificial selection: does the fact that breeders pursue a deliberate target, while natural selection does not, show that gradient descent is the better analogy for breeding and that natural selection is something else entirely, or does Darwin's use of domestic races suggest a different lesson?
