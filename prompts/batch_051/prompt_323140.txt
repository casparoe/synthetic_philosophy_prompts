I direct the environmental health review office of a midsize city of about 200,000 people. My office approves routine permits for activities that impose small but real risks on people who were never asked to consent: aerial or truck-mounted mosquito spraying over residential blocks, a wastewater bypass into a stream used by swimmers and fishers after heavy rain, a cell repeater mounted on a building facing a daycare, propane burners at street festivals, and similar applications. We review roughly a dozen a week, and the stakes are high in both directions: an overly permissive decision can mean elevated rates of asthma, poisoning, or injury, while an overly restrictive one generates lawsuits, delayed public works, and complaints about overreach. The current practice is a loose cost-benefit test, and our staff usually approve unless there is organized opposition.

I want to replace that with a reusable decision procedure that operationalizes the best philosophical thinking about the ethics of risk imposition without consent. The procedure has to be usable by a three-person review team with no ethics training, in under an hour per file, by someone doing the job for the first time. The city attorney and the medical director must co-sign any decision that approves a non-consensual risk above the city's measured ordinary-background level, so the procedure must tell reviewers when a signature is required and what those signatories are being asked to certify.

The procedure should address, in a form a nonspecialist can apply, at least these distinctions: the difference between ex ante and ex post justification; the idea that a risk below a de minimis threshold may not require consent at all; the reasonable-person test for the acceptability of an imposed risk; whether the risk is reciprocal (part of a shared background scheme from which the affected parties also benefit) or instead falls on identifiable losers; whether repeated small risks to the same person must be aggregated; and the difference between risking a harm and actually inflicting one. The procedure should not let "expected benefits exceed expected costs" settle a case on its own, since aggregating benefits to some people against harms to other people is precisely what consent would normally license and exactly what is absent here.

The deliverable should be an ordered set of tests or checklist questions with explicit decision rules. For each test, state in one sentence which philosophical idea it operationalizes and which refinement it deliberately drops. Flag every point at which judgment cannot be eliminated, and say who in the office must make the call at that point. Then apply the procedure to three worked cases: mosquito spraying that drifts onto adjacent patios where children play; a cell repeater facing a daycare on a building the daycare does not own; and a storm-driven wastewater bypass into a swimming stream. One of the three worked cases should be one in which the procedure tells us to reject an application we would previously have approved without difficulty. End by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of cases it will not settle and the kinds of wrong it will still fail to catch.
