I write about minds and machines for a living, and an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-AI reading group has asked me to come defend a piece I did about the Gödelian analogy in Gödel, Escher, Bach. My argument is that people defend that analogy for the wrong thing: instead of the usual claim that self-reference gets you self-awareness or consciousness, I argued the transferable part is much narrower — a Gödel sentence is a toy model of a system carrying a representation of a limit on what it can itself derive, and that looks more like a blueprint for an agent that knows when to stop and defer than like a story about a self-aware mind. The room will be split. Half are logicians and formal AI people who think any Gödelian talk about minds is a category mistake and will demand to know exactly which formal property is supposed to transfer; the other half are Hofstadter-leaning cognitive scientists who think I've drained the analogy of the one thing that made it interesting. I'm not an academic, so prep me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased in each camp's own voice, what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest joint in my own argument I should be ready to defend rather than hope nobody notices.
