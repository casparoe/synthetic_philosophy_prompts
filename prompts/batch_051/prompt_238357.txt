A colleague has been pressing me with an argument about promissory obligation, and I have come to think the conclusion is impossible, though I cannot find the error. I want a diagnosis before I incorporate any of this into my own work.

The argument:

1. If I have a promissory obligation, it must have its source in one of the following: the promisee — her reliance, her interests, her standing to demand performance; the promisor — some act or state of my own; or the social practice of promising — the conventions that constitute promise-making and give breach its normative consequences.

2. Consider a promise made to someone who then dies before performance, where the promised act is to be done after her death. The source cannot be the promisee. The dead no longer rely, form expectations, or suffer; there are no experiential states left to be made worse. Nor can they release me. If the obligation were held in the promisee, there is nothing left in her now to hold it.

3. The source cannot be the social practice of promising, at least not for the part of the obligation that makes breach a wrong against this particular promisee. A practice rule can tell us why a community would blame me or why it wants the convention upheld, but that is a wrong against the practice or its participants, not a directed wrong to the person who is gone. And if the promise were made outside any shared practice, the practice account would not even get started.

4. The source cannot be the promisor's act of will. A bare volition to be bound cannot generate a genuine obligation; if it could, I could manufacture obligations at will and dissolve them the same way. My intending to remain bound is not the same as my being bound, since an intention is mine to revise, while a promise hands another person authority over my future conduct.

5. Therefore, a promise to a now-deceased person, to be performed after her death, creates no obligation. Its apparent bindingness is an illusion sustained by grief and social expectation.

6. But this is precisely the kind of case I need a theory of promising to respect. I promised my collaborator as she was dying that I would finish and publish the translation we had begun together. If I leave the manuscript in a drawer, I do not merely fail to honor a wish or fail to produce something valuable; I break faith with her. The wrong is a wrong against her — the specific, directed wrong of breaking a promise.

7. So at least one step above is false, or the argument slips between two senses of some term without my noticing. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost the rest of the view of promising? If the natural answer is to keep my conviction in step 6, I want to know which premise I am paying with.
