Does Kripke's sceptical solution to the rule-following paradox survive large language models? On Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein, the challenge runs roughly as follows. No finite record of my past arithmetical performances can settle whether by the sign + I meant addition or the gerrymandered function quus, which matches addition on every sum I have so far computed but diverges on some fresh case, returning 5 for 68 + 57 where addition returns 125. So no fact about me in isolation—my past behaviour, my dispositions, or my occurrent mental states—makes it true that I meant addition rather than quus. Kripke's sceptical solution concedes the sceptical point and then relocates correctness: my answer 125 is rightly called correct not because a fact about me makes it true that I meant addition, but because my answer accords with how the community of speakers among whom I learned to speak would go on. Rule-following and meaning are thus understood socially, as a matter of agreement in judgements within a shared form of life.

Now consider this development. Large language models are trained on a vast corpus of human text. Without being initiated into our practices, subject to our corrections, or sharing our form of life, they routinely produce continuations the human community would endorse: they finish arithmetical series, extend terms in line with ordinary usage, and apply rules in ways we treat as correct. They can even be fine-tuned on our corrections without ever becoming participants in the practice, and they are not normally regarded as fellow speakers who are answerable to a rule.

Does Kripke's sceptical solution survive this development?
