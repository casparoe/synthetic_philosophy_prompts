I am an undergraduate currently taking an upper-division seminar on the ethics of consent, and I help run a 75-minute discussion group of 16 students connected to the course. The course has already worked through coercion (threats versus offers, the baseline problem) and competence (intoxication, cognitive disability, age), but it has spent almost no time on deception. I want the session I run next week to make participants feel the force of a specific problem in the theory of consent: when one person's "yes" is based on a false belief that the other party deliberately created or knowingly exploited, everyone agrees that some deceptions invalidate consent, but no one has a satisfying way of saying which deceptions those are. In particular, I want the group to discover for themselves that a natural principle—roughly, that deception invalidates consent whenever it concerns something the consent-giver would have treated as a reason to refuse, even if it makes no difference to the risks, the experience, or the identity of the other party—is initially very attractive in easy cases and then becomes uncomfortable once applied to cases of "harmless" deception. The point of the session should not be to settle the principle; it should be to feel why the dispute is about what consent is for (protecting a person's control over the encounter, versus ensuring adequately informed choice about the encounter itself) and why neither side can win by simple intuition.

Please design a runnable 75-minute classroom activity for this group. It may be a structured ranking exercise, a role-play, a forced-choice deliberation, or a game with small fictitious stakes (no real money). The activity should satisfy the following:

1. There is no lecture up front; participants should not hear the name of the "dealbreaker principle" or the control-versus-information contrast until the debrief.
2. The first phase should present a case so clear that nearly all participants agree the consent is invalid, and participants should be required to articulate the rule behind their judgment. The later phases should then present variants that strain that same rule from different directions (for example, deception about a feature that is a stated dealbreaker but never affects the encounter; deception about a feature the consent-giver never thought to ask about; and a case in which the deception makes the act less risky rather than more).
3. Include the rules or roles, the materials (which should be simple paper cards or handouts), and a timeline with approximate minutes, including a debrief of at least 20 minutes.
4. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the conceptual points: the distinction between invalidating someone's consent and wronging them in some other way, the difference between deception about the nature or identity involved in the act and deception about peripheral facts, and the fact that the same principle can be extended to cases the participants do not want to count as invalid.
5. The activity should translate to a non-sexual framing for any participant who is uncomfortable with sexual cases, and the facilitator should be able to run the same structure with, for example, medical, financial, or research-participation scenarios.
6. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run—especially participants who settle the easy case with an unprincipled "it depends," participants who refuse to call any harmless deception invalidating and so never feel the attraction of the principle, and participants who conflate "the consent was invalid" with "the deceiver should be punished"—and give concrete instructions for handling each.
7. End with an honest note on which feature of the real phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must therefore supply in the debrief. I expect the main gap is the stakes: in the classroom the coercion-free, low-stakes setting makes invalidating consent feel like a merely theoretical classification, whereas in real life it marks the difference between permissible and seriously wrongful conduct.

The activity should not depend on named readings or citations; any positions it needs can be stated in ordinary terms.
