I hold a view about the ethics of belief, and I want a systematic account of what could refute it rather than a defense or rejection of it.

The view has three parts. First, doubt is a moral duty: for any belief that plays a non-negligible causal role in how one treats other people, one is morally obligated to subject it to serious, good-faith scrutiny before acting on it. Second, the scrutiny required is active rather than passive: it is not enough to be disposed to revise the belief if a decisive objection happens to arrive; one must have made a genuine effort to find the strongest arguments against the belief and to consider whether the belief persists only because that effort has never been made. Third, the duty is stakes-weighted and its breach is truth-insensitive: the obligation grows heavier as the foreseeable harm of acting on a false belief grows larger, and one is blameworthy for breaching it even when the belief turns out to be true and the action turns out to be correct. I accept the consequence that, on this standard, most of our confidently held moral and political beliefs are morally impermissible to act on, because almost no one has actually discharged the duty for them.

My question is not whether the view is right but what would count against it. I want a detailed map of its vulnerabilities: what would have to be true for the package to fail, and what would merely force a revision of one clause. Specify the kinds of considerations that could do the work — arguments about the structure of moral obligation, hypothetical cases of justified unexamined belief, empirical findings about human cognition or decision-making under time pressure, or results about whether the duty can be known to be discharged. Distinguish the pressures that its own defenders would concede from the objections that only critics would press. And tell me whether the view, as stated, has been insulated by vagueness in "serious scrutiny" or by the open-endedness of the duty, such that no possible case could decisively count against it; if so, say whether that insulation is a feature of the position or a defect in its formulation.

The defeat I personally would find most telling is this: if it turned out that no finite agent could ever know when the duty has been discharged, the view would collapse into a counsel of despair rather than a live norm. Tell me whether that is the deepest threat to the view, or whether some other threat cuts closer to its core.
