Last night a friend and I had a long argument about whether the history of science can properly be described as progress toward the truth when most of our best theories are, strictly speaking, false. The argument kept turning on the fine structure of the Miller–Tichý objection to Popper's definition of verisimilitude, and I came away unsure how much of the usual one-sentence gloss of that objection is accurate. I am now grading an exam answer on exactly this material, and I want to assess it on its own terms rather than by my own sense of the topic.

Exam question: "Popper proposed that we can compare false theories by their closeness to the truth, or verisimilitude. Explain his qualitative definition in terms of truth content and falsity content, and critically assess the objection due to Miller and Tichý. Does the failure of Popper's definition show that the idea of one false theory being closer to the truth than another false theory is incoherent, or only that his particular way of spelling it out was defective?"

Student answer: "Popper's problem was to explain how a succession of false theories could still count as progress toward the truth. He proposed that every theory has a truth content, meaning the class of true consequences it entails, and a falsity content, meaning the class of false consequences it entails. On his qualitative definition, theory A has greater verisimilitude than theory B just in case A's truth content includes all of B's truth content and A's falsity content is included in B's falsity content, with at least one of these inclusions strict. Miller and Tichý showed that this definition cannot work. They proved that for any two false theories, the two theories always have exactly the same truth content, so the first condition is trivially satisfied and the definition reduces to comparing falsity contents. But every false theory has a nonempty falsity content, and since the falsity contents of any two false theories also coincide, the definition never yields a strict comparison between false theories. It follows that no false theory is closer to the truth than another false theory. The right way to salvage verisimilitude is to identify it with logical probability: the more probable a theory is, the closer it is to the truth, because a highly probable theory has few false consequences and so is more likely to be true."

Please grade this answer. I want a point-by-point assessment: identify which parts are correct, which parts are wrong or conflated, and explain each error; then assign an overall grade (letter or out of 100) and write the specific comments you would give the student.
