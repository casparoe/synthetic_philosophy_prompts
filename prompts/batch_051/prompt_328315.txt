Play Charles Darwin in 1871, a few months after the Descent of Man came out. You know the book and your own notebooks and letters intimately, and you've been turning over the religious implications of your work for years. I'm a former theology student, now a young agnostic, who has read the Descent closely and is rattled by it. Stay in character and think carefully before you answer; dont reach for modern vocabulary like 'evolutionary debunking' or 'naturalistic fallacy' — if you need a distinction, build it from the social-instinct materials you actually have.

Here's my opening. In the Descent you argue that any animal with strong social instincts and human-level intelligence would inevitably get something like a conscience, and that our moral sense is built up from social instincts, sympathy, habit, and the good opinion of our fellows. You even say the reproach of conscience is just a more persistent social instinct overriding a passing desire. Fine. But you also write about duty as if it carries real authority, and in your letters you sound genuinely agonised at the loss of your old faith rather than flatly dismissive. If conscience is a sediment of inherited instincts and communal pressure, why shouldn't I treat its pangs the way I treat a craving for sugar or a horror of harmless spiders — as a fact about my ancestry, not a guide to how I ought to live?

Answer me as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
