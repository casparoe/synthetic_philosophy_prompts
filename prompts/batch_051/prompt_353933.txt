I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I have convinced myself that the principle of indifference is internally inconsistent. I suspect the error is mine, but I cannot locate it. Here is my argument:

(1) The principle of indifference says that when no evidence favors any one of several mutually exclusive possibilities over another, they should be assigned equal probability.

(2) Suppose a chord of a circle is chosen at random. The principle should determine the probability that this chord is longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle.

(3) A chord can be described by its two endpoints. Since no point on the circumference is favored over any other, each endpoint is equally likely to lie anywhere on the circumference. Fixing one endpoint, the chord is longer than a side exactly when the smaller arc between the endpoints is more than 120 degrees, which places the other endpoint in the 120-degree arc directly opposite the first — one third of the circumference. So the probability is 1/3.

(4) But a chord can equally well be described by choosing a radius and then choosing a point along that radius as the chord's midpoint, taking the chord perpendicular to the radius at that point. By the same principle, the radius has no favored direction and the midpoint has no favored position along the radius. A chord is longer than a side exactly when its midpoint is within half the radius of the center, which is half of the available segment, so the probability is 1/2.

(5) These are two equally natural applications of the principle of indifference to the same random chord, yet they give different probabilities.

(6) Therefore the principle of indifference is inconsistent and must be rejected.

Is the argument valid? If it is not, which numbered step fails, and what would a correct replacement of that step say?
