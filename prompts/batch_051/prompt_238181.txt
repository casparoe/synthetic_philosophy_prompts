I'm in a small seminar on the international relations of emerging technology, and we've been stuck on Fearon's Rationalist Explanations for War (International Organization, 1995) for two weeks. The fight is about whether his three rationalist mechanisms — private information plus incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and issue indivisibility — actually transfer to the current AI competition between the U.S. and China (and maybe the leading labs). One side says the transfer is tight: the U.S. is like a declining power in Fearon's preventive-war passage, where he gives the logic that a state losing ground may rationally strike first because the rising side cannot credibly promise to accept a stable bargain once its capabilities overtake the other's. The other side says this is exactly the kind of forcing-foreign-policy-concepts-on-tech that lets the analogy do all the work; our competition is diffuse, there's no agreed war outcome to avoid, and no one has battle outcomes with probabilities to misrepresent.

The instructor proposed an adversarial collaboration, and I'm assigned to draft the joint document. It should be the document the two camps could both sign: a numbered list of claims both accept about AI competition and the Fearon mechanisms; the exact remaining disagreements phrased neutrally; the genuine cruxes (we keep arguing about two: whether there is a bargaining range over future AI leadership at all, and whether verification of model capabilities and intent is bad enough to produce misrepresentation or commitment failure); and for each crux at least one concrete piece of evidence or near-term observation both camps agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about what they'd see. Don't resolve the dispute—just make the disagreement precise enough that someone could run the test later.
