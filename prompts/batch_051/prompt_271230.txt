I'm in an algorithmic fairness seminar and a classmate walked us through an argument that made the whole field feel stuck, and I keep going back and forth on whether it's actually sound or whether she pulled a fast one somewhere. The setup: a binary classifier, say a pretrial risk score that rates people on a 0 to 10 scale and then some cutoff flags some of them as high risk. Two conditions everyone in the room wanted to impose on a fair classifier:

(1) Calibration: the score should mean the same probability for both groups. If people from group A and group B both got a 6, roughly the same fraction of each should actually reoffend; otherwise the number doesn't mean what it says.

(2) Equalized odds: the two kinds of error should fall equally. The false positive rate (flagged high risk but didn't reoffend) and the false negative rate (not flagged but did reoffend) should match across groups.

Then the argument itself. (3) Suppose the groups have different base rates: group A reoffends at 40% and group B at 20%, which is the usual real-world situation. (4) Under (2), each group's overall flag rate is a weighted average of the two common error rates, with the weights being that group's base rate; for any model worth deploying, the true positive rate sits above the false positive rate, so group A's flag rate comes out higher than group B's. (If the two rates are exactly equal the model has no skill, and I'll fold that corner case into (7).) (5) So the high-base-rate group gets flagged more often. (6) But calibration means the flag itself has to mean the same thing in both groups: the fraction of flagged people who actually reoffend, the hit rate, should be the same across groups. (7) Now the arithmetic. In the non-degenerate case, where the model has some true positives and some false positives and a true positive rate above its false positive rate, group A has both a higher base rate and a higher flag rate, and the hit rate formula (Bayer's rule applied to the two groups) forces group A's hit rate above group B's. In the corner case where the two error rates are equal, the flag carries no information about reoffending within a group, so the hit rate in each group is just that group's base rate—and those differ too. Either way, equalized odds pushes the hit rates apart. (8) So calibration and equalized odds can't both hold for any realistic imperfect model, and the fairness debate is demanding two things that are mathematically incompatible.

I'd really like to keep both (1) and (2), so I'm hoping the problem is somewhere in how she gets from (4) to (7), or in the way (6) is worded. But I can't find a step I'm willing to just call false. Is the argument sound? If not, exactly which step goes wrong, and what two things are being run together there? And if it is sound, which of the two conditions should someone actually give up, and what do they owe to the people who lose out under whichever condition they keep?
