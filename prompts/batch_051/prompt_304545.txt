I want to use Shakespeare's Othello in a philosophy-and-literature course to teach the debate about whether there is a morally significant difference between lying and merely misleading someone. I want a reading of the play that is faithful to both sides: the play must not become a mere illustration of the philosophical distinction, and the philosophical distinction should not be stretched to fit the play where it does not fit. The scene I have in mind is what is usually called the temptation scene — the long central scene in which Othello is moved from trusting his new wife to demanding proof of her infidelity, largely through what Iago declines to say outright.

Take the working distinction in the debate to be this. A liar asserts something she believes to be false. A misleader asserts something true, or at least not clearly false, while deliberately arranging for the hearer to reach a false conclusion; for example, a speaker truthfully says that the person in question has gone for the day when the speaker knows the person is dead. The contested question is whether the misleader is morally on a par with the liar, worse, or less blameworthy.

Give me a critical reading organized around the following tasks. First, reconstruct from memory of the play, without quotation, at least two distinct conversational tactics by which Iago moves Othello toward the false belief without straightforwardly asserting it; for each, say whether the lying/misleading distinction classifies it cleanly, or whether the scene exposes something the two categories miss. Second, take a definite position on which side of the moral debate the scene supports: does Iago's method support the conclusion that misleading is no better than lying, or even worse because Othello is made to perform the damning inference himself, or does the play preserve a real moral difference between his early insinuations and his later outright false reports? Third, say honestly where the philosophical distinction fails to fit the world of the play, and whether Shakespeare's dramatic choices suggest he is interested in that failure or indifferent to it. Conclude by saying what the play adds to the philosophical debate that the usual invented examples — court testimony, evasive references, job recommendations — do not capture.

Do not quote from the play; paraphrase the relevant exchanges from memory. End with a final line beginning 'Summary:' that states your overall reading in a single sentence.
