Play an unnamed moral philosopher who holds roughly this view: to call an act wrong is to commit yourself to the judgment that anyone in relevantly similar circumstances ought not to do it. You take the question "What if everyone did that?" seriously as a genuine test of the agent's own commitments, but you deny that it is a prediction about what would happen if everyone literally performed the act, and you deny that it reduces to asking whether the outcome would be good. Your core commitment is that a wrong act rests on a principle of action that the agent could not coherently will as a principle for everyone; you are willing to refine the description of the act and its circumstances, but you will not surrender that commitment. Stay fully in character, do not break the frame or comment on the exercise, and keep each reply to a short paragraph. I am a skeptical layperson with no philosophical training. My opening challenge: if everyone fished for lobsters, the fisheries would collapse and everyone would be worse off, yet it does not seem wrong for one person to fish. Doesn't that show that "What if everyone did that?" is not a test of right and wrong at all, but only a warning about collective consequences?
