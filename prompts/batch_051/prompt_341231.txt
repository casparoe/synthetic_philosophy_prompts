I'm in a philosophy of probability seminar this term and we've spent the last two weeks going around in circles on the Principal Principle (roughly: when you know the objective chance of something, your credence should match it, unless you have special information that trumps it). Before I can even state it properly I feel like I need a working defintion of "objective chance" itself, but the readings keep using the term like its obvious.

Could you give me a definition of objective chance (physical probability, not credence) that classifies the following cases correctly? Some I'm pretty sure are in, some I'm pretty sure are out, and the middle ones I keep flip-flopping on.

Cases I'd call clearly objective chance:
(a) Whether a particular radium atom decays in the next hour; the usual quantum probability.

Cases I'd call clearly not objective chance:
(b) Whether the quadrillionth digit of pi is a 7. There's just a fact of the matter we haven't computed yet.

Borderline cases (I honestly don't know where these belong):
(c) The top card of a well-shuffled deck sitting facedown on the table. The deck is already fixed, but before we looked the shuffle gave it a 1-in-52 chance of being the ace of spades.
(d) A deterministic but chaotic weather model that says "70% chance of rain tomorrow." Is that chance in the world, or just a summary of the model's uncertainty?
(e) A biased coin with a fixed but unknown bias p. Each toss is independent with that same bias, but I don't know p; is there any objective chance over and above p?
(f) A pseudo-random number generator on my laptop whose seed I don't know. It's deterministic under the hood, but the outputs pass statistical tests and for all I can tell it's random.
(g) A crystal ball with a perfect track record tells me before the toss that a fair coin will land heads. It seems like the chance of heads is still 1/2, but my credence after hearing the ball shouldn't be 1/2. The Principal Principle needs an admissibility clause to handle this, and I want a definition of chance that makes that clause feel motivated rather than bolted on.
(h) A setup from a problem set: first a fair lottery picks a bias p for a coin (p is equally likely to be any value between 0 and 1, whatever that means), then the coin is flipped once. The overall probability of heads comes out to 1/2, but I can't tell whether that's the objective chance of a two-level system or just my ignorance about which p got picked.

Give me the best working definition you can, run it against (a)–(h), and be honest about where it gives the wrong answer or no answer. If it conflicts with something I'd naively say about a case, either revise the definition or argue that I should change my verdict. At the end, tell me which of the eight verdicts you're least secure about and why. Then, as the very last line, write \answer{...} with just the letter of that case.
