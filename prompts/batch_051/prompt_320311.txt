Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether states have a right to exclude would-be immigrants, and the argument kept coming back to an analogy. He said a country is like a house: a homeowner may refuse to let a stranger sleep in the spare bedroom, may do so without giving any reason, and is not unjust for it, so a state may refuse to admit foreigners without being unjust. When I objected that a country is not much like a house, he replied that every analogy breaks somewhere and the only question that matters is whether it breaks at a point that affects the conclusion. When I pressed on the fact that a country is not private property, he shifted to a second analogy: a country is like a club. A club may turn away an applicant for no stated reason, because the members have a right to choose with whom they associate; citizens, he said, have the same associational right, so the state may choose whom it lets in.

I want something more precise than my unease with both of these. Please audit the two analogies as arguments for the right to exclude. For each, give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the homeowner or the members, the spare room or the clubhouse, the stranger or the applicant, the door or the admissions process, the key or membership card, the invitation, the property line or club charter, and the basis of the supposed discretion. Then identify where each mapping holds and where it breaks. I especially want to know which features of the source each analogy quietly imports into the target—private ownership, voluntary association, intimate personal space, the fact that a turned-away stranger or applicant can generally go elsewhere, the difference between a short-term guest or clubmate and a prospective member of a political community, and the difference between choosing to join something and being born into it—and whether each of those features is present, absent, or only partly present when the target is a state and a would-be immigrant. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely superficial, and say whether the conclusion—that a state's refusal to admit foreigners is as little in need of justification as a homeowner's refusal to host a stranger—survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If one or both analogies fail, tell me what a better analogy would be and what it would license.
