I chair the standing grants panel of a small public foundation that makes about twenty awards a year. Applications are scored against six published criteria by three external assessors, the panel turns the reports into a total score on a 0–100 scale accurate to a tenth of a point, and in practice the scores are coarse and the totals often conceal more than they reveal: several times a year two applications finish with identical totals, and in other rounds they differ by a fraction of a point that no member believes reflects a real difference in merit. Our founding rules say only that the panel must award funds to the applications that best satisfy the criteria and must record written reasons for every decision. The panel meets for one afternoon per round, all three members must sign the reasons before they leave, and an unsuccessful applicant can request a review in which the procedure itself would be examined. We are split about what to do. Some members want to toss a coin, on the ground that once the rubric has exhausted the relevant considerations there is nothing further for the panel to add; others reply that a coin toss makes a mockery of the requirement to give reasons; others want to fall back on alphabetical order or the date of submission; and one member keeps insisting that a genuinely rational committee would be unable to act at all, the way the ass in the old parable supposedly starved between two equal bales of hay. We need one reusable procedure for every case in which the criteria fail to separate the applications.

Write me that procedure. It should be a step-by-step, repeatable method the panel can apply each time two or more applications cannot be separated on the stated criteria, grounded in what the philosophy of action and the theory of rational choice actually show about choosing in the absence of a preference, from the problem of Buridan's ass onward: what counts as a reason, whether a tie-breaking device changes what is being chosen, and how equal goodness differs from a case in which two proposals are strong in incommensurable ways. The procedure should state the order of operations, which facts about an application may enter at each step, and which tie-breakers are permissible and why. Then work through three test cases: a dead tie on every subcriterion; a tie on total score between a proposal that is excellent in one criterion and weak in another and a proposal that is steadily mediocre across all six; and a near-tie in which the gap is smaller than the assessors' own scoring error. Include at least one case where the procedure instructs the panel to do something its members will dislike, and be explicit about the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgement and a person has to decide. Please write the answer in British English, as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a letter to a colleague; fold the steps and the worked cases into the prose and mark the residual judgements clearly in the text itself.
