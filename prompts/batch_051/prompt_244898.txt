In my work on the semantic paradoxes, I have come to think that the familiar taxonomy is misleading in a specific way. Curry's paradox shows that negation is inessential to paradox: from a transparent truth predicate, the T-schema, and a conditional obeying modus ponens and contraction, one can derive any sentence whatsoever. Yablo's construction purports to show that circularity is inessential as well: an infinite sequence of sentences, each saying that all later sentences are untrue, yields a liar-like antinomy even though no sentence refers to itself. The view I hold is that the real culprit in both cases is the unrestricted T-schema, or the corresponding naive introduction and elimination rules for truth, and that neither negation nor self-reference was ever doing the essential work in the liar family. What would count against this? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they are. In particular, which formal results or diagnoses would show that Curry's paradox does depend on a self-referential fixed point after all, that Yablo's construction is circular in some subtler sense, or that restricting the T-schema is the wrong remedy because it merely relocates the pathology? And tell me honestly whether this diagnosis has been qualified into unfalsifiability—for example, by redescribing every rival diagnosis as a disguised instance of the unrestricted T-schema—and whether that would be a problem for a diagnosis of this kind. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on truth and paradox, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
