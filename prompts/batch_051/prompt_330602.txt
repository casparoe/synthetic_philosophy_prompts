Last night a friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether it is true to say that mosquitoes carry malaria. He took the sentence to be plainly true; I kept objecting that almost no mosquito carries malaria, since only a tiny fraction of mosquitoes ever transmit the disease, and he replied that I was being obtuse, because the sentence says something about mosquitoes as a kind, not about any particular mosquito or any statistic. After the argument, what lingered was not that sentence but an asymmetry I could not stop turning over. The same friend who accepted that mosquitoes carry malaria rejected without hesitation the claim that mosquitoes are female, even though female mosquitoes vastly outnumber malarial mosquitoes. He endorsed the claim that ducks lay eggs, though only some ducks ever do, while dismissing the claim that ducks are female, though female ducks are far more common among ducks than egg-laying ducks. And he accepted that sharks attack bathers on the strength of events rare enough to be newsworthy precisely because they are rare.

So the assertability of these terse generalizations about kinds does not appear to track how many members of the kind actually have the property in question. It tracks something more like whether the property is striking, dangerous, characteristic, or tied to what the kind is for. I would like to know whether this specific phenomenon has a name. I am not after the general term for this kind of sentence, if such a term exists, but for the pattern itself: the fact that some properties can be rare among the members of a kind and still ground a true, uncontroversially accepted generalization, while other properties can be far more common and ground nothing. If it does not have a name, what are the nearest established concepts, how exactly does this pattern differ from them, and where would a sharp account draw the line between the properties that license the generalization and the properties that do not?
