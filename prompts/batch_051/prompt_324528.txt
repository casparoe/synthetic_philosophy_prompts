I have repeatedly encountered an assumption in my work as a novelist and in the craft advice offered to writers, and I would like to learn whether philosophy has examined it under a precise name. The assumption is that there exists a layer of experience that is simply present to a person before interpretation, inference, or language touches it: the pressure of a seat, the exact color of a wall, the particular smell of a room. The corresponding craft claim is that the novelist's task is to render this raw layer faithfully, and that if the rendering succeeds, readers will receive the experience directly, with concepts and judgments arriving only afterward. The same idea appears in warnings against telling a reader what a scene means, as if careful showing could deliver experience uninterpreted, so that any conclusion the reader draws is grounded in material the reader has simply been given.

As a practitioner, I find this puzzling: the selection of a detail is already interpretation, and different selections shape the reader's understanding from the first sentence. But I do not have the vocabulary to state the error precisely. My question, then, is whether this picture has an established name and a developed critique. If the standard objections target a thesis about perception and empirical knowledge, whereas my case concerns literary representation, I want to know how deeply the two are connected: which parts of the assumption survive that distinction, which fail, and what the nearest named positions are that resemble but do not quite equal what I have described.
