Analyze the Sally Clark case through the lens of the philosophical debate about naked statistical evidence and proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

I am interested in the case not as legal history but as a test of the standard distinction between bare statistical evidence and individualized evidence. Clark, an English solicitor, was convicted in November 1999 of murdering her two infant sons. The pediatrician Roy Meadow testified that the chance of two sudden infant deaths in a family like hers was 1 in 73 million, a figure he reached by squaring an estimated 1-in-8,500 rate of a single unexplained infant death. The convictions were upheld on appeal in 2000 and were later quashed in January 2003, but the quashing turned on the prosecution pathologist's failure to disclose bacteriology reports rather than on the statistics; the Royal Statistical Society nevertheless issued a public statement criticizing the use of the 73-million figure in court.

In the tradition I have in mind, the blue-bus and gatecrasher cases are supposed to show that a merely statistical ratio, however strong, cannot justify a finding of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt: the plaintiff who knows only that the defendant company runs most of the buses on the route has not proved much of anything about this particular bus, and the prosecutor who knows only that most of the audience did not pay has not proved that this particular attendee did not pay. Use that framework to say what the finder of fact in Clark should have made of Meadow's testimony. Be precise about the mapping: is Meadow's evidence best classified as naked statistical evidence, as individualized evidence, or as something the two-way distinction does not handle cleanly? How much of the damage was done by the transposition of a conditional, the prosecutor's fallacy, and by the implicit independence assumption behind the squaring, rather than by the bare use of a base rate? Does the fact that the convictions were ultimately quashed on a different ground matter for the assessment?

Then run the analysis in the other direction. Does Clark confirm the standard lesson of the blue-bus and gatecrasher cases, does it complicate that lesson, or does it show that the category of naked statistical evidence is too coarse to capture what actually goes wrong in a real prosecution? Be explicit about the points where the theory is silent or forced.

Please write the analysis in American English.
