I'm a PhD student in machine learning, and I work a bit on questions about AI alignment on the side. Lately I've been stuck on a parallel I can't decide about. In ML, when a deployed model's performance decays because the world changes around it, we call it concept drift (or sometimes data drift, covariate shift, label shift, depending on what exactly moves). The usual fixes are monitoring outputs, detecting distributional shift, and recalibrating or retraining against fresh labels. In the alignment and longtermist world, people worry about value drift: an advanced system's goals or values changing over time, maybe gradually, so that by the end it's optimizing for something nobody originally intended. On the face of it these look like the same problem: a system that was good with respect to some target stops being good because the relationship between its inputs, its behavior, and the target has drifted.

The disanalogy I keep tripping over is this. Concept drift is diagnosed relative to a fixed ground truth: there's a true label or a stable target distribution we can sample, even if only expensively. But value drift doesn't seem to have that anchor, because there's no external fact about what the system should value; the "labels" would just be the system's own earlier values, or the designers' current values, and neither is obviously the analogue of ground truth. Does this break the analogy, or is it actually fine to treat the designers' initial values as the stationary target in the same way the training distribution is the stationary target in ML? And if the analogy does go through, does anything from the concept-drift literature—retraining schedules, canaries for detecting shift, the distinction between covariate shift and concept shift—genuinely transfer to thinking about value drift, or is the word "drift" doing all the work? I can't tell if I've found a real isomorphism or just a tempting piece of jargon.
