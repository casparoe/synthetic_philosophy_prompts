I have been teaching an undergraduate module on the ethics of belief, and I often start with the opening case from W. K. Clifford's The Ethics of Belief. As I remember it, Clifford describes a shipowner who talks himself into believing that an emigrant ship is seaworthy, even though he has not had it properly inspected, because believing it is cheaper and less anxious than carrying out a full survey. When the ship sinks, Clifford treats the belief itself as a moral failing rather than an unfortunate prediction error.

One of my students defended a two-tier policy that she says she already follows. In high-stakes matters—legal advice, medical instructions, safety checks—she demands the sort of evidence Clifford would demand. In low-stakes matters—where the meeting has moved, which café is open, whether a colleague's anecdote is literally true—she allows herself to move from weak evidence to outright belief, because she thinks the value of forming a belief quickly is greater than the small risk of being wrong. She insisted this is not Clifford's shipowner case writ small, because there the stakes were high and the shipowner's belief was badly under-evidenced even for a fallible judgement.

I want to know whether her policy is a defensible principle rather than a rationalisation. Is it coherent to make one's evidential threshold for belief sensitive to practical stakes, and if so, does that show that belief itself is partly a practical attitude, or only that we use the word believe loosely for different mental states at different stakes? What would a Clifford-style evidentialist say in reply? I am less interested in what to call her policy than in where its edges are: does it collapse under high-stakes/low-stakes comparisons, could it be applied consistently by a juror, and might it justify culpable trust in matters one happens not to care about?
