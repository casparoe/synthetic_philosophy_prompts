I'm a high school student, and this semester my philosophy class has left me with a problem I can't settle. I'm fairly convinced that determinism is true: every event, including every choice I make, is fixed by the past together with the laws of nature. And I'm drawn to the compatibilist reply that determinism doesn't threaten free will, because the freedom actually worth having is the capacity to deliberate, to act on reasons I endorse, and to be free from coercion and compulsion — not the capacity to have acted differently with the past and the laws held fixed. That reply has a lot going for it. It keeps the ordinary causal story of action intact, it explains why we excuse someone who is forced but not someone who merely chose badly, and it doesn't require believing in a gap in the causal order for which there is no evidence. But I can't fully accept it. When I'm torn between two options, it seems to me, in a way I find hard to dismiss, that both actions are open to me in a sense that determinism flatly rules out; and if my decision was already settled before I existed, then I don't see how I'm its author in the way responsibility requires. So I'm asking what should give. What would justify deciding that this intuition is unreliable and should be let go? What would justify revising the compatibilist view instead? Or should I think the two aren't really in conflict, because being able to do otherwise turns out to mean one thing during deliberation and another thing in physics?
