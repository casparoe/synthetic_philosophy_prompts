In Leviticus 16 (King James Version), the Day of Atonement rite divides the removal of the people's sin between two goats. One, selected by lot 'for the Lord' (16:8), is killed: the priest is to 'bring his blood within the vail' (16:15), and the text says this is to 'make an atonement for the holy place, because of the uncleanness of the children of Israel, and because of their transgressions in all their sins' (16:16). The other goat, called 'the scapegoat', remains alive: Aaron is to 'confess over him all the iniquities of the children of Israel, and all their transgressions in all their sins, putting them upon the head of the goat, and shall send him away by the hand of a fit man into the wilderness' (16:21), and the text adds that 'the goat shall bear upon him all their iniquities unto a land not inhabited' (16:22). The first passage seems to treat sin and uncleanness as dealt with by blood inside the sanctuary; the second treats them as loaded onto a living substitute and carried outside the community. Are these two descriptions one coherent mechanism for disposing of the people's sin, or do they embody two different, perhaps competing, theories of how sin is removed? Lay out the strongest readings on each side and the candidate reconciliations (for example, a division of labor between the two goats, distinct kinds of impurity or sin, one process with two phases, or an editorial fusion of originally separate rites). Then say which reconciliation is most faithful to the text and which would count as special pleading.
