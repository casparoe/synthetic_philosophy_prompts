I’m a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I’ve been trying to understand why the debate about mind uploading through gradual neural replacement has stalled. The basic question seems straightforward: if we replace your neurons one at a time with artificial components that perform exactly the same information-processing functions, do you remain conscious throughout, and does the resulting system continue to be you? But people who clearly understand the thought experiment still arrive at opposite conclusions, and no one seems to move.

Around a decade ago, David Chalmers discussed this in his essay on the singularity. The passage I keep thinking about sets up a scenario in which each neuron is gradually replaced by a functionally equivalent chip. Chalmers then considers what would happen if consciousness faded or changed during the procedure. He argues, if I’m reading him right, that a subject whose consciousness was fading would apparently be disposed to notice and say something about it, and that once the behavioral dispositions are held fixed by the functional equivalence of the replacements, the idea of silent fading becomes hard to make sense of. On that view, the only coherent outcome is that consciousness is preserved. Yet I’ve now seen thoughtful people reject that argument without quite saying where it goes wrong. One side treats it as nearly decisive; the other treats it as a clever trick that smuggles in the conclusion by assuming that consciousness can never come apart from functional or behavioral organization.

I’m not asking for a survey of the positions, and I’m not asking who is right. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is this a verbal disagreement about what the word “consciousness” means once we move to artificial systems? Is it a substantive disagreement about an empirical fact that we happen not to know how to test yet? Are the two sides actually answering different questions under one heading—say, one about what preserves experience, and another about what preserves personal identity or what should matter prudentially? Or is the impasse a sign that the original question is ill-posed? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here: a sharper distinction, an experiment, a formal argument, a change of question, or simply a reason to stop expecting convergence?
