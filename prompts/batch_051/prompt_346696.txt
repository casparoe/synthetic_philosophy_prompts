I cover language tech and philosophy for a magazine, and I keep coming back to David Lewis's paper Scorekeeping in a Language Game. The part I'm stuck on is his account of presupposition accommodation. He describes a conversation as having a scoreboard that tracks what's been presupposed, and then says, roughly, that when a new utterance needs a presupposition that isn't on the board, the scoreboard tends to update to include it—ceteris paribus, within limits. His model seems to assume a live exchange where events happen in order, each new utterance arrives at a time, and the score moves forward like a baseball score.

But here's the development I'm seeing. In a chat interface, especially with an LLM, the so-called conversation is a editable transcript. You can send a message, then revise an earlier one, then ask the model to re-read everything. The model doesn't have a single live scoreboard that only moves forward; it reconstructs context from the whole text each time. So does Lewis's rule of accommodation survive that shift? Which assumption is doing the real work—the idea of a score, the rule's ceteris paribus clause, or the idea that there's a well-defined time t when an utterance occurs? Or is the problem just that an editable transcript is a different language game entirely, so his scorekeeping model was never supposed to cover it? I'd love a diagnosis of what exactly breaks, and whether a repaired version of the accommodation rule can handle non-linear, revised conversations.
