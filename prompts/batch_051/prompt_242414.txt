I write fiction for a living, and my working vocabulary is the vocabulary of narrative craft: point of view, focalization, free indirect style, the unreliable narrator, subtext, an avowed motive versus an effective motive, dramatic irony, and the recognition scene in which a character finally admits what the prose has been insinuating all along. I am fluent in that vocabulary and largely a novice in formal philosophy. I have found myself repeatedly struck by the thought that when philosophers discuss self-deception, they are describing, in a more cumbersome idiom, something a novelist does every time she writes a character who keeps a truth from herself. I do not trust that thought, and I want to know how much weight it can actually bear.

Restate the philosophical problem of self-deception in my vocabulary, as precisely as you can. Lay out the central tension — how can a single person be both the deceiver and the deceived, both in possession of the truth and shielded from it? — and include the main families of response: roughly, the view that the mind must be divided or stretched over time so that one part can keep the secret from another, and the view that self-deception is not an intentional act at all but a motivated bias in how a person seeks, weighs, and remembers evidence. Translate each of these into terms I would actually use at the desk or in a workshop: narrator and character, free indirect discourse as a technique for holding two levels of awareness inside a single sentence, the gap between the motive a character announces and the motive her actions disclose, the reader's dramatic irony, and the scene in which the self-deception collapses.

Then tell me what does not survive the translation. Which joints of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto narrative craft, which can be carried across only if you distort them, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? Be systematic rather than illustrative; the interest is in the points of resistance. In particular, I want your honest judgment on this: my working intuition is that a self-deceived person simply is an unreliable narrator about her own motives, with the truth playing the part of the reader who watches and knows. Tell me whether that intuition is right, or whether it quietly imports an author standing outside the self — which may be exactly the thing the philosophers are arguing about — and treats as settled machinery what ought to be the object of explanation. If the analogy fails at certain points, I want to know whether it fails because narrative craft is the wrong instrument for this problem, or because the philosophical claim was less precise than it first appeared.
