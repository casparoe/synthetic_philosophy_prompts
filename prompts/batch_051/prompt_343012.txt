So I spent all last night arguing with a friend about George Berkeley's views on money, of all things, and now I'm worried my talk won't survive the Q&A. Next month I'm presenting to a history of economic thought seminar. My paper defends the claim that Berkeley's Querist gets something importantly right: that money is better understood as a kind of ticket or counter that records and moves around claims on the industry and credit of a community, not as a commodity whose value comes from the metal in the coin. From there I argue Berkeley has a coherent (if underdeveloped) story about why a poor country like Ireland could use a national bank issuing paper notes to put idle hands to work and keep goods circulating, and that dismissing him as someone who just didn't learn the lesson of John Law's Mississippi scheme misreads the actual argument. My friend's counter, all night, was that the whole thing is just Law's system with a bishop's collar on it, the bank was never built, and the economists were right to move on.

The audience: two historians of economics who work on eighteenth-century money and banking, one macroeconomist who is a committed quanity theorist, a Berkeley scholar from the philosophy department, and some graduate students who mostly know Berkeley as the immaterialist who thinks physical objects are just ideas. I need a Q&A prep sheet. What objections will they actually raise, roughly in order, phrased the way they'd put them? For each one: is it a genuine problem for my position, what should I concede, what should I hold, and where is my reply softest? And include the question I'm praying nobody asks, because I can already see it coming and I don't have a good answer for it.
