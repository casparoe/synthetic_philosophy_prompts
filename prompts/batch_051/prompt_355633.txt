Okay so I'm a PhD student in philosophy of law and I keep tripping over a feature of American antidiscrimination doctrine that my supervisors seem to just take for granted, and I'd like someone to actually pull apart the theory it implicitly commits us to. Here's the practice: courts treat race, national origin, and religion as suspect classifications that get strict scrutiny (basically the government loses unless the law is narrowly tailored to a compelling interest), while age, disability, and poverty get rational-basis review (the government wins if there's any conceivable rational ground). Sex sits in the middle with intermediate scrutiny. I want to know what account of what makes a ground of discrimination suspect this tiering presupposes. The obvious single-principle answers all fail: if the theory is immutability, religion is mutable but still top-tier; if it's historical subordination, disability and poverty arguably qualify but don't get heightened scrutiny; if it's irrelevance to one's abilities, then lots of non-protected traits would make the cut and parts of sex and age would flunk. So it looks like the doctrine is presupposing some sort of hybrid, maybe about caste-like status hierarchy plus political powerlessness, even though the cases never say that cleanly. Can you reconstruct the implicit theory, seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit with (where several theories would license the same tiering), and then say what antidiscrimination law would actually have to look like if we swapped the presupposed view for a pure group-subordination view or a pure immutability view? I'm after the philosophical commitments, not a doctrinal summary.
