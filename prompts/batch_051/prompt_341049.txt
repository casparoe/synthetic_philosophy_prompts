I'm the new chair of our college's academic conduct commitee, and I'm tired of us making these cases up as we go. We see maybe fifteen cheating and plagiarism cases a term, the stakes run from a quiet warning up to expulsion, and right now two basically identical offenses can end up in very different places depending on which of us is in the room. I once read Strawson on the reactive attitudes years ago, and the contrast that stuck with me is between the normal stance where you resent somebody, you're hurt, you hold them to a demand for good will—and what he called the objective stance, where you set all that down and treat the person as something to be understood, managed, fixed, or worked around. I want to turn that into a procedure my committee can actually use: a step-by-step checklist for deciding when a student who's clearly broken the rules should be treated as a responsible person we're entitled to blame and sanction, versus when we should switch to the objective stance and treat them as a management problem—accommodations, support, remediation, maybe not a punishment case at all.

Please write it as questions to ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the final call to a person because it's a values or judgment call rather than a fact. Keep the theory in the background; the committee is mostly chemists and accountants, not philosophers.

Then run the procedure on these three cases:

(a) a first-generation first-year who pasted two paragraphs from a website into a paper and clearly was never taught citation rules;

(b) a senior who paid somebody to write a chapter of her honors thesis and got caught;

(c) a student with a recent, documented mental health crisis who cheated on a final by texting a friend during the exam.

Make at least one of those come out in a way I'm not going to like, and at the end tell me honestly what the procedure can't decide and where it's most likely to misfire. For context: we have about forty-five minutes per case, nobody on the committee is a lawyer, and the dean has to sign off on anything above a warning.
