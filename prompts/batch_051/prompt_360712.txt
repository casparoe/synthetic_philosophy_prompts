I'm in a philosophy of education course right now and we keep going around about what actually makes something a school, as opposed to a family doing school-like stuff in private. The rough criteria we've been kicking around are: (1) there's a planned, sequenced curriculum aimed at particular learning outcomes, not just exposure or childcare; (2) the teaching is done by someone in a defined instructor role — not only parents instructing their own kids; (3) there's a standing peer group of unrelated kids, not just siblings; (4) there's some assessment or progress tracking that can catch when a kid isn't learning; (5) the thing is answerable to some public or outside authority — registration, inspections, outside testing, accreditation, something. I realize some of these sound like features of a school and some sound like features of a *good* or *legitimate* school, and part of what I want to know is weather that distinction survives contact with the case.

The case: ten families in a rural county pull their kids out of the public schools and form an outfit they call a homeschool co-op. They rent two rooms in a church building and meet Monday through Thursday, 9 to 2. They hired one licensed former public-school teacher to cover reading and math, while the parents rotate through history, science, and civics using a Christian curriculum they chose themselves. Kids are divided by age, there's a discipline policy and a dress code, and they do standardized tests at the end of the year — but the families interpret the scores on their own, nobody outside sees them, and last year three parents vetoed the evolution unit. The co-op deliberately avoids registering as a private school because that would trigger state oversight.

Is this thing a school? Go through the five criteria honestly and say where it clearly meets them, clearly fails them, and where the facts I gave underdetermine the answer. Then tell me whether the sticking points are in the case or in my criteria — that is, whether the list needs changing, and if so which criteria are doing real conceptual work and which are just policy add-ons. Also: does the fact that they call it a co-op partly to avoid the label do any philosophical work, or is that just branding?
