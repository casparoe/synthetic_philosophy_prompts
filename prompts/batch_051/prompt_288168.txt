I keep coming back to a question my dissertation advisor raised: when someone explains a country's economic performance by invoking its work ethic or its family values, is that a causal explanation or just a way of dressing up the outcome in cultural vocabulary after the fact? I want to think this through using Max Weber's Protestant ethic thesis as the lens, applied to a case Weber himself did not treat: the postwar industrialization of Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and Singapore. I already know the European version of the argument, so do not summarize it except as needed to state the mechanism: ascetic Protestantism generating anxiety about salvation, the resulting need for evidence of election, and the channeling of that anxiety into methodical work, frugality, and reinvestment, with an affinity for rational bookkeeping and accumulation. My question is about the machinery of the explanation, not the label. Treat the East Asian record as the test case. What exactly would a cultural explanation of those economies have to claim: which values were the operative ones, through what mechanism were they supposed to produce savings, education, entrepreneurship, or law-abidingness, and what counterfactual does the explanation commit itself to? Then apply the thesis honestly: which features of the East Asian record does a Weber-style account actually explain, which features does it have to stretch to fit, and which features does it stay silent about because they belong to geopolitics, American security guarantees, land reform, industrial policy, and the brute timing of export markets? Finally, run the case back through the theory. Is East Asia a confirmation, showing that something like the Protestant ethic can appear in non-Protestant dress, or does it expose the thesis as too easy to retrofit onto any success, and therefore weaken the claim that culture play the causal role Weber assigned it? Please write this as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the way one would write an email or a letter.
