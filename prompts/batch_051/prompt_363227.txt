I am a PhD student in sociology, working at the intersection of social ontology and quantitative social science, and I am currently on a research attachment with a public opinion and consumer analytics firm. The firm's analysts routinely slice every survey and customer dataset by "generation" — Boomers, Gen X, Millennials, Gen Z — and clients increasingly ask for generational profiles as though these were natural groupings. My supervisor has asked me to turn what is actually defensible about generational categories into a decision procedure the analysts can run, without philosophical or methodological jargon, to decide whether a given dataset and research question support a generational interpretation or should be described in terms of age, period, or something else.

The firm needs a reusable procedure, not a one-off judgment. Analysts without background in social theory must be able to run it in half a day per project, before findings go to a client; whenever the procedure passes or kills a generational claim, a senior analyst signs off. Please write the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to pass each question, where the sequence stops with a "no," and where the procedure must hand the matter to a senior analyst because a judgment call or value decision is doing the work. Ground it in whatever social ontology and the age–period–cohort problem actually support about whether cohorts like Millennials or Gen Z are real kinds — in a single cross-section, age, cohort, and period are confounded — but keep that theoretical vocabulary out of the procedure itself. In particular, the procedure should force the analyst to separate three questions that are usually run together: whether there are real differences between cohorts; whether "generation" is the best explanation of those differences rather than age, period, or some more specific variable; and whether generational language should still be used in client-facing material when the first two questions fail.

Then run the procedure on three cases, showing exactly where each stops and what the analyst writes:

(1) a single-wave cross-sectional survey of 2,000 adults showing that adults under 30 hold systematically different attitudes toward remote work than adults over 55;

(2) a twenty-year panel tracking the same individuals' political trust across repeated waves, so that age, cohort, and period are at least partly separable;

(3) a client commission for a "Gen Z consumer" segmentation intended to guide pricing strategy.

I expect, and my supervisor expects, that the procedure will kill generational language in most of what the firm currently does; I also suspect — and want to be warned about — at least one case where the procedure licenses generational talk that I personally find reifying. Do not soften that. Where the procedure cannot settle a question, say so explicitly rather than pretending the methodology decides more than it does.
