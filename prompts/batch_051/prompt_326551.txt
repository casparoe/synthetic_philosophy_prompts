I'm trying to figure out what I actually think makes a statement racist, working up from my gut on cases instead of down from a definition. Here's where I land so far.

My uncle, told that a Black family is moving in next door, says "I've got nothing against them, I just worry the neighborhood will go downhill." I think that's racist. The words are careful, but everyone knows the move.

A white actor in a satire plays a clueless bigot and uses an old slur in character, and the whole joke is on the bigot. I don't think the actor's line is racist; the scene is about laughing at him, not with him.

The same slur from a Black stand-up telling a story about his own grandmother: also not racist to me, but for a different reason.

A manager passes over a Black candidate and, asked why, says "he just wasn't a culture fit." I think that's racist even though the words are vague and he could deny anything.

My aunt says "I don't see color, everyone's the same to me." I know some people call this racist, but my gut says it isnt. Wrongheaded, maybe, not racist.

A politician runs an ad about "restoring law and order in our neighborhoods," and the footage behind it is all Black protesters. The sentence never mentions race. I think the ad is racist anyway, a dog whistle.

A friend tells an Asian-driver joke at a dinner party. He means nothing by it and would be mortified if anyone got upset. I still think the joke is racist.

A teacher tells a Black student "You're so articulate, not like... well, you know," and trails off. Racist. The literal content is a compliment, which is weird.

My seven-year-old repeats the n-word from a song and has no idea what it means. I cant call what he said racist, even though the word is a slur.

The one I keep going back and forth on: a white director tells a Black actress her hair is "too ethnic" for the role. Some days it's obviously racist; other days it's just a clueless aesthetic judgment.

The nearest I can get to a principle is that a statement is racist when it deliberately trades on racial hostility or hierarchy, or signals that race indicates something bad. But that lets the dinner-party joke off the hook, since there's no intent and no signal he's aware of, and it maybe drags the aunt in where my gut says she doesn't belong.

Can you give me a precise principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that captures as many of these verdicts as possible? I want it stated clearly, with which cases force which clauses. Then run it on the hair case and on a case I haven't settled either: a doctor tells a Black patient that the hospital's standard kidney-function formula adjusts upward for Black patients because it assumes, on average, higher muscle mass. Is the doctor's statement racist, or not? And tell me straight which of my original verdicts a consistent principle would force me to give up.
