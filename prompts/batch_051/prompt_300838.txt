I do compliance review for a discount retailer that buys closeout kitchen gadgets and cleaning products, so most of my week is deciding which incoming products get stopped, which get a warning label, and which go straight to shelf. There's two of us doing this, maybe a dozen products a week, and we get about an hour per product using the lab report and the physical sample. Anything I reject has to be signed off by my manager, and our insurer cares a lot about the exact wording of our rule. That rule, as the lawyers prefer it, is that a product gets stopped if it's disposed to injure someone under normal, foreseeable use.

The problem is a family of products where everyone agrees the mechanism would injure someone, but in practice nobody ever gets hurt, because the product stops being dangerous in exactly the moment the danger would be realized, or because something sits between the dangerous mechanism and the user the whole time. Two products on my desk show the split. One is a benchtop blender that only runs the blades when the lid is locked, so any scenario where a hand can reach the blade is automatically a scenario where the blade isn't spinning; in no realistic scenario does it actually cut anyone. The other is a drain cleaner with a genuinely corrosive lye solution inside, but the bottle has a one-way valve and a child-lock so tight that skin can't ever contact the liquid while it's the product we sell. My manager says pass both: a blade that isn't spinning doesn't cut fingers, and a chemical that can't reach skin doesn't dissolve it. My colleague says that's nuts: the first product is still a finger-remover and the second is still a skin-dissolver; the safety hardware only masks what the thing is, and if you count the guard as part of "what the thing is," you could safety-wash any hazard in the store. We've been going around on this for months, and it's slowing the whole queue down. We also get plenty of products where the guard works only most of the time — a lab note like "chute guard slipped in 2% of pulls" — and those are their own mess.

Please write me a decision procedure — ordered questions, each with a note on what counts as a sufficient answer — that a non-philosopher reviewer can run in under an hour from a lab report, and that settles when a product still counts as "disposed to injure" even though the injury never actually happens in normal use. I want it to say pass, label, or reject at each step, and to say plainly where the procedure has to stop and hand the thing to a human. I know philosophers have a literature on exactly this kind of question but I don't know the terms, so translate it: nobody on my team should ever have to hear the jargon. Then run the procedure on these three and show the outputs:

1. The blender with the lid interlock.
2. The drain cleaner with the one-way valve.
3. A window cleaner whose ammonia was already neutralized into a non-irritating buffer at the factory, so the liquid can't irritate skin even spilled undiluted — though the jug still says "ammonia" on the front.

And tell me where the procedure will misfire, or where it's really just a judgment call dressed up as a checklist.
