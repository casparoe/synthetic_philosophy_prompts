You are an expert philosopher of science who works on confirmation theory and Goodman's new riddle of induction. I'm building a taxonomy of responses to the grue problem for a graduate seminar, and a colleague insists there is a seventh family of response that doesn't fit my six boxes, but he won't tell me what it is. I don't need a bibliography or citations; I need the idea and the argument.

My taxonomy so far has six families. (1) Entrenchment: project only predicates with a history of successful projection; green is entrenched, grue is not. (2) Natural kinds: the asymmetry is metaphysical; green picks out a sparse, law-governed property, while grue is gerrymandered or disjunctive. (3) Simplicity: the green hypothesis is simpler than the grue hypothesis relative to our current language, and induction legitimately favors simpler hypotheses. (4) Bayesian priors: a rational agent should assign higher prior credence to the green hypothesis, so equal fit to the data does not yield equal posteriors. (5) Pragmatism: there is no objective fact about which predicate is projectible; grue loses only relative to our interests, practices, or conventions. (6) Qualitative-predicate constraint: grue is positional because it is defined by reference to a particular time, and inductive projection should be limited to purely qualitative, non-positional predicates. (I know (3) and (4) are often run together, but I count them separately because one locates the asymmetry in the hypotheses and the other in the agent's credences.)

I want a seventh response with a different structure from all six. By 'different structure' I mean it must locate the green/grue asymmetry somewhere other than the history of usage, the metaphysics of properties, the complexity of the hypotheses, the agent's prior credences, our interests or conventions, or the syntax of the predicates. It should not be a rewording, special case, or hybrid of items already on the list. Before you settle on an answer, brainstorm a few candidate ideas and discard any that collapse into one of the six families. Then present your strongest candidate and explain (a) exactly where it locates the asymmetry, (b) why it doesn't reduce to any of the six, and (c) one thing it handles better or worse than the listed responses. If, after genuinely trying, you conclude the space saturates at these six, say so and defend that conclusion; but don't reach for it too quickly.
