I'm taking a seminar on Aristotle's De Anima and I keep getting stuck on what looks like a flat contradiction in Book III. Early on, in III.3, Aristotle works pretty hard to distinguish phantasia from thinking: phantasia is something like an appearance, it can be false even when perception is true, it shows up in some nonhuman animals, and he explicitly denies that it is thought or judgment. Then in III.4–5 he goes further and describes the intellect as unmixed and separable, which makes it sound like thought can operate apart from anything bodily like an image. But then in III.7 (around 431a) and again in III.8 (around 432a) he seems to say the opposite: the soul never thinks without a phantasma, and when it contemplates it has to contemplate some image or other. I'm closely paraphrasing, not quoting any particular translation, but the passages are pretty famous.

So can these be recounciled, or is this a genuine tension in the text? I'm not just looking for a yes or no. What are the best candidate readings — different senses of phantasma, a difference between what thought depends on and what thought is, a difference between kinds of thinking or kinds of intellect, or maybe Aristotle just changed his mind or assumed a distinction he never actually draws? For each candidate I'd want to know what it costs exegetically, and which ones are honest readings of the text versus which are just special pleading to save the system. And if you think they can't be reconciled, what exactly should we say Aristotle is doing here: contradicting himself, missing a tension, or reaching for a distinction he never managed to spell out?
