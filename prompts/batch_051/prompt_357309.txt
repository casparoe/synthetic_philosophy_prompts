I was arguing with a friend about a local housing fight—one side wants more density, the other wants neighborhood character preserved—and it got me onto a parallel I can't decide is deep or just pattern-matching. In econ, gains from trade: two people with different preferences and endowments can swap and both end up better off than before, without either adopting the other's values. Ann trades something she cares about less for something she cares about more, Bob does the reverse, and the final point is Pareto-better than the no-trade point. Voluntary exchange pushes them toward the contract curve: the set of points where you can't make one person better off without making the other worse off.

Moral compromise looks like the same shape. Two people or groups with very different value systems each rank their own commitments by how central they are. They each give ground on items low in their own ranking to secure wins on items high in their own ranking. If their rankings differ enough, the compromise can look, from inside each side's value system, like a strict improvement over refusing to budge—even though nobody has updated about what's ultimately right. It's transactional: you trade away a scruple you can live without to get something you care much more about, and the other side does the same. The housing case would be each faction letting the other build density in some blocks and preserve character in others, with both feeling they won something and sold something out.

So: is this a genuine identity of structure, a loose metaphor, or a confusion? Could you set up a moral contract curve by treating each side's values as a preference ordering, and would the gains-from-trade logic actually carry over? Or does it break because values aren't commensurable the way goods are, because moral compromises aren't enforceable contracts, or because conceding on a value changes who you are and your standing to hold it in a way exchanging goods doesn't? I want the mapping stated precisely, what would have to be true on the ethics side for the economic results to transfer, and at least one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false.
