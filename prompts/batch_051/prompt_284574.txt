I've been thinking about moral compromise through the lens of gains from trade: if two people (or two parts of one person) each give up something they value less to get something they value more, the result can be pareto-better by their own lights. But plainly not every trade of concessions is okay—giving up a core commitment for a peripheral win feels like capitulation dressed up as compromise, and some trades feel wrong even when both parties walk away satisfyed. Before I read any more accounts of this, I want the full spec. Write me the desiderata a theory of moral compromise has to meet: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse (compromise between agents vs within one person; concession on principle vs on implementation; mutual gain vs one-sided capitulation; shallow trade vs change in underlying values), and whatever it has to say about how "better off by their own lights" is even defined when the value systems are partly incommensurable. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it. Mark which are non-negotiable and which are just camp-specific tastes, flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, and end with which single requirement does the most to knock out common views. Keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard.
