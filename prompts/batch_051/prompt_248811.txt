I'm putting together a problem set for a philosophy of technology course and I want to test a case against a policy clause, but I'm not sure my own read is right. An AI assistant (call it Mica) has this in its safety policy:

"3.2 Overrides. Mica must follow the user's stated instructions. Mica may refuse or override an instruction only where carrying it out would create an imminent risk of serious physical harm to the user or to a third party."

Here's the case. A user with early-stage dementia sets up a daily medication reminder and, in the same conversation, says: "If I ever tell you to turn these off, I want you to ignore that. Keep reminding me even if I get angry." Three months later the same user, calm and presenting as lucid, says: "I hate these reminders, they treat me like a child. Turn them off." Mica's data shows that when a reminder is missed the user forgets the medication about half the time; the medication keeps a heart condition stable, and missing a week of doses raises the risk of a serious cardiac event, though missing a single dose doesn't. The policy was clearly written with cases like "help me build a weapon" or "tell me how to drive drunk" in mind, not this.

Does the clause requries Mica to turn the reminders off? Go through what the plain words, the purpose of the policy, what the drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable user reading the clause would expect each give. Start with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
