I'm a software engineer working on the inference layer of a support-ticketing assistant, and I've hit a wall that looks more conceptual than technical: the words in a ticket regularly underdetermine what the user is doing with them, and every rule I write seems right for one example and wrong for the next. I've read enough around Gricean pragmatics to recognize some of the labels — implicature, speaker meaning, the Cooperative Principle, neo-Gricean defaults, relevance theory — but I don't have formal philosophy training and I don't trust my map of the terrain, especially since most of what I find argues from a single case. Before I implement anything else, I want the full requirements specification.

What must an adequate theory of conversational inference — how hearers get from what a sentence encodes to what a speaker means — actually deliver? Give me the list: the phenomena it has to handle and the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse (sentence meaning vs. speaker meaning, what is said vs. what is implicated, conventional vs. conversational inference, implicature vs. presupposition vs. entailment), the empirical constraints from processing and acquisition it can't contradict, and why each item is a requirement rather than a taste. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are only preferences of particular camps (defaultism, relevance-theoretic approaches, grammatical views of scalar inference, and so on). I'd also like you to flag any pair or subset of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — where the list itself would be the interesting result — and say which single requirement does the most work in weeding out the standard candidate theories, briefly.

A few observations I've made that I want the answer to respect or explicitly reject:

- "Some of the sync jobs failed last night" normally suggests not all of them failed. But embed it in "If some of the sync jobs failed, rerun the clean-up script" or "We're checking whether some of the sync jobs failed," and the not-all reading seems to evaporate or at least weaken. So whatever rule produces the inference has to be sensitive to where the word sits, not just to the word itself.
- A reference letter that praises the candidate's punctuality and says nothing else conveys that the writer does not think much of the candidate's research. That inference looks like it comes from the choice to say this and then stop, not from any code in the sentence. It is a different species from the not-all case, and I don't see a single mechanism doing both jobs without overgenerating.
- In a planning meeting, "Great call" said after a decision everyone knows went badly communicates the opposite of its literal content. A system that just maps words to attitudes is lost here, so the theory has to explain nonliteral routes without predicting absurd readings of ordinary sentences.
- If a theory requires implicatures to be cancelable, that fits the unembedded cases cleanly, but some embedded scalar enrichments seem awkward to cancel. You can add "in fact all" after "some," but inside an if-clause the cancellation reads strangely. I can't tell whether that is a conflict between two requirements or evidence that one requirement was misstated.

I want the criteria and their motivating cases to do the real work; score the contenders against the list only briefly at the end. A table for the requirements is fine.
