In C. B. Martin's paper Dispositions and Conditionals he gives the case of a live wire connected to an electro-fink: a device that would interrupt the current the moment a conductor is brought near, so that the wire is live and yet would not conduct if touched. I have always taken the lesson of this case to be narrow. It refutes only the crudest subjunctive conditional analysis of dispositions, the one that equates an object's being disposed to M when S with the bare counterfactual that it would M if subjected to S. The position I now hold is more guarded: once the analysis is supplemented with a ceteris paribus clause, or restricted to intrinsic dispositions whose causal basis is held fixed, the conditional analysis can still serve as a reductive account of what it is to have a disposition.

That is the position I want stress-tested. What would have to be true for this fallback position to be wrong? I want a map of the actual and possible defeaters, sorted from decisive to merely awkward. In particular, what exactly must be shown for a fink or a mask to defeat a ceteris paribus or basis-relative conditional analysis, rather than merely force another refinement? Which formal results or carefully constructed cases should move a defender of the analysis, and which should be dismissed as changing the subject? Has the conditional analysis, in your assessment, been insulated by its qualifications into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a problem for a reductive account or a tolerable cost of philosophical explication? I am not asking for a summary of the standard literature; I want an honest assessment of how exposed the position actually is.
