In A Treatise on Probability, J. M. Keynes argues that not all probabilities are numerical: an agent may rationally judge one hypothesis more probable than another on the evidence without there being any definite number measuring the degree of probability, and some evidential comparisons may not even be possible. For Keynes, this is a fact about the logical relation between evidence and conclusion, not a defect in the agent.

Since then, a formal literature on imprecise probability has developed, representing belief states by sets of admissible probability functions, interval-valued probabilities, Dempster-Shafer belief functions, and similar structures rather than by a single precise credence. But many Bayesians have replied that rational agents must have fully precise credences, and that apparent imprecision is only an introspective or elicitation failure—an unwillingness or inability to identify a precise number that is nevertheless there.

Does Keynes's argument that some uncertainty is genuinely non-numerical survive this formalization and the Bayesian reply? Identify exactly which premise of Keynes's position the precise-credence norm challenges, assess whether the imprecise-probability frameworks offer a defensible reconstruction of that premise, and say whether the original Keynesian thesis needs repair, can be restored in revised form, or should be discarded.
