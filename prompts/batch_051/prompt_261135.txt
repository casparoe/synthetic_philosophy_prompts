I want a working definition of joint action — acting together, as opposed to several people merely acting at the same time, in the same place, or toward the same outcome. The distinction keeps doing real work in how I assign responsibility and decide when it's fair to say "we," and I suspect I'm applying it inconsistently. I'm not after ordinary usage for its own sake; I want a criterion that tracks whatever difference in kind there is here, if there is one.

Below are ten cases. I've marked some with my tentative verdicts; the others I leave genuinely open. Please propose a definition, run it through all ten, and say where the definition gives a verdict you would want to revise or defend rather than accept. If no single criterion gets all your verdicts, say which case exerts the most pressure to revise the definition, and which of my tentative verdicts you would abandon first.

1. A friend and I agree by text on Saturday morning to carry a couch down a flight of stairs and into a van; when the time comes, we each take an end and carry it. (Clearly yes; if this doesn't count, the definition is wrong.)

2. On a narrow trail, a hiker stays fifteen feet behind me at exactly my pace for a mile; neither of us acknowledges the other. (Clearly no.)

3. Two strangers get out of their cars at an intersection and, without speaking, take opposite ends of a fallen branch blocking the road. Each sees the other, sees that the other sees him, and sees the other lifting; they carry the branch to the curb and walk back to their cars. (I lean yes, but I can't articulate why the mutual awareness should matter.)

4. Same branch, but it is past midnight and each driver lifts his end in complete ignorance of the other; the branch reaches the curb only because both happen to lift at the same moment. (I think no.)

5. A surgical team — surgeon, anesthesiologist, and scrub nurse — performs an appendectomy, each playing a practiced role and adjusting to the others' movements. (Clearly yes.)

6. Two killers independently target the same official; one replaces his heart medication with a fatal substitute, the other cuts his brake line, and the first method kills him before he reaches the car. (Clearly no, despite the shared target and shared outcome.)

7. Two neighbors carry a heavy dresser to the curb. One does it because a charity is about to collect it; the other does it because he intends to load it into his own truck later. Each knows the other's ultimate goal differs, but neither mentions it, and they carry the dresser efficiently together. (I genuinely don't know: the carrying feels joint, but the larger project doesn't.)

8. A warehouse foreman tells four workers to move a pile of pallets. None of the workers speaks to any other; each carries individual pallets from one bay to another exactly as instructed. (I don't know whether the workers act together with one another, jointly with the foreman, or neither.)

9. Two colleagues divide a report by email, never discuss its contents, each writes half, and the report is submitted under both names. (I lean that something joint happened, but not obviously enough to count.)

10. Thousands of people sign an online petition in a single week. Each sees the public signature count rising and signs partly because others are signing, but no one coordinates beyond watching the number. (I have no idea.)

Please answer in American English. Close by stating how confident you are in the definition overall and in each classification, and single out the one point — a definitional clause or a case verdict — about which you are least sure.
