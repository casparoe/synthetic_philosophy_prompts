A colleague keeps pressing an argument I cannot find the exit from. It runs as follows.

(1) String theory has produced no novel, confirmed empirical predictions, yet many of its defenders take themselves to be rationally warranted in believing it is approximately true, not merely in pursuing it as a research program.
(2) The considerations they invoke are non-empirical theoretical virtues: internal consistency, apparent uniqueness, simplicity, and the unification of gravity with the other forces.
(3) If non-empirical virtues of this sort can warrant belief in an empirically unconfirmed theory, then they can warrant belief in any other theory that instantiates comparable virtues.
(4) Several rival frameworks can be represented as instantiating comparable virtues: an anthropic multiverse that makes fine-tuning unsurprising, a creator hypothesis with a maximally simple designer, and a simulation hypothesis that extends familiar computational regularities.
(5) So either the string theorist must grant rational credibility to some of these rivals, or she must identify a further virtue that string theory possesses and they lack.
(6) But any further virtue she could cite would itself be non-empirical, so at the next level the same problem simply recurs.

Where does the argument go wrong? If it is valid, which premise is least costly to abandon? Please identify the step doing the illicit work or the premise that should be rejected, explain why it initially looked sound, and say what rejecting it costs for the broader program of non-empirical theory assessment.
