In The Theory of Moral Sentiments, Smith appears at first to ground our capacity for self-judgment in the imagined perspective of other people. In Part III he argues, in substance, that a person cannot survey his own sentiments and motives or form any judgment of them until he removes himself from his own natural station and tries to see them at a distance, as an impartial bystander would; he says that society functions as a kind of mirror through which alone we can scrutinize the propriety of our own conduct. On this picture, the internal spectator seems to be built up out of our accumulated experience of how actual observers respond, not only to us but to one another.

Yet Smith later gives that internal spectator a far more independent role. He describes it as an imagined, well-informed, and impartial judge within, to which the wise person can appeal when the opinions of actual people are distorted by ignorance or partiality. In such cases the inner tribunal can acquit a person whom the world condemns, or condemn someone applauded by his contemporaries. This is not merely one source of opinion among others; it is treated as the higher court.

Those two claims pull in different directions. If the inner judge is originally manufactured from the eyes of actual bystanders, it is unclear how it gains authority to override those same eyes when they disagree. But if it is an idealized spectator rather than a mere internalization of social opinion, then the earlier claim about needing society as a mirror looks like only a developmental story, not an account of what gives the spectator its moral standing. Can Smith consistently hold both? Please give a committed answer: take a definite position on whether the two strands can be reconciled, defend a particular reading of how the impartial spectator is formed and why it has the authority Smith assigns to it, and say which proposed reconciliation—different senses of the spectator, a distinction between origin and authority, a change of mind, or some other—is most faithful to the book and which ones amount to special pleading.
