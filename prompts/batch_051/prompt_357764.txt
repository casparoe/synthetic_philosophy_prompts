I hold the following view, and I want you to map its exposure rather than argue for it. My view is that the replication crisis in social and biomedical science was not fundamentally a failure of frequentist inference, and that the common proposal to fix the field by adopting Bayesian methods wholesale mistakes a social-incentive problem for a technical one. The operative causes are publication filters, career rewards for surprising positive results, and the tolerated analytical flexibility usually called researcher degrees of freedom; all of these would persist under any inferential framework. I make the further claim that a Bayesian reformation would make the policing of scientific practice harder rather than easier: optional stopping becomes harder to classify as a violation on a Bayesian account, the prior becomes one more dial to tune, and the space of models becomes one more place for flexible choices to hide. The point is causal and sociological, not a claim that Bayesian inference is internally incoherent: under a widely adopted Bayesian regime, I claim, aggregate replication rates would look much the same.

What I want is a sorted account of what would count against this position. What observations from the actual reform movement—preregistered replications, registered reports, adversarial collaborations, meta-analytic estimates of heterogeneity—would most directly threaten it? What would have to be true of labs that genuinely moved to Bayesian practice for my position to fail? Would it matter if Bayesian analyses under the same incentive pressures showed measurably less selective reporting, or if Bayesian reanalyses of weak frequentist results tended to overturn those results rather than merely reframe them at a different level of uncertainty? Which formal results, simulation studies, or published reanalyses should a defender of the view treat as decisive evidence against it, and which would be merely awkward? What should I concede that defenders of my own view would themselves regard as counting against it—for example, that the position has degenerated into a generic suspicion of any methodological reform, or that I am describing incentive effects at a grain too coarse to falsify? I would personally be most moved by natural experiments or audit studies of working scientists, more than by simulations alone. Finally, tell me honestly whether the position is insulated: if every apparent counterexample can be redescribed as another downstream effect of bad incentives, has the view stopped risking anything, and is that a defect or the appropriate grain for a causal claim of this kind?
