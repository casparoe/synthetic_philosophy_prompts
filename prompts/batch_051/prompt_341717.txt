A popular philosophy book I read recently gives the following account of one response to the sorites paradox:

"Epistemicism holds that the sorites paradox is not really about vagueness in language at all, but about our ignorance. On this view the predicate 'bald' has a sharp cut-off: there is a particular number of hairs such that a person with that many hairs is bald and a person with one fewer is not. The catch is that no one can ever know where the cut-off lies. Since the premises of the paradox are jointly inconsistent, the epistemicist concludes that the inductive premise is false, but says we can never know which premise is the false one — and with that the paradox is dissolved. The view is motivated mainly by the thought that classical logic and bivalence are worth preserving unless we have a compelling reason to abandon them, and the sorites provides none. Critics reply that this turns cut-offs into occult facts with no connection to how speakers actually use the word: the line is supposedly sharp even though every possible observation leaves it undetermined."

I have a nagging sense that this sketch is partly fair and partly a caricature, but I cannot confidently separate the two. Could you give me an accuracy audit of this passage?
