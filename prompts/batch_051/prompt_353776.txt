Throughout my career as a geochemist I served on appointment, promotion and grant committees, and I retired with the sense that practising science and administering science rely on two different pictures of rationality. In the laboratory we behaved as optimisers under constraints: an experimental design was defective if a better use of instrument time was available, and a merely adequate measurement was something to improve rather than accept. But when we became an institution, we switched logic. We would fix explicit bars — a candidate needed such-and-such a publication record and degree of independence, a proposal needed to clear thresholds of originality, feasibility and significance — and once an item cleared the bar, debate effectively ended. We hired, promoted or funded the thing in front of us without asking whether a later candidate or proposal would have been better still. We sometimes noticed this and defended it by saying that a committee has only so much time and attention, and that holding out for the best can leave you with nothing.

Now that I have taken up philosophy, I recognise this as close to satisficing, and I want a more careful account of the commitment than we ever gave each other over coffee. What theory of rationality, or of practical reason, does this ordinary institutional practice presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose bounded rationality of the sort associated with Herbert Simon, or can it be derived from standard expected-utility reasoning once search costs, deliberation costs and the option value of stopping are properly priced in? Does the practice presuppose that its thresholds can be given a non-arbitrary justification, and what would that justification look like? And if the institutions of science were rebuilt so that every appointment, promotion and grant decision involved thoroughgoing optimisation, what would be gained and lost, and would such institutions be more rational on your preferred account? Please take a definite position on what the practice genuinely presupposes and defend it, rather than laying out the available positions and leaving me to choose; and please write in British English.
