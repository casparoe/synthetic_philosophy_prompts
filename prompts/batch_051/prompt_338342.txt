Here are the criteria I was taught for a belief's counting as knowledge: the belief is true; the believer is justified in holding it; and the justification is connected to the fact that makes the belief true in the right non-accidental way — roughly, the belief is held because of that fact, not merely in its presence.

Now consider a subject in a pain experiment. A thermal stimulator is heating her forearm to a moderately painful temperature, and she forms the judgment, "I am in pain, and there is something it is like for me right now," which she then reports aloud. She is a careful and honest reporter, and she knows the experimental setup. She also accepts, on philosophical grounds, an epiphenomenalist account of phenomenal consciousness: the felt quality of the pain is caused by brain activity, but the felt quality itself never causes any physical event, including the neural activity that produces her judgment and her speech. On her own view, her belief that she is in pain is caused entirely by the brain processes that also cause the felt quality, and not by the felt quality itself. In a physically identical counterpart of her with no felt quality at all, the same brain processes would produce the same judgment and the same words.

Does her judgment count as knowledge under the three criteria? Work through the criteria one by one. Where a criterion does not decide the case, say what further fact about justification or knowledge would be needed to settle it. Finally, say whether the case should be taken to show that the third criterion needs revision — for example, that a judgment can be justified by direct acquaintance with a fact even when that fact does not cause the judgment — or that the epiphenomenalist's position is self-undermining.
