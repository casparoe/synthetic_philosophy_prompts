I'm in this philosophy club at school and two people got stuck in a weird argument about the paradox of hedonism last week. We'd read a bit of Henry Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics, and the passage we looked at says, basically, that if you aim directly at being happy or pleased, you often end up with less of it then if you aimed at something else for its own sake. I don't have the exact wording in front of me, but that was the gist.

One friend, call him A, said this is a devastating problem for hedonism as a practical view. If pleasure is the only thing that matters, then the rational thing is to pursue it directly. But Sidgwick's point shows that pursuing it directly tends to fail. So hedonism tells you: the best way to get the only thing that matters is to stop trying to get it and care about other stuff for its own sake. But you can't just switch off the belief that pleasure is the only thing that matters, so you're stuck pretending. He said that's self-defeating.

The other friend, B, said A is overstating it. First, the paradox is not universal. It's true for certain kinds of complicated, self-conscious pleasures, like trying to force yourself to have fun at a party or trying to be happy. But there are plain sensory pleasures where aiming directly at them works fine: if I'm hungry and eat something really good, the direct aim at the pleasure of eating doesn't destroy it. Second, even when the paradox applies, the hedonist can decide to focus on activities for their own sake while still believing those activities are justified by the pleasure they'll bring. Motivation and justification are different. You don't need self-deception, you just need to get absorbed in the thing. People do it all the time.

A came back: that's exactly the problem. A hedonist's reason for choosing the activity is the pleasure it will bring, so the "for its own sake" focus is instrumental all the way down. If you truly believed the activity mattered only as a means to pleasure, you couldn't genuinely get absorbed in it; the absorption would depend on hiding your own reason from yourself. Maybe it's possible for a while, but it's fragile, and the moment you remember why your really doing it, the paradox bites again.

B said: no, because we can have two attitudes at once. You can know the moon is a huge rock, but still see it as a beautiful glowing thing. You can know a game is just made of rules invented for fun, and still care about winning within the game. The hedonist is in the same position: she knows her ultimate value is pleasure, but she can still take the activity seriously while she's doing it.

Neither would budge. I'm supposed to write a short paper on who's right about what. Can you go through their back-and-forth and say, not just who wins overall, but which specific claims each one gets right and which they get wrong? And if there's a version of hedonism that escapes the paradox, what does it have to give up?
