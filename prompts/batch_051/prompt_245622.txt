I've got a bit stuck on something about photographs and how institutions treat them. Courts seem to treat photographs as a special kind of evidence: a photo of a bruised face or a crashed car is shown to the jury and they're expected to see the injury or the scene pretty much directly. A passport photograph is there so a border officer can see who you are. And when a newspaper prints a photo, people will say they saw it happen even though what they saw was a picture. Yet the same institutions also know perfectly well that photographs are cropped, staged, lit, filtered, chosen from a burst of shots, and now AI-editted. A careful sketch of a suspect, drawn from a witness's description, would not be treated the same way.

So what exactly does this practice presuppose about what a photograph is? Does it need the strong claim that we literally see through the picture to the thing photographed, or would a weaker causal story (light bounced off the object onto film or a sensor) be enough to justify the special treatment? And is the practice consistent, or does it quietly switch between two incompatible views depending on whether a photo is being used as evidence or as illustration? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory, say where the practice strictly needs the transparency idea and where it could get by without it, and spell out what would have to change in, say, a courtroom if we gave up the idea that photographs let us see the past directly.
