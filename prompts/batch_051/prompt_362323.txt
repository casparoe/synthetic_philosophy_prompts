I'm a retired sedimentary geologist, and after forty years of thinking about which strata will hold a load and which just look nice, I've ended up in the philosophy of language, where I keep reaching for a distinction I can't find stated cleanly. I call it load-bearing grammar versus decorative grammar. A rule is load-bearing when breaking it changes what a sentence means or leaves it genuinely unparseable to a competent speaker — think of scrambling the word order in "the dog bit the man." A rule is decorative when the meaning and the parse survive untouched and the only thing that changes is whose dialect gets treated as "correct": split infinitives, "less" versus "fewer," sentence-final prepositions, singular "they."

My claim is that most of the classic fights between prescriptivists and descriptivists are really fights about decorative rules that one side treats as load-bearing, and that descriptivists make the mirror-image mistake of treating even load-bearing rules as if they were just prestige conventions, because from a high enough altitude everything looks arbitrary. I want an honest assessment: is this already a thing under some name I don't know (it feels to obvious not to be)? Is the distinction coherent, or does "load-bearing" collapse once you ask load-bearing for whom, and in which dialect, and for a first-language versus a second-language speaker? And is the second claim actually true, or am I just caricaturing descriptivism?
