I've been working on risk scoring for machine learning models, and I keep returning to a distinction that John Maynard Keynes draws in A Treatise on Probability, chapter VI, titled "The Weight of Arguments." I'm an engineer with no formal philosophy training, so I want to know whether the idea I've built on top of it is sound or whether I've forced a philosophical point into a technical mold.

In that chapter, as I understand it, Keynes argues that probability is not the only magnitude we should care about when evaluating a judgment. He distinguishes probability, which reflects the balance of favorable and unfavorable evidence relative to what we know, from something he calls the weight of the argument, which reflects how much relevant evidence the probability rests on. His point, as I read it, is that new relevant evidence can leave the probability unchanged, or move it up or down depending on which way the evidence points, and yet the judgment has still improved in another respect: it now rests on a more substantial body of evidence. So two conclusions can agree in probability while differing in weight.

I've been trying to operationalize this as something I call "inference density." For a model's prediction, define the reference set as all the evidence dimensions that a fully informed reasoner would treat as relevant to that prediction. The model's inference density is the fraction of those dimensions that are actually reflected in the evidence behind the prediction, with each dimension counted equally unless there is a specific reason to weight it. My claim is that inference density is a separate quantity from probability and that rational reliance on a prediction should be a joint function of the two. Two churn models might both output 30 percent for a given customer; the first is trained on 50 rows from one quarter, the second on 50,000 rows spanning several years and multiple customer segments. I want to say the second model's prediction has higher inference density and should be treated as more robust, even though the probability is identical.

Is this concept coherent? Does it already exist in decision theory or formal epistemology under a better name? And does it actually capture what Keynes was after, or have I imported a frequentist idea of a total relevant-evidence set that his notion of weight is supposed to push against? I'm genuinely unsure whether this is a small useful engineering tool or a category mistake, so I would welcome the strongest reconstruction of the idea, the three most serious objections to it, and a candid verdict on whether any of them is fatal.
