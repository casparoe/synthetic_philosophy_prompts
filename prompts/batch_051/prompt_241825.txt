You are a professional philosopher of law with expertise in the Hart–Devlin debate and in the structure of arguments about the moral limits of the criminal law. I am writing a paper on legal moralism—the view that the criminal law may legitimately prohibit conduct simply because the conduct is deeply immoral, even if it causes no harm or offense to any non-consenting person. To organize the paper, I have compiled the following four arguments that a defender of legal moralism might give against a Millian or Hartian harm principle:

(1) The social-disintegration argument: a society is constituted partly by a shared moral code; when the criminal law stops enforcing that code, the shared morality erodes, and with it the bonds that hold the society together.
(2) The moral-environment argument: just as law can regulate pollutants or nuisances that degrade a shared physical environment, it may regulate conduct that degrades a shared moral environment, even where no assignable individual is harmed.
(3) The education-of-character argument: the criminal law is a society's most emphatic form of moral teaching; by refusing to criminalize vice, the state normalizes it and gradually corrupts citizens' characters.
(4) The democratic self-government argument: if a democratic majority regards some private conduct as abhorrent, and if law is the normal vehicle through which a community's moral judgments become public norms, then the majority may criminalize it without any further showing of harm.

I suspect these four cover the familiar moves in the debate, but I am looking for an additional argument for legal moralism that is genuinely distinct from all of them—not a rewording, not a special case, and not a mere combination of (1)–(4). If you can construct such an argument, develop it carefully, state its premises, explain exactly how it differs from each item on my list, and then give me your honest assessment of whether it is stronger or weaker than the standard moves. Please start by brainstorming several candidate arguments and then argue for the one you consider most promising, even if your final verdict is that it does not ultimately succeed.
