My research group had to decide whether to adopt a new data-processing pipeline before the semester started, so we each ran a small pilot on our own projects. At the meeting the PI had us go around the room. The three people before me all said we should adopt it; my own pilot had been lukewarm — the tool crashed on me twice and only gave a modest speed-up. I still said adopt it. My thinking was: these are careful people, and if any of them had seen the mess I saw, they probably wouldn't have sounded so confident; so three yeses are themselves evidence about what their pilots must have shown, and three yeses outweigh one lukewarm no. The pipeline ended up being a disaster and we dropped it three weeks later. I keep going back and forth: part of me thinks my reasoning in the room was basically sound, but I also can't shake the thought that this is how a group ends up confidently doing something that almost no one privately believes — each later person treats the earlier yeses as information, but the later yeses may just be echoing an aggregate that no one actually saw in their own data. And to make it worse, I don't know whether the second and third people were swayed by the person before them rather than by their own pilots; does that matter, and how should it change the update? Was my vote rational? Was the group irrational even if each of us, individually, was being reasonable? At what point should I stop updating on how other people voted and just trust my own pilot?
