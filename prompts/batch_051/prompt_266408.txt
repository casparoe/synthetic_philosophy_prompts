I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have spent the past few months reading around the old ethics-of-belief dispute: roughly, W. K. Clifford's position that there is something morally and intellectually wrong with believing beyond one's evidence, versus William James's position that when a genuine decision is forced, urgent, and not decidable on the evidence, our non-evidential commitments may properly tip the balance. I have ended up with a framework of my own, and I would like a candid professional assessment of it before I decide whether to build an article around it.

I call it the blast-radius account of evidence.

The central claim is that the amount of evidence you need before you may believe a proposition should scale with the belief's blast radius: the range and importance of the other beliefs and practical commitments that are conditional on it, in the sense that they would have to be revised, abandoned, or re-justified if the belief turned out false. A belief is load-bearing to the extent that other beliefs and plans are built on top of it; a belief is cladding to the extent that it can be removed without the rest of the structure changing. For example, the belief that an experimental drug is safe has a small blast radius for someone who merely finds the trial data intriguing, and a very large one for the clinician who has written it into a treatment plan. Clifford-style strictness, on this account, applies fully only to load-bearing beliefs. For cladding beliefs, Jamesian latitude is acceptable because an error there is cheap and revisable.

So far this may look like nothing more than stakes-sensitivity. The less obvious parts are these three theses.

1. The Migration Thesis. Beliefs do not arrive labeled load-bearing or cladding. A belief typically starts as cladding and quietly becomes load-bearing as other beliefs and plans accumulate on top of it, and the same proposition can migrate for the same person over time. The central epistemic duty is therefore not a one-time vetting at the moment of adoption but a recurring audit: noticing which of one's under-evidenced beliefs have silently become structural.

2. The Permission Thesis. Justification for belief is comparative and continuous rather than binary. For any proposition P, the amount of evidence required for believing P is proportional to P's blast radius at that moment. Two people with identical evidence toward P can differ in how well-justified they are in believing P because they have built different structures on top of P. There is no flat evidential threshold that applies across all contexts.

3. The Scaffolding Corollary. Because anything one builds on an under-evidenced belief eventually turns that belief into a load-bearing one, the Jamesian liberty to believe beyond one's evidence is at most a provisional loan, never a right. You may hold an under-evidenced belief only while you explicitly treat it as provisional and refuse to build anything on it. The moment you begin to draw inferences from it or make decisions on its basis, you incur the full Cliffordian debt, and you must either pay it down with evidence or dismantle the belief.

A concrete illustration: a reporter may provisionally accept that an anonymous source's account is accurate in order to know which leads to chase, as long as that acceptance is scaffolding to be dismantled rather than a finished floor. But once the account becomes the factual basis for further reporting or for a public accusation, its blast radius has grown enormously, and the same evidence that was adequate for the provisional attitude is no longer adequate for belief.

I read enough of this literature to suspect that I have reinvented something, perhaps pragmatic encroachment or a cousin of James's doctrine, and I want to know exactly where the overlap and divergence lie. Please adopt the role of a professor of epistemology with a strong working knowledge of decision theory, and give me the honest assessment you would give a colleague. Before offering your verdict, brainstorm two or three ways of making the notion of a blast radius precise, and state the most charitable formalization you can find. Then present the three strongest objections to the account, say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable, and tell me whether the framework is genuinely distinct from existing ideas or mostly a repackaging of them.
