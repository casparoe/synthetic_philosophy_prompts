I'm a bio major taking a philosophy of biology course this term, and we've spent the last few weeks on the Machamer–Darden–Craver picture of mechanistic explanation, with all the entities-and-activities machinery and the idea that a good mechanism has to be organized so that it actually produces the phenomenon without gaps. On the side I'm on the editorial board of our university's undergrad biology journal, and the editor asked me to turn what we've been reading into something the journal can actually use, because we keep recieving short research notes (about 2,500 words) where undergrads knock out a gene or find a correlation and then write "this reveals the mechanism by which X happens" when there's no mechanism there, just a black box with an arrow. Our reviewers are undergrad bio majors with maybe 15–20 minutes per manuscript, mostly haven't read any philosophy, and have to mark accept, minor revisions, major revisions, or reject, with a short note for the authors. So I need you to write the thing for me: a decision procedure or checklist, in plain language, that a reviewer can run in order to decide whether a paper is actually giving a mechanistic explanation in the M-D-C sense, as opposed to a description, a prediction, or a mechanism sketch that doesn't do enough yet.

You're the philosopher of biology who actually teaches this class. Please think carefully, maybe start by listing out what a mechanism has to include for M-D-C (entities, activities, spatial-temporal organization, productive continuity, start and termination conditions, maybe levels) and then translate each of those into ordered questions with rules for what counts as a good enough answer, where the procedure stops with a "not a mechanism" verdict on an explanation claim, and where it has to pause and hand the call to an editor because it's a judgment about how much detail a short note can reasonably demand — e.g., when a mechanism sketch with explicitly flagged gaps is acceptable and when it isn't. Also be honest about what the procedure can't do: for instance, it can't tell you whether the claimed mechanism is true, just whether the paper has actually specified one. Then run the procedure on these three summaries I've simplified from our last batch, and show exactly what a reviewer would write: (1) a paper showing that microRNA-21 levels rise in stressed cells and that knocking it down changes a stress marker, then concluding it has found the mechanism of stress-related damage, with no intermediates, no spatial localization, and no activities spelled out; (2) a paper on a two-protein circadian feedback loop that names the proteins, says where and when they bind, shuttle, and get modified, and shows how the loop produces sustained oscillation; (3) a paper that draws a wiring diagram where receptor X leads to protein Y via an arrow labeled activation, but the authors say the precise activity is "to be characterized" and call their account a mechanism sketch anyway. I should warn you I have a soft spot for paper (1) and suspect your procedure will tell me to reject it; that's okay, just don't pull punches.
