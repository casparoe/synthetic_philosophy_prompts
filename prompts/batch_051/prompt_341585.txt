In the *Meno*, after the demonstration with the enslaved boy, Socrates concedes that true opinion is no less useful than knowledge for guiding action. He then compares true opinions to the statues of Daedalus: while they remain in place they are valuable, but they tend to run away unless fastened down. What turns them into knowledge, he says, is being tied down by reasoning out the cause, which he links to recollection. I noticed a parallel with modern machine learning. A model that has merely memorised correct input–output pairs can perform well on data similar to its training set, yet it is easily broken by distribution shift or adversarial examples; a model that has learned something closer to the underlying mechanism can generalise, recalibrate, and sometimes recognise when it is out of its depth. Both seem to say that having the right answer is not enough: what matters is having the right kind of tie to the world. My question is whether this is a genuine structural parallel or just a loose analogy. Does Socrates' "reasoning out the cause" function like learning an invariant or causal representation, or does the comparison break because Platonic causes are eternal objects of recollection, not regularities acquired from data? What exactly has to be true on both sides for anything substantive to transfer?
