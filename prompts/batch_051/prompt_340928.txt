I direct a small program at a university humanities institute, and we are putting together a funding proposal for a twelve-month project at the intersection of economic history and the philosophy of explanation. The topic I want funded is not another answer to the Needham question or the Great Divergence debate but an examination of whether contrastive questions of that form are well posed in the first place. The Needham question asks why modern science arose in early modern Europe rather than in China; the Great Divergence debate, in one of its sharpened versions, asks why the decisive break into sustained economic growth happened in England rather than in the lower Yangzi delta. My half-formed suspicion, which I would like the proposal to sharpen, is that much of the literature quietly dissolves these questions into parallel comparisons of institutions, factor prices, and contingencies without ever defending the original two-case contrast, and that a question of the form "why X and not Y?" only becomes well posed once the comparison set, the foil, and the causal field are specified well enough that the answer could be wrong. A colleague of mine keeps insisting that the question is perfectly fine as long as a single model can retrodict both outcomes, but that seems to assume the two cases belong in one explanatory frame, which is precisely what needs to be shown.

Please write the one-page proposal in continuous prose, as a letter a foundation officer could read, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and in American English throughout. It should state the central question precisely; explain why existing positions in the comparative-history and philosophy-of-science literatures leave it open, referring to positions and arguments in general terms without citations or a bibliography, since I will fill those in myself; argue for a specific approach and against the obvious alternatives, such as a purely empirical survey of how historians frame comparisons or a purely formal treatment in terms of counterfactual causal models; describe what a useful deliverable would look like for people actually writing comparative history and what would count as failure; and give a realistic scope for twelve months. The proposal should also make the case both that the question is genuinely open and that a philosophical rather than empirical project is the right way to make progress on it.
