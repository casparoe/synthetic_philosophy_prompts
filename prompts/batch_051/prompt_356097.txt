Kurt Gödel found an exact solution to Einstein's field equations that describes a rotating, homogeneous universe containing closed timelike curves — worldlines along which an object could in principle return to an earlier point in its own history. Gödel took this to show that the ordinary conception of time as a global, objectively advancing present is an idealization rather than a mind-independent feature of reality. Please give me a numerical credence for the claim that Gödel's argument succeeds in showing at least that much. I am currently at about 0.3; a colleague who finds Gödel philosophically persuasive is at about 0.7. Decompose the claim into at least the following terms, assign each a rough probability, and then combine them into your own number: (i) the probability that a universe of this kind, or some physically reasonable solution of general relativity with closed timelike curves, is genuinely possible given ordinary matter and plausible initial conditions; (ii) the probability that, granting such a universe is possible, closed timelike curves would show that there is no objective global present within it, rather than merely showing that the spacetime has a strange global time-structure; (iii) the probability that a merely possible counterexample to a supposedly essential feature of time is enough to show that the feature is an idealization in our actual, apparently non-rotating universe. Finally, identify which term most plausibly explains the gap between the 0.3 and 0.7 estimates and what evidence would most move your credence.
