The exam question was:
"Herbert Simon argued that expected utility maximization is infeasible for agents with limited time, information, and computational capacity, and proposed satisficing instead. Some critics reply that satisficing is just maximization in disguise: once search costs are included, stopping at the first option that meets an aspiration level is an optimal stopping rule. Explain what satisficing is, why Simon thought maximization is infeasible, and assess whether the search-cost objection shows that satisficing reduces to constrained maximization. Be specific about how aspiration levels are set and revised, and whether their adaptability matters for that objection."

The student wrote:
"Satisficing means considering options one at a time and choosing the first one that clears an aspiration level, rather than ranking all the options and picking the best. Simon's claim was that an agent with finite time, information, and computational capacity often cannot do expected utility maximization at all: she would have to enumerate every alternative, assign probabilities and utilities, and optimize, which is impossible for the problems real agents face. So the satisficing procedure is meant to be feasible, not an approximation that gets the same answer.

The obvious objection is that satisficing is just constrained maximization with search costs folded in. If evaluating an option costs something, then a policy of stopping at the first acceptable option can be redescribed as maximizing total utility net of search costs. But this redescription requires the agent to have already solved a much harder problem: optimal stopping requires knowing the distribution of option quality, the cost of continued search, and the value of what might be found. A satisficer with a simple threshold doesn't solve that second-order optimization problem, so the reduction is formally possible but descriptively hollow.

For Simon, the aspiration level is just whatever threshold the agent happens to start with; he doesn't have much to say about how it changes, and search-cost models have to treat it as a fixed reservation wage anyway. In any case, I don't think the satisficing-vs-maximizing debate is empirically decidable. Because any consistent choice behavior can be represented as maximizing some utility function, a threshold-using agent and an optimizing agent are just two descriptions of the same behavior, with no fact of the matter about which one is really running. The search-cost objection only succeeds as a representational point, not as a psychological one."
