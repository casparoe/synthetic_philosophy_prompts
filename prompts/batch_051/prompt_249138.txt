Our school's equity and inclusion seminar has spent several sessions on what the facilitator calls epistemic injustice: the idea that people can be wronged specifically in their capacity as knowers — disbelieved, talked over, or left without the concepts to describe their own experience. To make this concrete, the facilitator keeps returning to a picture of credibility as a kind of currency. She says society runs a credibility economy: some people are born with a large inherited account, others must earn every cent, and some begin overdrawn. And just as we would correct an unfair distribution of money, she argues, we should correct the unfair distribution of credibility. In practice, this means that when a member of a marginalized group reports a slight, or describes an experience the rest of us have not had, the right response is to lend extra credibility to their account — to treat their word as if it had already been discounted and needed topping up.

I find the picture genuinely illuminating, but I suspect it is doing more work than we have checked. One student pushed back by saying that believing someone is not like handing over a fixed sum, since you can extend credibility to one person without taking it from anyone else; the facilitator replied that attention and airtime are finite, so the budget picture still holds. That exchange is what made me want a full audit. Begin by giving a correspondence table: what in the credibility case corresponds to currency itself, to inherited wealth, to earning, to a transaction, to inflation, to debt. Then identify the places where the economic picture breaks down or quietly imports features that credibility may not actually have — for example, fungibility, measurability, zero-sumness, or the idea that credibility can be transferred from one person to another. For each disanalogy, say whether it matters for the facilitator's redistributive conclusion or is merely cosmetic. Finally, say whether her practical recommendation — systematically lending extra credibility to marginalized speakers — survives the audit, and, if the currency analogy cannot carry that conclusion, offer a better analogy that can.
