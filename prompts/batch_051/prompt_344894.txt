I'm a software engineer, and I keep finding myself in arguments about whether current text-to-image models are genuinely creative or only produce images that look creative by recombining patterns from their training data. One specific version of the skeptical claim, which I want to put to a test, is this: a diffusion model trained on text-image pairs has no representation of the objects it depicts as three-dimensional things. When a prompt like "an armchair photographed from directly above" produces a plausible image, that is only because the training distribution contains enough top-down armchair images for the model to interpolate against, not because the model has inferred anything about the chair as a shape that persists across viewpoints. On this view the model is doing something close to a high-dimensional lookup over 2D pixel statistics, and the apparent depth, occlusion, and lighting consistency in its outputs are properties of the training data rather than anything the model knows. The supposed creativity is therefore really the creativity of the distribution, not of the model.

I can think of ways to gather suggestive evidence in either direction: prompting for unusual viewpoints; generating a scene and then asking for the same scene from another angle; probing internal activations; checking whether shadows, reflections, and occlusions stay consistent when the scene changes. But I don't trust my own judgment about what any of these would show, because "having a representation" is not a quantity I know how to measure directly, and I've seen myself operationalize concepts into metrics and then quietly drop whatever didn't fit the metric.
