Since I stepped back from a long career in geochemistry, I have been reading the sixteenth-century essayists with the kind of attention I used to give to mineral thin sections. I recently spent several weeks with Montaigne's essay about the Tupinambá of Brazil, in which he reports their custom of eating their prisoners and then turns the comparison around: he suggests that Brazilian warfare, for all its strangeness to European eyes, is more candid and less cruel than the religious massacres and judicial tortures of his own France, and that we are quick to call anything barbarous simply because it is not customary. What keeps me returning to the essay is how easily it can be taken in three different directions: as relativist, denying that any culture may judge another; as skeptical, denying that human reason can settle which customs deserve to prevail; or as universalist, using a distant people to condemn real cruelties in his own society without giving up the idea that some practices deserve condemnation.

The question I want to work through is this. Suppose Montaigne could have listened in on the debates of the 1940s, when the United Nations was being created and a declaration of universal human rights was taking shape. Some anthropologists at the time warned that such a declaration would merely dress Western values up as universal ones, while others answered that the preceding decades had shown exactly why some standards must hold across cultures. What position do you think Montaigne would have defended in that argument? Would he have been more drawn to the caution against a parochial universalism, or more moved to insist that one can criticize one's own society's cruelties while still making cross-cultural judgments? I do not want a survey of the scholarship; I want a careful reconstruction of the position he would most plausibly have taken, including the points where it would have been internally strained.
