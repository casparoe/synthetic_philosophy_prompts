Locke's story about how unowned things become property has two moving parts: acquisition happens by mixing one's labour with the thing, and the acquisition is legitimate only if it leaves others a sufficiency of similar quality in common and does not let the thing spoil while excluded from others. Imagine a jurisdiction that decides to make exactly this story positive law. An unowned resource—land, or an attached natural resource such as a forest, a water source, or a fishing ground—can be claimed by filing a plan of labour and posting a bond equal to one fifth of the resource's appraised value. Title vests after six years, and only if, in each of those years, two neighbouring non-claimants certify that the planned improvement is actually continuing; a missed certificate in any year reverts the claim and forfeits the bond to a commons fund. Once title vests, the holder must each year either pay into the commons fund a proviso dividend equal to the resource's current rental value, or reserve a comparable unimproved portion of the resource for open common use. Finally, any portion of the resource, or of its yield, that the holder lets spoil while excluding others from it voids the claim to that portion.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a reading of Locke. What would it incentivize among would-be claimants, among the neighbours who sign the certificates, and among people who had previously relied on the commons? Where does the scheme get gamed first? Which failure modes are defects of this particular implementation, and which are defects of the labour-and-proviso theory itself? Can the scheme be patched without gutting it, and at what point does patching turn it into a system that is no longer recognizably Lockean?
