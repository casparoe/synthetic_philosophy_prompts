I'm putting together a session on just war theory, and a friend has been pressing an argument on me that I can't find an exit from.

(1) If you aim at the deaths of innocent people as a means to winning a war, then what you do is not just an act of war; it's murder. (This is, roughly, the Anscombe line on Truman.)

(2) In area bombing—the deliberate bombing of whole civilian districts to wreck morale and break the enemy's will to fight—the civilian deaths are intended as a means to that end, not merely accepted as a side effect.

(3) The atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki were, on the official understanding, a form of area bombing: the point was to shock Japan into surrender by destroying cities and killing civilians in one blow.

(4) So, by (1), the atomic bombings were murder.

(5) But the same reasoning applies to the firebombing of Tokyo, Hamburg and Dresden as well, and it then says the leaders who ordered those raids were murderers, with Truman conspicuously among them.

(6) That conclusion seems to prove too much: it turns a standard, widely accepted method of the war into one long exercise in murder, and it makes the atomic bombings no worse in kind than routine strategic bombing. So, unless I'm missing something, at least one step has to go.

Personally I'd rather keep (1) than abandon it, but (6) pulls hard in the other direction. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does?
