I recently inherited my family’s small cattle operation, and I have been trying to think through whether killing a cow that has lived a decent life is morally wrong. In Anarchy, State, and Utopia, Robert Nozick suggests that the correct approach may be to treat nonhuman animals on a broadly utilitarian basis while treating persons as protected by inviolable moral side constraints. I want to know whether the following reconstruction of that suggestion goes through. Please tell me whether the argument is valid, and if it is not, at which numbered step it first fails and why.

(1) A being is protected by moral side constraints only if it is capable of forming, revising, and acting on a conception of the good.  
(2) Nonhuman animals, including cattle, are not capable of forming, revising, and acting on a conception of the good.  
(3) Therefore, cattle are not protected by moral side constraints.  
(4) If a being is not protected by moral side constraints, then the permissibility of any treatment of that being is determined entirely by the balance of pleasure and pain it produces.  
(5) Therefore, the permissibility of killing a cow is determined entirely by the balance of pleasure and pain it produces.  
(6) Killing a cow painlessly and replacing it with another that would not otherwise have existed can yield at least as much pleasure as pain.  
(7) Therefore, painlessly killing a cow that has lived a decent life and replacing it is permissible.

I am particularly suspicious of step (4), but I cannot tell whether that is where the argument fails or whether some earlier step is doing hidden work. In your answer, please state how confident you are in your overall assessment and single out the point you are least sure of.
