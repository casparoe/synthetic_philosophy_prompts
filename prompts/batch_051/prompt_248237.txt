In the course of reporting a piece on the science and philosophy of consciousness, I have been struck by how static the philosophical zombie debate has been since it took its current form in the mid-1990s. The disputants appear to share the same physical facts, the same thought experiment, and the same battery of replies and counter-replies, yet one side finds it nearly self-evident that a molecule-for-molecule physical duplicate of a person could lack phenomenal consciousness and that this conceivability is a reliable guide to what is metaphysically possible, while the other side finds either that the scenario is not genuinely conceivable or that conceivability cannot carry that metaphysical weight. My working hypothesis is that the two sides mean different things by conceivability and that the argument only looks compelling when one slides between those senses, but I am not sure that diagnosis survives contact with what each side actually says. Is this a hidden empirical bet about what a completed neuroscience would eventually explain, a verbal dispute, a clash of bedrock modal intuitions, or two camps answering different questions under a single heading? Diagnose the disagreement with reasons, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given the diagnosis—or whether the impasse should be expected to persist.
