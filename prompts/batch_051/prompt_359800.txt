I'm running a discussion section for an intro philosophy of probability course and next week we're doing Bertrand's paradox, mostly as a way to get at what the principle of indifference can and can't do. I'll give my students the basic setup: an equilateral triangle inscribed in a circle, a chord chosen at random, and the question is how probable it is that the chord is longer than a side of the triangle. The three classic ways of making the chord random give different numbers—uniform endpoints on the circumference gives 1/3, picking a radius and a uniformly distributed point along it (chord perpendicular to the radius) gives 1/2, and picking the chord's midpoint uniformly in the disk gives 1/4. I'd like a decision tree to hand out that takes a student from that conflict to a named position on it, the kind of flowchart where each branch is a question they can answer without already knowing the philosophical labels.

The tree should start from the basic split of whether "choose a chord at random" already determines a unique probability before any coordinate system or parameter gets picked, and then route through questions about whether changing the way you parameterize counts as changing the problem, whether symmetries (rotation, translation, scale) are enough to single out a right answer, and whether the principle of indifference needs to be restricted to finite or specially partitioned spaces. For leaves I want at least these four positions represented: (1) the problem is ill-posed or incomplete until a chord-generating procedure is specified; (2) subjectivism—there is no canonical "random chord" out there, probabilities are assigned relative to a procedure or an agent's information, so the different answers aren't really in conflict; (3) the invariance-based objective view, where a well-posed geometric probability must respect rotation/translation/scale invariance and that prunes all but one answer; and (4) the retrenchment view, where the principle of indifference is fine but only after you've fixed a privileged partition or class of equivalent cases, and the paradox is really a warning about applying it to continuous spaces carelessly. If there are other natural leaves—someone who thinks one of 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 was right all along, say—include them if they fit, but make sure the four above are in there.

Please phrase every node as a concrete question rather than "are you a subjectivist?", and flag any leaves the tree can't cleanly separate from each other. Also mark unoccupied combinations—like maybe nobody wants to hold that there's a unique right answer but also that invariance considerations are irrelevant—and say briefly whether each is empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just underexplored. Audience is undergrads, so keep it to maybe 8–12 branches total. Its tempting to just lecture them, but I want the handout to do the sorting.
