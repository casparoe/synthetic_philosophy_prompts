I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on is partly set in a city-state that's a lot like Singapore, with a bit of China hovering in the background. I keep getting stuck on something in the way people talk about politics, and I'm hoping you can help me think it through rather than give me sources. There's a weird asymmetry in how easily we let performance buy legitimacy. When a state delivers fifty years of growth, safety, clean streets, and a functioning economy, otherwise liberal people will say that maybe authoritarian-but-competent is a real kind of legitimacy, maybe the trade-off is defensible. But nobody accepts that same logic at smaller scales. A mining corporation that runs a company town—pays the wages, builds the school, keeps the peace—is still called coercive even if life there is materially better than outside it. A gang boss who ends street crime in a neighbourhood and takes his cut doesn't become a legitimate governor just because the place got safer and richer. We let the state earn legitimacy by performing well in a way we would never let a company or a boss do. So what justifies the asymmetry? I can guess at the standard moves: the state has a monopoly on force and you can't exit it; it provides public goods at a scale private actors can't; there's no higher authority to appeal to. But those all sound like descriptions of the state's power, not reasons the people under it owe it allegience. I want to know whether the asymmetry between state and non-state coercive actors can actually be defended, and if it can't, whether we should extend performance legitimacy to the company town and the gang boss, or stop granting it to the state. If you defend it, defend it against the case where the company town and the state deliver the same material goods, with the same threat of force behind them, and the only difference you're allowed to appeal to is that one is a state and the other isn't.
