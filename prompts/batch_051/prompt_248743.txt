I’ve been reading in the species problem for a while, and I’m struck by how little the main positions have moved. The evolutionary facts are not in dispute: populations change, lineages split, gene flow is often partial, and reproductive isolation comes in degrees. Yet systematists and philosophers still divide among biological species concepts, phylogenetic species concepts, ecological species concepts, and pluralist or conventionalist views. Darwin himself already treated “species” as largely a term of convenience, saying it differs from “variety” mainly in being more distinct and more permanent. The debate has continued since, with genomic data mostly sharpening the anomalies rather than forcing convergence.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a survey of the positions. Is this a verbal dispute about how to define a term, or a substantive dispute about how the world is divided? Are the sides answering different questions—one about speciation mechanisms, another about units of classification, another about the targets of conservation—under a single heading? Is there a hidden metaphysical question doing the work, for example whether species are natural kinds or individuals? And if it is a mix, which strand is doing most of the work? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or an admission that the term “species” does too many jobs for a single account to succeed? Please also separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
