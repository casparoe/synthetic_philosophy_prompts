At work I spend all day deciding where one thing ends and another begins — modules, services, database tables — and I'm comfortable saying a "service" is just a boundary we drew, not a real thing in the system. But I've noticed I'm inconsistent outside work: I can be talked into saying a heap of sand isn't really a thing over and above the grains, but I would never say the equilavent about my laptop or my dog. The dog is just a dog, full stop, while the heap is only grains arranged heap-wise. What, if anything, could justify that line? Go through the candidate principles someone might use — physical cohesion, causal unity, being alive, having a function, having a point of view, being made of parts that only work together, whatever else seems plausible — test each one against awkward cases (a dead dog, a swarm of bees, a corporation, a rock, a laptop with parts swapped out over the years), and tell me whether I should keep the asymmetry, dissolve heaps and laptops both, or treat heaps as real too. I'd like that as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion.
