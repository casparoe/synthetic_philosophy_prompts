In Nick Bostrom's "The Fable of the Dragon-Tyrant" (2005), a fable about aging and death, he writes: "Other orators argued that the dragon has its place in the natural order and a moral right to be fed. They said that it was part of the very meaning of being human to end up in the dragon’s stomach. Others still maintained that the dragon was good for the human species because it kept the population size down." A colleague takes this passage to do more than satirize the justifications people give for a fate they cannot escape. She thinks it suggests a testable psychological thesis: that a large share of the conviction that death is natural, meaningful, or even beneficial is rationalization generated by the unavailability of any alternative, and that much of it would disappear if biological aging became optional. This is the core of what its critics call "deathism." I want to know whether that thesis is genuinely testable, and how far any test could take us.

Design the strongest empirical test you can of the claim that death-acceptance is typically rationalization rather than autonomous evaluation. Be concrete: the population or comparison, the manipulation or natural variation, the measures, and the pattern of results that would favor the rationalization reading as against the sincere-evaluation reading. Then say exactly what a positive result would and would not settle. In particular, address whether any test can distinguish "this belief is motivated" from "this belief is also well-supported," and whether "rationalization" is doing explanatory work or merely marking the causal origins of a belief the labeler dislikes. If part of the claim is untestable even in principle, say which part and why the remainder would still be worth testing.
