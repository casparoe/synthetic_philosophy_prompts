Over the past few months I have become persuaded of a view I used to resist: that moral trade is both underrated and, within appropriate limits, rationally required. By moral trade I mean the practice of two agents with different or even incompatible moral views exchanging actions rather than arguments — each doing something the other values more, in exchange for the other doing something they value more. For instance, if I care intensely about one cause and only mildly about another while you have the reverse weighting, we can each act disproportionately for the other's cause and both end up having produced more of what we each value than we could have produced by acting alone. The position I now hold is that refusing such a trade, when no independently impermissible act is involved, is usually a failure of instrumental rationality — a kind of moral fastidiousness that keeps one's hands clean at the cost of doing less of what one actually claims to value.

What I want is an honest map of the position's exposure: what would have to be true for it to be false, and which considerations should actually move me. I would like the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which of them a committed defender of moral trade would concede as genuine vulnerabilities rather than dismiss. In particular, I want the view stress-tested in the places where it seems most exposed: value incommensurability; whether trading changes the meaning or integrity of one's actions; epistemic and enforcement problems in real deals; and the risk that moral trade launders or enables wrongdoing by leaving one's counterpart with resources they will use in ways one condemns. Are there cases in which a beneficial trade should be refused even though it would produce more of what both parties value, and would those cases show the position is false or only that it needs constraints? Finally, I want to know whether the view is in danger of insulating itself from refutation by absorbing every counterexample as a side constraint, and, if it is, whether that is a fatal problem for a view of this kind or a reasonable feature of a view about instrumental rationality.
