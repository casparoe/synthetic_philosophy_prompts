I'm a software engineer and I barely know the philosophy here, but I've been mulling something. The argument I keep seeing against open borders goes roughly: you can't have open borders and a generous welfare state, because people will come for the benefits, and the ones who come will be net takers, paying less in taxes than they consume in services, so the welfare state blows up. Now, my whole team is distributed, and it seems like a huge amount of work has become location-independent. A person can live in a high-benefit country and work remote for an employer somewhere else, so their wage is set by a different country's labor market, and they might not pay much local income tax at all. What I want to know is whether that new fact actually breaks the argument, or makes it worse, or just changes which people it applies to. Does the argument loose its force once work is decoupled from residence, or can it be patched without depending on local wages? And if the argument still works, which step is doing the work now—the magnet effect, the fiscal drain, or something else? I'm less interested in the policy conclusion and more in whether the underlying argument survives in a world where a lot of labor can be done from anywhere.
