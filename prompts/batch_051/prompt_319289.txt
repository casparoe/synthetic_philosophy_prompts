Something has been bugging me since I started reading about Morgan's canon, from Conwy Lloyd Morgan's An Introduction to Comparative Psychology. The canon, as it is usually summarized, tells you not to interpret an animal's action as the outcome of a higher psychical faculty if it can be interpreted as the outcome of one lower on the psychological scale. That sounds to me almost exactly like the principle of least privilege in computer security: a process should be granted only the permissions it needs to do its job, not more, even if granting more would also be consistent with what it is doing. In both cases the rule does not say the animal or the process lacks the richer capacity; it says, given the observable behavior or system trace, you should not posit the richer capacity until the poorer one fails.

I want to know whether this is a deep structural identity or just pattern-matching. In particular, least privilege is usually justified by engineering costs and security: a compromised process cannot do more damage than its permissions allow. Morgan's canon is often justified by evolutionary continuity or by a warning against anthropomorphism. Are those justifications analogous, or does one justify a deflationary principle while the other justifies a precautionary one? If the parallel is real, what transfers: does the difficulty of defining least privilege under unknown future tasks correspond to the difficulty of defining lower versus higher psychological faculties? And does the way least privilege gets violated in practice when developers do not know what permissions will be needed correspond to the way animal researchers might violate the canon when they do not know what is already in the animal's behavioral repertoire? I'm trying to figure out whether this is more than a metaphor.
