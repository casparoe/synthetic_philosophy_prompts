Ok so I'm taking a class on the political economy of the welfare state and we've been going over conditionality and the whole 'undeserving poor' framing. I keep turning over this policy idea and I want to figure out how it would actually behave, not whether it's good or bad in the abstract.

The state replaces its usual sanctions regime for unemployment assistance with a placement-bonus system run by a private contractor. The contractor manages the mandatory workfare placements — painting park benches, sorting recycling, that kind of thing. It gets a flat fee per participant per week, plus a large bonus for every participant who leaves the rolls for 'employment or another approved exit' and stays off for six consecutive months. The bonus is clawed back in full if the person comes back onto the rolls within a year. The contractor also has discretion over what counts as a missed appointment or a failed job-readiness step, and those failures move the person into a lighter-touch track that pays the contractor less per week and doesn't count toward the bonus.

Walk me through what this system actually does. Who does the contractor have an incentive to push off the rolls first? How do they game the discretion over missed appointments and the six-month/one-year windows? What happens to participants who are hardest to place? And where does the scheme get gamed first — and once it's gamed, does it end up looking any different from the old punitiveness, even though nobody set out to punish anyone?
