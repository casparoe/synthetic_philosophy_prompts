I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and a question from a colleague has been nagging at me. Some political philosophers, I'm told, hold that coercive laws are legitimate only if they can be justified by public reasons—reasons that could in principle be accepted by all reasonable citizens regardless of their religion or comprehensive worldview (a "comprehensive doctrine," in the jargon). A standard refinement, sometimes called a proviso, allows citizens to introduce religious or other comprehensive arguments into political debate provided they also supply, in due course, the public reasons.

The step I want to probe is the one where a religious argument gets translated into a public reason. Suppose someone proposes a law on religious grounds and also offers a secular-sounding rationale for that same law. How would one test the claim that the secular rationale is a genuine translation of the religious argument—that it carries the argument's actual weight rather than being a post-hoc rationalization? In software, I would test two interfaces for compatibility by checking whether the same inputs produce the same outputs across a range of cases. Does something analogous work here?

Design the strongest possible test to distinguish two hypotheses: (a) the public reason is doing the real justificatory work, so the advocate's support for the law would persist if the religious reason were removed; and (b) the public reason is a rationalization that tracks the religious conclusion and would be revised or discarded when it failed to support that conclusion. Specify the observations or decision procedure, state which outcomes would favor each reading, and be explicit about what the test would leave unsettled even in a clean result. I also want to know where the software-translation analogy breaks down: in what respects is translating a religious argument into a public reason not like checking interface compatibility?
