In my philosophy of mind course we have spent two weeks on Hempel's dilemma, and I still cannot tell how much of physicalism survives it. Give me your actual credence that physicalism can be stated as a substantive, determinate thesis: substantive in that the doctrine could turn out to be false, determinate in that it supports a stable contrast between what is physical and what is not. Please commit to a single number rather than treating the question as settled by one's preferred definitions. Decompose the estimate, assigning a rough probability to each sub-question and explaining how they combine. (i) Can the current-physics horn be blunted by the thought that current physics will be approximately preserved in future theory, rather than simply discarded? (ii) Is the ideal-completed-physics horn really trivial, especially given the possibility that a completed physics posits fundamental phenomenal properties? (iii) Does some placeholder or schema reading of "physical" — such as whatever the completed science of physics describes, with no prior restriction on its ontology — yield a formulation that is both substantive and determinate? (iv) Is physicalism better defended as a family of theses, such as the supervenience of the mental on the physical or the causal closure of the physical, rather than as one proposition? Identify which sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to, and say what evidence or argument would move that sub-question most.
