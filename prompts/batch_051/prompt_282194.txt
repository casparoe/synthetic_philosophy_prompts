1. An ordinary perceptual assertion — a speaker sees a barn in clear view and says "that's a barn" — is proper.
2. The same assertion made in fake barn county, where the one real barn is surrounded by indistinguishable facades, is improper.
3. A holder of one ticket in a fair million-ticket lottery who says "my ticket will lose" on purely statistical grounds is improper.
4. An assertion of a mundane matter based on the testimony of a trusted, reliable colleague is proper.
5. An assertion based on glancing at a stopped clock that happens to read the correct time is improper.

Next week she will rule on this case: Hari believes truly that the seminar meets at noon. He formed the belief by reading a reliably maintained departmental calendar, and in all nearby cases the same method would have produced a true belief; but the belief rests on a false intermediate assumption, because he trusts the entry only because he thinks the administrator updated it this morning, when in fact the entry is correct because the calendar auto-synced overnight. The question is whether Hari may properly assert that the seminar meets at noon.

Predict her verdict. Which norm of assertion — knowledge, safety, sensitivity, a no-false-lemmas condition, or something else — best fits her track record so far, and which of those norms does the new case most cleanly distinguish? What further case, if she ruled on it next, would settle which rule she is actually running? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
