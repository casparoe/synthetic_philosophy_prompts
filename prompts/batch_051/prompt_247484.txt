You are a professional philosopher specializing in the epistemology of reasoning, particularly how cognitive work can be divided across agents and recombined. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming several candidate principles before testing them against my cases.

I have been thinking about an idealization of deliberation sometimes called factored cognition: the idea that a hard cognitive task can be decomposed into smaller tasks, each done separately, and the results combined into a reliable overall answer. A recursive version of it, often called HCH, works like this. A single reasoner takes a question; whenever they encounter a subquestion, they may hand it to another copy of the same setup — another careful reasoner with the same question-answering policy, who may in turn do the same — and then combine the subanswers into their final answer. I want a principle that captures my verdicts about when such a process is more trustworthy than one person simply thinking hard.

Here are my verdicts.

- When a hard mathematical problem is decomposed into lemmas that are each checked independently before being combined, the result can be more reliable than any single mathematician working alone.
- When the subanswers are produced by the same person at different times, with the same knowledge and the same blind spots, I do not see how combining them adds anything over that person just thinking longer.
- When a strategic decision is split into narrow pieces, each assigned to someone who does not know the overall goal, the result is often worse than one competent person's holistic judgment.
- I nevertheless believe that with the right decomposition, the process can be more trustworthy than the best individual, even though each step is ordinary human reasoning.
- When subanswers are combined by simple majority vote without modeling how they depend on one another, I distrust the result even when each subanswer looked fine on its own.
- I also cannot shake the worry that the whole procedure is just laundering one person's judgment through many copies unless the decomposition introduces genuine, independent checks.

Is there one principle that captures all or most of these? What does it predict for a case I have not listed: a legal question split into factual and normative subquestions, each answered by a different expert and then combined by a single judge? And which of my verdicts, if any, should I drop?
