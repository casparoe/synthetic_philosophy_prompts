I keep coming back to a parallel between two ideas that are rarely discussed together, and I cannot tell whether it is a real structural resemblance or just shared imagery. On one side is the postmodern critique of grand narratives: the claim, associated with Lyotard, that the large legitimating stories of modernity — universal progress, the emancipation of human beings through reason, the movement of history through class conflict — present themselves as the ultimate framework within which every smaller claim becomes intelligible, yet each is itself a historically local story that was promoted to the rank of a universal condition. On the other side is the ancient skeptical problem of how a chain of justification can begin at all: a belief is justified by a reason, which itself requires a justification, so the chain must either continue without end, circle back on itself, or stop at a starting point that is simply asserted. The resemblance I see is that a metanarrative and a foundational stopping point are the same kind of move at different scales: each halts the endless chain of why-questions by installing one local starting point and then presenting that choice as the very standard of justification. If that is right, the postmodern claim that every grand narrative is just a local story dressed as necessity would be a sociological restatement of the skeptical claim that every foundation is just a stopping point dressed as a ground.

Please spell out the mapping in detail and tell me whether it is deep or superficial. What would have to be true of both sides for conclusions to transfer in either direction? And where does the analogy break? In particular, please identify at least one thing the parallel would predict that turns out false — for instance, something about whether the skeptical problem captures what is actually objectionable about a metanarrative in social and political life.
