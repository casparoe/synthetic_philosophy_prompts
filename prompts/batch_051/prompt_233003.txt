I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about a child-welfare reform that has been circulating in policy circles. I'd like you to analyze the proposal as a mechanism — how it would actually behave once embedded in real institutions — rather than as a moral slogan to be endorsed or rejected.

The proposal goes like this. Instead of the blunt, long-debated idea of licensing people before they may become parents, the state would designate every legal guardian as a trustee of the child's open future. The operative idea, borrowed from philosophy of childhood, is that a child has a strong interest in reaching adulthood with many worthwhile life paths still open, and that parenting is partly responsible for whether that range of options is preserved or foreclosed early.

Concretely: from the child's sixth birthday to their sixteenth, each guardian files an annual horizons report. The report must list, under three headings — education, health, and life-shaping commitments — the options the guardian has deliberately kept open for the child that year. A local panel of retired teachers, pediatricians, youth workers, and one former family-court judge reviews a random sample of reports, cross-checked against school-enrollment, immunization, and child-protection records the state already holds. If the sampled report indicates that a guardian has closed a major future path early and with little reversibility — for example, withdrawing the child from school for good, refusing a medical treatment whose omission has lifelong consequences, signing the child into an exclusive vocational or religious placement before adolescence, or severing the child's relationship with one side of the family — the panel may escalate: first a conversation, then mandatory family-support services, and finally a limited guardianship order that removes the guardian's authority only over that foreclosed domain and gives it to an independent advocate, with the child's own wishes weighted by age. The panel is instructed never to second-guess ordinary day-to-day parenting.

I can see why the designers think this threads the needle: it does not stop anyone from becoming a parent, and it intervenes only when options are actually being closed. But I want to know how it would behave in the wild. Would parents treat the report as a compliance exercise, writing aspirational prose while changing nothing? Would the panel become a magnet for activists who want it to enforce a particular vision of a good childhood, or for parents skilled at performing open-endedness while quietly steering the child? What does the scheme do to families whose conception of flourishing is early devotion to a craft, a vocation, or a faith? Does it actually protect open futures, or does it mostly select for parents who can produce reassuring paperwork and penalize the inarticulate?

Structure your response in these sections, in this order:

1. Mechanism in brief — restate the proposal's logic in your own words (about 150 words).
2. Direct incentives — what behavior the scheme rewards and punishes among guardians, panel members, and professionals such as family lawyers and therapists (about 250 words).
3. First games — the concrete strategies each actor would use to work the system, in rough order of likely appearance (about 250 words).
4. Who gains and who loses — the distributional and cultural effects, including effects on children who are never asked what they want (about 250 words).
5. Three failure modes — your definite list of the three most consequential ways the scheme goes wrong, each with a one-sentence severity rating (about 300 words).
6. Patches — whether any of the three failure modes could be fixed by altering the scheme, and whether the fixes would destroy what made the proposal attractive (about 200 words).
7. Bottom line — your committed verdict on whether, on net, the mechanism would protect children's open futures or mostly produce paper compliance and new forms of gaming (about 150 words).

I want a definite position throughout: make choices, give reasons, and do not leave the question sitting among options. If you think two readings of the proposal are genuinely possible, commit to the one you find most plausible and mention the alternative only in passing.
