I am a philosophy professor preparing a graduate seminar on constitutional precommitment and Ulysses contracts, and I have been wrestling with a passage in Federalist No. 49, written by Publius and standardly attributed to James Madison. The paper is Madison’s response to Jefferson’s proposal, in his draft constitution for Virginia, that breaches of the Constitution by two of the three branches should be remedied by calling a convention of the people. The passage I want to think through carefully is this:

In the next place, it may be considered as an objection inherent in the principle, that as every appeal to the people would carry an implication of some defect in the government, frequent appeals would, in a great measure, deprive the government of that veneration which time bestows on every thing, and without which perhaps the wisest and freest governments would not possess the requisite stability.

I am having trouble stating the argument’s exact structure. In the standard Ulysses-contract model, a constitutional precommitment is usually understood as an agent binding itself against predicted future weakness of will or passion: the present self does not object to future constitutional attention as such, but only to the future self’s likely irrationality or impetuosity. Madison’s veneration argument seems different. It appears to locate the danger not in passionate or irrational revision, but in the mere act of raising constitutional questions with the people. Is the claim that frequent appeals to the people weaken government because they signal defects, because they erode habitual reverence by inviting scrutiny, or because they do both through some single underlying mechanism? And does the argument commit Madison to the stronger view that constitutional stability depends on some degree of unexamined prejudice—perhaps even on citizens not inspecting the Constitution too closely—rather than on their considered judgment that the Constitution is good? I would like help isolating the premises, clarifying what work “veneration” and later “prejudices” do in the same paper, and determining whether this passage can be presented to my students as a coherent alternative to the Ulysses-contract story rather than merely as a piece of eighteenth-century rhetoric.
