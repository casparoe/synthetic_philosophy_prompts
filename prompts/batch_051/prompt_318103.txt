I keep going back and forth on whether effort is valuable in itself, and I want to use a poem as a way into it for a small seminar I'm teaching. One side says effort is just the price you pay, its value entirely borrowed from the outcome: effort poured into a failed or pointless project is pure loss, maybe worse than loss because it compounds disappointment. The other side says the effort is part of the good itself—that a hard-won result is better than the same result handed to you, and that struggle has a kind of worth even when it gets you nowhere. I want a villanelle, nineteen lines, from the point of view of someone mid-struggle (say, an adult trying to learn a difficult piano piece). The two refrains should be these two positions, phrased as the speaker's own thoughts, and the form should do the arguing: the refrains alternating and then landing next to each other as the final two lines should show why the two views keep circling each other and why ending with them side by side leaves the question standing rather than resolved. Then, under a section titled "Commentary on the choices made" (roughly 150–250 words), walk me through how you mapped each refrain to each view, any formal choices that struck you as doing real work (or not), and where you think you leaned on a contested reading of effort's value. In that commentary also tell me how confident you are that the poem captures the structure of the debate, and which single point you're least sure about.
