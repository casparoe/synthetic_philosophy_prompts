I am a PhD student working at the intersection of psychophysics and philosophy of mind, and I have been trying to understand why one debate in the postdiction literature seems structurally unable to move. What I want is not a verdict on which side is right but a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, together with an account of what would count as progress.

The debate concerns the colour-phi phenomenon. Two small discs are flashed in quick succession at different locations, each with an appropriate brief duration and spatial and temporal separation. Under the right conditions, observers do not report two stationary flashes; they report a single disc moving continuously from the first location to the second. When the two discs differ in colour, the report becomes stranger still: the moving disc appears to change colour partway along its trajectory, at a point earlier than the arrival of the second flash. The later stimulus seems to reach backward and determine how the earlier stretch of experience is described.

Two families of explanation have contested this for decades. On the Orwellian account, the episode is initially experienced in something close to real time: the subject is aware of the first disc, then of a moving disc in the original colour, and only subsequently of the second disc and its new colour. A later process of memory revision then overwrites the record, so that when the subject reports the episode, the narrative has been retroactively cleaned up into a single moving disc that changed colour before it could have. The revision happens after the fact, at the level of memory or narrative, the way a history is rewritten. On the Stalinesque account, the revision happens before the fact, at the level of the experience itself: the brain withholds awareness until enough sensory evidence has arrived, constructs the finished percept of a moving, colour-changing disc, and only then presents the whole scene to consciousness, already edited. No misleading experience is ever conscious and then corrected; the staging is done in advance.

The difficulty I keep running into is that both accounts appear to accommodate every relevant datum, because all the data arrive through retrospective report, and a retroactive edit introduced before report can always be re-described as an edit introduced after experience. The debate keeps resurfacing in modern descendants of the phenomenon — the flash-lag effect, the cutaneous rabbit, claims that consciousness lags behind input versus claims that memory is retroactively updated — and papers continue to announce that one architecture has finally been disambiguated, yet the field does not converge.

My working hypothesis is that the dispute is not an ordinary empirical disagreement but is generated by a shared hidden assumption: that there is a sharp, well-defined boundary between the experience itself and the memory of, or report of, the experience. If no experiment can in principle locate that boundary, then the two models may be notational variants of the same underlying causal structure, differing only in where they place an unobservable line. I can, however, also see a reading on which the dispute is genuinely substantive — for example, a question about the temporal properties of a neural state under masking — so I do not want to assume my hypothesis is correct.

I would like you to assess that hypothesis seriously and give a precise diagnosis of the disagreement: is it a hidden factual question, a verbal dispute about the word "experience," an architectural disagreement about stages of processing that could in principle be tested, or an ill-posed question that should be dissolved rather than settled? Given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress — a specific experimental design, a formal constraint, a conceptual distinction, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
