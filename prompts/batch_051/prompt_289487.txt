I teach a senior undergraduate seminar in modal logic, and this term we have covered Kripke semantics for quantified modal logic, including the Barcan formula (BF), ∀x□φ → □∀xφ, and its converse (CBF), □∀xφ → ∀x□φ. I am writing the final exam: three hours, one essay question on this material, worth 25 marks, with a recommended 75 minutes.

Please draft the essay question, a model answer of about 700–800 words, and a marking scheme out of 25. The question should be self-contained—no quotations, citations, or bibliography—and should be answerable from the stated semantics and notation alone. It should also be unanswerable by reciting the standard validity conditions: the difficulty should come from building and reading the models, not from remembering labels.

The question should require candidates to do three things. First, on a two-world frame with W = {w0, w1} and w0 Rw1, using the usual actualist Kripke clauses (∀x quantifies over D(w) at world w, and the extension of an n-ary predicate at w is a subset of D(w)^n), construct two models: one in which BF is false at w0 while CBF is true, and one in which CBF is false at w0 while BF is true. They should specify D(w0), D(w1), and the extension of a monadic predicate P at each world, and identify the evaluation clause responsible for each failure.

Second, candidates should state the exact structural conditions on the domain function D that are necessary and sufficient for BF and for CBF to be valid on such a frame, explain them in terms of new objects and disappearing objects along the accessibility relation, and then show that on an S5 frame, accepting both formulas forces every world to share a single domain.

Third, candidates should critically assess the student slogan that in accepting both Barcan formulas one has thereby accepted that everything that exists exists necessarily. They should separate the model-theoretic claim from its metaphysical interpretation, say precisely which of the two formulas does which work in sustaining or undermining that slogan, and describe at least one way of resisting the conclusion while retaining classical quantifiers.

The model answer should be precise about the two countermodels and the two domain conditions, and should not blur the point that the formulas themselves are object-language schemata whose consequences arise only through the chosen semantics. The marking scheme should allocate the 25 marks across the specific moves an A answer must make—the two countermodels with the responsible clauses; the inclusion conditions; the constant-domain argument; the model-theoretic versus metaphysical distinction; the assignment of work to BF and CBF; and the discussion of resistance within classical quantification. For each marking point, include a one-line note on the typical error candidates make there, such as reversing the two inclusion conditions, conflating BF with CBF, or treating the formulas as if they literally asserted necessary existence independently of the interpretation of the quantifier domain.
