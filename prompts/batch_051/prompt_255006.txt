In recent reading on the Anthropocene I keep meeting the same argument: since geological time units are standardly defined by golden spikes — ratified markers at single reference sections, such as the first appearance of a fossil or a geochemical excursion — the question of when the Anthropocene begins should be settled by applying that same method; the Anthropocene Working Group has even proposed a candidate spike at Crawford Lake, with mid-twentieth-century plutonium fallout as the primary marker. As a retired natural scientist now working through the philosophical side of this debate, I want an audit of the analogy rather than a summary of the ratification dispute. Lay out the correspondence table: what in the Anthropocene case is supposed to play the role of the boundary section, the primary marker, the auxiliary markers, and the requirement of global synchroneity. Then show where the mapping breaks, distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the argument from the ones that are merely incidental, and say what a better analogy would be, if there is one. Finally, judge whether the conclusion the analogy is being used to carry survives: that standard stratigraphic practice is the right procedure for deciding when the Anthropocene begins, and even whether it is a genuine epoch. I am particularly interested in whether a boundary fixed from inside an ongoing interval can be the same kind of thing as a boundary fixed retrospectively for a completed interval.
