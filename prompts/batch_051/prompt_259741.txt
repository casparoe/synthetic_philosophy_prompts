I run a little discussion group that's mostly engineers and science people plus a couple of us who just like arguing about free will, and we keep going in circles about determinism, chaos, and whether the future is predictable even in principle. I'd like a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz for the group — something people can take at the start of a meeting so we know what we're actually disagreeing about. Eight to ten questions, each a concrete case or scenario with two or three plain-language answer options. No questions that just say "do you believe in determinism?" or "are you a compatibilist?" because half the group doesn't know the labels; sort people by what they say about cases, not by what they call themselves.

Cases I want covered: a double pendulum started twice from as-close-to-identical conditions as we can manage and then diverging; the weather and the butterfly effect; a pseudorandom number generator; someone with Laplace's-demon-style perfect knowledge of every particle and all the laws; rewinding the universe to the exact same state and rerunning it; and whether a perfect prediction of what you're about to choose would take away the choice. Also work in the idea that even in a deterministic world the only way for a predictor inside that world to know what happens might be to just run the system at the same speed — no shortcut — and ask whether people take that as a real limit on predictability or just a practical human one.

For the scoring key, build it from the answer patterns first and only then attach names to the positions, instead of starting from a list of views and writing a question for each. I want at least these leaves, worded like a normal person would: (a) determinism means the future is fixed and in principle predictable, so all unpredictability is ignorance; (b) the world can be deterministic and still unpredictable in a deep way, because chaos or the no-shortcut thing means even a perfect finite predictor can't beat just waiting and watching; (c) the bottom level is genuinely chancy, so some unpredictability isn't just ignorance; (d) chance is just a useful model and the whole determinism-vs-indeterminism thing doesn't matter; and maybe (e) even perfect prediction wouldn't undermine choice no matter what the physics says. If two leaves can't be seperated by the questions — like (b) and (c) when both just cash out as "the future can't be predicted" — say that outright and don't pretend the quiz can tell them apart. Also flag any answer combination that's logically possible but that no one would actually hold, and say briefly why that corner stays empty. Keep it readable for smart people who haven't taken a class on this.
