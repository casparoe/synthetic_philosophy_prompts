Consider two ways of winning a desirable position. A candidate who succeeds because of an inherent quality that the position rewards—an exceptional memory, a preternaturally steady temperament, a physique made for the sport—is usually said to have earned the place, or at least not to have gotten it unfairly. A candidate who succeeds because a relative within the institution arranges the appointment is usually said to have been given an unearned advantage: nepotism, a rigged contest. The two advantages differ in how they feel and in whom we can blame, but they are alike in the respects that many theories of fairness take to matter: neither was chosen, neither was earned, both came from the candidate's family, and both were decisive. Is there a principle that justifies condemning the one while accepting the other? The apparent candidates—reward only what is within a person's control; select on qualifications relevant to the position; exclude advantages that arise from others' favor rather than from facts about the person—each seems either to collapse the distinction or to redraw it somewhere equally contentious. Give the strongest principle you can construct that preserves the ordinary asymmetry, test it against the intermediate case of a parent who deliberately cultivates in a child exactly the qualities the position rewards (the tennis parent, the academic family preparing a child for the academy), and then say honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or abandoned.
