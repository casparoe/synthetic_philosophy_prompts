I am a novelist building the backstory of a fictional world, and I need a designed case to think with. My trouble is that the word "collapse" keeps doing too much work when I discuss my drafts, and I want a scenario that forces two definitions of the word apart.

Here are the two positions I want the case to separate. Call them the institutional-bureaucratic account and the everyday-repertoire account.

According to the institutional-bureaucratic account, a society has collapsed when its central political authority, literate administration, monumental building tradition, and ranked settlement system disappear without being replaced. What matters on this account is the integrative top layer.

According to the everyday-repertoire account, a society has failed only when its people's ordinary way of life is abandoned: their language, subsistence practices, kinship structure, local crafts, and burial customs. Losing a court, a script, or a tax-collecting system is, on this account, a change of regime rather than the death of a society.

Please construct a one-paragraph scenario, realistic and set on the same land, with no total depopulation and no conquest by outsiders, in which the institutional-bureaucratic account yields "collapse" while the everyday-repertoire account yields "changed but not failed." Do not give me the easy version in which a palace vanishes while contented farmers carry on untouched: the population must fall by at least a quarter, the old capital must be abandoned, and writing must be lost, yet the everyday-repertoire account must still have a credible case that the society changed rather than failed.

Then walk me through what each account says about the case and why, tell me which feature of the scenario is doing the work and what would happen if I removed it, and tell me how a defender of the account that comes out badly should resist the verdict.

Please respond in American English as an exactly five-item numbered list with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the scenario, kept to a single paragraph; (2) the institutional-bureaucratic verdict and reasoning; (3) the everyday-repertoire verdict and reasoning; (4) the feature doing the work and the effect of removing it; (5) the losing account's best rebuttal. Items 2 through 5 should each be a sentence or two.
