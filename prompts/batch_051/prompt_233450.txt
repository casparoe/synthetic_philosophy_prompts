I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I keep stalling on a book about a forty-year marriage. The question underneath it all is whether a finite mind can actually exhaust its experience — use up a person, a place, a piece of music — or whether attention and repetition keep generating new experience out of the same old materials. Before I lean on any view of this, I want the spec. Write me the desiderata a theory of the exhaustibility of experience has to meet. It has to handle the obvious cases (boredom, the song you play to death, the addict chasing the first high, the child's endless fascination with a stone), and it has to respect distinctions I suspect are getting collapsed: novelty in the object vs. novelty in the experience of it, satiation vs. exhaustion, a finite set of possible contents vs. a finite capacity to care about them. Give the case or consideration behind each requirement, mark which are non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of one camp, and flag any pair that can't both be satisfied. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work in the fight between "a marriage is genuinely infinite from the inside" and "a finite nervous system just runs down combinatorially." Along the way, seperate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't make one contested camp sound like the consensus.
