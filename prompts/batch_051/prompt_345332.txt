Question (10 pts): A handout defines one micromort as a one-in-a-million chance of death, and gives these figures: a typical 20-mile round-trip car commute carries 0.08 micromorts; a single skydive carries 8 micromorts; and the annual background risk of just being alive for a healthy 40-year-old is roughly a thousand micromorts. Using these numbers, evaluate the claim that a commuter who drives to work every day but refuses to skydive "because life is too precious" is being inconsistent, and then say whether routine commuting is worth its mortality risk.

Student answer: "A micromort is a unit for comparing small death risks, equal to one chance in a million. The commuter faces 0.08 micromorts per trip, which over 250 workdays comes to 20 micromorts a year, or a 0.002% chance of dying that year from the commute. That's negligible compared with the roughly thousand micromorts of background risk we all run just by existing. The skydive is 8 micromorts per jump, so a year of commuting equals two and a half jumps. Someone who accepts 20 micromorts a year to get to work but refuses 8 micromorts of skydiving as too dangerous is therefore judging the risks inconsistently; the jump is actually below the annual risk they already tolerate. Obviously consistency also depends on benefits, and the lowest-micromort option need not be best. But there's a deeper point: a risk that's negligible for one person stops being negligible when spread over many. If 250,000 people each make that commute, the expected number of deaths is five per year (250,000 times 20 divided by 1,000,000). A death is a death whether it's statistical or named, and we'd never accept a transport policy that deliberately picks five drivers to die. Since most commutes could be avoided with remote work or fewer trips, the framework ultimately shows that routine commuting isn't worth its mortality risk."
