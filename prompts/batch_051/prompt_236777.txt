Since retiring I have begun reading seriously in the philosophy of physics, and I have found myself relying on a distinction I would like checked before I use it any further. I call it numerical adequacy versus temporal adequacy. An interpretation of quantum mechanics is numerically adequate if it recovers the observed statistics of measurement outcomes. It is temporally adequate if, in addition, it gives an uninterrupted account of what the system is doing between preparation and registration — what properties it has, or what trajectory it follows, during intervals when no measurement occurs. On my present understanding, Copenhagen-style interpretations are numerically adequate but decline temporal adequacy, whereas Bohmian and Everettian interpretations are, in their different ways, attempts to supply it. I am tempted to conclude that temporal adequacy is a scientific virtue rather than a metaphysical indulgence, and that the refusal to say what happens between measurements is an admission that a formulation is incomplete. I would like to know whether this distinction is doing real work, whether it already exists under a better name, and where it most clearly breaks down.
