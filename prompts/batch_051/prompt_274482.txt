Over the past year I have been moving between two bodies of thought — programming language theory and recent debates about which computations matter morally — and it seems to me that a very specific structure shows up in both. I would like an assessment of whether the parallel is real or merely an artifact of shared vocabulary such as 'semantics' and 'behavior.'

In programming language theory there is a standard distinction between two ways of giving a program meaning. A denotational semantics assigns the program a mathematical object, typically the input-output function it computes. An operational semantics describes how the program computes it — the step-by-step state transitions of an abstract machine. Two programs can share a denotation while differing operationally: merge sort and bubble sort denote the same sorting function but proceed through different internal steps. Against that, there is a third notion: observational, or contextual, equivalence. Two program fragments are observationally equivalent when no surrounding program context into which they can be plugged makes any difference to observable output. A denotational semantics whose equalities exactly mirror observational equivalence is called fully abstract. In pure functional languages one can often achieve this; in richer languages full abstraction can fail, and the standard response is to enrich the observations — timing, allocation, nondeterminism — until the semantics tracks the distinctions we actually care about.

Now consider the moral status of computational systems — organoids, large AI systems, insects, or arbitrary simulations. The debate very often takes this form: two systems are behaviorally indistinguishable, yet one is said to be sentient and the other not, because only one runs the 'right kind' of internal computation. Behavior looks like denotation; internal computational structure looks like operational semantics. The question 'which computations matter morally?' then looks like this: which intensional properties of a computation may the moral semantics distinguish, even when the systems are observationally equivalent in every external respect? Integrated information theory is a useful limiting case, since it assigns different degrees of consciousness to systems that pass the same behavioral tests — in effect, a semantics that deliberately refines behavioral equivalence, taking internal causal differences to matter even when behavior does not distinguish them.

The parallel I want checked is the following mapping:

- program fragment ↔ system or subsystem;
- denotation ↔ behavioral disposition;
- operational semantics ↔ internal computational structure;
- surrounding program context ↔ embedding environment, such as a neural subsystem inside a larger organism or a simulation inside a larger system;
- full abstraction ↔ the claim that what matters morally supervenes on observational or behavioral equivalence;
- enriching the observations ↔ the hunt for new empirical tests of sentience.

If the mapping is sound, something should transfer: the history of full abstraction says that choosing what counts as an observation is itself a modeling decision, and that an equivalence class can be split either by enriching the observations or by declaring finer internal distinctions semantically relevant. That seems to fit the sentience debate well — the live dispute is whether some fact of the matter forces the finer semantics, or whether the field is, in effect, choosing what to care about. But I can also see where the analogy might break. In programming languages, observations are made by an external context, and there is no hidden fact about what a program 'really means' apart from the semantics we assign. In the moral case, the whole point of caring about internal computation is that there is allegedly a hidden fact — the system's own experience — and it is first-personal rather than publicly observable. So the analogy seems to predict a kind of 'moral full abstraction theorem,' yet such a theorem may be unstatable because there is no independent notion of moral observational equivalence against which a semantics could be judged correct.

I would like to know whether this is a genuine structural parallel, a loose analogy, or a confusion. In particular: where does the mapping fail, if it does; what exactly would have to be true on both sides for results about full abstraction to transfer to the moral case; and what is one concrete prediction the parallel makes that turns out false?
