I've been poking at the wild animal suffering stuff for years — mostly from old forum debates, not the academic side — and I've never actually sat down and checked whether my verdicts on concrete interventions add up to a principle. So here's my gut, not my official view. Tell me what principle, if any, gets all or most of these, what it commits me to in a case I haven't listed, and which verdicts I'd have to overturn to keep the principle clean.

1. A viral disease (myxomatosis, say) is working through a rabbit population and killing a lot of them slowly. I'd donate to a vaccination program. Fine.

2. I'm in a game reserve and see a lion start to chase down a lame wildebeest calf. I have a cheap way to scare the lion off. I'd do it. Fine.

3. Same situation but as park policy: rangers should systematically intervene to protect prey from predators, relocating lions if necessary. Not fine. That stops being a wild place and I hate it, but I can't cash out the difference.

4. A deer population on an island is about to crash over a hard winter. I'd approve a humane cull in the autumn. Fine-ish — grim, but fine.

5. Same island, but instead a long-term contraceptive program that gradually shrinks the population, so fewer deer are ever born into starvation and predation. My gut says no, even though I can't say why its different from the cull. Not fine.

6. A gene drive that eliminates a nematode whose larvae kill wild ungulates from the inside. Fewer slow deaths, obviously good in expectation, but the gene-drive part makes me want to wait. Not fine for now, though I could see updating.

7. A conservation group is deciding whether to restore a drained swamp. Restoration would create a huge amount of invertebrate life, most of which will die as juveniles; if their lives are net negative, restoring it is net bad. I'd oppose the restoration on that basis alone? No. I get off the bus. It feels monstrous and I don't trust the calculation, but I couldn't say exactly where the calculation fails.

My tentative principle was something like: it's fine to avert a particular bad event you're right their for, especially one we caused, but not to manage populations or systems to drive down total expected suffering. But (4) already breaks that, and (6) pressures it too. Maybe the real principle tracks something like ecosystem autonomy or wildness — but then (2) should've been wrong and it isn't.

The case I keep going back and forth on: a team distributes a painkiller in bait for wild deer with a painful chronic hoof condition, treating pain without changing population size. Is that like the vaccination in (1), or is it the thing I can't settle between (2) and (5)? I genuinely have no stable verdict.

Please give me the principle precisely enough that I could run a new case through it. And separate what's settled here from what's contested: I know the claim that wild animals' lives on average are net negative is not agreed upon by everyone who works on this, and my verdict in (7) in particular rests on an empirical guess. Don't write as if that's the consensus; flag where my cases load the dice that way.
