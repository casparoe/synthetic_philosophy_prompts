I spent most of my working life as a research scientist — running a laboratory, supervising trainees, and later sitting on institutional review boards — and I have only recently begun to read philosophy seriously. The distinction between manipulation and legitimate persuasion now seems to me to be one of the most important ideas I never had a name for while I was practicing. I look back on laboratory life and see influence everywhere: a principal investigator pressing a nervous graduate student to continue with a risky project, a senior collaborator framing a dataset so that a junior author will describe the results the way the senior collaborator wants them described, a funding body's reporting template quietly steering research toward certain questions, a mentor telling a postdoc that a line of work would be career suicide. I could feel that some of these were acceptable and others were not, but I could not have said what the difference consisted in.

I would like you to write a decision-tree or sorting instrument for a workshop I am helping to run for early-career researchers in the sciences. The instrument should classify a concrete influence attempt as legitimate persuasion, manipulation, or genuinely ambiguous. Please build the tree outward from the logical space rather than downward from a list of labels. Each branch node should be a diagnostic question about the situation — for instance about the means of influence, the target's capacity to deliberate, the transparency of the attempt, the role relationship between the parties, the target's interests, or anything else you think does the real sorting work. Each branch question should be answerable by a first-year doctoral student from the facts of the case, without already knowing any philosophical labels. Aim for something a participant could work through in twenty minutes, so roughly six to nine branch questions.

Each leaf should carry the name of a position in the philosophical literature if one exists, and a single sentence stating that position's most awkward commitment. The leaves should include at least: the view that manipulation consists in bypassing or subverting the target's rational deliberation; the view that influence is illegitimate when it guides the target in a way the target could not reflectively endorse; a relational or role-based view on which the legitimacy of influence depends on the norms of the governing relationship; and a vulnerability- or exploitation-based view on which manipulation works by preying on a weakness or dependency. Where your branch questions generate combinations of answers that no one occupies, leave those as empty branches and say briefly whether each is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or probably underexplored.

Then apply the instrument to the three cases below, and for each case say which question does the most classificatory work, and whether any two leaves in your tree would deliver the same verdict for different reasons:

1. A principal investigator gives a postdoc a flattering but highly selective account of the PI's own past successes in order to persuade the postdoc to take over a struggling project.
2. A public-health campaign uses loss-framed messaging about the risks of remaining unvaccinated to increase uptake.
3. A supervisor tells a trainee that publishing in one particular journal would be a poor career move, without disclosing that the supervisor serves on the editorial board of a competing journal.

Finally, tell me where the instrument breaks down: which cases or distinctions the tree cannot separate, and what those limits suggest about whether the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion tracks a single underlying distinction or only a loose family of related judgments.
