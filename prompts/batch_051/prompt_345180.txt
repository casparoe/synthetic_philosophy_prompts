The argument over whether philosophical intuitions vary across cultures has been spinning in place for a long time now: the same studies get re-scored, the same methodological objections get exchanged, and the camps seem to grow more entrenched rather than closer. I am looking for a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a fresh survey of findings. As I read the positions, the universalists hold that apparent cultural differences — in Kripke-style cases about whether the name "Gödel" refers to the man named Gödel or to the hidden Schmidt who actually produced the proof, in Gettier-style cases about justified true belief resting on a false intermediate step, and in some moral dilemmas — are mostly artifacts of translation, of vignettes too complex to be comprehended uniformly, or of comparing one group's fast first-pass responses with another's reflective judgments; once those are controlled for, the core intuitions are shared. The variationists reply that the differences reappear even after such corrections, that the participant pools were for decades a narrow and unusual sliver of humanity, and — this is the sharpest move — that the universalists' criteria for which responses count as competent are themselves calibrated to the populations that produced the theories, so the "noise" explanation is not a neutral test of variation but a defense of universality in the costume of one. A third camp concedes that the variation may be real and denies that it debunks philosophical practice: intuitions would then resemble perception — theory-laden and culture-bound, yet still something to calibrate rather than abandon.

My working hypothesis is that the deadlock is not, or is no longer, really an empirical question about how much variation there is. I suspect the word "intuition" is carrying two unresolved disputes at once: one about what should count as the intuition worth explaining — a first-pass, uncorrected verdict or an idealized judgment after reflection — and one about what role intuitions are supposed to play — culture-free evidence, or theory-laden starting points that inquiry may legitimately revise. On that hypothesis, no cross-cultural study, however well designed, would end the argument, since the design of any study would already encode an answer to one of those prior questions. But I am not confident. Diagnose the structure of this disagreement from what the sides actually say: is it a hidden empirical question better methods could settle, a difference about standards of evidence, a verbal dispute about "intuition," a clash of bedrock philosophical commitments, or something not on my list? And given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress — a new kind of study, a distinction, a change of question — or, if you think progress should not be expected, explain why and say what the argument is actually doing for the people in it.
