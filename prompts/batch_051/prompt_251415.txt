I am a science journalist working on a long-form piece about the conceptual foundations of causal inference, and I keep encountering a confident line of thought about Judea Pearl's causal hierarchy — the ladder that runs from association, through intervention, to counterfactual reasoning. The claim is that the top rung of the ladder is presentation rather than substance, because the do-calculus already exhausts what there is to know at the middle rung. I want the reasoning pressure-tested before I either repeat it or debunk it in print. Reconstructed as carefully as I can, it runs like this.

(1) In a structural causal model, an intervention does exactly one thing: it replaces the equation for the intervened-on variable with a constant, while every other equation, and the distribution of the exogenous disturbances, stays as it was. The do-calculus computes the resulting distribution over the rest of the system.

(2) Grant, for the sake of argument, that the do-calculus is complete at this interventional level: given the causal graph, its rules will derive any intervention distribution that is identifiable from observational data, and will correctly report when identification is impossible.

(3) A counterfactual question such as this patient died without the drug; would they have survived had they been given it? asks for the very same thing as (1) — the outcome under the equation replacement — with the sole addition that we condition on what actually happened to that patient.

(4) Conditioning on an observed fact is an ordinary probabilistic operation. Once the intervention distribution is in hand, bringing in the actual outcome should be no different in kind from updating any probability distribution on new evidence.

(5) Therefore every counterfactual query is within reach of the do-calculus: first compute the relevant intervention distribution as in (2), then condition on the actual observations as in (4). The third rung of the hierarchy introduces no inferential problem of its own; it merely repackages the middle rung.

Is this argument valid? If it is not, identify the exact step — or the exact move between steps — where it fails. My suspicion is that the trouble lies around steps (3) and (4), because the do-calculus seems to deliver a population-level answer while the counterfactual question is about one unit, but I cannot yet turn that suspicion into a precise diagnosis. State the correct relationship between an intervention distribution and a counterfactual claim about a particular individual, with the failing step revised accordingly. I need the specific flaw, not a survey of causal inference.
