For a paper in my philosophy of mind seminar on which computations matter morally, I am working through a variation on the China brain thought experiment. The aim is not just to restate the two sides but to trace how the variation changes what each side should say. Please write the result as a formal essay with the following titled sections.

1. The baseline case (≈300 words). Present the original thought experiment as it is usually given: a billion citizens are linked by two-way radios, each one implementing the input–output rule of a single neuron, so that the whole network is functionally equivalent to a human brain for an hour. Then set out the two positions the seminar has been testing. The functionalist holds that the network literally has the mental states specified by its neural organization — including pain — and therefore is a locus of moral concern. The anti-functionalist holds that none of this adds up to anyone being in pain: the citizens feel nothing, and the network has no point of view, so it has no moral status in virtue of pain.

2. The variation (≈350 words). Change the implementation in one precise way. Remove the radios. The simulation now runs in discrete rounds. In each round, every citizen writes the current state of their assigned neuron on a card; the cards are collected, sorted, and copied into the input-slots of the citizens assigned to downstream neurons. One round takes one week and represents 100 milliseconds of brain time. The network is configured so that, according to its original functional description, it is in one uninterrupted episode of severe pain for a single simulated minute — stretched across 600 rounds and roughly eleven and a half years of wall-clock time. No citizen knows the larger pattern, no citizen is in pain, and between rounds the only physical trace of the system is a warehouse of filed cards. State clearly what this variation preserves and what it does not.

3. The functionalist analysis (≈450 words). Work out what the functionalist is committed to saying about this version: whether the card-driven network is literally in severe pain, whether running it is morally serious, and whether the functionalist can add a real-time, continuous-causal, or simultaneity requirement without abandoning the view. Discuss whether time itself is part of the functional role, or whether a mental state can be stretched across months without becoming a different state.

4. The anti-functionalist analysis (≈350 words). Explain what the anti-functionalist should now say, and whether the slowdown and discretization give that view a new argument it did not have against the original China brain.

5. The temporal-structure problem (≈400 words). Consider an intermediate position that accepted the original China brain as conscious but wants to deny consciousness and moral status to the stretched card-driven version. What feature could justify the difference — lack of simultaneity, lack of continuous integration, the long gaps, the serial card system, or the absence of any unified point of view at a time? Assess whether any of these is principled or merely ad hoc.

6. My verdict (≈300 words). Defend a conclusion about whether the stretched card-driven network has moral status and whether running it would be wrong. Take a definite side; you may distinguish the original and the variation, but do not end in suspension of judgment.
