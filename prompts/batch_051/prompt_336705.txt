A friend and I keep rehashing whether you can actually read off what a system is "really" optimizing from what it does, after a seminar on inverse reinforcement learning where half the room treated revealed preference as settled and the other half treated it as a joke. I want the full spec before I read any more. The version I care about is practical: you have an agent—a trained policy, a firm, a person, a foraging animal, whatever—and you can watch alot of its choices, including mistakes, but you can't open it up. What would a theory of objective-ascription have to get right to count as adequate? I want the requirements list: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse (objective vs. constraint vs. search procedure vs. the set of options it even considers), the facts from decision theory or optimization it can't contradict (utility equivalence classes, Goodhart-style stuff, satisficers and optimizers leaving the same behavioral trace?), and the consideration behind each. For each one, say whether it's non-negotiable or just a camp preference. Then the part I most want: wich single requirement knocks out the most naive views, and are there any pairs of desiderata that can't be jointly satisfied? Keep the scorecard of existing theories light—I want the criteria, not the book report.

I also suspect part of the problem is that people use 'behavior reveals the objective' as a convention rather than a discovery, and a good list would say exactly where that line sits.
