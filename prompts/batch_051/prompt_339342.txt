So this has been bugging me since I watched my city council handle a vandalized monument. They approved the restoration money in about ten minutes, even though a few weeks earlier they'd shelved a request to fix a damaged bus shelter that would have cost about the same. A council member said the monument isn't just public property, it's sacred, and defacing it is a different kind of wrong — not a bigger harm but a whole different type. When someone pointed out that the memorial is for a fairly obscure regiment and no veterans were around to be upset, the same member scrambled and said it would still offend the dead, which felt like a justification reached for after the fact. I'm not asking whether the council was right or wrong; I'm asking what that move commits you to.

I keep seeing the same structure elsewhere: desecrating a corpse or a grave is treated as a crime even when no living person is harmed and no family or mourner ever finds out; burning a flag is punished more harshly than burning the same cloth; some foods are banned because they're disgusting even when nobody is hurt and the animal is already dead; and consenting adults aren't allowed to do certain things with human remains no matter how willing everyone involved is. I've heard people wave this away as just the yuck factor, not real ethics, but that's exactly what I want to understand. I dont want to argue about whether these are good policies. I want to know what picture of ethics they presuppose. Do they presuppose that disgust, purity, or sacredness is a basic moral value on its own, alongside harm, fairness, and consent? Or could someone who thinks wrongness is entirely about harm and rights still accept all of them? Are the practices even consistent with each other, or are they a pile of gut reactions that the law happened to enforce? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit with, and say what would have to change if you tried to run the same rules on harm and consent alone.
