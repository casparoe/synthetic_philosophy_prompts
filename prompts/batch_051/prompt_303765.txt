One practice I keep returning to as a journalist covering science and ideas is the monitoring of chain-of-thought output in modern AI systems. Developers train systems to produce running natural-language commentary while solving difficult tasks, and safety teams may inspect that commentary for signs of deception, disallowed strategies, or goals the system is trying to hide. The underlying assumption appears to be that the commentary is evidence about the system's actual reasoning, not merely another piece of behaviour. I would like to know what philosophical commitments that practice carries. It seems to presuppose, first, that the system has a determinate reasoning process distinct from the words that report it; second, that natural language can track that process faithfully enough that the commentary is not just a post hoc rationalisation; and third, that optimising a system to produce a plausible running commentary does not corrupt the very thing the commentary is supposed to reveal. Which of these commitments are genuinely required by the practice, and which could be dropped without changing its results? Could monitoring be justified on a deliberately thin view, on which the chain of thought is just another output whose predictive usefulness is tested behaviourally? Or does the practice become incoherent if the commentary is best understood as a performance constructed for the benefit of the reader? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the relation between reasoning and language that chain-of-thought monitoring appears to require, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.
