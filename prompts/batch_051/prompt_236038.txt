In my philosophy of economics course we have been working through revealed preference theory, and I keep noticing a parallel with evolutionary biology that I cannot decide is deep or merely seductive. The way the course presents it, utility is not an inner magnitude that causes choice; it is a numerical representation of a pattern of choices satisfying certain consistency axioms such as the weak axiom of revealed preference, and the empirical content of the theory lives in those axioms rather than in any claim about felt pleasure or desire. In evolutionary theory, the concept of fitness is often defended against the old tautology objection in a structurally similar way: fitness is only defined relative to a specified environment and a specified set of fitness components, and the substantive content comes from constraints on how those components combine, how they can be measured independently, and what they predict about changes in gene frequencies. Both cases seem to follow the same retreat — an intuitive explanatory notion is stripped down to a summary of the very outcomes it was meant to explain, and the theorist replies that the real content sits elsewhere, in the consistency or structural constraints.

Is this parallel deep or superficial? I would like an answer that states the mapping precisely, says what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer from one field to the other, and names something the analogy appears to predict about one field that turns out false about the other. In particular, I want to know whether the tautology objection fails for the same reason in both cases, and whether treating utility as a mere representation of choice would, if transplanted into population genetics, leave fitness with the right kind of empirical content.
