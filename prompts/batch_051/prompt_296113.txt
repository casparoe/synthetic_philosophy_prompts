I'm a retired physicist who fell into philosophy a few years ago, and I keep coming back to the fine-tuning argument. Let me state the position I've more or less adopted: the apparent fine-tuning of the physical constants and the initial conditions of the universe is strong evidence for *some* kind of designer or teleology behind the whole setup — not necessarily the full God of theism, just an intelligent or purposive cause. I've heard the standard replies, and the two big ones — the anthropic line ("we could only ever observe a universe that supports observers") and the multiverse line ("enough universes and one like ours becomes likely") — both feel to me like reframings rather than refutations.

What I want from you is an honest map of what would count against this position. Not the usual objections recited at me, but the actual exposure: what would we have to discover, calculate, or think through for the view to be seriously weakened or just false? For instance — if a future theory showed the constants couldn't of been otherwise, or that the life-permitting range is far wider than the argument assumes, or that a multiverse model actually makes our kind of universe *generic* instead of rare, or that the whole probability distribution over parameter space is undefined (the measure problem). Sort these into fatal, damaging-but-survivable, and merely awkward, and say which ones a committed defender of the fine-tuning argument would have to concede are genuine defeaters rather than things we get to waive off. I'm especially curious about the measure worry: is it an internal defect in the argument, or just an epistemic limitation we have to live with? And finish by saying straight whether the position is practically insulated, and if so whether that's a bug in the argument or just how it is in this corner of philosophy.
