I teach an upper-level philosophy of religion seminar and I'm setting the final essay. I'm sick of grading answers that just recite the free will defence: "God couldn't make free creatures who always choose right, therefore evil." Fine, but that's the general theodicy problem. What I actually want to test is the logic of an angelic fall specifically. If angels are created good, with free will, in a wholly good creation, how does the first evil choice even get off the ground? It seems like to choose evil you'd need some prior evil inclination or motive, but ex hypothesi there's none — so the first sin looks either unmotivated or circular. My students and I have been calling this the "bootstrap problem" for a first sin. I also want them to see that the "Satan chose a lesser apparent good" move may not explain anything; it might just relabel the gap, or it might be the whole story. And I want them to separate freedom-as-explaining-possibility from freedom-as-explaining-actuality.

Could you draft: (1) the exam question itself — upper-level undergrads, 90 minutes, 20 marks total, and it should be answerable without citing particular historical figures or texts (no bibliographies, no names needed); (2) a model answer of about 600 words (hard cap 650); and (3) a marking scheme built around five specific moves a top answer must make, 4 marks each, plus a one- or two-sentence note on what separates an A answer from a B answer. The whole deliverable should be under 1,400 words. Please make the crux be the bootstrap/first-sin problem — how a wholly good creature can actualize a first evil act — not a generic theodicy question. And please don't end the model answer by just declaring it a mystery; if it goes there, it should say what work the mystery move is doing.
