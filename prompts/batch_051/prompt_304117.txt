I have a conversational policy and I want to know whether it's defensible. When someone says something that only makes sense if some background claim is true, and I actually think that background claim is false or at least doubtful, I ususally just go along with it. So if a colleague says 'I've stopped worrying that my paper is late' and I don't think it's late, or says 'the best students in my class are the ones who...' and I don't think there's any fact about who's best, I register the presupposed stuff, update my picture of what's been added to the conversational scoreboard, and answer as if I'd accepted it. I only object later if the presupposition turns out to be doing real work in the conclusion. My rationale is that objecting to every dubious presupposition would make conversation impossible; language has to move, and the scoreboard adjusts rather than getting stuck. But I worry I'm basically granting the most important stuff silently, and that people take my silence as agreement with the presupposition itself. Is this a reasonable policy, or a way of letting bad assumptions in for free? When should accommodation be the default, and when should I stop and object? Is there a version of the rule that keeps conversations moving without me becoming a pushover?
