I've been reporting on the longtermism fight and keep hitting the same wall. One side says caring about the far future is just applied moral arithmetic: if there could be trillions of future people, a modest reduction in existential risk can outweigh an awful lot of present suffering, so we should treat x-risk like an overriding priority. The other side says that's exactly the move that makes authoritarian or totalitarian control look licit — maybe even mandatory — because once the stakes are framed that way, present rights, democratic constraints, and ordinary wellbeing start to look like rounding errors. The odd thing is that the two sides often share the same numbers and the same stated concern for future people, and still nobody moves. My working hypothesis is that this isn't really a disagreement about how risky things are, but about what a moral theory is supposed to do: whether aggregating across enormous futures can override the claims of people alive now, or whether that very kind of aggregation is what's in question. I'd like a diagnosis rather than a survey. What kind of disagreement is this actually — a hidden factual question, a verbal confusion, rival starting intuitions, an ill-posed question? And given the diagnosis, is there anything that could realistically move it, or should I expect the standoff to be permanant?
