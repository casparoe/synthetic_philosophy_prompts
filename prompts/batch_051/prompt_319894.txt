I am a final-year undergraduate reading philosophy, currently taking a research seminar in formal epistemology. In a fortnight I present my seminar paper, and I want a Q&A preparation sheet rather than merely hoping the discussion is gentle.

The position I shall defend is this: Van Fraassen's Reflection principle, read as an unconditional synchronic requirement to defer to one's future self, is false; the familiar counterexamples involving foreseeable memory loss, credence-altering drugs, and known future irrationality are decisive against it. But the principle has a defensible core, which I call plan-grounded Reflection. It says: to the extent that an agent currently endorses a plan for revising her credences in response to the evidence she now regards as possible, her present credence in any proposition A, conditional on that plan's leading her future self to credence r in A, must itself be r. Violations of the original principle are rationally permissible only where the future credence would not be produced by a plan the agent now endorses.

My paper argues three connected things. First, the diachronic Dutch-book argument is best read as a constraint on revisory plans: the bookie's guaranteed profit depends on the agent currently assigning conditional credences to how her future self will respond, and the bet is enforceable only against an agent who is committed to a sufficiently determinate policy of revision. Second, once Reflection is restricted in this way, the standard counterexamples no longer refute it, because in each of them the future credence is not the output of an endorsed plan; the principle simply does not speak to those cases. Third, the unrestricted principle is the product of a mistaken idealisation: it treats one's future self as an expert to be deferred to across all possible futures, whereas deference is warranted only when the future self is following the evidence-driven policy one now approves. I acknowledge that this third move is the least worked-out part of the paper.

The audience is the seminar itself: roughly a dozen postgraduate and advanced undergraduate students, the convenor, and two other faculty members. The convenor works on accuracy-first epistemology and has defended a global, scoring-rule-based argument for conditionalisation; she will want the plan-relative norm shown to follow from, or at least not conflict with, considerations of expected accuracy rather than Dutch-book considerations alone. One of the other faculty members works on imprecise credences and will press me on whether anything analogous holds for sets of probability functions. The other is a decision theorist who regards diachronic coherence arguments as proving less than is commonly claimed and will probe where the money is actually lost. The postgraduate students know the technical literature well, including the limitations of the original Dutch-book proof, and the undergraduates are broadly sympathetic to Bayesian updating but unconvinced that Reflection, as opposed to mere conditionalisation, deserves a special constraint on future belief.

I already command the standard moves in the published literature on Reflection; what I need is a map of how this particular room will push back, including the objections that are unlikely to appear in print. Please produce a preparation sheet containing: the eight or so objections I am most likely to receive from this audience, ordered by expected severity, where severity combines likelihood of being raised with the damage each can do; each objection phrased as the actual questioner would put it, with an indication of which member of the audience it would most likely come from; a reply of roughly two minutes for each; for each reply, an honest note on where it is soft and what I should concede rather than defend; and, finally, the question I most hope nobody asks, with a note on why it would be damaging and how I might at most limit the harm.
