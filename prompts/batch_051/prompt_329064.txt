I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so excuse the messy phrasing. In my current book there's a character — a lapsed seminarian whose been running a seaside B&B since losing his faith — and after a funeral he corners a young couple and lays out the following argument, which I've been trying to stress-test:

(1) A punishment is just only if it is proportionate to the offence.
(2) Every sin a human commits is finite in gravity: we act with finite knowledge, finite powers and finite time, and we cause at most finite harm.
(3) The fact that the sin is against an infinite God doesn't change this. An infinite victim doesn't turn a finite act infinite — slapping a king is worse than slapping a stranger, but it still isn't regicide.
(4) Eternal torment is an infinite punishment. It never ends, so the suffering it inflicts has no bound.
(5) A finite offence can never be proportionate to an infinite punishment.
(6) Therefore eternal conscious torment is unjust, and the only defensible positions are annihilation or universal salvation.

I want a second character to put their finger on the weak spot, but I can't decide where the objection should land. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what's the strongest version of that step — would it make the argument go through, or make it collapse? If the argument does go through, have I smuggled in a premise a believer could reject without absurdity?
