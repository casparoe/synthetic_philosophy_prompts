im in a Chinese philosophy class right now and we just did the Mencius–Xunzi dispute about human nature. I wanted to put Xunzi's core argument into numbered steps so I could see exactly where Mencius would have to push back, and I ended up making the argument look tighter than the standard reading, so I want a sanity check.

Here is the reconstruction of Xunzi I came up with:

(1) A thing's nature (xing) is whatever it has and does without learning, training, or deliberate effort — the raw, inborn equipment.

(2) Human beings, left without teachers, models, or ritual, act from the desires they are born with: wanting food, warmth, rest, sex, and avoiding harm.

(3) If people act from only these inborn desires, each person will pursue his or her own satisfaction, goods will run short relative to everyone's wants, and the result will be contention, violence, and disorder.

(4) Whatever someone is like by nature, when acting on it predictably leads to contention and disorder, that nature is bad.

(5) Therefore, human nature is bad.

(6) But the same inborn material can be reshaped by ritual and standards of rightness, which the sages invented; this reshaping is deliberate effort (wei), and it is what makes individual people good.

(7) So goodness is not present in nature from the start; it is an artificial product imposed on a nature that would otherwise run to disorder.

I keep thinking the argument goes all the way through — the only reason people in section hated it is that they don't like the conclusion. The objection I hear most is to (4): leading to disorder doesn't automatically make a nature bad, any more than a hurricane is evil because it destroys a town. My TA says the fishy step is (2), cause it treats our inborn nature as nothing but the appetitive desires and just leaves out whatever else might be inborn, like Mencius's sprout of compassion, which is equally unlearned. But that sounds like a disagreement with Xunzi's picture of human nature, not a logical flaw in the inference.

So: is the argument valid, and if not, which step exactly fails and under which reading of the terms? If it is valid given Xunzi's premises, is Mencius's only real move to deny a premise (probably (2)) rather than attack the logic? And is there an equivocation somewhere between xing as the bundle of what is inborn and xing as what someone would do if left alone, or does the argument not actually slide between those?
