I'm on the credit committee of a development finance fund that makes small agricultural loans across four countries, to cooperatives and first-time borrowers. Every application arrives with a risk model's default probability, printed to one decimal place, but our field officers keep saying the number is overconfident when there isn't much local history behind it. I've been reading Keynes on probability, and the bit that's stuck with me is his distinction between the probability of a proposition and the weight of the evidence behind it: a 12% default probability built on thirty years of repayment records from the same region isn't the same 12% as one built on a model fitted to data from another country's land-tenure system. And his point that some probabilities aren't really numerical at all — you can know a loan is riskier than average without being entitled to say it's 14%.

I want that turned into a reusable checklist our committee can actually run, not an essay about epistemology. We make maybe forty of these decisions a season, each loan is small but a bad season can sink the fund, and we get about twenty minutes of discussion per application. The committee is a former agronomist, an accountant, and one person who actually read Keynes, so the checklist has to work without the jargon. The fund director signs off on every decline and the board is always pushing us to deploy the pipeline, so the checklist has to be honest about when we're just leaning on convention or confidence rather than evidence.

Set it up as a sequence of steps we walk through in a meeting. For each step, say what we need to have in hand to pass it, which steps should end in a decline, and which steps mean the right move is to stop treating the number as a number and hand the call to a person. Then walk it through three applications we're currently arguing about:

(a) a cooperative with a twelve-year repayment history in a crop we know well — model says 8%;
(b) a first-time borrower in a new high-value crop, no comparable borrowers anywhere in our portfolio — model says 14% by proxying from a different sector, and our field officer has known the family for years and vouches for them;
(c) a mid-size farm where the model says 22%, but the number is built almost entirely on data from a neighboring country where land tenure, irrigation, and bankruptcy law are all different.

Tell us where the checklist gives answers we wont like, and where it runs out and can't decide.
