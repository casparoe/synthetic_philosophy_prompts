hey — i'm a phd student in economics working on decision theory, and i've been trying to get clear on the ergodicity economics stuff (Ole Peters and collaborators, time averages vs ensemble averages, the St. Petersburg setup). i'm drafting a short essay for a workshop and would really appriciate point-by-point comments on the argument before i send it around. i'm not looking for copy-editing; i want to know where the move is unsound, where it strawmans the other side, and what the strongest objection is that i haven't handled. here's the current draft.

title: growth-rate maximization is a statistic, not a decision theory

The ergodicity economics programme claims that expected-value reasoning goes wrong in non-ergodic settings, and that the right criterion in those settings is to maximize the time-average growth rate rather than the ensemble average. I want to press on a specific ambiguity in that claim.

First, a mathematical fact. For a multiplicative process — say, repeatedly staking some fraction of wealth on a favourable gamble — the time-average growth rate is the geometric mean, and over a long horizon it can lie far below the arithmetic mean. The ensemble average is pulled upward by a few lucky paths, while almost every path realizes something close to the geometric mean. So far I have no quarrel.

But notice that this is a fact about a given stochastic process. A decision problem doesn't hand us a fixed process: the process is determined by the policy. If you change the policy, both the ensemble and the time average change. So the advice to maximize the time-average growth rate has to be read as: among the available policies, choose the one whose associated process has the largest time-average growth rate. That's a well-defined ranking. The question is whether it's a decision theory or just one statistic someone might happen to care about.

This is where I think the single-path-versus-parallel-worlds story does less normative work than its proponents think. Ex ante, before the path is realized, you don't know which path will happen. For a finite horizon the realized time-average growth rate is itself random; in the long-run limit it becomes a deterministic functional of the process, but that just means it's one summary of the distribution rather than another. So the choice isn't between a well-defined quantity that your path gets and a fictional ensemble quantity; it's between different features of one distribution. To rank policies by their time-average growth rate you still have to evaluate the distribution of increments, which is exactly the ensemble-style machinery the rhetoric says we're escaping. The ensemble hasn't been eliminated; it's been moved into the construction of the statistic.

Second, I don't see exactly where the disagreement with expected utility theory is supposed to be. Bernoulli's log-utility solution to the St. Petersburg problem already tells you to maximize expected log wealth, and for multiplicative gambles that yields the same policy as maximizing the geometric mean. If the ergodicity claim is to use log utility because it is the time-average growth rate of wealth dynamics, then the real move is to treat the utility function as derived from the dynamics rather than from taste. That's an interesting move, but it isn't a counterexample to expected utility theory: the theory allows any utility function, and log utility is consistent with it. The substantive disagreement would have to be normative — why must an agent care about the time-average growth rate, especially in large-stakes choices where maximizing it conflicts sharply with expected-value maximization and asks the agent to forego large positive-expectation gambles. And there the ergodic framework by itself seems to assume the thing it needs to establish, namely that long-run wealth preservation is the objective.

A natural fallback is to restrict the claim: the time-average criterion is the right ranking when the question is how to maximize what actually happens to a single agent over time in a stationary, long-run, multiplicative environment. But that's a much narrower claim than expected value is wrong, and it may not bear on the one-shot, non-repeated policy decisions where people sometimes want to apply the framework.

so what i'd love from you: (i) is the ex-ante objection in the fourth paragraph actually an objection, or am i just assuming an ensemble view that the ergodicity people have already answered? (ii) am i being fair to the single-path-versus-ensemble story, or is that a strawman of Peters's position? (iii) does the last paragraph undersell the scope of the framework, or does the framework need even stronger assumptions than i say? and (iv) anywhere else the argument is wrong, overstated, or unclear — i'd rather be told now than at the workshop.
