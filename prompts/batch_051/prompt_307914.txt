In Chapter I of *The Subjection of Women*, John Stuart Mill writes:

> So long as an opinion is strongly rooted in the feelings, it gains rather than loses in stability by having a preponderating weight of argument against it. For if it were accepted as a result of argument, the refutation of the argument might shake the solidity of the conviction; but when it rests solely on feeling, the worse it fares in argumentative contest, the more persuaded its adherents are that their feeling must have some deeper ground, which the arguments do not reach; and while the feeling remains, it is always throwing up fresh intrenchments of argument to repair any breach made in the old.

I noticed a possible parallel between Mill’s explanation here and contemporary work on motivated reasoning or belief perseverance: both seem to say that a direct argumentative loss can make a belief *more* entrenched, because the believer treats the feeling as evidence for some deeper justification not yet articulated. But I cannot tell whether Mill is making an empirical claim about the psychology of belief, a normative claim about burden of proof, or both. Which is it? And is the parallel with motivated reasoning deep enough that results from that literature—for example, about when counterevidence actually backfires—transfer back to Mill’s argument, or is it only a loose analogy? Part of what makes this matter is his later treatment of appeals to “nature” in the same essay: his opponents say that women’s subordination is natural, and Mill wants to explain why this appeal persists even when its supporting arguments fail. Does his critique of appeals to “nature” depend on the psychological mechanism described in this passage, or would his political argument go through even if the psychology were false?
