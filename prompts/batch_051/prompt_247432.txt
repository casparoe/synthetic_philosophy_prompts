I retired a few years ago from a research career in the natural sciences and have lately been reading political philosophy. I find that my convictions about particular cases are much firmer than my grasp of the principle behind them, and I would like you to help me find that principle, or tell me which of my convictions I should revise.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

A researcher who uses a wheelchair after a spinal cord injury needs an adjusted bench, voice-controlled instruments, and a part-time assistant to continue the same experimental work she did before the injury. The institution would spend considerably more on her than on an able-bodied colleague doing the same work, and I think it should pay these costs.

A colleague, by contrast, insists that he can only do his best work if the department buys him a particular imported espresso machine and gives him a quiet office with a view of the hills. Without them he is merely competent; with them he is excellent. He says his case is structurally identical to the first. I do not think equal concern requires the department to buy the espresso machine or the view, even though they would raise his performance.

A postdoc with a chronic pain condition has adapted so completely that she sincerely reports being just as satisfied with her life as anyone else. She has, however, quietly given up fieldwork, conference travel, and evening work; her days are shorter and her options narrower than they were. I think she is still owed something—schedule flexibility, a lighter teaching load, or extra support—despite her satisfaction.

Two incoming graduate students have the same measured talent. One arrives from a well-resourced background and is already fluent in the methods; the other arrives from a background with far fewer opportunities and must spend the first year catching up. I think equal concern requires giving the second student extra tutoring and an extended timeline, rather than treating the two identically from the first day.

A senior scientist has a rare combination of skills and is close to a result of great public importance, but he has developed a condition that is expensive to treat. A junior technician has the same condition and the same prognosis, but her work is competent and easily replaced. My instinct is that the senior's treatment may properly be prioritized, though I am uneasy about this and cannot tell whether it follows from any principle of equality or is an entirely separate consideration.

A colleague is persistently discontented by temperament. He is healthy, secure, and respected, but he reports low satisfaction and argues that equal concern requires spending whatever is needed—therapy, sabbaticals, a different role—until his satisfaction matches the average. I do not think equal concern requires this, but I cannot articulate the difference between his case and the postdoc's.

My working hypothesis is that what we should equalize is not how satisfied people feel, and not how much money or assistance they receive, but the range of genuine options open to them—what they are actually able to do and be. I am not sure, however, that this hypothesis survives the second and fifth cases, and it may mishandle the last.

Please state the principle or small set of principles that best captures these verdicts, precisely enough to be applied. Tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which you would overturn and why. Then apply the principle you settle on to a case I have not listed: a researcher whose productivity is limited not by any medical condition but by the need to care for an aging parent, and who asks for the same accommodations as the first researcher.
