I sit on the grants committee of a small family foundation whose mission is to reduce severe suffering, with explicit priority for the very worst forms of it. The founder's letter of intent directs us to treat the worst forms of suffering as mattering more than all lesser suffering combined. At next week's meeting we must choose between two fully prepared proposals, only one of which can be funded this cycle.

Proposal A funds a rescue and intensive veterinary program for animals seized from severe neglect and abuse. It would lift a relatively small number of animals out of extreme, sustained pain, but it is expensive per animal and some cases will prove intractable.

Proposal B funds low-cost preventive care and parasite control for working animals across several regions. It would reduce chronic but moderate pain and disease for tens of thousands of animals, and on a straightforward cost-effectiveness calculation it prevents far more total suffering per dollar spent.

The empirical estimates are contested at the margins, but both factions in the committee accept their broad direction: A reaches a few cases of extreme suffering, B reaches a great many cases of moderate suffering. The committee is split, and I have been asked to draft the decision memo for circulation before that meeting. Some members read the founder's instruction as committing us to Proposal A, on the grounds that a single case of the worst suffering outweighs any quantity of milder suffering. Others reply that the principle is unworkable and that we should minimize total suffering, with severity weighted heavily but numbers allowed to compensate.

Draft the memo. It should recommend one proposal and state the weighting principle behind the recommendation, with an actionable bottom line the committee can vote on. It should engage seriously with the strongest consideration against the recommendation, and it should address the objection the committee keeps circling: whether an absolute priority for extreme suffering can be stated in a form that actually guides decisions, where the line between extreme and merely severe falls, and whether a priority honored case by case could lead to choices that, in aggregate, betray the mission it was meant to serve.
