At a recent seminar, two colleagues of mine got into an argument about American exceptionalism that I am still trying to sort out.

One argued that the only intellectually respectable version of American exceptionalism is the comparative-historical thesis: compared with the major European powers, the United States never developed a mass socialist or social-democratic party, and the standard explanation traces this to a political culture that was born liberal, with no feudal institutions, no entrenched estate hierarchy, and a broadly Lockean consensus. On that narrow reading, he said, exceptionalism is an empirical claim about a real and lasting difference, not chauvinism.

The other objected that the narrow reading is not nearly as innocent as it looks. First, choosing Europe as the comparison class is itself a loaded choice; the same facts look unexceptional if one compares the United States with Canada, Australia, or other settler societies. Second, what supposedly explains the difference—the absence of feudalism—did not prevent Canada from developing social democracy, so the explanatory story and the outcome do not line up. She concluded that American exceptionalism survives only as an ideology masquerading as a scholarly hypothesis.

The first replied that the comparison-class objection proves too much: all comparative claims must select cases, and saying that the United States looks less exceptional against a different comparison set does not refute the claim; it merely restates that the claim was relativized to a comparison set all along. On the second point, he granted that the Canadian case matters, but said it shows the thesis needs refinement—for example, by bringing in federalism, political institutions, or timing—rather than showing that the underlying contrast is worthless.

The second then said: notice what just happened. The moment the thesis is challenged, you supplement it with a different set of factors. That makes the exceptionalism claim protean: it can always be saved by adding a new variable, and a hypothesis that can absorb every counterexample is no longer an empirical thesis at all.

The first replied that adding variables is how comparative-historical explanation normally works; no one treats American exceptionalism as a one-variable law. The charge of being unfalsifiable, he said, sticks only if the new variables are introduced ad hoc, not if they identify real institutional differences that do explanatory work.

Could you walk through that exchange and say who is right about which specific moves—not simply who comes out ahead overall—and what, if anything, both of them are missing about the difference between a descriptive historical claim, a normative claim, and an ideology?
