I got lunch with a friend who does incident reviews for a living, and we ended up going in circles on a parallel I still can't decide about. On the game-theory side there's the idea people call superrationality: in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma where two players are exact copies of eachother — same code, same priors, same everything, and each knows it — you shouldn't reason that whatever they do I'm better off defecting, because your move and theirs aren't independent; they're two runs of the same decision procedure. The superrational move is to ask what happens if everyone in exactly your situation runs the same reasoning, and then cooperate, since two cooperators beat two defectors.

On the reliability side there's the point about redundant systems: if you install two copies of the same buggy component to back each other up, you haven't bought real independence, because one design flaw can take both down at once. The fix is to model a common cause and usually to make the copies diverse — N-version programming, different reviewers, that sort of thing. That looks to me like the exact same move: stop treating two runs of one design as two separate coin flips and treat them as one underlying object instantiated twice. But the practical lessons point in opposite directions — the engineer adds diversity precisely when common-cause failure is dangerous, while the superrationalist needs exact sameness to make cooperation rational. Is this a genuine identity of structure that breaks exactly where the prescriptions diverge, or am I just pattern-matching on the word copy? If it's superficial, what does one side have that the other doesn't?
