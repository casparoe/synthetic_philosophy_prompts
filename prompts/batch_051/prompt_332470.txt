I'm taking a seminar on the evolution of communication, and I keep getting stuck on something that looks like a design flaw but is clearly universal: natural languages tolerate a great deal of homonymy and polysemy. The same form does different work depending on context—"pitch" is a throw, a black substance, a musical property, and a sales talk; a single Mandarin syllable with a fixed tone still corresponds to many unrelated morphemes, while the writing system partly disambiguates them with different characters; English writing occasionally confuses in the opposite direction, keeping one spelling for words pronounced differently, like "lead" and "bow". A deliberately designed signaling code would seem to want one form per meaning, yet real languages not only preserve these ambiguities but generate new ones through sound change, borrowing, and metaphorical extension—and communication only rarely collapses.

Before I read any more specific theories, I want the standard by which to judge them. Please write the desiderata that an adequate theory of how languages live with homonymy must meet, where adequacy means explaining how the form–meaning mapping can be massively many-to-many while everyday communication still works. For each requirement, give the concrete phenomenon or case that motivates it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or a parochial preference (say, a bias toward written language or toward strongly functionalist explanations). Make sure the list addresses at least the following unless you can argue one should be rejected: rapid, context-driven disambiguation in comprehension; the difference between stored homonymy and productive polysemy; why ambiguity persists and is even generated despite communicative pressure against it; the conditions under which ambiguity succeeds, fails, or is deliberately exploited (as in puns or in legal drafting); and the asymmetry between speech and writing in where ambiguity is tolerated or resolved. Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise plausible theories.
