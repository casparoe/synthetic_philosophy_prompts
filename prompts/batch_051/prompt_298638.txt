Quine's argument in Two Dogmas of Empiricism against the analytic-synthetic distinction turns on a circle: analytic truths are said to be either logical truths or truths reducible to logical truths by substituting synonyms, so analyticity must be explained through synonymy; yet every attempt to characterize synonymy independently—by definition, by interchangeability salva veritate, or by semantic rules—either presupposes analyticity or falls back on equally unclear intensional notions. Since no independent characterization of synonymy is forthcoming, Quine concludes the distinction is not a principled one. The argument, however, was made when meaning was studied by reflection on idealized languages and dictionaries. Now suppose semantics is done the way much of contemporary NLP does it: word and sentence meaning is represented as high-dimensional vectors induced from distributional patterns in a large corpus, and sameness of meaning is operationalized as closeness in that vector space, computed without anyone first having to state a definition or semantic rule. Does Quine's circularity argument survive this new development? Identify exactly which premise it puts pressure on—the claim that synonymy cannot be characterized independently, or the assumption that analyticity must be explained via synonymy in the first place—and say whether a weakened analytic-synthetic distinction could be recuperated from distributional semantics even if it is not the one Quine attacked. If you think the argument survives intact, say why vector-space similarity is not the semantic relation the argument needs.
