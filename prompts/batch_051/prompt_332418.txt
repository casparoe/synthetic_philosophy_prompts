I am a novelist working on a historical novel set in Weimar during Goethe’s later years, and I have been reading Eckermann’s Conversations with Goethe to understand how to make his political attitudes persuasive to readers who know him only as the author of Faust. One of the things that puzzles my early readers is his settled hostility to revolutionary politics, which sits oddly beside his youthful Sturm und Drang reputation. So I have been compiling the reasons he gives, in that book and in his later letters and notes, for preferring gradual reform over revolutionary rupture. So far I have five: (1) the horror argument, that the French Revolution’s killings and wars were so near and so grim that they discredited the whole enterprise, whatever abstract aims it claimed; (2) the organic argument, that durable political change must grow like a plant, by small increments, and that violent rupture destroys the roots from which better institutions could have developed; (3) the responsibility argument, that revolutions are usually produced by the errors and abuses of rulers, so the chief remedy is not rebellion from below but better government from above; (4) the culture argument, that political liberties are empty unless people have first acquired the self-mastery and communal habits that make freedom usable, so granting rights prematurely merely exchanges one tyranny for another; and (5) the Napoleonic argument, that his admiration for Napoleon was not a late surrender to revolution but a recognition that Napoleon closed the revolutionary chaos and re-established law and order.

I suspect this list covers the usual things one finds in biographies and surveys, and I am looking for a sixth reason that does not merely reword one of these five. It should be a distinct reason Goethe has, or could fairly be inferred to have from recorded statements, for opposing revolution while still calling himself a friend of freedom. It should not be a special case, a combination, or an application of the five I already have. If the best candidate is a weak argument, say so, and explain what its weakness might reveal about Goethe’s political thought more generally.
