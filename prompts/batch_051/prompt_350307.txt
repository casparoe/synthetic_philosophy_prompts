A colleague working on interpretability defends the following claim: a model can be trained to be understandable rather than merely correct, where this means that the explanation it offers for an answer states the actual basis on which the model produced that answer, and not a post-hoc reconstruction that merely sounds plausible. I suspect that 'the actual basis' is doing a lot of work, and that the claim may collapse into something trivial once we try to test it.

Design the strongest test you can of this claim. Be concrete: specify what two conditions you would compare (for example, a model trained with an explicit legibility objective versus one trained only for accuracy and asked to explain itself afterwards), what you would measure, and what pattern of results would count as evidence for genuine faithfulness rather than mere plausibility. Then say exactly what a positive result would and would not show—in particular, whether it would establish that the model's reasoning has become transparent to a user, or only that the explanation and the answer covary under interventions. Finally, address the worry that the distinction between reporting the actual basis and producing a plausible reconstruction is itself under-specified: is that a defect in the proposed experiments, or a defect in the claim?

Please write your answer in British English.
