I've been prepping a unit for an undergraduate "Language and Mind" seminar — about 15 students, 80 minutes — and I keep coming back to the idea that a writing system is a theory of the language it writes: a set of bets about which pieces of the speech stream matter enough to be represented. This stopped feeling abstract when my partner started learning English spelling as an adult and kept asking why the plural marker is written -s even when it sounds like /z/, or why sign has a g. My answers were all ad hoc spelling trivia, not an account of what a script actually does, and we've been half-arging about whether English spelling is broken or quietly optimizing for something else, like keeping morphemes recognizable. So I want a session where the students find this out with there hands rather than hear me announce it.

Design a runnable classroom activity or game for this group. No lecture up front, and I don't want the point named in advance. I'm imagining small groups each get a different invented mini-language on a page — one with a phonemic tone contrast, one with predictable allophony so two phonetically different sounds count as "the same sound" to them, one with heavy morphophonemic alternation like the English -s/-es case, one full of near-homophones — and each group has to design a way to write a short text in their language plus a key for reading it. Then groups swap and try to sight-read each other's texts aloud. The debrief should map what they built onto the distinction between shallow and deep orthographies, and onto the question of what a script can encode — phonemes, syllables, morphemes, tone — and what any choice leaves out. Give me rules, cheap printable materials, a timeline for the 80 minutes, a debrief guide, and a frank note on which feature of real writing systems this game can't reproduce and what I'll have to supply myself. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Hard cap: 1,200 words.
