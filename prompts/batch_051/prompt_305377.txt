I am a novelist applying for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship that funds artists who can formulate a philosophical question their work-in-progress cannot avoid. I need a one-page research proposal for the application (roughly eight hundred words), and I would like you to draft it. I will supply citations and a bibliography myself; do not include either, though you may refer to positions and arguments by their content.

The novel turns on a quarrel between two characters about a recording. The recording was assembled by an engineer from dozens of session takes: the final track splices fragments recorded on different days, repairs wrong notes, overdubs a piano line onto an ensemble take, and corrects pitch and timing so that the result is cleaner and more controlled than any single performer could produce live. The composer, near the end of her life, calls this recording the definitive form of the piece. A younger musician objects that no one has ever performed what is on the tape: a work fixed in a set of instructions for performance, she argues, is instantiated by performances, and a spliced studio artifact is no more a performance of the work than a composite photograph is an event.

Turn this into a proposal centered on a precise, tractable question. My own best formulation is this: under what conditions, if any, does a studio recording assembled by editing many takes count as a performance, a token, of the musical work, rather than as something distinct from any performance—a phonographic work, a representation of possible performances, or neither? The proposal should state the question so that it admits a determinate answer, explain briefly what is at stake beyond the novel, and explain why the familiar ways of thinking about musical works leave it open. The obvious candidate conclusions seem to be: the recording does count as a performance, because it is causally derived from score-driven performances and is intended and received as a presentation of the work; or it never counts, because a token of a performed work must be the product of a continuous act or process of music-making, and editing severs exactly that link; or a revisionary third view, on which the type/token framework needs an additional category, such as a version of the work for a medium, instantiated directly by recordings.

Then describe the approach, and say why it is philosophical rather than empirical and why it is better suited than the obvious alternative of surveying listeners or cataloguing recording practices. The work would proceed by constructing paired cases that vary one parameter at a time—an untouched live-to-tape recording; a live take with one wrong note repaired; a record assembled from fragments as written; a fully synthesized realization involving no performance of the score at all—and by tracing which considerations actually decide between the positions: score-compliance versus causal history, the composer’s declaration, the difference between repair and construction, and whether judgments that seem right for classical splice-edited records carry over to studio-constructed popular music or force separate ontologies.

The proposal should also say what a useful deliverable looks like—an essay defending one of the conclusions, which the novel can then draw on without simplifying the dispute—and what would count as failure: for instance, if the middle cases prove intractably unstable, or if the three positions collapse into a merely verbal difference. End with an honest assessment of what could make the project too large for six months, such as its being unanswerable without first settling the entire type/token framework.

Throughout, mark which points rest on widely held commitments in the philosophy of music and which are genuinely contested among people who work on the subject, and do not present a contested view as though it were the field’s default.
