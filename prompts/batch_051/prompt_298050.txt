In my political philosophy seminar this term, we have been working through Rawls's account of public reason and the place of religious argument in democratic politics. A puzzle keeps surfacing in our discussions, and I would like an essay addressed to it. People who otherwise take Rawls's side are quick to object when a legislator justifies a vote on, say, physician-assisted dying or the regulation of embryo research by citing Scripture or church teaching; they say the legislator is letting her private comprehensive doctrine dictate law and is violating the duty of civility. But when another legislator justifies a vote by citing an equally controversial secular comprehensive doctrine—a particular Kantian ideal of autonomy, or a utilitarian principle optimizing aggregate welfare—the same people tend to hear her as offering reasons, not as imposing a worldview. As I read Rawls, this is strange: the public reason restriction applies to comprehensive doctrines generally, whether religious or secular, and a doctrine's being secular does not by itself make it the kind of reason that other reasonable citizens can be expected to share. I want to know whether the asymmetry in these ordinary judgments can be defended. Construct the strongest case for treating religious comprehensive doctrines as specially problematic in public justification while allowing secular comprehensive doctrines to pass more easily. Then test that case against a hard nearby example, such as a legislator who appeals to a hedonistic utilitarian premise that most of her fellow citizens reject, and against Rawls's own treatment of how comprehensive doctrines may legitimately enter public debate. End with a clear verdict: should the asymmetry be kept, abandoned, or replaced by a more refined position?
