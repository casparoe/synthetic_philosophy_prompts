Three of us were at dinner when Mara and Dev got into it about bugs. Someone had pulled up a news story about a company farming black soldier fly larvae by the billions for animal feed, and the article mentioned, almost as an aside, that some people think we should take insect welfare seriously. That was all it took.

Mara's first move was what she called the scale argument. She said: suppose you're only 2% confident that one of these larvae can actually suffer. A facility processing a billion larvae a year still gives you an expected 20 million possibly-suffering lives per year — and that's before you start talking about wild insects and pesticides. If you'd hesitate before doing something that carried even a small chance of hurting a dog, then the fact that the number here is a billion should't make you round the doubt down to zero. She said people hear "we don't know if they feel pain" and treat it as a reason to do nothing, but if you actually do the math, the uncertainty shows up with a giant multiplier.

Dev said that's a misuse of probability. Sentience isn't a lottery, he said. Its not that 2% of the larvae in the barrel are the ones that feel pain and the other 98% are duds; either this kind of animal has experience or it doesn't. You can't take a degree of belief and a headcount and come out with 20 million "expected" suffering lives. That's adding up fractional bugs into whole ones.

Mara said that's exactly how every real decision under uncertainty works. "Either my house burns down or it doesn't" is also true, but the insurance company still multiplies the chance by the cost. Probabilities are about what you should expect given what you know, not about there being an actual randomizer inside the thing you're uncertain about. If you're allowed to act on a 2% chance of a bad outcome in every other area of life, why is this the one place where 2% gets rounded to zero?

Dev's next move was the guardrail problem. Fine, he said, but if you start multiplying small probabilities by huge populations, where does it end? There's some tiny chance plants can suffer, some tiny chance of much weirder things being conscious. There are always enough leaves and blades of grass for the arithmetic to blow up. If the headcount is doing the work, you've just licensed a Pascal's mugging. You need an independent reason to give the insect hypothesis real weight, and "there are lots of them" isn't it.

Mara said the independent reason is the evidence. Insects have nerve cells that respond to injury, some species groom a damaged limb, some trade off danger and feeding in ways that look like there's somebody home. That's a much better case than plant pain, which has almost no behavioral or structural support. I'm not pulling the hypothesis out of the air to make the math scary. And she asked Dev what his threshold was. If 2% is too low to act on, what's the cutoff — 50%? Why would that be less arbitrary? A bridge with a 2% chance of collapsing gets closed; nobody waits for 50.

Dev came back with the other side of the equation. Even granting your degree-of-belief stuff, he said, you're still doing the arithmetic wrong. You've quietly assumed each insect counts like a dog, just discounted by the probability. But if larvae are sentient at all, the badness per larva is plausibly tiny — a faint, barely-there flicker, not a dog's terror. You can't just add up a billion faint flickers until they equal some number of dogs. Severity matters, not just headcount. And once you scale the badness down to match a minimal mind, your 20 million becomes a lot less than an emergency; it might even come out lower than the ordinary animal suffering in the soy or fishmeal your bug farm would replace.

Mara said that's already inside the calculation. If you think an insect's pain is a ten-thousandth as bad as a dog's, run it: a billion larvae times 2% times one ten-thousandth is 2,000 dog-equivalent pains per facility per year. If you want to get that down to trivial, you need the per-insect badness to be essentially zero, and that just is the claim that they feel nothing — which is what the 2% was supposed to leave open. You can't say "we don't know" and then assume the number is negligible; that's rounding down on the other side. And if the alternative protein is worse, fine, show it in the same units.

Dev's last move was about what happens if you actually live with this. Because once the arithmetic is allowed, he said, ordinary life becomes a moral disaster. You squash bugs when you walk, drive, mow the lawn. Growing vegetables kills insects in droves, organic or not. There's no stopping point. A view that makes a Tuesday commute a quiet atrocity has to be wrong somewhere, because our obligations can't be impossible to live with.

Mara said the demandingness point is about blame and decision procedure, not about whether insects matter. If the calculator says the food system has a hidden moral bill, that's bad news, not a math error. The honest response would be to go looking for cheap interventions — slaughter methods, feed, lighting, pesticides — not to declare the bill invalid because it's uncomfortable. Lots of true facts are inconvenient.

We broke up for the night not long after that. They repeated versions of these moves a few more times, but I've given you the main ones. I honestly couldn't referee it; every time one of them seemed to win a round, the other's response sounded right too. Could you go through it step by step and tell me which moves actually hold up, which don't, what neither of them considered, and who is more right overall? I'm not just looking for "both have good points."
