I sit on the collections committee of a university museum. We have been offered a bequest of a contemporary work consisting of several hundred unconnected, individually replaceable ceramic tiles, each roughly the size of a hand, which the artist's estate instructs us to install loose on a gallery floor according to a plan; the estate says the arrangement may be altered and individual tiles replaced without changing the work. Our deed-of-gift forms force a binary classification: the bequest is either a single work or a collection of separate objects. The classification determines our insurance schedule, our condition-reporting obligations, whether individual tiles may be lent or sold, and what counts as damage to the work. The committee is split. One faction says the tiles compose one object because the artist designated them as a single work and the museum would exhibit, catalogue, and insure them as such. A second says there is no single object here at all, only many tiles arranged in a pattern: without physical connection or a unifying process, composition has not occurred, and treating the plurality as one thing is a bookkeeping fiction. A third says composition requires the parts to function jointly as an integrated whole, which these scattered, replaceable tiles do not. Draft a short decision memo for the committee that recommends a classification, gives criteria we can actually apply to future unconnected multi-part works, and engages the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously. The members will recognize the underlying issue as the special composition question; you may use that vocabulary, but the bottom line must be a policy we can act on.
