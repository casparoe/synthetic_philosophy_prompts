Spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the St. Petersburg family of problems, and we ended up back at Nicolaus Bernoulli's original dice version in his 1713 letter to Montmort. The bit that got us stuck is where he asks:

"[W]hat is the expectation of B … if A promises to B to give him some coins in this progression 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 etc. or 1, 3, 9, 27 etc. or 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 etc. or 1, 8, 27, 64 instead of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 etc. as beforehand. Although for the most part these problems are not difficult, you will find however something most curious."

My friend thinks this is really just a puzzle about divergent series and the right response is to say the question is badly posed. I think it's a problem for expected value as a guide to action. We tried to list all the standard moves we could think of: (1) replace money with concave utility of money, so the infinite series has a finite expected utility; (2) impose bounded utility, so even a convergent series can't command arbitrarily large fees; (3) truncate the game because no actual payer could honor the giant prizes, so the real expectation is finite; (4) argue that extremely tiny probabilities of astronomically large payoffs shouldn't drive our choices, whatever the expectation says; (5) abandon expected value maximization entirely for satisficing or some non-maximizing rule. I'm worried these five just re-describe the problem or change the game, and they don't really tell me why a rational person shouldn't pay a huge fee. Is there another response to Bernoulli's dice version that's genuinly different from all five, not just a combination or restatement of them? Or if one of the five is right, can you say what makes it the correct diagnosis without just appealing to the fact that it makes the math come out finite?
