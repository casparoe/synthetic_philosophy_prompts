I spent four decades as a field biologist, most of them watching animals manage the ordinary business of staying alive, and I have only recently begun reading philosophy of mind in earnest. The position I keep encountering under the name of Morgan's canon is, in the form in which it now circulates, roughly this: never explain an animal's behavior by appeal to a higher psychological process if a lower one can be made to cover the facts; begin with associative learning, fixed action patterns, and simple heuristics, and move up to representations, intentions, or inferences only when the lower accounts have demonstrably failed. I want to give this principle its full due, because I recognize its virtues from within my own science. It supplies a single evidential standard across taxa and across laboratories. It keeps comparative cognition from turning into the writing of animal biographies. It is a bulwark against the observer's tendency to project a human mental life onto any behavior that has a familiar look. And it can be stated as a decision procedure: locate the behavior on the lowest rung that predicts it, and decline to add structure above that rung. One can even modernize the idea without its Victorian ladder: prefer, other things equal, the model that attributes the least representational structure, and require any richer attribution to earn its keep by predicting something the leaner model cannot.

But I also have an intuition I cannot shake, and I do not regard it as a sentimental one. During a long season watching ravens, I repeatedly saw a bird cache food in view of a conspecific and then, after the other bird had gone, return and move the cache; birds that cached in private almost never did this, and when a watcher was present the cacher would sometimes carry the food behind a boulder or a rise that blocked the watcher's line of sight. The lower-order story can, of course, be made to fit: caching in view of a competitor has in the past led to loss, and the competitor's presence had become a discriminative stimulus for moving food; the bird needs no conception of what another can see. I can always construct such a story. The difficulty is that the lower story can always be made to fit, because with enough added associations and sensitivity to cues a patient modeler can reproduce almost any sequence of movements without attributing a single mental state. That flexibility is purchased with exactly the vice the canon was meant to banish: a theory so accommodating that it ceases to be informative. What I cannot do is believe that the associative story, in the case I watched, is the explanation and not merely a redescription of the bird's movements that deliberately declines to notice the structure of what it was doing. The parsimonious account preserves the sequence of events the way a rough prose paraphrase preserves a poem: nothing false, and nearly everything missing.

I am therefore torn. I am drawn to a principle that is genuinely economical, unifying, and protective against self-deception, and I hold an intuition that in at least some cases the principle does not merely counsel caution but systematically misdescribes its object. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for each side to give way, and what a negotiated position would look like. In particular: what would justify treating my intuition as projection to be debunked rather than as data the theory must respect? What would justify amending or abandoning the canon, and what would replace its default conservatism if it were given up? Is there a reading of lower process or of explanation on which they are compatible, for instance the canon as a rule about what the evidence licenses while my intuition is a claim about what is true, and how much of the conflict would that reading actually leave standing? I am asking for the costs of each move, honestly stated, not for a side.
