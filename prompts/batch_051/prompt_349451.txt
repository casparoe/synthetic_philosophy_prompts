For the past several years I have served on the small committee that decides reuse questions for a university special collections library, where most of our hard cases involve orphaned works and abandoned software — material still in copyright whose owners, heirs, or corporate successors cannot be found. Our decisions have felt consistent, but I have never had to state the principle behind them. Here is the record. We approved controlled digital lending of a still-in-copyright novel from the 1950s whose publisher folded decades ago and whose author's heirs are untraceable: one patron at a time, no downloads, with a standing takedown page. We approved preservation scanning and on-site reading-room access to the unpublished diary of a poet who died in the 1970s, but rejected an academic press's proposal to publish an edited edition of the diary for sale. We approved running an emulated copy of 1990s educational software, made by a company that dissolved around 2002, in the library's media lab for research use, while rejecting a tutoring firm's request to duplicate and resell that software to its students. We approved indexing and non-commercial research viewing of a defunct newspaper's photographic archive, but rejected a proposal to license selected images as stock photography for advertising. And we approved lending a digitized 1980s educational filmstrip, from a dissolved distributor, to a schoolteacher for classroom use at no charge, with a takedown notice on file.

The new proposal is this. In the late 1990s a small company published a CD-ROM database of transcribed medieval guild records by subscription, available only to research libraries. The company dissolved in 2005; its owners, programmers, and investors cannot be located, and no successor acquired the rights. Our staff have documented a good-faith search. A digital-humanities unit at our university now asks permission to extract the records from our copy, clean them up, and put them on a free public website with no access restrictions, with a takedown page in case a rights holder ever comes forward. They would not charge and would not claim any new rights in the data.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which of our precedents is the closest analog? And if our record genuinely underdetermines the answer, what principle would we be adopting with each choice — and what further case would most sharply separate the two principles?
