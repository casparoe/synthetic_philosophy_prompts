John Locke, in Chapter V of the *Second Treatise of Government*, writes:

"Though the earth, and all inferior creatures, be common to all men, yet every man has a property in his own person: this no body has any right to but himself. The labour of his body, and the work of his hands, we may say, are properly his. Whatsoever then he removes out of the state that nature hath provided, and left it in, he hath mixed his labour with, and joined to it something that is his own, and thereby makes it his property. It being by him removed from the common state nature hath placed it in, it hath by this labour something annexed to it, that excludes the common right of other men: for this labour being the unquestionable property of the labourer, no man but he can have a right to what that is once joined to, at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others."

I am compiling readings of the clause "at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others"—the Lockean proviso—for a seminar, and I already have four:

(1) the equal-share reading, on which the proviso requires that any appropriation leave others able to appropriate unowned resources of comparable quality;
(2) the no-worsening reading, on which the proviso survives into full property regimes and makes an appropriation illegitimate only if it leaves others materially worse off than they would have been in a state of nature;
(3) the historical-supersession reading, on which the proviso was satisfied automatically in the early state of nature because of natural abundance, and its normative force is suspended once money and convention make scarcity inescapable;
(4) the theological reading, on which the proviso is a corollary of God's grant of the earth to mankind in common and is to be enforced by natural law.

Give me another reading of that clause that is genuinely different from these four—not a restatement, special case, or combination of them—and then say what it explains in the passage that the other readings do not. End your answer with a one-sentence summary of the reading as the last line.
