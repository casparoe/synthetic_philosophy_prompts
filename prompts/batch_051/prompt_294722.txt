I've ended up committed to a view about self-knowledge, and I'd like a map of where the view is exposed, because I suspect I've been shielding it without noticing.

The view is this: there is no mental condition — an ache, a mood, a belief, a want — whose presence by itself puts the person in a position to know they are in it. In the vocabulary I've picked up from reading on my own, I'm denying that any mental condition is "luminous," and I'm denying the "KK principle" as I understand it: that if you know something, you're in a position to know that you know it. My route in was a gradual-change argument. Someone can slide by tiny, unnoticed steps from plainly feeling cold to plainly not feeling cold. If feeling cold announced its own presence, you'd be entitled to claim knowledge of it all the way along the chain, and you'd end up claiming knowledge where you obviously don't have it. So I concluded that access to our own minds is at best merely reliable.

But I can already see two pressure points, and I don't know how seriously to take them. First, the argument seems to work only for conditions that come in degrees. If some mental conditions are all-or-nothing — or if some are partly constituted by how the person would report them when asked — the argument may never get a grip. Second, denying any guaranteed access might commit me to more than I want. I'd like to know what my view predicts about ordinary cases, such as confabulation, self-report, and unnoticed pain, that a believer in guaranteed access would predict differently, and which of those predictions would actually expose my view if they came out wrong.

I'm not looking for the standard objections a textbook would already count as settled. If the view turns out to be so qualified that nothing could count against it, say that plainly, and say whether that insulation is a sign of an empty view or a defensible one.
