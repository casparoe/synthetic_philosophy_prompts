Two people in my decision theory reading group got into it over a game, and neither of them would budge. The game is this: you flip a fair coin until it lands heads. If the first heads is on flip n, then when n is odd you win 2^n/n dollars, and when n is even you pay 2^n/n dollars.

Their argument went roughly like this.

A said the expected value is the sum over all n of (1/2^n) times the payoff for flip n, which comes out to 1 minus 1/2 plus 1/3 minus 1/4 and so on, which converges to ln 2. So a rational person should be willing to pay up to about 69 cents to play.

B said that's a mistake. She said expected value is only defined when the sum is absolutely convergent. Here the positive terms alone sum to infinity and so do the negative terms, so the expected value is undefined. The alternating series is a conditional convergence trick, not an expectation. Since you can rearrange a conditionally convergent series to converge to any number you like, there's no fact of the matter about what the fair price is.

A said no, the game comes with a natural order: the outcomes are indexed by the flip number. The sum in that order is the right one. Rearranging terms corresponds to playing a different game, where the payoffs have been shuffled among the possible outcomes.

B said the probability space doesn't care about the order of the natural numbers. If merely relabeling the outcomes changes the price, then the price wasn't a property of the gamble to begin with. Expectation should be invariant under how you list the outcomes.

A said okay, but if you truncate the game at any finite horizon, the expected value is finite, and as the horizon grows these truncated expected values approach ln 2. So that's a perfectly natural way to assign a price.

B said that doesn't help, because different truncation schemes or groupings can approach different limits. The finite-horizon expected value is the expectation of a different gamble, not the original one. If your decision procedure depends on an arbitrary cutoff, it's not a theory of the original game.

A said maybe the real lesson is that utilities have to be bounded. Once you put a bound on utility from money, the expectation becomes well-defined and all this goes away.

B said fine, but that's abandoning expected value for dollars, not showing that the original problem was misconceived. The original game is a coherent offer, and expected value theory ought to say something about it, even if that something is 'undefined.'

They asked me to referee and I couldnt. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each step from clearly mistaken to clearly right, explain who is right about what, and say who is more right overall.
