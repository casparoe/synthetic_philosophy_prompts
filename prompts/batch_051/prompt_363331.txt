I teach ethics, and I've been trying to write down a position on eating animals that isn't just calibrated to rich people with good grocery stores. I'd like a consistency audit before I inflict this on a reading group. Here's the list of commitments I seem to have ended up with. Tell me which ones actually pull against each other and which only look like they do; order the real tensions by how much they'd force a rewrite, from severe to mostly cosmetic. For each real tension, say what the cheapest fix is and what it costs, and then tell me which one revision of one commitment would clean up the most overall.

1. Animal suffering is bad, and its badness doesn't vary with geography or culture: a hen crammed into a battery cage in a low-income country is living just as bad a life as one in Iowa.
2. But a person's food obligations track their feasible alternatives. If going fully plant-based in a given place would mean chronic undernutrition, or a child not getting the animal-source foods that prevent stunting, then eating some animal products isn't itself a wrong against animals.
3. So I reject one-size-fits-all veganism: the duty to go plant-based reaches different in different food environments.
4. That doesn't make me a relativist. A tourist from a rich country who eats the local meat just for the cultural experience isn't off the hook, and a long tradition of force-feeding geese doesn't make that practice fine.
5. Consider this case: a mother in a food-scarce area whose underweight child needs cheap complete protein, and the only affordable source is factory-farmed eggs. I want to say that her buying the eggs is permissible—not just that she's not blameworthy, but that the act itself is fine.
6. At the same time, I hold that buying products from a seriously unjust farming system is wrong, and the wrong-making feature attaches to the purchase itself, not to the buyer's circumstances.
7. I'm also welfarist-leaning about what's bad here: most of what's wrong with animal agriculture seems to me to be the suffering and deprivation the animals actually live through, so an animal that had a good life and was killed without pain hasn't been wronged much.
8. But I also don't want to say that a rich person who starts a small 'humane' farm and sells its meat to a luxury market is doing something fine.

There's a draft paper somewhere in this. Pull it apart.
