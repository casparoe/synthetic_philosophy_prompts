I am drafting the philosophical-position section of a paper for a workshop on narrative theories of the self, and before I circulate it I would like a careful consistency audit of the commitments listed below. I hold all of them, but I suspect they exert pressure on one another in ways I cannot quite locate, and I need someone to trace out exactly where the strain is.

1. Ontological parsimony. There is no immaterial substance, Cartesian ego, or separate owner of experiences; the only concrete reality involved in a human life is the biological organism and its ongoing processes.

2. The self as a center of narrative gravity. The self is an abstract object we posit to make sense of the self-telling behavior of persons, not an additional concrete particular inside or outside the organism.

3. The reality of the abstract. Calling the self an abstraction does not make it unreal. A center of gravity is not a physical thing, but it is not a mere fiction either; it is as real as an explanatory posit ever needs to be, and the self is real in exactly that way.

4. Desert-entailing responsibility. The self is nonetheless a proper target of the reactive attitudes. People can be genuinely praiseworthy or blameworthy for what they have done in a backward-looking sense, and attitudes such as guilt and resentment are fitting responses to a person, not merely useful instruments for shaping future behavior.

5. Self-narratives answer to the facts. A person can be systematically mistaken about who he or she is: the arrogant person who takes himself to be humble, the habitually cruel person who believes she is kind. Friends, therapists, and biographers can sometimes be better placed than the person himself to say what he is actually like.

6. No hidden true self. I deny that this requires a fixed, narrative-independent essence waiting to be discovered. The self is not a pre-existing object that accurate stories merely describe; accurate self-understanding cannot be modeled on finding a buried artifact.

7. Diachronic commitment survives. If I promised something yesterday, that promise gives me a reason today even when my present self-narrative has shifted and I no longer identify with the considerations behind the promise. The continuance of a self through time is not an illusion that my view forces me to abandon.

Please work through these seven commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which pairs only appear to be. For each genuine tension, rank it by severity, state precisely what would have to give in order to resolve it, and say what the philosophical cost of that revision would be. End by identifying the single revision that would restore the most coherence for the least loss.
