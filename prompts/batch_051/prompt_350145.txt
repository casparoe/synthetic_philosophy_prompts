I’m a PhD student in political theory, and I have been stuck on an argument that seems to prove too much. In Aristotle’s Politics 1317b he says the institutions following from democratic equality include: “election of officials by all from all; government of each by all, and of all by each in turn; election by lot either to all magistracies or to all that do not need experience and skill; no property-qualification for office, or only a very low one; no office to be held twice, or more than a few times, by the same person, or few offices except the military ones; short tenure either of all offices or of as many as possible...” Now consider a modern state with universal suffrage, competitive elections, and no property qualification. Its officials are chosen by election rather than lot; its main offices are thought to require experience and skill; the same people hold office repeatedly and often make politics a career; and much governing is delegated to ministries and courts rather than exercised directly by the assembly. The argument then runs: (1) democratic justice is equality according to number, not worth; (2) such equality requires that citizens rule and are ruled in turn; (3) ruling in turn requires short tenure, no repeated office, and selection by lot for offices not needing special skill; (4) a system of permanent elected office-holders violates these conditions; (5) therefore modern representative democracy is not democracy but an elective oligarchy. That conclusion seems too strong, since it would classify almost every existing liberal democracy as non-democratic and would imply that any complex state without mass sortition is oligarchic. Where does the argument go wrong, or which premise should I give up, and what does rejecting it cost? Lead with your diagnosis in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
