While reading the Confessions, Book XI, I have been struck by two lines of thought in Augustine's treatment of time that seem to pull against each other. Early on, he says he knows well enough what time is until someone asks him to explain it, and then he no longer knows. He then seems to dissolve time altogether: the past is no longer, the future is not yet, and the present, if it has no duration, cannot be; under that analysis time looks as though it cannot strictly be said to exist at all. Yet later in the same book, when he asks how we can measure intervals of time, he gives a positive account. What we call past, present, and future, he suggests, are better described as three ways the mind holds things: memory holds what has gone, attention holds what is now, and expectation reaches toward what is coming. He then appears to conclude that time is a stretching-out of the mind's own activity.

Are these two passages in genuine tension, or does the later account resolve the earlier aporia? Does the claim that time is a stretching-out of the mind contradict the earlier thought that time lacks existence, or does it change the subject by relocating time from the world to inner experience? I would like you to lay out the strongest reconciliations—different senses of existence, a distinction between a destructive phase and a constructive phase, a distinction Augustine never drew explicitly—and say which of them is honest and which is special pleading. If the reconciliation succeeds, what sort of reality does time turn out to have for Augustine, and does that outcome serve his larger point about God's eternity and the temporality of creation?

Please say how confident you are in the interpretation and single out the point you are least sure of.
