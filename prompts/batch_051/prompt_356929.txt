So this is the third week my reading group has gone around in circles on Hume's essay about the standard of taste, and I've realized the problem isn't that we disagree on the answer, it's that we never agreed on what the question demands. One person points at Shakespeare and claims that settles it; someone else points at two thoughtful people deadlocked about a film and claims that settles it the other way. I want the spec sheet before the theories. What would an adequate account of aesthetic judgement have to actually deliver, where adequate means good enough to make sense of our ordinary practice of arguing about, training, and deferring over matters of taste? I don't mean a ranked list of theories — I mean the requirements, with the case or consideration that forces each one. It should cover verdicts about particular works, and verdicts about whether someone is even in a position to judge (I have a hunch these are different and that collapsing them is how a lot of views cheat); it should make sense of taste being trainable and correctable without reducing that training to social pressure; it should handle disagreements where neither side seems to be making an error; and it should say something about our relationship to our own past verdicts, since 'I was wrong about that album' and 'my taste just changed' feel like very different claims but I cant articulate why.

For each requirement, tell me whether it's non-negotiable or just a local preference of the subjectivist or objectivist side. I also want to know which distinctions can't be collapsed: for example, I'm tempted to collapse the distinction between 'I like it' and 'it's good', but that would make a huge amount of real aesthetic talk come out as incoherent, and a good theory shouldn't have to declare the practice deluded unless it's forced to. Then answer the two things I actually care about: which single requirement does the most work to exclude the lazy view that beauty is just individual liking, and is there any pair of requirements that probably can't be jointly satisfied? My suspicion is that first-person authority (I'm usually right about whether I find something beautiful) and genuine error (I can be wrong that something is beautiful) are in real tension, but I can't tell if that's a deep point or just me being sloppy. Keep the scorecard short — a sentence each on subjectivism, realism, a Hume-style response-dependence view with an ideal judge, and a deflationary view that says there's no real puzzle here — only to check that the list has teeth. And when you finish, say how confident you are in the list itself and what single item you're least sure belongs on it.
