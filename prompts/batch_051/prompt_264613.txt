Let's mess with the Allais paradox a bit. Standard setup: Choice 1 is A, $1M for sure, versus B, 10% of $5M, 89% of $1M, and 1% of nothing. Choice 2 is C, 11% of $1M and 89% of nothing, versus D, 10% of $5M and 90% of nothing. The usual finding is A over B but D over C, which violates independence once you factor out the common 89% branch. Here's the variation I want you to work through. Leave everything the same, but change the 1% "nothing" in the two risky options to a 1% chance of losing $50,000 out of pocket: so B becomes 10% of $5M, 89% of $1M, 1% of -$50k, and D becomes 10% of $5M, 89% of nothing, 1% of -$50k. First give me the explicit common-branch decomposition for both pairs and say exactly what independence requires now: which preference profile would count as a violation, and why. Then work out what a certainty-effect account, a regret account, and a loss-aversion account would each predict for the modified pair—you don't have to settle the empirical question, just be precise about the mechanisms. Does the classic Allais pattern survive, reverse, or come apart, and which feature of the original is doing the real work: the literal certainty of the $1M, the zero outcome, or the size of the common branch? If the accounts pull in different directions, tell me the cleanest follow-up variation that would tell them apart.
