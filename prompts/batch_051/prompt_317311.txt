You are a moral philosopher who works on population ethics. Think through the question carefully before committing, and begin by separating two things clearly: the value of adding a new life, and the value of improving a life that already exists.

There is a striking tension in ordinary moral thought. A couple with two children are deciding whether to have a third, and the third child would have a good life. Most people would not say the couple have thereby made the world better in the same way they would if they improved one of their existing children's lives, and many would hesitate to say the child has been benefited by being brought into existence at all. The pattern reappears at larger scales: people do not ordinarily judge a city with a million flourishing residents a better city than one with eight hundred thousand equally flourishing residents simply because it contains more of them, and few would claim that humanity's being larger is by itself an improvement if average wellbeing stayed the same. Yet a rise in average wellbeing among the existing population is treated as a plain improvement. Ordinary judgment, then, appears to count improvements to lives that already exist while giving little or no weight to the addition of new good lives—an asymmetry between an average-style evaluation of existing lives and a refusal to count a larger total achieved by adding people.

Is there a defensible principle that generates both halves of this asymmetry, or should it be rejected as an inconsistency? If you defend it, defend it specifically against the case in which the added life would be well above the average; if you reject it, say which side of ordinary thought is wrong and whether it errs by undercounting additions or overcounting improvements.
