You are a professional philosopher who works on epistemology and on the question of whether human judges can reliably evaluate arguments whose full reasoning they cannot reproduce, including arguments produced by sophisticated AI systems. I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I need you to think hard with me about a rule I have followed for years. Please begin by brainstorming, for yourself, the situations in which my rule would help me believe the truth and the situations in which it would cost me the truth. Then give me the strongest version of the argument for the rule, the strongest version of the argument against it, and a final structured verdict that separates what is defensible from what is not. Do not just tell me to defer sometimes and distrust sometimes; I want to know exactly what makes the difference.

Here is my rule. I do not allow an argument to move my beliefs unless I can restate the argument in my own plain words—not merely repeat its conclusion or describe its shape, but actually walk through the reasoning in prose I could read aloud to my sister, who is sharp but has no specialized training in the relevant field. If an argument depends on a formal model, a proof, a simulation, or a technical vocabulary that I cannot translate in this way, I suspend judgment on its conclusion. I give it no weight as evidence, no matter how much I trust the person offering it, until someone supplies a translation I can verify for myself.

The situations that make this rule feel necessary are real and recurring. Last month a transport economist friend told me a congestion-pricing plan would not hurt the poor in the way I assumed; pressed, he walked me to the equilibrium of his model and a table of parameters, and I could not follow the path from the mathematics to his conclusion, so I withheld belief. An acquaintance who works on AI safety has told me that a scheme in which two AI systems debate one another in front of a human judge could keep powerful systems honest, and that there is a formal result showing that, under certain assumptions, truth survives even against a debater trying to bury a flaw. I could not parse the reasoning, so by my own rule I gave the claim no weight. As a novelist I know that opacity is also a hiding place: the most persuasive sentence is often the one whose reasoning has nowhere to stand.

But I can see the other side. Carried out honestly, this rule would leave me unable to form beliefs about much of anything technical; I would simply never update on arguments I find hard. Some truths may not be statable in a paragraph my sister could follow, and a rule that treats lucidity as an entry condition may be an aesthetic preference dressed up as an epistemic principle—a way of protecting the beliefs I already hold from people who outmatch me. I cannot tell from the inside whether I am enforcing a sensible standard or using my own limitations as a shield.

My question, then, is this. Is my rule a rational response to the genuine problem of obfuscated argument, a flattering name for a resistance to being outmatched, or both at once? Could it be reformulated so that it keeps the protection against hidden flaws without dismissing knowledge that is genuinely technical? And is there a version of the rule I am entitled to apply in high-stakes, time-sensitive cases—such as deciding what to believe about AI safety proposals made in terms I cannot follow—and what exactly would that version say? I want your answer to be as precise as you can make it about the features of the source, the domain, and my own position that determine when a "no opaque arguments" policy is justified and when it becomes a way of not listening.
