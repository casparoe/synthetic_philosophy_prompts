Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether natural selection deserves to be called a stupid optimizer, and I want to get clear about what could actually change my mind. The position I was defending runs roughly as follows. Natural selection is a myopic hill-climber: it accepts any available mutation that happens to improve inclusive fitness here and now, has no representation of the global fitness landscape, cannot cross adaptive valleys, and cannot favor an innovation because it will pay off later. What it produces looks designed, but the process is opportunistic and accretive — frozen accidents, irreversible constraints, and arrangements no competent engineer would settle for. Gradient descent, by contrast, at least follows a well-defined local gradient, and even its failures look more principled than the accidents selection is stuck with.

I want a map of what this position is committed to and where it is exposed. Specifically: which observations, arguments, formal results, or developments in evolutionary biology or machine learning would count against it, and which would merely be awkward for it? Please sort them by decisiveness, and include what a defender of the position would themselves admit counts against it, not only what critics say. I also want to know what the view predicts that its main rival does not — the rival being that selection over deep time is a surprisingly effective search process — and where those differing predictions would show up, whether in comparative biology, experimental evolution, or the behavior of optimization algorithms. I am not after a reading list; what I want is the substantive map of where the view risks being wrong.

There is one objection my friend pressed that I want addressed head-on. Modern deep learning suggests that a blind, local, gradient-following procedure can arrive at solutions that generalize better than anyone expected, while stochastic gradient descent turns out to have its own pathologies: sharp minima, brittle shortcuts, dependence on initialization, and failures on rugged landscapes. Does that undercut the contrast between a supposedly intelligent gradient descent and a supposedly stupid selection, or does the position need to be restated so that it no longer leans on that comparison? If it needs restating, give the version you think is most defensible.

Finally, give an honest assessment of whether the view can be insulated by re-describing every apparent success of selection as still just a sequence of local steps, and whether that insulation has already occurred. If the view can be qualified into unfalsifiability, say so, and say whether that is a genuine problem for a claim of this kind, or whether a claim about the character of a process may legitimately be interpretive rather than empirical. Please answer in American English.
