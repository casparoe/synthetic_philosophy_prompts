ok so my friend and i just spent most of an evening going back and forth about the born rule in many-worlds and i came out of it with a homemade account that i want someone to actually stress-test. im calling it successor stakes, mostly because i needed a name to keep the argument straight.

the setup: in many-worlds, when you measure something, there isnt one outcome that happens with some probability. you split. there is a successor of you in each branch, and all of them are equally you going forward. so the usual question which branch will i end up in is ill-posed. the real question is how much present-you should care about each successor before the split.

my claim is that you should care about each successor in proportion to the branchs squared amplitude, and that this is not the born rule being assumed, its derived from a coherence condition. here is the condition: your caring-weights have to be additive under splitting a branch into two sub-branches that are, for all practical purposes, identical. if you take one branch and split it again into two copies of the same world, your total concern for that family of successors shouldnt change. branch count fails this, since splitting doubles the count. raw amplitude fails it too: if a branch has amplitude 0.8 and you split it into two equal sub-branches, each has amplitude about 0.57, and 0.57 plus 0.57 is way more than 0.8. but squared amplitude adds: 0.64 equals 0.32 plus 0.32. so squared amplitude is the unique caring weight that survives arbitrary re-description of how finely you slice the branches.

so the born rule, on this view, isnt a probability rule at all. its a bookkeeping rule for how a rational agent distributes concern across future selves. probabilities are just caring weights seen from before the split. when you bet on a 70/30 quantum coin, you arent guessing; you are allocating 70 percent of your concern to the successor who wins and 30 percent to the successor who loses, and expected utility is just the arithmetic of that allocation.

my friend pushed back that this is just the born rule wearing a costume, and that caring-weights are exactly as mysterious as probabilities, just with the word probability crossed out. i dont think thats fair, because the additivity argument gives you a reason the weights have to be squared amplitudes rather than anything else, which the bare born rule does not. but i also worry ive just rederived something decision theorists already settled, and that my successor talk is smuggling in alot of personal-identity assumptions i havent noticed.

so: what am i actually getting right, is this already a thing under some name, does it dissolve the born rule problem or just relocate it, and where does it break?
