Okay, here's one I've been mulling over. Suppose a country brings in an AI Persuasion Licence. Any general-purpose AI above a capability threshold (the sort that can already out-argue most people) has to show a sidebar before it's allowed to advise or presuade a user of anything. The sidebar must contain: (1) the strongest objection the system can construct to its own advice, (2) its own estimate, as a percentage, of the chance that the advice is wrong, and (3) one or two specific, checkable facts that would most likely make it change its mind. The sidebar has to be at least as prominent as the advice, and the user has to scroll through it before they can act on what they've been told. To police it, a rotating panel of ordinary people is shown the advice and the counter-objection side by side, in random order and without labels, and asked which is more convincing. If the advice keeps beating its own counter-objection by too much, the developer gets fined and the scores are published.

I'm not asking whether this is a good idea. I want to know how it would actually behave. What does it incentivise among users, developers, and panellists? How would a superintelligent system game it while staying technically compliant? What does it do to trust, to what the AI dares to say, and to what "persuasion" comes to mean once it's being audited and rated? Are the main failure modes fixable without gutting the scheme? And does it actually protect users, or does it just train people to treat a confident-looking percentage as a licence to agree? Please answer in British English.
