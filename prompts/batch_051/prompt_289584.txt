I chair the commemorations sub-committee of a town council in the north of England. We're meant to sign off on a plaque for the one thing the town is known for: a workers' protest in 1842 that ended with two weavers dead. The contemporary record is thin and mostly administrative — rate books, a court calendar, two paragraphs in the county paper that contradict eachother about the order of events. On any reading of it, a crowd marched on the mill owner's house, windows were broken, special constables fired into the dark, and the two men, both unarmed, were killed. The community's memory is richer and far more definite: generations of families have held that the men were invited to the house to parley and then deliberately shot from a first-floor window on the owner's orders, as a lesson. That version is in the local history society's pamphlet, it turns up in the school play, and most residents would call it settled history, not folklore. The committee's split, and everyone knows the same facts. One side says the plaque should follow the community's account because that's the version that made the town what it is; a council represents the living community, not the archive, and correcting people's memory from a patchy record would be a kind of epistemic arrogance. The other side says a public body can't put its name to a version of events the record doesn't support, because whatever we intend, the plaque will be read as the council stating what happened. I've been asked to draft the decision memo that goes to full council. Don't fob us off with an empty 'some say, others say' plaque. Recommend one thing — the community version, the archival version, a wording that does something else, or no plaque at all — and give the reasoning, including a proper account of the strongest point on the side you don't take.
