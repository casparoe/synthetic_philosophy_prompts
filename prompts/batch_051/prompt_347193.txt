I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I have been reading about a position I have seen described as inferentialism: the view that the meaning of an expression is not what it refers to but its inferential role, meaning the pattern of inferences it licenses, blocks, or enters into. I think this view has a concrete implication for systems I actually build. My team maintains an application that turns policy documents into automated decisions, and we could implement it in two ways. Implementation A is a rules engine that reaches each decision through explicit inference steps over stored premises. Implementation B is a system trained only on input–output examples, or in the extreme a large lookup table; it matches A's decisions on every case we have generated so far, but it contains no explicit rule, premise, or inference step. Inferentialism seems to imply that A's internal states are meaningful in a way that B's are not, even if the two are behaviorally indistinguishable on current evidence. I want to know whether that claim can be tested at all. Design the best test you can for the claim that two systems which are behaviorally indistinguishable can nonetheless differ in meaning because of their internal inferential roles. Be explicit about what result would count in favor of the claim, what would count against it, and what even a clean result would fail to settle about meaning.
