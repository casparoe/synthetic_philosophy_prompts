Two of my mates got into a long argument after a cosmology lecture about the Fermi paradox, and I've been turning it over in my head ever since. The trigger was a claim one of them had read in a popular article: that the very fact we exist should push us towards expecting the universe to contain vastly many conscious beings. Neither of them knew the technical labels (I'm fairly sure the debate has names), so the whole thing was argued out in plain language.

Priya kicked it off by saying this is just Bayesian hygiene. If one theory says there are trillions of observers and another says only billions, then the specific evidence "there exists an observer with my exact experiences" is more probable under the trillions theory, because there are far more chances for such an observer to arise. Dan replied that this was confused on two counts. First, once we already know we exist, our existence is a datum both theories had to accommodate — you can't treat it as a surprising observation that discriminates between them. Second, he set up a thought experiment: suppose two theories agree on everything you could ever directly observe, except that one says the universe contains 10^11 conscious beings and the other says 10^20, with the extra ones scattered beyond any possible contact. If Priya's principle were right, we'd be forced to be astronomically confident in the bigger theory while admitting we have no direct evidence for all those extra beings — and that, he said, is a reductio. Priya shot back that his thought experiment smuggles in the assumption that our evidence is not more likely under the bigger-number theory, which is exactly the point in question, so as a reductio it begs the question. Dan then switched tack: if you think of yourself as a random draw from all conscious beings, the bigger-number theory is worse, not better, because it makes it wildly improbable that the draw should be a 21st-century human on Earth rather than one of the trillions of aliens or posthumans. Priya replied that this random-draw move only works if you count aliens and posthumans as possible versions of yourself, which is a choice; you could equally gerrymander a reference class containing only people exactly like you, in which case both theories come out tied. And in fact, she added, Dan's random-draw reasoning gets even worse once you shrink the reference class enough: a theory saying that exactly one conscious being exists, and that it has your precise experiences, would be maximally confirmed, which is absurd. Dan said her own move has the same problem in the other direction — if more observers is always evidence, then a theory with infinitely many observers would beat any finite theory, so the principle can't be right as a general rule. At that point they had to run for their bus.
