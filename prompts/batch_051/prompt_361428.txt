I'm in a population ethics course right now, and last week we talked about lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. The idea, roughly, is that reducing suffering matters more than producing happiness, and that once a person's suffering crosses a certain threshold of intensity, it becomes incommensurable with ordinary well-being: no amount of mild pleasure or smaller pain avoided for other people can justify allowing or causing even one extra case above the threshold. A government guided by this would try to get everyone below the threshold first, and only then trade off everything else. I can't stop thinking about what would happen if an actual government tried to run itself on this.

So here's the counterfactual I want you to reason through. Suppose that in 2030 a rich mid-sized country passes a law called the Suffering Baseline Act that makes this into a hard fiscal and regulatory test. No regulation may be adopted if it is expected to create even one additional case of extreme suffering — defined roughly as severe, poorly controlled pain or distress expected to last more than a few weeks — whatever it's other benefits. And until domestic extreme suffering is pushed as low as the best available interventions can manage, the government cannot fund any program whose principal effect is to add positive well-being rather than reduce or prevent suffering. Predict what this country looks like around 2050. Which policy areas get massive budgets and which get hollowed out? What happens to mental health care, palliative care, animal welfare, and the criminal justice system? How do politicians, hospitals, insurers, and patient groups try to redefine the threshold or game the test? And does the rule end up delivering something close to what the negative utilitarians actually wanted, or does it collapse under definitional fights and unintended incentives? I'm less interested in whether you think the law is a good idea than in the causal chain, so try to be explicit about which predictions your confident in, which are guesses, and what would have to be true for the prediction to fail.
