I keep hearing the same argument in AI safety circles, and I want to know how much of it I should actually believe. The argument goes: doing alignment work well imposes an 'alignment tax' (extra cost, slower progress, or worse performance), and because there's a 'competitiveness constraint' (a lab that skips that tax can undercut or outrace the ones that pay it), safe AI is economically unstable — the careful actors get outcompeted, so the default outcome is rushed or misaligned systems. What's your actual credence in that conclusion, stated precisely: in a multi-actor race to powerful AI, competitive pressures make the systematically safety-investing actor unable to survive economically, so the 'market' outcome trends away from alignment? I don't want 'it depends on incentives.' I want a number and the decomposition. Terms I'd like credences on include: whether safety work is mostly a pure cost/externality or mostly pays for itself (reliability, avoiding accidents, recruiting, regulatory moat); whether buyers and partners can even observe or reward alignment work; whether the race is better modelled as a prisoners' dilemma or as something with enough state, insurance, or internal governance to change the payoffs; and whether 'losing the race' even matters, given that a slower careful lab's systems might still be deployed once capable. Then tell me which of those sub-claims your final number is most sensitive to, and what evidence would shift it most. And please don't present one side of the alignment tax debate as the settled consensus — mark which parts are actually empirically supported and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this.
