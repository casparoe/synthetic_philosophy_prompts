Construct a thought experiment in which prototype theory and exemplar theory classify the same novel object under the same everyday concept, while the theory theory classifies it under a different, mutually exclusive everyday concept. For the task, assume the standard versions of the three positions: prototype theory classifies by similarity to a stored central tendency for each category; exemplar theory classifies by similarity to stored individual examples; the theory theory allows causal-explanatory beliefs—about origin, internal structure, intended function, or socially conferred status—to override brute similarity. The object must be ordinary, something a person could encounter under realistic circumstances, not a science-fictional or surgically transformed creature, and the case should be newly constructed rather than a rewrite of a published experiment.

Specify the prior experience all three accounts draw on in enough detail to check their verdicts, and give every account the same information: neither similarity-based account may be handed extra exemplars, a better memory, or features the others cannot access. The point of the case is to isolate what the theory theory adds to binary classification by getting two similarity-based accounts to converge on one answer while a theory-based account, using the same evidence, pulls to the other—without any confound from information asymmetry.

After constructing the scenario, do each of the following. (a) Show step by step why prototype theory and exemplar theory both return category K, and identify at least one respect in which their verdicts, though identical at the binary level, are computed by different routes—a typicality rating, a diagnostic feature, or a sensitivity to a particular stored instance on which the two accounts diverge. (b) Show why the theory theory returns category L, identify the causal-explanatory property in the story that does the work, and explain why that move is not merely a disguised similarity judgment. (c) State which structural feature of the case produces the split, and say what would happen if that one feature were removed: would the case collapse into an ordinary similarity judgment on which all three accounts agree, or would it collapse in some other way? (d) Give the strongest objection a defender of the similarity-based accounts would raise against the case—for example, that the object is not really a K, that the two similarity-based accounts have been given an impoverished feature set, or that the theory theory's causal property is question-begging—and say how persuasive the objection is. If no case can meet the specifications as stated, give the minimal weakening that makes them jointly satisfiable and state why it is needed.
