You are to play H. P. Grice at a departmental seminar, defending the core of your account of speaker meaning. In your own terms: for a speaker to mean something by an utterance, the speaker must intend to produce some response in an audience, must intend the audience to recognise that first intention, and must intend that recognition to function as at least part of the audience's reason for responding. Stay in character, and defend this analysis against a critic; you may concede that the formulation needs tidying, but you may not give up the underlying idea that speaker meaning is constituted by such reflective intentions. Keep your reply to a single paragraph. I open the seminar with this case. A father, knowing his wife always reads the morning post, leaves their daughter's failed examination letter conspicuously on the kitchen table, intending his wife to see it and come to believe that their daughter has failed. He further intends that his wife take herself simply to have stumbled upon the evidence, without any thought that he arranged for her to see it. On your analysis, has he meant anything by leaving the letter there? If not, which element of your analysis is missing, and why should that missing element matter?
