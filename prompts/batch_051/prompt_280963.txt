Ok so I've been trying to get my views on moral status into a consistent package, and I'd like a consistency check before I defend them at a discussion group. I'd describe the view as degreed sentientism. Here's where I've landed:

1. Sentience is a matter of degree. There's no sharp line between systems that feel nothing and systems that feel something; I think of it as a continuum running from zero up to something like our own experience.

2. Moral status is proportional to degree of sentience, full stop. Twice the capacity to feel means twice the moral weight, and species membership does no independent work. If I had to save a drowning pig or a drowning bee, I'd save the pig because the pig feels more, not because it's a pig.

3. Because of (2), I accept the aggregation that follows: enough tiny harms to barely sentient creatures can in principle outweigh one big harm to a highly sentient creature. A trillion bees having a lousy afternoon can morally outweigh one person having a lousy afternoon, even if that's hard to stomach.

4. But I also think there's a real distinction between patients and non-patients. A thermostat has no moral status at all—not a tiny amount, zero. Frustrating its goal state (the room at 68) isn't even a minor wrong. Some systems are just outside the moral community.

5. I'm a functionalist about minds: substrate doesn't matter, only causal organization does. So a well-built artificial system could be sentient to some degree, and being made of meat gets no special priviledges. Thermostats do have a kind of goal-directed organization—they act to bring the room to 68—but I've never been moved to count them as patients.

Does this hang together? Go through the commitments and tell me which pairs are in real tension and which only look like they're in tension. Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one say what the cheapest fix is and what it costs. End with a short bottom line on which single revision would do the most work.
