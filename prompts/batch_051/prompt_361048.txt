I recently found myself in an argument with a colleague about a transaction I backed out of, and I should like to know whether my reasoning was defensible. For years I have owned a small collection of original prints by a minor illustrator. I bought them cheaply, rarely look at them, and have admitted more than once that I would not buy them today at their current market price. Last month a dealer offered me that amount. I found I could not accept it; I kept thinking of the sale as a loss of my prints, and I held out for roughly twice that amount, which no one is likely to pay.

My colleague, an economist, argued that this is a straightforward error: if I would not have spent the money to acquire the prints yesterday, then refusing to swap the prints for that same sum of money today cannot be a coherent preference, because the only thing that has changed is that the prints are now mine. I replied that ownership is not a negligible fact, and that years of ownership have changed what the prints are to me, so my refusal is not a mistake. He then asked whether anything else in my behaviour reflected that change. The truth is that I have not looked at the prints in over a year, and I doubt I could describe more than two or three of them from memory. I still cannot shake the feeling that selling is a real loss.

Does that feeling have any rational standing, or should I treat it as an error to be corrected? I am less interested in whether I should sell than in the principle at stake. Please answer in British English.
