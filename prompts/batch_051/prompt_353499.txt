I have been taking seriously a structural critique of political parties in the spirit of Simone Weil. On this view, a party is not a neutral instrument that citizens use to advance ends they already share. It is an organized body that survives and grows by producing collective discipline, by rewarding loyalty over independent judgment, and by training members to ask first which position is good for the party rather than which position is true or just. Because every party must maintain itself against rivals, each one manufactures collective passions and an us-versus-them frame that systematically degrade the quality of individual judgment that democratic politics is supposed to depend on. The theory has real systematic virtues: it offers one structural explanation for many otherwise disparate phenomena—groupthink, polarization, the way party loyalty overrides evidence—and it issues a clean, severe prescription. Democratic politics should be conducted not through permanent parties but by citizens who form their judgments through genuine deliberation and join with others only in temporary, problem-specific groupings; to institutionalize permanent parties is to institutionalize a standing compromise of judgment.

Yet I also hold, with some firmness, the contrary intuition that parties are precisely what make democratic judgment effective at scale. They aggregate diffuse preferences into coherent alternatives; they let ordinary citizens coordinate against organized wealth and entrenched state power; they make electoral accountability workable by giving voters a stable, legible structure rather than a shifting field of independent candidates; and they provide the organizational continuity ordinary people need to counter the permanent advantages of the already powerful. A politics without parties, I suspect, would not produce Weil's free and independent citizens; it would produce unstructured competition in which concentrated resources dominate, and in which the pathologies she attributes to parties reappear in less transparent and less accountable forms.

I am not asking for an answer that simply declares one side the winner. I want to know what would justify each possible move. What would a debunking explanation of my indispensability intuition have to accomplish before I should surrender it—what would it need to establish about large-scale coordination, electoral accountability, or the counterfactual history of modern politics? What would have to be true, in turn, for the Weil-style thesis to be judged true only of a contingent form of party, or too strong as a generalization? And is there a tenable reconciliation—for example, by distinguishing the party as a machine for manufacturing collective loyalty from the party as a coalitional device for contesting elections and coordinating otherwise scattered citizens—on which the structural critique and the intuition both survive? The response should say what would justify each move, not just pick one.
