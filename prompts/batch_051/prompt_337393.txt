I'm in a philosophy of science seminar and a friend who's been reading Goodman has me tied in knots. After class he scribbled this argument on the whiteboard, and I keep rerunning it. The conclusion seems to be that induction has no objective content, which I can't accept, but I can't find the step doing the damage.

Here's the argument as he wrote it:

(1) If a large and varied sample of Fs has all been observed to be G, and no counterexample has turned up despite a lot of searching, then that evidence supports the generalization "all Fs are G."

(2) Fix a future cutoff, say the year 2100. Define "grue" as follows: an object is grue just in case it is first examined before 2100 and is green, or it is first examined on or after January 1, 2100 and is blue.

(3) Every emerald examined so far has been green. Since all of those emeralds were first examined before 2100, every emerald examined so far has also been grue.

(4) So, by (1), the very same evidence that supports "all emeralds are green" supports "all emeralds are grue" to exactly the same degree.

(5) But these two generalizations make conflicting predictions: "all emeralds are green" says an emerald first mined and examined on, say, July 1, 2101 will be green, while "all emeralds are grue" says it will be blue.

(6) A rule that liscenses two conflicting predictions from the same evidence gives no real guidance about what to expect. So induction, as stated in (1), is not a rational guide to the unobserved.

(7) But induction is a rational guide to the unobserved—at the very least, it would be crazy to say the next emerald is no more likely green than blue. So something in (1)–(6) has to give.

I'd most like to hang onto (7), but if I reject (1) I don't know how to restate induction without just brute-listing which predicates are okay and which are somehow time-gerrymandered, and that feels circular. So: which step should I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost?
