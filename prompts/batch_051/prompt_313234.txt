I am not an academic, but I have spent a long time trying to state the golden rule in a form that does not break under pressure, and I have ended up with an argument that this cannot be done. I would like you to check the argument step by step. Either it is valid, in which case I should accept the conclusion, or it fails at a particular step; I want to know exactly which step and on what reading of it.

The argument is this.

(1) In its usual form, the golden rule says that I should treat others as I would want to be treated if I were in their position.

(2) To follow the rule, I have to imagine what I would want in the other person's position. But there are only two things I can mean by that: I can imagine keeping my own present preferences while standing in their circumstances, or I can imagine adopting their preferences along with their circumstances.

(3) If I keep my own preferences, the rule tells me to act on preferences the other person may not share. Whenever our preferences diverge, it recommends, from their point of view, the wrong treatment: the person who would welcome blunt criticism is told to be blunt with someone who would be hurt by it, and the person who values a harsh penance is told to impose one on someone who does not.

(4) If I adopt their preferences, then what I would want in their position is simply what they in fact want, and the rule collapses into a different rule: do what the other person wants to be done. That rule is not a harmless restatement; it gives obviously bad guidance when people want what is destructive, servile, or cruel.

(5) There is no third alternative. Any third alternative would have to be some mixture of my preferences and theirs, and a mixture only inherits the defects of both ingredients.

(6) Therefore the golden rule either prescribes what the other person does not want, or it stops being the golden rule and becomes the separate rule of doing what the other person wants. On the first horn it is wrong; on the second it is no longer the rule I set out to defend. So the golden rule cannot be both correct and do any independent work as a moral rule.

I half-suspect the weak point is step (5), but I cannot see what a third alternative would actually be, and if there is one I do not know whether it leaves the golden rule as I originally understood it. Is the argument valid? If not, identify the exact step that fails and state the corrected version of it; if it is valid, say whether the conclusion really disposes of the golden rule or only of one crude reading of it.
