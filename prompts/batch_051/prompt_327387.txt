You are a philosopher of language with a good working knowledge of historical linguistics; answer as if you were leading a seminar, and take time to think before you commit. I am a retired natural scientist — biology was my old field — and since retiring I have been reading about language change. The claim I cannot get past is that Italian descends from Latin. Descent itself is not the problem: a continuous chain of learners, each transmitting a slightly modified system, is something I understand. What I do not understand is where along that chain Latin stops and Italian starts, any more than continuous reproduction by itself tells me where one species ends and the next begins. I have been collecting the standard answers people give to the question "when did Latin become Italian?", and my list so far is this: (1) the mutual-intelligibility criterion, which places the boundary at the first generation that could no longer hold a conversation with a speaker of the classical language; (2) the documentary criterion, which dates Italian from the first texts deliberately written in the vernacular rather than in Latin — the tenth-century placiti preserved at Monte Cassino are the usual exhibit; (3) the grammatical criterion, which places the boundary when the case system had effectively given way to prepositional constructions and demonstratives had finished becoming articles; (4) the phonological criterion, which places it when cumulative sound changes, above all the loss of distinctive vowel length and the palatalization of velars before front vowels, had so altered the system that its inventory of contrasts could no longer be mapped item by item onto Latin; (5) the social-identity criterion, according to which Italian begins when a speech community takes itself to be speaking Italian rather than Latin — the old joke about a language being a dialect with an army and a navy belongs here; and (6) the literary-standard criterion, which dates Italian from the emergence of a self-conscious written norm and an explicit debate about which variety should fix it, from Dante's effort to identify an illustrious vernacular through the later questione della lingua.

My worry is that all six are, in the end, different ways of selecting a point on a single gradient, and that they differ only in where they cut and in what kind of evidence they consult. I want a criterion with a different logical structure rather than a different location. So: give me one further criterion that is not on this list, and that is not a rewording, a special case, or a combination of items already listed. It may be borrowed from another discipline — I keep suspecting biology has useful analogues, though not the simple ones — provided it does genuine work. Before committing, brainstorm two or three candidate criteria; then argue for the strongest one, say exactly what it captures that the six miss, and show how it would classify some disputed historical case differently from them. End by telling me what would most likely change your mind about your proposed criterion: which counterexample, empirical fact about how transmission actually works, or argument that it collapses back into one of the six strikes you as the likeliest point of failure.
