I spent four decades as a research chemist and have only recently begun reading philosophy seriously, which means I bring a laboratory temperament to arguments: I want to know what each side would have to observe before it changed its mind. The debate that has most intrigued me is the one between neo-Aristotelian virtue ethicists and situationist critics of virtue ethics. The virtue ethicist holds that character traits such as honesty, courage, and compassion are stable dispositions that explain much of what people do and are the proper target of moral evaluation and moral education. The situationist holds that the best social psychology — the Good Samaritan study, the phone-booth helping study, Milgram's obedience experiments, and the early schoolchildren studies of honesty — shows that behavior tracks features of situations far more reliably than any global trait, and that virtue ethics therefore rests on an empirically inadequate moral psychology.

Please draft the joint statement that a careful neo-Aristotelian virtue ethicist and a careful situationist critic, trained as a social psychologist, could both sign after a year of disciplined adversarial collaboration. The document should have two parts.

First, a numbered set of agreed claims. These should be substantive enough that each side regards signing them as conceding real ground, not trivial enough to be costless. They should cover at least: what a virtue would have to be like as a psychological disposition; what the classic experiments establish, and what they leave open; and the conditions under which an empirical result would count against a normatively freighted trait concept.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should contain: (a) the question at issue; (b) the virtue ethicist's position, worded so that a knowledgeable virtue ethicist would accept it as their own; (c) the situationist's position, worded so that a knowledgeable situationist would accept it as their own; and (d) whether the row is a genuine crux or a merely verbal dispute, briefly justified. For every genuine crux, the row should also state a concrete test, comparison, or observational design that both sides agree bears on the crux, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

The document should not glide over the following technical questions; it should force the collaboration to take a stand on each:

- Whether the real dispute concerns the existence of stable behavioral dispositions, the breadth of such dispositions, or their explanatory priority relative to situation features.
- The local-traits proposal — courage on the battlefield but not in committee rooms, honesty at work but not in examinations — and whether accepting such traits is a friendly amendment to virtue ethics or an abandonment of the strong notion of virtue.
- The aggregation problem: whether consistency improves when behavior is aggregated across many acts, occasions, and observers rather than correlated act-by-act, and what pattern of results would count as vindication for each side.
- The asymmetry between negative and positive evidence about a trait: it is comparatively easy to find a situational variable that overrides honest or compassionate behavior, but much harder to specify in advance which situations are trait-relevant and what counts as expressing a trait through, rather than in the absence of, situational pressure.
- An update clause: for at least two of the cruxes, each party should state in advance the minimum result that would force a nontrivial revision of its view, and what that revision would be.

Do not include a bibliography, reading list, footnotes, or citations. Assume the two parties already share an account of the relevant experiments; the task is to extract and refine what they can and cannot jointly assert. Keep the prose precise and low in rhetoric, in the register of a statement both parties would put their names to.
