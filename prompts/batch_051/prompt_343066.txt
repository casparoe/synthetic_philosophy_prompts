I've been arguing with a roommate who's in a linguistics course over what a grammatical rule actually is, and I've landed on a pretty deflationary view: a rule like "form the English past tense by adding -ed" is just a compressed description of a regularity, plus a mental list of the words that happen not to follow it. On this picture, the regular/irregular distinction isn't a difference in kind — both "walked" and "went" are things you know because you've stored them — and suppletion (go/went, good/better) is simply the limiting case where there's no structural resemblance left for the "rule" to summarize. I think sound change backs me up here: a change like the Great Vowel Shift applies across the board to words that happened to have the relevant vowel, but the change is blind to grammar, and speakers still have to learn word by word which items have which vowels. The "rule" is a summary, not a piece of mental machinery.

I'm sure I'm setting myself up to be flattened by someone who knows more morphology than I do, so I want a serious good-faith case against me. If I'm wrong, where does it show up first? I'd like a ranked list of what would count against the deflationary story, from evidence that's merely awkward to evidence that should make me abandon it. Where does my view make predictions a rule-realist view doesn't, and where does it just re-describe the data after the fact? I'm particularly interested in which of the usual suspects — wug-style productivity tests, overregularization in kids (goed, foots), blocking effects, the way novel or shifted uses of irregular words regularize (computer mouses, the Toronto Maple Leafs), neuro cases where one system is selectively damaged — a committed deflationist would concede as real problems, and which they could resonably wave away as theory-laden or model-dependent. Are there versions of this view that have become unfalsifiable, and would that be a genuine problem for a claim about mental representation, or not?
