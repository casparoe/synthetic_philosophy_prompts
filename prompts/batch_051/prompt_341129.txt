I've been working through the two-factor account of monothematic delusions, and I've reached a point where my gears are spinning without traction. On that account, a delusion such as Capgras — the fixed conviction that one's spouse has been replaced by an identical impostor — is produced by two stacked failures. The first factor supplies an anomalous experience: the spouse's face is recognized visually but carries none of the usual affective sense of familiarity, so the face presents itself as a stranger wearing the spouse's appearance. The second factor is meant to be a deficit in belief evaluation, which keeps the person from rejecting the bizarre impostor explanation the way you or I would if the same feeling washed over us. If that is right, the account already sounds like a story about a broken belief machine. But then I lose my grip on the debate over whether delusions are beliefs at all: if one factor is literally a damaged belief-evaluation system, haven't we already assumed the output is a belief — just a badly formed one? Whereas if the output is an imagining, an avowal, or something that floats free of the rest of cognition, then the second factor is not a belief-evaluation deficit, and we owe a different story about what it damages.

What I'd like is a working definition of "belief" — a criterion or set of criteria I could apply to a single case without already knowing the diagnosis — that classifies the following cases correctly, or at least returns definite verdicts and tells me which ones are forced. Here are the cases with my shaky priors. (1) I glance at the table and come to hold that my keys are on it; this is clearly a belief. (2) A driver has the intrusive thought "I may have hit someone," recognizes it as baseless, but circles back to check the road anyway; this is clearly not a belief. (3) A child at play says the floor is lava and jumps onto cushions; clearly not a belief but pretense. (4) A visitor on a glass observation deck fully endorses that the deck is safe, but their body refuses to cooperate: they clutch the rail, walk gingerly, and feel the drop in their stomach; I lean toward saying only the endorsed judgment is a belief, but the bodily state and the judgment are in genuine conflict. (5) A person with Capgras says unprompted that their spouse has been replaced by an impostor, yet continues living with the spouse, sharing meals, and never calls the police or tries to find the "real" spouse; I lean toward saying this is not a belief, or not fully. (6) A person with Cotard delusion says they are dead, and their behavior partly coheres: they refer to their body in the past tense, refuse food, and begin planning their own funeral; this looks more belief-like than (5), even though Capgras and Cotard are supposed to share the same two-factor structure. (7) A person says there is a 70 percent chance of rain, then goes out without an umbrella; pressed, they say "I do think rain is more likely than not, I just don't care enough about getting wet to carry one." This looks like a belief that simply fails to guide action. (8) A person raised in a religious tradition avows a doctrine and can produce it when asked, but it has never guided their choices, and they seem confused by the question whether they "really believe" it; I'd call this an in-between case.

Propose the definition, run it through all eight, and tell me where it surprises you. If the first pass returns the wrong answer or no answer on a case, revise the definition, or else defend the surprising verdict as forced and say why the case was misdescribed. Then use the result to say whether the two-factor account should count delusions as beliefs, or whether that question was badly posed from the start. I'm especially interested in whether your definition handles (5) and (6) differently, and whether that difference should survive.
