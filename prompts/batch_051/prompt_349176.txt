In my third-year module on the foundations of human rights I keep hitting the same wall, year after year. We spend two seminars on the idea that dignity grounds rights, and the argument between the deflationists and the defenders of dignity never moves. The deflationary camp follows Ruth Macklin: strip dignity down to what it actually does and it simply amounts to respect for autonomy, so it adds nothing to the moral toolkit; calling something a violation of dignity is a way of sounding portentous while giving no usable criterion. The other camp, drawing on the Kantian idea that dignity attaches to the capacity to set ends and govern oneself, says this is a straw man: dignity was never meant to be a decision procedure. It names the standing that makes a being a rights-bearer at all, and it explains why some rights cannot be waived — the wrong of being tortured or sold isn't cancelled by the victim's consent. Both sides agree on the paradigm cases and object to the same practices. So the disagreement is purely over what the concept is doing, and after a while the seminar just chases its tail.

I've come to suspect the stall isn't a normal first-order moral disagreement at all, but I can't work out what it is. Sometimes it looks verbal: the two sides use 'dignity' for different things — one means a capacity, another a status, another an expressive norm about not humiliating people — so of course one side can show the concept is doing no work for their referent while the other insists it is doing indispensible work for theirs. Sometimes it looks like a dispute about what foundational concepts are for: the deflationist demands that a normative term earn its keep by deciding hard cases, and the defender of dignity thinks that is like demanding that the concept of mass tell you where a particular stone will fall. The assisted dying debate makes it worse, because both sides claim dignity — one says choosing the time of your death protects it, the other says the sanctity of life is an expression of it — which is either proof of vacuity or proof that we have fused several different ideas. My hypothesis is that the real disagreement is about whether a ground-level concept needs to be operationalisable to be legitimate, but I'd like that tested. What kind of disagreement is this, actually: verbal, meta-theoretical, or bedrock normative? And is there any development — a distinction, a well-chosen case, a reformulation — that would genuinely move it, or should we expect it to stay stuck?
