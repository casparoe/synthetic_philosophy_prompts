I have been trying to determine whether qualia — private, intrinsic, introspectable properties of experience — are a coherent posit or a piece of philosophical confusion. I have verdicts on a set of cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them.

Consider two coffee tasters, both with decades of experience, who now agree that the house blend is no longer satisfactory. The first says the coffee tastes exactly as it always has; what has changed is his liking for it — the same taste simply no longer moves him. The second says his liking is exactly what it always was; what has changed is the taste the coffee presents to him. My verdicts are that the first report concerns a change in preference rather than in experienced quality, while the second is about a change in experienced quality rather than in preference, and I find myself trusting both reports.

Now consider two people whose red-green experiences are inverted relative to each other while their shared discrimination and color vocabulary remain exactly the same. I find it intelligible that there is a fact of the matter about whether the inversion obtains, even though no third-person test could reveal it. But if the inversion is said to have happened last Tuesday while they slept, and neither noticed, I cannot find a determinate fact about when it happened, or even about whether any discrete shift occurred. These two verdicts about inversion sit uneasily beside each other, and beside the coffee-taster pair.

Next, blindsight. A subject denies seeing anything in the blind field yet guesses above chance about the location and orientation of shapes. I take him at his word: he lacks conscious visual experience, and his denial is evidence rather than just one more piece of output. Now imagine a super-blindsight case: training has made the subject's "guesses" fluent, spontaneous, and confidently reported, though he continues to insist they are not experiences. I still withhold visual qualia from him, no matter how fluent his reports become. Yet I worry that this last verdict is in tension with my readiness to trust the second coffee taster's report of a changed taste.

The principle I keep reaching for is this: a sincere report commits us to an experiential quality just when the person presents the state as experiential rather than as a preference, judgment, or bare guess. But that seems to make the difference between the cases turn on the word "experience" as it appears in the report, and I suspect I am doing lexicography rather than metaphysics.

Please state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts. Show which case motivates each clause, apply the principle to a further case of your choosing — perhaps a machine that reports pain and winces but was designed without anything corresponding to pain — and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the resulting view requires me to give up. If you think the request itself rests on a confusion, for example that the very posit of qualia is what the cases are undermining, say so directly rather than forcing a principle out of material that resists one. Write your answer in American English.
