I've been in a small forecasting group for a couple years now—Metaculus-style questions, scored with log score—and I've settled into a policy I'm starting to worry is just a bias wearing a statistics costume. I never report a credence below 0.05 or above 0.95, no matther what the question is, and I've extended this to everyday life: I won't say I'm 100% sure of anything, even whether the sun will rise tomorrow, and I privately wince when people give 99.9% predictions. My reasoning comes from accuracy-first epistemology. If the only thing that ultimately matters for belief is inaccuracy, and I'm being evaluated with a log score, then getting a 1 or a 0 wrong carries an infinite penalty. Since no finite amount of evidence can rule out my being wrong, capping at 5% and 95% seemed like the strictly safer move: I can never be infinitely wrong. My friend says I've misunderstood the relevant theorem. As she puts it, a stricly proper scoring rule doesn't rank one agent against another like that; it makes your own current credence the unique thing that maximizes expected accuracy from your own perspective. So if I actually judge something at 99%, reporting 95% knowingly expects to do worse by my own lights, and 'I'd rather not be infinitely wrong if I'm wrong' doesn't fix that expectation.

I'm not sure she's right, because I also have genuine uncertainty about my own calibration. I know I get overconfident after reading one-sided arguments, so the credence that feels like 99% might not be trustworthy enough to report. So the question I actually want answered is: is the 5–95 cap defensible as a standing epistemic policy, or is it a bias dressed up as a rule? Under what assumptions about my metacognitive situation—miscalibration, overconfidence, group scoring incentives—would it be a reasonable hedge rather than a failure to maximize expected accuracy? Is there a restated version, something like report your true credence but shrink toward 0.5 only when you have independent evidence of miscalibration, that keeps the benefit without expecting to do worse by your own lights? And if the whole framing is confused—if I'm misusing what a proper scoring rule actually licenses—just say that plainly, and tell me where you're genuinely unsure rather than pretending the formalism settles everything. I'd rather have the premise corrected than get a polite answer on its own terms.
