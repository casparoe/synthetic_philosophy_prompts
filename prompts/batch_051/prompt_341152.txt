Consider a group of two dozen members who maintain a widely used open-source project whose tools can readily be repurposed for harmful ends. Any member may currently publish a new tool, dataset, or model component without consulting the others. The result is that publication tends to be triggered by the most optimistic member: a member who privately judges a release to be only slightly more beneficial than harmful will eventually go ahead, whereas the group, deliberating under its ordinary decision procedure, would demand a much stronger case and would very likely refuse. This is the structure of the unilateralist's curse, in which any individual can act but the consequences fall on everyone.

The group adopts the following rule to govern irreversible releases. A member who wishes to release something must first lodge a refundable bond, post a written justification of no more than one page, and allow fourteen days to pass. The bond is set at 5 per cent of the initiator's own estimate of the release's reasonable worst-case cost to third parties and to the group, subject to a floor and ceiling fixed annually by the group. During the waiting period, any other member may block the release by lodging a bond of the same size and posting a written rebuttal of no more than one page. If a block is lodged, no release occurs, and both bonds are held for one year. At the end of that year the full membership votes on a counterfactual question: whether, on the evidence available during the waiting period, the release would have been authorised under the group's ordinary decision procedure. If the vote is yes, the blocker's bond is forfeited to the initiator and the initiator's own bond is returned; if the vote is no, the initiator's bond is forfeited to the blocker and the blocker's own bond is returned; if the vote is tied or cannot be called either way, both bonds are returned. If no block is lodged, the initiator may release after the waiting period, and the bond is returned once the release is made. A block need not allege that the release would be harmful; it is enough for the blocker to hold that the group's authorisation standard would not have been met.

Analyse how this rule would actually play out. Who would initiate, who would block, and on what grounds? What does the bond do to incentives to gather and disclose information, and where is the rule likely to be gamed first — strategic sizing of the stated worst-case cost, coordinated or nuisance blocking, differences in liquidity or risk tolerance, lobbying ahead of the year-later vote, or something else? Does the rule correct the central bias of the unilateralist's curse, or does it mainly replace an over-action bias with a new bargaining game? Which changes to the parameters, such as the bond rate, the floor and ceiling, the waiting period, or the timing and composition of the vote, would improve it, and is there a cheaper or simpler amendment that preserves whatever the rule gets right?
