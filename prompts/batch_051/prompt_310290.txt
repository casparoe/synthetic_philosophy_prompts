I'm a longtime reader of adversarial-collaboration writeups, and I want one on a debate in philosophy of biology where the two sides have been arguing past each other for decades. Write the joint statement that a committed defender of the propensity interpretation of fitness and a committed statisticalist about natural selection could both sign after a good-faith, year-long adversarial collaboration. Frame the document around the tautology objection: the charge that the claim that the fittest organisms leave the most offspring is empty because fitness is defined simply as actual reproductive output. Both parties in this collaboration agree that the objection, taken as an objection to modern evolutionary theory, fails. They disagree about which revision of the fitness concept does the real work and about what kind of quantity fitness is.

The deliverable should have four parts.

First, a numbered list of agreed claims. These must be non-vacuous, and they must not merely restate each side's conclusion in softer language. They should cover at least: what exactly the tautology objection gets wrong; why fitness cannot be identified with actual reproductive success even though actual success is often the best available estimate; what drift and selection are at the population level, stated so that both sides can sign the same sentences; and the minimal adequacy conditions any account of fitness must meet. The agreed claims should also say explicitly whether natural selection ever explains anything, and if so what.

Second, a precise statement of the residual disagreement. Give each side's position in wording its own defenders would accept, and then state the logical relation between the two positions. One side holds that fitness is a probabilistic causal property of individual organisms, a propensity, and that differences in fitness causally explain differences in reproductive success. The other holds that fitness is a population-level statistical parameter, and that selection and drift are not causes but statistical summaries of the same births, deaths, and reproductive events. At some point in this section, taboo the word fitness and state what each side is really quantifying, so the reader can see whether the disagreement survives the taboo. If you judge that the two positions are not clean negations of one another, say exactly what the relation is.

Third, a table of cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, include a concrete case, manipulation, or argument that both sides agree bears on it; each side's advance prediction about what it shows; and a statement of the revision each side would have to make if its prediction failed. Your crux list must engage at least the following three candidates, and you should say for each whether it is a genuine crux or a pseudo-crux:

- The identical-twins case: two organisms with identical genotypes and phenotypes in the same environment, one of which leaves more offspring through no difference between them. What does this show about whether fitness is a property of individual organisms?
- The intervention test: whether a manipulation that changes an organism's fitness while holding the rest of the population fixed would change the expected evolutionary outcome in a way that supports a causal reading of fitness.
- The reference-class problem: the same organism can be assigned different fitness values relative to different reference classes, such as age, sex, environment, or trait. What fixes the reference class, and does this support a statistical reading of fitness?

Fourth, a short diagnosis of which parts of the disagreement, if any, are verbal. If some portion turns out to be a disagreement about which formalism to privilege rather than about the world, identify it as such and say why.

Format the document as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a statement of the disagreement, then a disagreements table with columns for the crux, the case, each side's prediction, and what would change each side's mind.
