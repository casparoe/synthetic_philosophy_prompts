As part of a dissertation chapter on Morgan's canon and the debate over how much cognition we may attribute to nonhuman animals, I'm compiling a taxonomy of objections to the canon's use as a methodological rule. By Morgan's canon I mean the principle that we should interpret an animal activity in terms of the lowest psychological process that can fairly account for the observed behavior, rather than in terms of a higher one. So far I have six objections, which I take to be the standard ones:

1. The scale objection. The canon's instruction to prefer the "lower" process presupposes a ranked scale of psychological evolution or development. But that scale does no independent work and inherits a pre-Darwinian, anthropocentric picture of mental life; the principle is therefore circular when the legitimacy of the ranking is precisely what is contested.

2. The export objection. The canon is a rule for interpreting a particular performance, but it is routinely exported to questions about which capacities a species has at all. A capacity can exist without being expressed in a given task, so the fact that a behavior can be "fairly" accounted for by a lower process does not license the conclusion that the higher capacity is absent.

3. The stopping-rule objection. Designed as a guard against over-attribution, the canon in practice functions as a license to stop inquiring once a low-level account fits the data. It thereby shelters deflationary hypotheses from further tests rather than protecting animals from projection.

4. The asymmetry objection. The canon treats over-attribution of mind as the standing risk and under-attribution as the sober default. That built-in asymmetry is not epistemically neutral; it is a bias in the opposite direction, toward denying cognitive phenomena that the evidence may support.

5. The underdetermination objection. Because the same behavioral pattern can be produced by mechanisms at many different levels, the canon cannot by itself tell us which of the competing interpretations is the fair one. It relocates rather than resolves the problem of selecting a psychological explanation from behavior.

6. The false-parsimony objection. The canon assumes that "lower" along the scale means simpler, better understood, or more economical. But many lower processes, such as associative learning in ecologically realistic settings, are computationally complex and theoretically contested, so the promised parsimony is often illusory.

I have the feeling that the standard objections are now all on the list, and I'm looking for an objection that is not among these six and is not a restatement, special case, or combination of any of them. State that objection as precisely as you can. Then identify which of my six objections it is most likely to be confused with, explain the difference, and give your honest assessment of whether the new objection is a good one and what it adds to the taxonomy.
