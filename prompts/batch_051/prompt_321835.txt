I teach a philosophy of cognitive science seminar, and in three weeks I have to give one seventy-five-minute session on heterophenomenology as a method for the empirical study of consciousness. My students are a mix of philosophy and neuroscience graduate students. The neuroscientists assume that studying consciousness just means looking for neural correlates, and the philosophers suspect that any method built on verbal reports must be either behaviorism or credulity about introspection. I know the method; don't explain it to me. I want a teaching plan for the session.

The working core, as I understand it, is this: the investigator treats subjects' first-person reports (a subject says "I saw a red afterimage," or "the flash seemed to happen before the beep") not as transparent reports of private facts but as speech acts and expressions of belief that together make up a text to be interpreted. The investigator then reconstructs the world as the subject seems to experience it—the heterophenomenological world—while staying neutral about whether the items in that world correspond to anything real inside the subject's head. The advertised payoff is a method that respects the first-person point of view without abandoning third-person method.

Give me:
(1) the order in which the pieces have to be introduced and what each depends on; in particular, which distinction must already be on the table before the idea of treating reports as data but not as transparent descriptions can do any work;
(2) the one carrying example I should build the whole session around, and what it must have and lack if it is to keep the neutrality from collapsing into either side;
(3) the misconceptions my students will form, at the moments in the sequence when they will form them—I expect at least "this is behaviorism with extra steps," "this treats subjects as infallible about their own minds," and "this is a theory of what consciousness is"—and where exactly to head each one off;
(4) what I should openly refuse to establish at this level and why;
(5) one final question that would show me whether a student has grasped the method rather than merely learned the vocabulary.

Open with a single sentence stating the pedagogical principle that orders the whole session, and keep the plan under 650 words.
