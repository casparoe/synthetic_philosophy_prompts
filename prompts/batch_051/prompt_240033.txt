In a passage of De Rerum Natura, Lucretius observes that we are not disturbed by the endless stretch of time before we were born, and he argues that this past nonexistence is nature's mirror of the time that will follow our death; since the former is nothing to us, the latter should be nothing to us as well. His symmetry argument treats the ordinary asymmetry as an inconsistency to be given up. Yet ordinary judgment remains stubbornly asymmetric: most people find the thought of their nonexistence before birth entirely untroubling, while the thought of their nonexistence after death can produce real dread. Is there anything that justifies this asymmetry? Consider at least these candidate grounds: the future, unlike the past, could have contained goods we were deprived of; a life is oriented forward, so future nonexistence deprives us of something we were still in a position to have; and concern for the future is a condition of agency, while concern for the past would be idle. Which, if any, of these grounds is principled enough to vindicate the ordinary asymmetry, and which is special pleading? If you would reject the asymmetry, say which consideration would bring you back to accepting it; if you would keep it, say which consideration would most likely lead you to abandon it, and where your reasoning is most exposed.
