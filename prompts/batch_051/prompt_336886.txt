I have a standing rule about information, and I'm starting to wonder if its just cowardice with a nicer name. If some fact is optional to learn and I already suspect that learning it would put pressure on me to change something I don't want to change, I decline to learn it. I don't check the labor or environmental record of the index fund I inherited, I don't look up whether the new running shoes I love are made in a sweatshop, and last year I skipped the free carrier screening my doctor offered before we started trying for a kid. My logic is that once you know, "I didn't know" stops being an excuse, and then I'm stuck either making a costly change or knowingly doing nothing and feeling like a hypocrite. So I keep the ignorance.

A friend who thinks about decision theory tells me this is basically a violation of Good's theorem: for a rational expected-utility agent, cost-free information can never have negative expected value, so decling it is always irrational. I don't think I'm an expected-utility agent in the way he means, but I also suspect that's just me saying something I don't fully understand. So: is my policy defensible, or is it motivated blindness dressed up as strategy? Which specific assumptions of Good's theorem would have to fail for my rule to actually be rational, and do any of them plausibly fail in the examples I gave — or do different ones fail in different examples? And is there a cleaned-up version of the rule that keeps whatever is genuinely good in it without the rest?
