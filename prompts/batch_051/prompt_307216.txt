I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar in probability and inductive logic, and I want it to force students to locate themselves in the debate over Bertrand's chord paradox rather than merely recite the three classical answers. The standard presentations introduce the random endpoints, random radius, and random midpoint procedures, which assign probabilities of one third, one half, and one quarter respectively to the chord being longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and then leave students with the vague suggestion that the principle of indifference is in trouble. I would like a decision tree, with roughly six to eight leaves, that begins from the question what lesson the paradox actually teaches and routes a reader to a stable position on the principle of indifference.

Please build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of established labels. The branch nodes should be questions that someone who has seen only the three standard procedures could answer without already knowing the taxonomy: for instance, whether a well-posed probability problem requires a specified chance mechanism, whether the circle's rotational and translational symmetries suffice to single out exactly one distribution over chords, whether the residual indeterminacy is epistemic or merely pragmatic, and whether rational agents are required to hold precise credences. The leaves must include the view that the original question is under-specified or ill-posed, the invariance-based objective Bayesian answer, a credal-set or imprecise-probability treatment, and a thoroughgoing subjectivist view on which any of the classical answers can be warranted relative to a prior. Each leaf should carry the name of the position if one exists, or "unnamed" if not, together with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. In addition, identify at least one combination of answers that no one occupies and say briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any two leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate.
