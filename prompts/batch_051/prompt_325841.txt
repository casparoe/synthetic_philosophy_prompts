I spent most of my career in theoretical physics, mostly QFT, and I retired a few years ago; lately I've been working through philosophy of science, and I keep circling back to how much the effective field theory revolution changed what we mean by "a theory of nature." Rereading Steven Weinberg's 2009 talk "Effective Field Theory, Past and Future," I got stuck on a passage about current algebra from before EFT had really been named:

"In this work it was not necessary to assume anything about the nature of the broken symmetry -- only that there was some approximate symmetry responsible for the approximate conservation of the axial vector current and the approximate masslessness of the pion."

I've come to think that little remark is the seed of a much stronger philosophical position I now want to defend, namely that scales are autonomous. At any given energy or length scale you have your own degrees of freedom, your own symmetries, and your own standards of success, and the short-distance physics is screened off—not just hard to compute, but genuinly irrelevant to most of what happens at longer distances. Decoupling and universality are the poster children, but I mean something a bit more epistemic too: a theory at a scale can be confirmed, understood, and improved mostly from within, without a UV completion, and the deeper level doesn't "own" the correct description of the emergent one. I also think the view, if right, predicts that reductionist rivals will keep mistaking our present best theory for the bottom of the world, while the EFT attitude treats every layer as provisional and still perfectly objective.

So here is what I want. I hold this position and I want a map of what would count against it—not what critics would say against it in general, but what observations, formal results, or worked cases should actually make me abandon it or deeply revise it. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed defender of scale autonomy would themselves concede as real blows and which ones they'd dismiss. I'm especially curious about: non-decoupling in chiral gauge theories, 't Hooft anomaly matching and how much UV information it forces on you, higher-dimension operators that turn out to be observable (the proton-decay sort of thing), and the hierarchy problem—does that last one count as a failure of autonomy or just a failure of our aesthetic standards? Tell me, too, whether the position has been qualified to the point of unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that's a vice here or just what a claim about how scientific description works ought to look like. I'd personally be most moved by a clean case where getting the low-energy behavior right forces you to know a specific high-energy fact that no amount of low-energy measurement could ever determine.
