I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I've been trying to work Hofstadter's knob-twiddling picture of creativity into a seminar paper. The idea, roughly, is that creativity is mostly parameter-tweaking: you take an existing concept or theme, turn a few dials on it, maybe cross it with a neighboring concept, and what pops out is what we call a new idea. Nothing comes from nowhere. I tried to make that precise, and now the argument seems to prove too much, so I'd like to know where it actually breaks.

Here's the argument as I've got it:
(1) Every creative output is generated by modifying some parameters of already existing representations.
(2) Modifying parameters of an existing representation just is making a variation on it.
(3) A variation on X is derivative of X, not original.
(4) So every creative output is derivative of some earlier representation, and nothing is ever original in the relevant sense.
(5) But our practices of attribution, plagiarism, authorship, and intellectual property all depend on a real distinction between original and derivative work.
(6) If nothing is original, that distiction is an illusion, and a verbatim copied paper differs from a creative extension of it only in how far the dials were turned.
(7) That can't be right: plagiarism isn't just a difference in dial-settings.
(8) So either the knob-twiddling model is false, or one of the steps is doing more work than it should.

I'd most like to keep premise (1) and premise (7), but I can't see yet what it costs to reject wichever step is actually carrying the inference. Which premise or step should I reject, and what do I have to give up to reject it? Or is the conclusion somehow tolerable after all, and if so, what would that mean for how we usually talk about originality?
