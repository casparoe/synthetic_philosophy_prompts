Lately I've gotten pretty convinced that consciousness is a kind of user illusion: the brain builds a simplified, functional model of its own processing, and the what-it's-like glow is just the readout of that model, not some extra inner ingredient with its own causal powers. I don't mean full eliminativism about experience — experience is real as a representational and functional phenomenon — but on the view I'm flirting with there's no phenomenal consciousness over and above the access, attention, and self-modeling stuff; our conviction that there's something more is itself an artifact of the model, like a dashboard the brain shows itself. I'd like to know what would actually have to be true for me to be wrong. Don't just give me the obvious pushback — the it-obviously-feels-like-something move, Mary's room, p-zombies — I know those and largely think they beg the question against the view. I want the serious defeaters: which findings, thought experiments, or conceptual arguments would a committed defender of the user-illusion picture have to count as genuine evidence against it, and which can they plausibly dismiss as more outputs of the illusion? For each one, tell me whether it would be fatal, whether it would just push me toward a weaker hybrid (real phenomenal properties but epiphenomenal, say), or whether it's only an embarrassment. And maybe most importantly: is this view set up so that no observation could ever count against it? If so, is that a problem, or is that just par for the course for a claim about a cognitive illusion? I'd rather hear that the view is unfalsifiable than get a fake list of ways to test it.
