Since I retired I've been working through some moral philosophy, and a thing that keeps nagging me is the bright line hospitals draw around dying. In the hospital where I've seen this up close, a doctor can put a terminally ill patient into deep continuous sedation and then stop all food and fluids, knowing the patient will die a few days later of dehydration; this is considered good palliative care, not killing. But if the same doctor, treating the same patient's suffering, gave a fast-acting injection to stop the heart, that would be a crime. Both are deliberate medical choices that end in the patient's death, and both are made because the patient's dying has become unbearable. So the practice has to be resting on some philosophical distinction that's doing all the work, and I want to know exactly what it is. Don't just lay out the candidate distinctions — take a definite position on what theory of action and of wrongdoing this practice presupposes (intention vs. foresight? killing vs. letting die? something about the means of death?), say whether that theory is coherent when the cases get close, and tell me what hospital practice would have to change if the distinction were dropped or replaced.
