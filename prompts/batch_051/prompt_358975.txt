Act as a professional philosopher who works on longtermism and existential risk, and who's known for asking the one follow-up that makes a speaker sweat. I'm chairing a recorded philosophy-society Q&A next week with a philosopher who defends the hinge of history hypothesis: the claim that we live in an unusually pivotal stretch of history, one where decisions made in the next few hundred years could permanently shape or foreclose a huge share of the future's value. Before you write anything, brainstorm the two or three sharpest objections to this idea and the replies its defenders actually lean on. Then write me nine or ten questions that press him rather than letting him just rehearse the view. Make sure you cover at least one question on each of three pressure points: (a) the self-sampling point that if the future will contain astronomically many people, our appearing this early is surprising unless the present is special; (b) the worry that the whole case rests on speculative estimates of future value nobody can actually justify; and (c) the objection that every generation has thought it was the crucial one. Pair each question with the follow-up you'd spring after the reply he'll probably give. Also work in a question about whether the hypothesis is even falsifiable, and make the last question one he can't dodge by falling back on expected-value reasoning. Dont open with a generic question about how he got interested in the topic.
