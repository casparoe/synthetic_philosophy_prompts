Here's the question:

Explain how luck egalitarians distinguish between brute luck and option luck, and why the distinction matters for distributive justice. Then discuss one major objection to the view, using at least one concrete example.

Here's the student's answer:

Luck egalitarianism is the view that inequalities are unjust when they result from brute luck but can be just when they result from option luck. Brute luck is luck you had no control over, like being born with a disability or into poverty. Option luck is luck from a risk you deliberately took, like gambling your paycheck or investing in a volatile stock. Dworkin introduced this distinction to give us a principled way of deciding which inequalities society should fix: compensate people for brute luck, and leave option-luck inequalities in place.

Cohen's expensive tastes objection shows that Dworkin's resource-based version of the view is in tension with its own rationale. If someone involuntarily develops expensive tastes, say because they were raised to love fine wine, that preference isn't chosen, so on luck egalitarian reasoning it should count as brute luck. But Dworkin treats preferences as part of the person, so he holds people responsible for their expensive tastes and won't compensate them. So Dworkin is less committed to the luck egalitarian idea than he seems.

Anderson's objection comes from the other direction. She says the view is too harsh on people who make bad choices: the motorist who drives uninsured and causes a crash, or the hiker who goes out without a map and gets stranded. Since these look like option luck, the luck egalitarian seems committed to saying the victim has no claim on others. She thinks this is cruel, and she proposes instead what she calls democratic sufficiency, where the point is to ensure everyone is above a basic threshold rather than to track how lucky they were.

The case I keep going back to is someone who loses his life savings at a casino. The decision to gamble was an option-luck risk, of course, but the loss itself wasn't something he chose, and the loss is what actually ruins his life. So I'm inclined to say the loss counts as brute luck, which means a luck egalitarian should compensate him after all. But that seems to make the option/brute distinction do almost no work, since nearly every bad outccome has an unchosen component to it. Still, maybe that's just the price of taking the view seriously.

Overall the distinction is attractive because it matches our sense that people should bear the consequences of their own choices, but the objections show that the line between the two kinds of luck is much harder to draw than Dworkin suggests, and that the view can be inhumane at the margins.
