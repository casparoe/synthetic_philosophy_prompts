I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but the book I'm writng has backed me into a conceptual corner and I'd rather sort the metaphysics before I draft any more scenes. The protagonist, Mara, is a sound archivist who, after a botched memory proceedure, finds that nothing arrives as new: a film she has never seen, a stranger's face, a city she has never visited all come with the same quiet "oh, this again." I don't want the cheap version where she has literally seen everything; I want the stronger version where her mind has become unable to add to her stock of experience even though the world keeps serving up fresh objects. The plot turns on another character trying to prove to her that her condition is incoherent, so I need to know whether a finite mind can even in principle use up the space of experience, or whether my premise is nonsense.

So here's what I'd like: the desiderata. Not the finished theory of whether a finite mind can exhaust experience, but the list of requirements any adequate theory of it would have to meet if the goal is to make Mara's condition coherent as something that could be true of a finite mind rather than a metaphor. What cases must it get right, what distinctions can't it collapse, and what facts about memory, attention, and discrimination must it respect? To seed it: a theory should probably have to face the combinatorial thought that a finite mind with finitely many discriminable qualities only has finitely many possible experiences in some abstract sense, but also the rejoinder that the experience of the same content inside a changed context, or alongside the memory of its previous occurrence, is not obviously the same experience. It also shouldn't collapse the distinction between having exhausted all possible experiences and having lost the capacity for novelty, since those are different conditions with different consequences. Give me the consideration behind each requirement, mark which are non-negotiable and which are just camp preferences or aesthetic assumptions about what experience is, and flag any pair of requirements that look jointly unsatisfiable, because that's where the real dramatic tension will come from. And if one requirement does the most work in killing off candidate views, say which it is and why.

Since this is for a London publisher, could I have the whole answer in British English? Ta.
