In *The Spirit of the Laws*, Book II, Chapter 2, Montesquieu writes:

"They have only to be determined by things to which they cannot be strangers, and by facts that are obvious to sense. They can tell when a person has fought many battles, and been crowned with success; they are, therefore, capable of electing a general. They can tell when a judge is assiduous in his office, gives general satisfaction, and has never been charged with bribery: this is sufficient for choosing a prætor."

I am in a political theory seminar on sortition and lottocracy, and this passage has been troubling me. We have been reading contemporary proposals for replacing or supplementing elected legislatures with standing chambers selected by random lot from the citizenry—sometimes called allotted chambers or citizens' assemblies with legislative powers. The standard lottocratic argument is that elections systematically select for the wrong qualities: name recognition, charisma, fundraising ability, and a narrow professional politician's skill set, rather than ordinary judgment. Montesquieu seems to be defending election on the ground that the people can judge visible, publicly available facts about candidates. What happens when this claim meets the lottocratic proposal?

Read one way, the passage counts against sortition: popular selection works because voters can detect obvious external markers of competence, and random selection abandons precisely this filtering function. Read another way, the passage exposes how weak the electoral heuristic is—the people can tell who won battles, not whether a general will govern well—so it may be compatible with, or even support, the lottocrat's complaint that elections do not select for governing competence. Is there a third reading?

I am less interested in the practical answer than in how the interpretive approaches differ. How would each of the following apply to this passage and the case of an allotted legislative chamber: a literal reading of the words, a reading guided by the function the passage plays in Montesquieu's account of democracy, an answer to the question of what Montesquieu would have said if asked point-blank about modern sortition, and a reading by a reasonable contemporary student who picks up the text without scholarly apparatus? Which approach should carry the most weight here, and why? I would also like to know whether Montesquieu's distinction between being qualified to choose and being qualified to hold office survives the lottocratic challenge, and whether his reasoning gives any reason to think a randomly selected chamber would be better or worse than an electorate at choosing magistrates.
