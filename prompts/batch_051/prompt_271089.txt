i've been thinking a lot lately about when it's okay to do something to an ecosystem that we cant really reverse—gene drives, de-extinction, that kind of thing. I wrote down my gut reactions to a bunch of cases and I want to know if there's a principle hiding in them, or if I'm just all over the place.

Here are the cases and how I honestly feel:

1. A gene drive to wipe out invasive mice on a remote, uninhabited island where they're eating seabird chicks. The mice would be gone from that island, but the species exists everywhere else, so in principle you could put them back. I say fine.

2. A gene drive to wipe out malaria-carrying mosquitoes across all of Africa, permanently, with no clear idea what would take their place. I say no, not fine.

3. A gene drive that's engineered to burn itself out after maybe ten generations, used to knock down malaria mosquitoes in a single river valley. The mosquitoes would creep back in from outside the valley, so it's only a temporary fix unless you keep doing it. I'm fine with this one, even though it's less effective in the long run than case 2.

4. Bringing back something like a woolly mammoth (made from edited elephant cells) and releasing herds in Siberia to restore grassland and maybe slow permafrost thaw. I'm tentatively fine, but I couldn't defend it well if pressed.

5. Bringing back the passenger pigeon, which used to show up in flocks big enough to darken the sky over North America, and releasing it into a landscape that's since been reorganized by farms, roads, and other introduced species. I say no, even though this is also a de-extinction and any damage would build up slowly.

6. Reintroducing wolves to Yellowstone. That was permanent in practice and reorganized the ecosystem in ways people couldn't fully predict. I'd do it again without hesitating.

7. A gene drive to crash deer numbers in the eastern U.S. to reduce Lyme disease, engineered to spread only within one county. I still say no, even though I accepted case 1. A county just doesn't feel like a real boundary, and deer don't respect lines on a map.

I can feel myself caring about reversibility, whether the thing was originally there, how contained the area is, whether the intervention is open-ended or burns out, and whether local people get any say. But my cases don't seem to line up neatly: cases 1 and 7 both involve shrinking a mammal population in a bounded area, yet I feel opposite ways. Cases 3 and 7 are both "safe" because they're contained or temporary, but I only like one. Cases 4, 5, and 6 are all about putting back something that was missing, and I feel three different ways.

Is there a principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that gets these verdicts right, or mostly right? If no good principle saves all of them, which of my verdicts should I give up and why? And what would your principle say about the one that keeps bothering me: bringing back the thylacine in Tasmania, where the habitat is mostly still there and it went extinct not that long ago?

And if you could, answer in American English.
