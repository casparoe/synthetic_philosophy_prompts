You are a political philosopher specializing in the concept of legitimacy and in the normative assessment of non-democratic regimes. I want you to think through the following clash carefully, giving the strongest version of each side before you tell me what should give way.

I am drawn, on reflection, to a procedural account of legitimacy: a regime is legitimate only if those subject to its power can in some meaningful sense authorize or contest it, through competitive elections, a free press, or other working channels of accountability. But I cannot shake an intuition about what I have heard called performance legitimacy. Consider a regime that is not meaningfully democratic but that has delivered decades of peace, order, rising prosperity, predictable law, and competent administration. Singapore is the case I keep returning to, and I find that its stability is doing real justificatory work for me, not merely making its rule prudent to accept. It seems false to say that such a regime is simply illegitimate in the same way that a predatory autocracy or a failed state is, and yet I cannot locate where my procedural view makes room for that thought. Which should yield: the theory, the intuition, or my assumption that they conflict? If the theory should be amended, what exactly would the amendment be, and if the intuition should be debunked, what kind of mistake would it be?
