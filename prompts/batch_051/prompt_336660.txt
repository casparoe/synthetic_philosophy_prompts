For a novel I’m writing, I’ve been reading Bentham’s *Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* — and something in that footnote about animals stuck with me. You know the one: he argues that the line shouldn’t be drawn at the number of a creature’s legs or the hairiness of its skin, and then asks whether the real question is not whether they can reason or talk but whether they can suffer. I don’t have the book in front of me, so that’s just the drift.

Here’s what’s been bugging me (no pun intended). My downstairs neighbor once called the landlord because I’d seen a palmetto bug in the hallway and, instead of killing it, kind of shooed it out the door with a magazine. She said it was a public-health issue. Yet if someone in the same building left poisoned tuna out for her cat, she’d call the police and, honestly, so would I. The exterminator my condo association hires sprays along the building’s foundation every spring and kills, I’d guess, tens of thousands of ants and roaches in an afternoon; nobody treats that as a moral event. But those insects have, let’s say, at least *some* chance of feeling something. If there’s even a tenth of one percent chance a roach can suffer, then a ten-thousand-roach extermination has an expected tally of ten suffering-bearing individuals — and suddenly it’s not obviously lower-stakes than one poisoned cat. Is the ordinary judgment here just a superstition, a cognitive failure about scale, or is there a decent principle that lets us make peace with killing the bugs and not the cat? I want the strongest version of that principle you can come up with, and an honest verdict on whether it actually works — or whether a consistent Benthamite should be mourning the ant hill and the hotel bed-bug heat treatment. Please answer in American English.
