In popular science writing and everyday decision-making, I keep seeing the "butterfly effect" used as a license for a kind of causal conclusion: that because a butterfly flapping its wings can allegedly set off a tornado, small choices or accidents in human life can be said to cause large downstream events in the same way, and therefore tiny actions should not be treated as causally negligible. Lately I found the original passage in Edward Lorenz's 1972 talk on weather predictability where the butterfly appears. As I understand him, Lorenz is not offering a single chain from one butterfly to one tornado; he is asking what it would take for such a chain to exist, and using the image to illustrate the forecaster's predicament: if the atmosphere is sensitive to perturbations at that scale, then the information needed to predict a specific tornado lies below any feasible observing network. So the image seems to be doing two different jobs: popular causality says small causes can be responsible for large outcomes, while Lorenz's own use seems to be about the limits on knowing which small causes will do what. I want an audit of this analogy. What maps onto what in the butterfly–tornado image, where does the popular version break against Lorenz's actual use, what is the analogy quietly importing about causation and responsibility that a nonlinear dynamical system does not contain, and can the popular conclusion—that tiny actions matter because they may be the cause of large events—survive once the disanalogies are out? If a better analogy exists for how small perturbations relate to later outcomes in a chaotic system, I would want that too.
