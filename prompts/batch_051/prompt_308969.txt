I am presenting a philosophy department colloquium next month in which I defend a deliberately narrow thesis: that a digital system implementing the complete causal organisation of an adult human brain would have full moral status, the same moral status as the biological original, even though it is a distinct individual rather than a continuation of the original person. My argument has three steps. First, moral status is grounded in capacities — centrally sentience, but for full status also something like rational autonomy — and not in the material in which those capacities are realised. Second, an emulation that preserves complete causal organisation preserves the capacities unless the capacities depend on something beyond causal organisation. Third, the candidate extras — biological material, organismic life, evolutionary history, continuous numerical identity — are either ad hoc or morally irrelevant; status travels with capacities, not with identity.

The audience matters. It is a general analytic philosophy department, not a group sympathetic to AI welfare and not a decision-theory crowd. The philosophers of mind are broadly functionalist about belief and memory but sceptical about artificial consciousness; they will resist the move from multiple realizability to the claim that a specific emulation has qualitative states. The moral philosophers include a Kantian who thinks dignity rests on authorship of one's principles rather than on the capacity to suffer, and who will ask whether a copy can be an author or only an imitation of one; and a utilitarian bioethicist who accepts that welfare matters but will press the distinction between being conscious and merely giving evidence of consciousness, and what morality requires across that epistemic gap. There is also a philosopher of biology who will insist that moral status attaches to being an organism with a life, not to a computational snapshot; and a doctoral student working on animal ethics who will ask why we are debating an emulation that no one has come close to building while the status of ordinary nonhuman animals remains contested.

For each objection, say whether it has real force or can be handled quickly, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and a reply short enough to deliver in two minutes.
