I am a retired comparative physiologist, and I spent much of my career advising aquaculture producers on stress responses and water quality. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mind, and I keep returning to a claim that now circulates in the industry I used to serve: that decapod crustaceans—prawns, crabs, lobsters—are capable of genuine pain, understood as a felt subjective state, and not merely of nociceptive reflexes. The claim has practical consequences. Some jurisdictions now require electrical stunning before slaughter, and several certification schemes have begun to restrict eyestalk ablation, the practice of removing one eyestalk from female shrimp to bring them into breeding condition. Each of those measures is warranted only if "pain" here means something more than an aversive stimulus triggering a hardwired motor program. I have tried to imagine what observation would settle the matter, and I cannot do it; so I want you to try. Design the strongest test of the claim that these animals feel pain. Be concrete: specify the experimental setup or the field measurements, state which results would count in favor of attributing felt pain and which would count against it, and say what a positive result would still not prove—where the empirical evidence runs out and a philosophical interpretation of the animal's behavior necessarily begins.
