In my statistics class we derived the birthday paradox, and it has been making me think about a pattern in everyday conversation. People often tell stories like "I ran into my old camp friend in an airport in another city — what are the odds?" and then treat the small probability as proof that something improbable or even fateful happened. I want to understand, precisely, how the combinatorial logic behind the birthday paradox applies to that kind of anecdote, and I would like you to build a case that forces the issue.

Please construct a realistic, ordinary thought experiment with concrete numbers — no psychics or exotic mechanisms — and have it meet the following specifications. Choose a personal-coincidence anecdote (for example, two people who know each other meeting unexpectedly in a place neither of them lives in). Specify the size of the relevant pool of people, the number of acquaintances each person has, the number of plausible encounter opportunities per day, the number of days in the observation period, and the probability that any one specified opportunity produces a striking coincidence. The numbers must satisfy three conditions. First, if one particular pair and one particular day had been named in advance, the chance of the striking meeting would be below one in one hundred thousand. Second, the chance that at least one equally striking coincidence happens somewhere in the pool during the observation period must be above ninety-five percent. Third, the gap between those two figures must be generated by the number of opportunities, in the spirit of the birthday paradox, and not by silently redefining the event halfway through. You may use the approximation that n independent opportunities, each with probability p, yield a 1 - e^(-np) chance of at least one hit; please show the arithmetic and flag any idealizations such as independence. For context, I plan to use the case to test how people update when they hear an amazing-sounding anecdote, so the probabilities have to fall out of the scenario rather than being asserted.

Once the case is on the table, work through the check: name the event, state the model and the numbers, and show why the two probabilities pull apart. Then discuss what the anecdote does and does not support. Why would a listener who reports the first probability as if it were the chance of the event be making a mistake? Which element of the scenario is driving the divergence, and what happens if that element is removed — in particular, would the anecdote become strong evidence if the event had been specified in advance rather than noticed after the fact? Finally, present the best objection available to someone who insists the coincidence cannot be mere chance, and explain how you would answer it. Please write the entire answer in American English.
