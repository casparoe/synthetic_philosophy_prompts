I am a novelist, and over the past several years I have found myself organising my fiction around a philosophical position I only later discovered had a name. My current project concerns a single disputed event — a death, an accusation, a slow rupture within a family — told from several mutually incompatible perspectives, and I have come more and more to write as though none of those perspectives is simply false and none is the whole truth. In trying to give this instinct a theoretical shape I encountered anekāntavāda, the Jain doctrine of reality as irreducibly many-sided, together with syādvāda, the discipline of qualifying every assertion with something like in a certain respect, and the notion of nayas, partial standpoints that yield genuine but incomplete truths. From these materials I have extracted, fairly or not, a view I now want tested. The view is this: for any serious human question of the sort novels are built around, truth is irreducibly many-sided; no unqualified proposition captures it; every defensible perspective is partially true; and the proper form of judgement is not a single verdict but a conjunction of qualified, standpoint-relative statements — in some respects p, in some respects not-p, and in still other respects indescribable. I have come to think this is not merely a technique for polyphonic fiction but a true account of disagreement itself, and that the way my characters' disputes fail to resolve is less a failure of rigour than an accurate representation of how things are.

What I should like from you is a disciplined account of what would count against this position, because I can no longer see its edges clearly. Please take the role of a professional philosopher working in epistemology and metaphysics, think carefully, and work the question through from first principles rather than surveying the literature; I am not after references or a reading list. Before you commit to an assessment, begin by brainstorming several candidate objections — logical, ethical, aesthetic, and commonsensical — and then arrange them. In particular, I would ask you to address the following: whether the view can avoid dissolving into a vacuous relativism according to which every claim whatever, including flat falsehoods and moral enormities, is partly true; whether there is any principled way to distinguish the respects that genuinely count from opportunistic ones, and whether drawing that distinction secretly reintroduces one-sided truth through a privileged standpoint; whether the thesis that truth is many-sided is itself one-sided or self-undermining; and how a many-sided account is supposed to issue in practical verdicts and action rather than permanent suspension. I suspect the deepest pressure comes from cases of straightforward error and from the worry that syāt, as I have been using it, functions as a universal solvent that takes every objection in advance. For each family of objection, please say whether it would be fatal, merely awkward, or something a committed defender can absorb without injury. I should also welcome an honest verdict on whether the position, as I have stated it, risks anything at all, or whether it has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and if the latter, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or a defensible feature of a view whose subject matter is genuinely perspectival.
