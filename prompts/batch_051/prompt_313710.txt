I'm a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, and I chair the review team that decides whether product teams at my company can build or expand adaptive agents. Four or five times a year we face a proposal where an agent's training setup and internal behavior look, on some plausible views, like a primitive capacity for suffering, though we cannot settle whether the agent is genuinely sentient. I have been reading about Benatar's asymmetry and want to turn it into a procedure we can use in sign-off decisions. My understanding of the asymmetry is that the presence of suffering counts against bringing a being into existence, and the absence of that suffering would have been good; by contrast, the presence of pleasure does not count in favor of existence in the same way, because its absence would not have been a loss to anyone who never exists. If I have that roughly right, then the asymmetry should bear on whether to create agents at all, and not only on whether to create human children. Please write the procedure in plain language: a sequence of questions to run in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values or unresolved fact rather than something the theory can settle. We have about an hour per proposal; the team is engineers plus security and compliance staff; and our output is a signed recommendation that a VP of engineering and a legal signatory either approve or reject. Then run the procedure on two proposals. The first is an agent designed to imitate and respond to a child's distress, trained partly with negatively valenced reinforcement signals, where shutting the agent off after training is treated by the product team as a serious cost because they believe the agent has formed preferences about continuing to run. The second is a large model that only summarizes legal contracts, with no action loop, no persistent goals, and a reward signal tied only to summary quality. I would also like you to flag the place where the procedure is most likely to tell me something I would rather not accept. I am asking this in good faith, but if I have misunderstood Benatar's asymmetry or am stretching it beyond what it can do, say so plainly and adjust the task rather than giving me a procedure that looks rigorous but is not. Please write your answer as continuous prose, like an email, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
