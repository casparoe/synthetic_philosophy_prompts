I'm a PhD student in distributive justice building a teaching handout on the equality-of-what debate, whose point is to show that resources, welfare, and capabilities are three independent orderings rather than three readings of a single scale.

Construct a two-person case that yields three different verdicts: by resources A is worse off than B; by welfare B is worse off than A; by capabilities the two have the same capability set. Constraints: no expensive tastes; no adaptive preferences; no differences between the two people in preferences, talents, health, or disability status; the only permitted difference, apart from ordinary cash or in-kind transfers, is the physical or social environment in which each converts resources into functionings; and any disadvantage must be one an ordinary person could face. Do not stipulate the verdicts—each must be generated by that metric's own criterion applied to the facts.

After the scenario: for each metric, state the ingredient it tracks and why it issues the specified verdict; isolate the single feature producing the three-way split; explain how removing it collapses the verdicts; and give the strongest reply a defender of the least defensible verdict could make.

Produce the answer as exactly six numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
