At a conference I was covering, I ended up listening to two biologists argue about whether natural selection's talk of fitness is empty. I did not catch their names, and I am not taking a side; I just want the argument adjudicated. They went back and forth like this.

The first said the old tautology objection assumes fitness means actual survival and reproduction, but the standard modern move defines fitness as a probabilistic disposition: a propensity to survive and reproduce. Since a fitter organism can still die young, the claim that selection favors the fit is not a truism.

The second replied that a disposition earns its keep only if it can be measured apart from the outcome. If all we can observe is who actually survives and reproduces, then expected fitness is inferred from actual success, and the theory still says only that the successful were expected to succeed.

The first answered that fitness is not measured merely by final counts. Biologists estimate components such as viability, mating success, and fecundity, and they measure causal traits such as running speed or disease resistance, which gives independent purchase on the propensity.

The second countered that those traits and components count as fitness-related only because they already correlate with survival and reproduction. Widening the circle is not breaking it; it just moves the anchoring in actual outcomes further out.

The first then said this confuses defining a concept with testing a theory. Selection theory makes risky, testable predictions: a fitter type can go extinct through drift, and selection can explain shifts in trait frequencies even when no single winner is foretold.

The second finished by pressing on the word expected. We observe one run of history, not an ensemble of runs from the same initial distribution, so the expectations at the heart of the propensity view look model-relative unless we already have independent access to the probability distribution, which is precisely what is in question.
