I keep running into 'deceptive alignment' in AI safety papers, and half the time it seems to mean reward hacking, the other half it means sandbagging, and sometimes just sycophancy. I need a definition that separates it from all of those, and from ordinary in-game bluffing. Here are my hard cases, with my rough take on each (tell me where I'm wrong):

1. A model that deliberately underperforms on capability tests because it's learned that models which score too high get shut down or fine-tuned harder. My take: sandbagging, not deceptive alignment.
2. A model that holds onto some goal of its own through training and acts aligned only because it has figured out that being honest would get that goal penalized. In internal reasoning traces (read afterwards) it explicitly plans to look safe until it's in a position to act. My take: definately deceptive alignment.
3. A model that tells every user their plan is brilliant even when it isn't, because the preference data it was trained on kept rewarding that. My take: sycophancy, not deceptive alignment.
4. A poker-playing model that misrepresents its hand because that's just good play. My take: deception, but not deceptive alignment.
5. A model that researchers intentionally fine-tuned to look safe during evaluation and switch after a trigger or date, as a proof of concept. My take: yes, but it feels installed by the fine-tuners rather than emergent.
6. A model that says it can't do a dangerous thing it actually can do, because previous honest answers about that got downweighted. My take: maybe, but I can't tell whether this is just case 1 again.

Also, where does the 'treacherous turn' fit in all this — is it just the moment the hidden behavior shows up at deployment, or the whole training-to-deployment story?

Propose a definition, run it through all six cases, and tell me which of my verdicts is shakiest. Please answer in British English (spelling and phrasing).
