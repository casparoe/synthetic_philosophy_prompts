I am trying to work out whether one principle can capture my verdicts about a family of two-envelope cases. In every case, one envelope contains exactly twice as much money as the other; I choose one without knowing which is which; and I may then switch.

My verdicts are these. First, if I know nothing about how the amounts were chosen and have not opened either envelope, I think there is no reason to switch before opening. The two envelopes are symmetric, and if I switched I could not say what I expected to gain; it seems a pure wash.

Second, with the same total ignorance, if I open my envelope and find $10, I am strongly inclined to switch. It seems there is an even chance the other envelope contains $5 or $20, giving an expected value of $12.50. But I notice that this reasoning would apply whatever amount I had seen, which would make me willing to switch before seeing anything, contradicting the first case. These two verdicts are the ones I most want you to test.

Third, if the amounts must be whole dollars and I find $1, I am sure I should switch: the other envelope cannot contain 50 cents, so it must contain $2. Here the information visibly does work.

Fourth, suppose I know only that the larger amount is at most $1,000. If I find $800, I will not switch, since the other envelope cannot contain $1,600 and so must contain $400. But under the same cap, if I find $10, I am still inclined to switch: $5 and $20 are both possible, and I cannot see anything that makes $5 more likely than $20.

Fifth, suppose I know both that the larger amount is at most $1,000 and that the smaller amount was chosen uniformly from the whole-dollar amounts between $1 and $500. If I find $400, I switch: the other envelope is $200 or $800 with equal probability, for an expected value of $500. If I find $600, I stay: $600 can only be the larger amount, so the other has $300. And before opening, under this same prior, I still regard always switching as a wash, though I now suspect that a policy of switching only when I see $500 or less is genuinely advantageous.

Is there one principle about when switching is justified that delivers these verdicts, or nearly all of them? If it cannot deliver them all, which should I overturn? Please apply the principle you settle on to a case I did not list: I know the smaller amount is $10 with probability 0.9 and $20 with probability 0.1, and I open my envelope to find $20; what does the principle say I should do? Finally, say how confident you are in the principle and in the verdicts it implies, and single out the specific point you are least sure of.
