Hey — I'm a PhD student in decision theory, and I've gotten myself into a rabbit whole on money pumps and diachronic rationality. The part that's bugging me is whether intransitive preferences are really ruled out by money-pump arguments, and I'm trying to make a list of the defenses someone can give on behalf of an agent with cyclical preferences. As far as I can tell, the standard moves are these:

(1) The sophisticated-chooser reply: an agent who knows the whole sequence of offers in advance can refuse the first trade, because she sees it launches a cycle, so the pump only gets myopic agents. (2) The pairwise-versus-sequence point: pairwise preferences don't automatically determine preferences over whole transaction sequences, so even if each swap looks good individually, the agent can reject the series as a whole. (3) The epsilon/ordinality problem: the pump needs the agent to pay a small positive fee at each step, so it only works if each pairwise preference gap is large enough to outweigh that fee; bare intransitivity says nothing about the size of the gaps, so not every cyclical preference set is exploitable. (4) The explotability-isn't-irrationality move: even if someone can be exploited, that shows there's a cost to her preferences, not that they're logically incoherent.

I suspect I've hit the point where everything I can think of is a restatement or combination of these. But before I write my advisor and say the literature is basically closed, I want to check: is there a further defense of intransitive preferences against the money pump that is genuinely different from these four?
