I'm working on a novel about a group of geophysicists in the late 1960s who end up on opposite sides of a major conceptual split, and I reread Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions to get the texture of that kind of break right. There's a passage, I'm paraphrasing from memory, where he says something like: after a paradigm shift the scientists aren't simply looking at the same observations and interpreting them differently; in an important sense they're working in a different world, and even the stable results of experiment look different to them because the paradigm tells them what counts as a meaningful result. As a novelist I found that image completely intoxicating. I've ended up wanting to hold the strong incommensurability position: rival paradigms are not just underdetermined by a shared body of data, there is no theory-free stock of observations, and the criteria by which one paradigm is judged better than another are themselves internal to one or the other paradigm. This matters to me because it would mean my characters can't resolve their dispute by pointing at the data, and that's the story I want to write. But I can't tell weather I've fallen for a piece of science-studies rhetorical drama rather than a serious claim about scientific change. So I want to test it. I hold the strong incommensurability reading of Kuhnian paradigm shifts. What would count against it? Give me exactly five points, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion, ranked from what would be most damaging to what would be merely awkward. And be honest about whether the position has been qualified over time into something that can't be tested, and if so whether that's a real problem for the view.
