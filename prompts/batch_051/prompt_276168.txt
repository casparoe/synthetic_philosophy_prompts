I've been trying to figure out how a single hospital can run two completely different tests for the exact same event, and every explanation I get makes me more confused. My uncle works in an ICU, and he told me that on one floor a neurologist will declare a patient brain-dead while the ventilator is still pumping and the body is warm and still has a pulse; the patient counts as legally dead, and then a transplant team removes the organs. On another floor, sometimes on the same day, life support is withdrawn from a different patient, the heart stops, and after only a couple of minutes of flatline the patient is declared dead by circulatory criteria and the team moves fast. In the second case, he said, if someone had started CPR instead of standing there, the heart might well have restarted, and parts of the brain were probably still working. Yet the whole system runs on a rule that vital organs can only be taken from someone who is already dead.

So the same institution seems to be saying, in one room, that death is the irreversible destruction of the whole brain, and in the next room, that death is the stopping of circulation for a couple of minutes when nobody plans to reverse it. What does this practice actually presuppose about what death is? Does it strictly presuppose one view, or is it compatible with several, and are the two criteria even presupposing the same thing? Be concrete about the rival conceptions of death (loss of integration, loss of consciousness, cessation of organismic functioning, whatever else), and say what the transplant protcols would have to look like if we demanded a single consistent definition—or if we instead gave up on the idea that organ donors have to be dead and just required something like valid consent plus no harm to the person's interests.
