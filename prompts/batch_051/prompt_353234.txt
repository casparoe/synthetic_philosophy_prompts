I'm a grad student and I've been in a decision-theory reading group for most of this year, and the one argument that has gone absolutely nowhere is expected value fanaticism. Everyone in the room grants the starting point: if rational agents maximize expected value, then a big enough payoff at a small enough probability swamps ordinary considerations, and once infinite payoffs enter, any nonzero probability seems to dominate everything. But when we get to the mugging cases — someone who claims he can give you an astronomically large amount of value if you hand over your wallet, with a probability of like one in 10^40 — the group splits down the middle, and not because anyone is confused. One side says the math is the math: you hand over the wallet, and if you're gut revolts that's just scope insensitivity, the same glitch that makes people care no more about a million lives than a thousand. The other side says the example is a reductio of unbounded utility or of taking tiny probabilities literally: either value has a ceiling, or below some probability the expected value calculation stops being action-guiding, or both. A third group keeps saying we're answering different questions — a criterion of rightness for an ideal agent versus a decision procedure for bounded creatures like us — and the rest of us nod and then argue exactly the same way next week.

I've started to suspect the impasse is actually three debates sharing a name: whether there's a maximum amount of value, what a tiny probability even permits an agent to treat as a live option, and whether the 'ought' here is about ideal or non-ideal agents. But I don't trust my own diagnosis, because everyone's been in the weeds so long we can't see the structure of the disagreement anymore. So what I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict: is it substantive, verbal, a clash of bedrock intuitions, a hidden empirical or formal question, or are the sides answering different questions under one heading? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a formal result, an experiment, a distinction that dissolves the appearance of disagreement, or an argument that nobody should expect movement?
