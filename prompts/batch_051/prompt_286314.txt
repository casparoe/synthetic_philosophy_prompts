Since retiring I've been reading philosophy, and I got stuck on the ethics of randomized trials—partly because I used to sit on an ethics board where we saw these protocols all the time. I keep trying to boil the standard defense down to a clean argument, but the one I produce seems to prove too much, so I suspect one of the steps is doing more work than it should:

(1) A doctor who enrolls her patient in a trial still has the same duty of care to that patient that she has outside the trial.
(2) That duty requires her to recommend the treatment she honestly thinks is best for this patient.
(3) But in a randomized trial the treatment is assigned by a randomizer, not by her judgment about which arm is best.
(4) So a doctor can legitimately enroll a patient in a trial only when she honestly has no view about which arm is better.
(5) Yet honestly having no view is a fragile thing: once a trial has been running a while and evidence starts to pile up around it, almost any thoughtful doctor will have a hunch.
(6) So almost no ongoing randomized trial is ethically legitimate.

I've seen people call the state in (4) "equipoise," but I can never tell weather they mean each doctor's private state of mind or the state of the whole field of experts, and I suspect that's where the trick is. Is the argument valid? If not, which step is the first bad inference? If it is valid but unsound, which premise should be rejected, and what's the correct version of it?
