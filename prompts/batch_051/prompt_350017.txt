I'm a novelist, mid-revision on a book whose ending stands or falls on an argument I've put in my protagonist's mouth. Adele works in the back office of a heavy-equipment exporter — she checks the shipping manifests, files the export declarations, collates the invoices. She's found out that one of the regular buyers, the one whose paperwork she handles, is a militia that uses the bulldozers and spare parts in a campaign of forced displacement. She keeps the job, for reasons the novel has spent three hundred pages making understandable, and in the final chapter she gives the following defense of herself. I can't find the flaw in it, and I need to know whether it's a real argument or a rationalization I've dressed up too well.

(1) You're only complicit in a wrong if you caused it.
(2) To cause a wrong, your action has to make a difference to whether the wrong happens: without your action, the wrong wouldn't have occurred.
(3) Adele's action made no difference. If she had refused to process the manifests, the company would have fired her, replaced her within days, and the same bulldozers would still have reached the same buyer, maybe a week later.
(4) So Adele didn't cause any of it.
(5) So Adele isn't complicit — and the ending lets her live with herself.

Its either the deepest thing in the book or a cheat, and I'm too close to tell. I keep feeling the sleight is in step 3 — the "someone else would have done it" move feels like a get-out-of-jail card — but every time I try to pin it down, the counterfactual seems true and I'm back where I started. So: does the argument actually go through, and if it doesn't, which numbered step is the one that breaks? Give me the strongest version of the failing step, and tell me if the argument can be repaired into something Adele could sincerely believe. And please keep clear the difference between what's settled among people who actually work on moral responsibility and complicity, and what's still genuinely contested — dont present a contested take as if it were the consensus.
