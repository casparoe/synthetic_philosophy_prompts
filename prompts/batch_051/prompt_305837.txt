I am a doctoral student working on the philosophical foundations of criminal law, and for a workshop I have to submit a concise statement of my current position on fair notice and the maxim that ignorance of the law is no excuse. I would like to know whether the position actually hangs together before I defend it. Here is where I have landed.

(a) Criminal liability should track the agent's blameworthiness. This is the baseline, and every departure from it, including the ignorance-of-law maxim, needs a justification rather than being taken for granted. (b) It is unjust to convict someone of conduct they could not reasonably have known to be criminal, where reasonableness is judged by what a person in their situation could have worked out, not by what they in fact believed. (c) Offenses must be defined by public, prospective, reasonably determinate law, because the point of legality requirements is to allow people to guide their conduct and to check state power. (d) Open-ended standards are nonetheless legitimate ingredients of criminal law: convicting someone for grossly unreasonable risk-taking under a negligence standard does not violate fair notice, because they could be expected to understand they were crossing a line even without being able to list the statutory details in advance. (e) Strict liability is inconsistent with the idea that criminal liability tracks blameworthiness, but I make an exception for trivial regulatory offenses where requiring proof of mens rea would make the scheme administratively unworkable. (f) On the maxim itself: ignorance that conduct is legally prohibited never excuses when the conduct is wrongful independent of the law, but when the offense is wrongful only because prohibited and the agent reasonably relied on an official misstatement of the law, conviction would be unjust. (g) A legal system is defective if ordinary people acting in good faith systematically cannot tell what the criminal law prohibits, because the law is then functioning for them merely as a set of sanctions rather than as a norm. Yet I also think the fact that most citizens are ignorant of most criminal statutes is not by itself a failure of the rule of law.

I would like an audit rather than a lecture. Tell me whether this set of commitments is jointly tenable: which pairs are in genuine tension, ordered by severity; which apparent tensions dissolve on a careful reading; what the cheapest fix would be for each genuine tension and what it would cost; and which single revision would do the most to make the package consistent. Please begin your response with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
