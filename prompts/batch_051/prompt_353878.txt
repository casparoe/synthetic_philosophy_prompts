In American campaign finance, the law draws a sharp line between two ways of spending the same money on the same candidate. A check written to the candidate's own committee is subject to strict contribution limits, because it is treated as a standing invitation to corruption. A check spent on advertisements urging voters to elect that candidate is treated as constitutionally protected speech, with no limit at all, so long as the spender does not coordinate with the campaign. The same expenditure, routed one way, counts as influence-peddling; routed the other, it counts as political participation. Is there a principle that justifies drawing the line exactly at coordination, or is the distinction an artifact of legal convenience that should be abandoned? Give the strongest defense you can, one that does not merely restate the legal doctrine, and then test it against these cases: an independent group run by the candidate's former campaign manager that echoes the campaign's message; a donor who gives the legal maximum to every majority-party member and then spends independently on ads praising the committee chair; and a wealthy challenger who self-finances far beyond what any donor could legally contribute. State which of these, if any, defeat the distinction, and conclude with a verdict: should the asymmetry be kept, reversed, or given up? Please write your answer in American English.
