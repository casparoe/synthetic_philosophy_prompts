I'm drafting a short essay on the value of negative emotions, and I'd like you to comment on it before I try to place it somewhere. The working title is "Grief is not a message." The essay is aimed at readers who are sympathetic to the idea that sadness has value but suspicious of the usual defense of it. Here is the draft, written out as continuous prose.

Most defenses of sadness begin with the idea that sadness is information. On this view, feeling sad is the way an agent registers that something she cares about has been damaged or lost, and the unpleasantness of the feeling is what ensures the registering sticks. Sadness is valuable, the thought goes, because it tells us, and keeps telling us, where our values are. I think this defense, in its usual form, answers a question about whether sadness is fitting without answering the harder question of whether a life that includes sadness is better than a life without it. Showing that a state accurately represents a loss shows that the state is not a malfunction; it does not yet show that we should be glad the capacity exists, as opposed to wishing we could register losses without pain.

The informational defense is weakest in grief. Grief is often cited as the paradigm case of valuable sadness, yet it is precisely where the information story fits least well. When someone has died, the griever normally already knows the person has died; that knowledge is not delivered by the grief, it is what sets the grief off. Nor does grief typically reveal that the person mattered, which was known, and acted on, before the loss. If grief is a signal, it is a signal that arrives after the message has been read and understood. A defender might say the signal still matters because it keeps the loss from being cognitively evaded over the years. But that concedes something important: grief becomes valuable only insofar as the agent is resisting the truth, which turns grief into a remedy for a further defect, not a good in its own right.

I want to suggest a different picture. Grief's value is not informational but constitutive. To grieve is not to receive a message about the significance of a person; it is one of the forms that acknowledging that significance takes once the person is gone. The desolation and the recurring thoughts are, as it were, the acknowledgment itself, not a device for producing it. On this view, a life from which grief were absent after the death of someone loved would not be a life that had learned the same truth more cheaply; it would be a life that treated the loss as less than it was, because part of treating a loss as significant just is having one's life disrupted by it.

But I can see two problems, and this is where I would value comments. First, the constitutive view seems to make grief's intensity self-justifying: if grief is the acknowledgment, then more grief would seem to be more acknowledgment, which flattens the distinction between grieving and being undone by grief. Any adequate view needs a proportionality norm, but it is not obvious where that norm comes from once we give up the idea that grief is for something. Second, the constitutive view may again only show that grief is fitting, not that a grieving life is better for the person who lives it. To block that objection I am tempted to say that we should stop asking only about welfare and ask about what makes a life good as the particular life it is, but I worry that this is a dodge unless I can say more.

What I'd like is a set of comments in continuous prose, written as a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Please address at least these four things: whether the contrast between informational and constitutive value is sharp enough, or whether the constitutive view is really just the informational view with the signal moved inside the acknowledgment; whether my treatment of the informational defense is fair to its strongest version, especially the version that says sadness trains attention over time; whether the proportionality problem can be solved within a constitutive view, and if so what the solution would look like; and whether the ending genuinely addresses the welfare objection or merely changes the subject. Be direct about whether the central move is interesting enough to carry a short essay, and if you think the whole framing should be changed, say so.
