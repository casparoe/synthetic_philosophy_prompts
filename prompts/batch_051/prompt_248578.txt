Me and a friend were arguing about whether history would have turned out basically the same even if a few small things had gone differently. He kept saying yes, because the big forces would have pushed toward the same result sooner or later. But the pattern I kept noticing is not about big forces or inevitability at all. Its about a situation where an early choice or accident, sometimes pretty arbitrary, creates a setup that gets more entrenched the longer it lasts. People keep following it not because they think its the best option, or even because they have deliberately decided to keep it, but because too many other things have been built around it. The original reason can disappear entirely, and everyone can agree that the current state is suboptimal, and still nothing changes because switching would be hugely expensive and would require a kind of coordination that nobody can pull off alone.

I can think of examples like a technical standard that stays in place because everyone is already using it, so each individual has no reason to switch by themselves even though they would all be better off if everyone switched at once. But I also see it in institutions and legal rules, where an old rule created to solve a problem that no longer exists keeps shaping behavior because too many present practices now assume it. Its not just the butterfly effect, because the issue isnt that a small cause had large downstream effects. The issue is that the downstream effects actively protect the original outcome from being revised. Is there an established name for this specific pattern? I know terms like path dependence and lock-in get used, but I want to know whether what I am describing is exactly one of those, or whether its a distinct mechanism with a different name. What distinguishes a merely contingent historical influence from a genuinely self-reinforcing trap, and where is the boundary between them?
