You are grading an undergraduate epistemology exam. Below are a short-answer exam question and one student's answer. Grade the student's answer out of 100, and give the specific comments that justify the grade.

Exam question:
"State Nozick's counterfactual, or truth-tracking, conditions for knowledge, and apply them to the following case. Henry is driving through an unfamiliar rural area. Unknown to him, the locals have built many extremely realistic barn facades: barn-shaped, barn-colored structures that are not barns. Henry sees one barn-shaped structure across a field and, solely on the basis of ordinary visual inspection, forms the belief 'That structure is a barn.' In fact, the structure is a barn — the only genuine barn among all the facades. On Nozick's account, does Henry know that the structure is a barn? Work through each condition and give an overall verdict."

Student's answer:
"Nozick analyzes knowledge as true belief that tracks the truth. The conditions are: (1) p is true; (2) S believes p; (3) if p were false, S would not believe p (variation); (4) if p were true, S would believe p (adherence). In Henry's case, (1) and (2) clearly hold: the structure really is a barn, and Henry believes it is. For (3), we ask: if the proposition 'That structure is a barn' were false, would Henry still believe it? No. Had the structure not been a barn, it would have been something else — a house, open ground, or a pile of lumber — and Henry would not have formed the belief that it is a barn. So variation holds. For (4), Henry's eyesight is normal and the structure actually is a barn, so in nearby worlds where it remains a barn he would still believe it is a barn. So adherence holds. All four conditions are satisfied, so Nozick's account says Henry knows. This shows the tracking theory's strength: it gets ordinary perceptual knowledge right without demanding that the subject possess any internal justification."
