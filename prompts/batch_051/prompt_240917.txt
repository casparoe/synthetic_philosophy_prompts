I have been working through Nozick's utility monster, and I have ended up defending a position that I would like pressure-tested. The lesson usually drawn from the thought experiment is that utilitarianism must be false, because a being who derives enormously more welfare than everyone else from each unit of a resource would be entitled, on the theory, to everything. I have come to think this is the wrong lesson. On my reading, the monster is not a self-contained refutation of consequentialism; it is a demand that the utilitarian supply a theory of the admissible shape of welfare. Once one separates the pure aggregation rule — maximize total welfare, whatever its distribution — from the auxiliary assumptions that let the monster get off the ground — unbounded or non-diminishing marginal utility, perfect interpersonal comparability, the absence of satiation, and so on — the monster can be excluded by an independently motivated account of what welfare is like. Any plausible account of welfare, I want to say, has enough structure to block the transfer: satiation, diminishing returns, the eventual collapse of marginal benefit, or some bound on a person's possible welfare. The monster therefore counts only against a utilitarianism that is offered as purely formal, empty of content about the good, and upwardly open-ended.

What I would like is a map of what could count against this position. Specifically: under what conditions would my proposed exclusion turn out to be ad hoc or question-begging? If the monster can be reconstructed with an entirely continuous, coherent utility function that violates no purely formal condition — perhaps a being whose need is genuinely endless, or a lexicographic preference ordering that is not even representable by a real-valued function — does that force me to concede that ruling it out is a substantive moral choice rather than a structural consequence of welfare? Which results in decision theory, measurement theory, or welfare economics would tell against the idea that there is some neutral, non-moral way to forbid the monster's utility function? What would a defender of my view himself regard as a genuine defeater — for instance, the charge that imposing satiation or boundedness has changed the subject from utilitarianism to a limited-fungibility theory of value, or that claims about diminishing returns in human welfare are merely contingent empirical generalizations that could fail for some possible rational creature — as opposed to objections he would dismiss as reasserting the monster rather than answering it? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether the position is in the end well insulated, and whether such insulation would be a virtue or a sign that it no longer risks anything.
