I am a philosopher whose current research concerns the metaphysics and epistemology of reasons, and I have been thinking about the view that a consideration can genuinely count in favor of a conclusion even when no particular mind is entertaining it—much as a theorem remains true and rationally compelling before anyone proves it. I want to stress-test that thought by embedding it in an institution, and I want your analysis of how the institution would actually run, not whether it is morally or politically attractive in the abstract.

Suppose a municipality creates a Reason Pool for every contested public decision made by its zoning board, school board, and city council. Any resident, official, or outside expert may contribute a rationale for a decision, using a pseudonym or no name at all. The Pool's software guarantees that no submission is traceable to its author, so contributors receive neither credit nor blame. Others may object to a rationale, but only by addressing the consideration on its merits; they may also revise it, and the revision advances as a successor to the original. At the close of a fixed deliberative period, the relevant body must publish its decision together with the three best-surviving rationales as ranked by the Pool's anonymous voting, and future decisions may reuse those rationales without attribution. Treat this as a mechanism. I want to know what it would incentivize among contributors, critics, members of the deciding bodies, and organized interests; how it would be gamed; where and why it would break or drift over time; whether its failure modes could be repaired without reintroducing authorship or reputation; and what its trajectory would show about the idea that reasons can float free of reasoners. Please keep the response to no more than 900 words, and begin with a one-sentence bottom line stating your overall assessment.
