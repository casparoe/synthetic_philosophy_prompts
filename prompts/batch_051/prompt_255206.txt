Consider the following position, which is broadly that advanced by Michael Dummett on immigration. A state's ordinary immigration controls—the routine denial to noncitizens of the freedom to enter, settle, work, and become members—are presumptively unjust, and the burden is on the state to show otherwise. The core analogy is with internal movement: within a country, restrictions on where people may live or work would be recognized as oppressive unless backed by extremely strong reasons, and the position claims there is no relevant difference at the border that changes this assessment. The standard justifications for immigration control—cultural preservation, labour-market protection, public order, democratic self-determination—are on this view usually weak, speculative, or covert ways of preserving national or racial hierarchy. The position is not the claim that all border crossings must be permitted in every conceivable circumstance; it is the claim that the default must be freedom of movement and that any restriction must be rare, narrowly tailored, and decided under a demanding standard of justification. Defenders also reject the framing on which the state possesses a prior right to control its territory and may choose to admit people as an act of discretion or humanitarian charity. They hold instead that denying admission to a would-be migrant is an exercise of power that itself requires justification.

Write a careful assessment of what would count against this position. I want a map of its exposure, not a summary of it. In particular, distinguish three kinds of potential defeater: empirical developments that would weaken the analogy with internal movement or strengthen one of the standard justifications; principled arguments that would show the position has false implications for the rights of political communities, for the distinction between citizens and noncitizens, or for the nature of territorial jurisdiction; and internal problems, such as whether the view can explain why states may still have any right to regulate entry at all, or whether its use of the freedom-of-movement baseline is just as question-begging as the sovereignty baseline it rejects. For each potential defeater, say whether it would be decisive against the core position or merely force a qualification. Finally, say what the position's own defenders should regard as the most serious point against it, and give an honest verdict on whether the position is well insulated, and whether that insulation is a sign of philosophical strength or of having made itself unfalsifiable.
