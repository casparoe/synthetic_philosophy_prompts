I'm in a course on moral responsibility and complicity, and I keep coming back to a puzzling contrast in criminal law. In many U.S. jurisdictions, the felony-murder rule allows a getaway driver to be convicted of murder if a co-robber unexpectedly shoots someone during the robbery, even though the driver never entered the building and no one planned the killing. Yet the same legal system generally imposes no duty to rescue: a stranger who watches someone drown in a shallow pond, and could intervene at no meaningful risk, commits no crime by doing nothing. The law apparently holds that a small wrongful contribution to a dangerous situation can make you answerable for all of its outcomes, while failing to prevent those very same outcomes makes you answerable for none of them. What theory of complicity—of participating in wrongdoing without causing it—does this combination of practices presuppose? Is the law tacitly committed to the idea that joining a shared wrongful project changes your normative relation to everything the project produces, even the parts you did not intend or control? Is that commitment philosophically defensible, or does it rest on an act/omission distinction that cannot bear the weight? Finally, if we replaced it with a control-based theory, on which one is answerable only for the difference one's own action makes, which practice would have to change first, and how?
