I run a small philosophy-and-everyday-life discussion group at our public library, and I have been assigned next month's session: forty minutes, a whiteboard, and an audience of attentive adults with no formal philosophy, on what you should believe when someone every bit as intelligent, honest, and well-informed as you are looks at the same body of evidence and reaches the opposite conclusion. The question stopped being academic for me this spring. My sister and I have comparable professional training and read the same medical studies, yet we ended up on opposite sides of a treatment decision for our father, and I still cannot tell whether holding my ground was integrity or pigheadedness. I do not want to teach my own case; I want to teach the underlying structure of the problem so people can recognize it before they are inside it.

I would like the session built around the contrast between conciliationism and steadfastness as two live answers to a single question: upon learning that an epistemic peer disagrees with you, how far, if at all, should your confidence move, and on what grounds. I am not asking you to write the talk itself; I am asking you to design its architecture.

Please provide:

1. The order in which the components must be introduced, and the dependency structure behind that order. In particular, I need to know whether the audience must first get a grip on what an epistemic peer is or on what shared evidence means, and why the two cannot be made precise independently of each other.

2. One concrete running example that can carry the entire session, with an account of what it must have and what it must lack. I assume it must involve two people of equal relevant competence, a genuinely shared body of evidence, a determinate answer, and no available authority to settle the question on the spot; and that it must avoid prestige asymmetry, checkable answers, and the noise of values and identity. Correct anything wrong in that assumption, and say what each feature is doing. I also want the example built so that it can later make vivid the asymmetry between your access to your own reasoning and your access to the other person's.

3. The misconceptions the audience will predictably bring or form, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be defused rather than corrected later. I am especially worried about treating any respected person as a peer, conflating conciliationism with social compromise or with splitting every difference, taking steadfastness to be mere stubbornness, and reading the peer's disagreement as direct evidence about the world rather than as evidence about one's own reasoning.

4. A single check for understanding at the end that would distinguish someone who has grasped the difference between the two views from someone who has merely learned the labels.

5. What should be deliberately left out at this level, and why, so the session does not slide into skepticism, etiquette, or the psychology of persuasion. I want to know how to name and set aside the tempting detour into what conciliationism says about disagreement over conciliationism itself.

The plan must stand on the whiteboard alone: no readings, no citations, no named authors, and nothing the audience could not take away from the session itself.
