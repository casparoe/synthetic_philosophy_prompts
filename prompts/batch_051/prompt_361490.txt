I am a novelist, and I have been asked by a small country's arts ministry to help design a public-funding scheme for fiction that is not built around point-of-sale sales figures. The proposal under discussion is a national Reading Commons. Twenty years after first publication, any work of fiction becomes eligible for state purchase. The state commissions independent assessors to compute a persistence score for each eligible title: a composite, roughly equally weighted, of (a) the number of public and school libraries holding at least one physical copy of the work, (b) the number of university and college syllabi on which the work has appeared in the preceding five years, and (c) the number of later published books, fiction or nonfiction, that cite or discuss the work. The state then offers the rightsholder a lump sum proportional to the score. If the offer is accepted, the work enters a free public digital lending library, with unrestricted educational use, while the author retains adaptation, translation, and print rights. Rightsholders may refuse the valuation and remain under ordinary copyright. Treat this as a mechanism rather than a statement about the value of fiction. Analyze how it would play out: what it would incentivize among novelists, publishers, teachers and syllabus-builders, librarians, and the assessors themselves; where it would be gamed first; whether it would end up funding teachable, citable books at the expense of strange and unruly ones; what second-order effects it would have on the form and content of fiction; and which failure modes, if any, could be repaired without abandoning the mechanism's intent.
