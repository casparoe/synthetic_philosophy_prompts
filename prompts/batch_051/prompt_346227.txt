Something I keep coming back to: the inside view / outside view distinction is usually applied to construction projects or corporate mergers, but it should apply to empires and states just the same. I mean the version where you first slot the thing into a reference class of broadly similar past cases, start from the distribution of outcomes in that class, and then adjust using the distinctive facts. I want to think through a specific historical episode that way, as if we were sitting inside it without hindsight.

Take the Soviet Union. You're in January 1985. Gorbachev isn't in charge yet, the economy is visibly stagnant, Afghanistan is a mess, but the party has been in power for nearly seventy years, the state survived Stalin, the war, Khrushchev's chaos, and two decades of Brezhnev drift, and most observers figured it would keep lumbering along. By the end of 1991 it's gone.

Analyze that episode through the outside-view / reference-class lens. I want the machinery taken apart, not just the slogan that empires fall. Specifically:

- Build the reference classes you would actually have used in January 1985: what counts as a member, what the boundaries are, and what outcome you are forecasting (territorial breakup? end of one-party rule? elite-led regime change? dissolution into successor states?). Explain how you code the borderline cases, like an empire that slowly decays over decades or one where the state formally continues under a new name and new geography.
- Give base rates from those classes, as a per-decade or per-year hazard for whatever outcome you coded. Don't worry about exact historical statistics; where your numbers are guesses, say so and say which direction the uncertainty should push things. The point I most want to see worked through is that different defensible classes (land empires, multi-ethnic autocracies, one-party communist states, empires whose founding ideology has gone stale) imply quite different base rates, so the class choice is doing a lot of the work.
- Then run the actual outside-view-first update: base rate as the prior, adjusted by the inside-view specifics of the USSR in 1985. The economy, the nationalities, oil prices, party elite incentives, the Afghan war, whatever you think genuinely moves the needle. Where do you end up on collapse or major regime change within, say, fifteen years?
- Then reverse the lens. Does the Soviet case make the outside view look good or bad? What would the method still have failed to predict: timing, mechanism, the mostly peaceful elite-moderated breakup, the fact that a big chunk of the Soviet state and personnel kept running Russia afterwards? And the worry I have: after the fact you can always pick a reference class that makes collapse look almost inevitable, while beforehand nobody could know which class to pick. Say whether the episode actually supports the claim that reference-class base rates beat expert inside-view judgment for large rare events, and be explicit about how much of that verdict depends on the coding and class choices you already made.

Stay in the forecasting stance for the first three parts, then step out of it for the evaluation. I don't need a summary of what happened; I need a serious run of the method, with its weaknesses left visible.
