I am a PhD candidate in epistemology, and I teach a third-year undergraduate unit on knowledge and evidence. The class has 22 students and meets for one 80-minute session. I want a runnable classroom activity — a game, role-play, or structured deliberation with real choices or small stakes — that makes participants feel the force of the dogmatism paradox before it is named. They should come away seeing that the move from knowing something to treating any counterevidence as misleading is both apparently licensed and apparently unacceptable, and that resolving the tension requires saying something more subtle than simply trusting one's first-order evidence or always deferring to new evidence.

Here is the target idea, stated informally: if you know some proposition on the basis of solid first-hand evidence, then you can reason that any evidence that would tell against that proposition must itself be misleading, and this seems to justify declining to examine the counterevidence. Yet there is also something epistemically arrogant about refusing to look. The activity should make students actually confront a choice with this structure and feel both pulls at once.

Please design the activity in enough detail that I could run it tomorrow: exact rules, roles, materials, timing for the 80-minute session, and small stakes or payoffs that do not require real money. Then give a debrief that maps specific moments in what the students did onto the relevant distinctions — knowledge, evidence, the closure of knowledge under known entailment (if you know p, and know that p entails not-q, you can come to know not-q), first-order versus higher-order evidence, and what it is for evidence to be misleading. The students should run into the problem in the activity before the debrief names it; there should be no lecture up front, and the term dogmatism should not appear until afterwards.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with a concrete fix for each failure mode, and be explicit about which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied in the debrief. Keep the whole response under 1,200 words.
