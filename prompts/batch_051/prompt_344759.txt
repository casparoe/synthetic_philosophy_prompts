So I was talking to someone at an AI lab, off the record, and they described a policy they're actually debating. The basic idea: no safety-relevant claim about a frontier model goes to the board unless it's supported by an interpretability report written by a second, separate model. But then someone on the alignment team pointed out that this just moves the question up a level — how do you know wether the second model was actually reading the first model's internals, rather than producing the style of report it was trained to produce? So the proposal got a recursive rule attached. Every interpreter has to be interpreted by another model, and that one by another, until the chain bottoms out. Here's the part they can't settle: the terminal check is a human, but the human is only allowed to read the last link in the chain — the explanation of the explanation — not the original model or any of the earlier reports. And auditors are paid a bonus whenever they catch the model one level below them making a mistake, even if the mistake didn't change the final conclusion. I'd like you to game this out for me: what incentives does it create for the auditors and for the people training them, how should we expect the reports to drift as they move up the chain, where does the scheme acutally collapse, and is the recursion doing real work here or just laundering the trust problem through extra floors?
