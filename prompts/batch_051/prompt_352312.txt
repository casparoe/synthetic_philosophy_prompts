As a software engineer, I keep reading the Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec cases as a recursion problem, and the following argument has been bothering me. (1) A national group — a community with a shared language, distinctive institutions, and a territorial homeland — has a prima facie right to self-determination, including secession when it forms a clear majority in that territory. (2) That principle seems to explain why the Scottish, Catalan, and Québécois independence claims look legitimate. (3) But the test is self-similar at every scale: if a region within a state can invoke it, then a sub-region within that region — a county, a city, a borough, even a neighborhood with a strong local identity — can make the same move, and so can a sub-region within that. (4) Every successful secession would create a new state with its own internal minorities, to whom the principle would immediately grant the same right. (5) So the principle does not yield a stable map of nation-states; it yields a recursive cascade that stops only when no two people can be forced to share a polity. (6) Since no plausible political principle could mandate that outcome, the original principle must be false. Where does this argument go wrong? In particular, can a defender of self-determination draw a non-ad-hoc line that blocks step (5) without also blocking the claims in step (2)? If forced to choose, I would rather keep the legitimacy of the claims in (2) than accept the cascade in (5), but I want to know what holding that line costs.
