A transitional pact contains the following amnesty clause: "Amnesty shall be granted for violations of human rights carried out in the course of the conflict in furtherance of a political objective, provided the applicant makes complete and truthful disclosure of every violation for which amnesty is sought. Amnesty shall be refused where the violation was carried out principally for private profit, private enmity, or personal ill-will."

After the transition, a former military officer applies for amnesty for deaths caused by an automated targeting system he designed and deployed during the final year of the conflict. The system selected targets from intercepted communications and authorized lethal strikes without human review of each decision. The officer did not select any particular target and never saw the names of many of the dead, but he wrote the selection rule, approved deployment, and knew from pre-deployment tests that the system would produce a predictable rate of civilian deaths. He argues that the deaths were violations carried out in furtherance of a political objective, not for private profit or ill-will, and that he can make complete disclosure because the system's logs are complete. The victims' representatives argue that a death no person chose is not a violation "carried out" by him at all; and if it is not his act, disclosure cannot bring it within the clause.

How should the clause apply to the officer's application under four approaches: (a) the plain words of the clause, (b) its underlying purpose, (c) what the drafters would have said if asked about automated targeting, and (d) what a reasonable reader would take the clause to mean? If the approaches diverge, which should govern, and why?
