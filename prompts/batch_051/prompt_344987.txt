Suppose a national arts council decides to fund fiction by its long-run public value rather than its first-year sales, and replaces its advance-grant program for novelists with the following mechanism. For each traditionally published novel, the council tracks three indicators over a 20-year window: library copy-years, defined as the total number of copies held in public libraries summed across each year; the number of subsequently published novels whose acknowledgments or author's notes explicitly name the book as an influence; and the number of school and university syllabi on which the book appears, as self-reported by instructors. At the end of the window, the council pays the author, or their estate, a dividend proportional to a weighted sum of the three indicators. No panel ever evaluates the work's quality. To provide income during the window, the council allows authors to sell the future dividend rights to private investors at any point after publication; the buyer then receives the eventual payout. Treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed, not as a claim about what literature is for. What incentives does it create for the council itself, for authors, publishers, librarians, instructors, and investors? How would each group game the indicators, and what would the scheme actually select for? What second-order effects would it have on literary culture and on the public value of fiction, and which failure modes, if any, could be repaired without gutting the mechanism?
