I'm in a contemporary ethics seminar and we've spent two weeks on Scanlon's contractualism. A classmate sent around a study guide they found online that summarizes the view and the aggregation problem like this: Scanlon says, very roughly, that an act is wrong if it would be ruled out by every set of principles for the general regulation of behavior that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed and unforced general agreement. Because the test looks at each person's reasons for rejection one at a time, rather than adding them across people, contractualism is anti-aggregative. That means you should save one person from a serious harm rather than a billion people from a mild itch, since the one person's complaint is stronger than any single itch-sufferer's complaint and the complaints can't be summed. But it also seems to mean that if you can save either one stranger or five strangers from drowning, you have no reason to save the five, since no individual among the five has a stronger claim than the one, and their claims can't be added up. Scanlon's fix is a tie-breaker: when each side has an equally strong complaint, you're allowed to let the numbers count and save the five. Critics say this is just aggregation in disguise, because something has to explain why five equal complaints outweigh one equal complaint. So Scanlon's view either collapses into a kind of utilitarianism or is stuck saying that saving one is just as reasonable as saving five.

I've read a bit of this before, and the tie-breaker part feels like a caricature, and the final either/or seems way too strong, but I can't tell weather that's because the guide is wrong or because I'm misremembering. Can you go through the summary and separate what's accurate from what's oversimplified or just wrong? And then tell me whether that last either/or actually follows, or whether there are other moves a contractualist could make that the guide just ignores.
