I've been stewing on a distinction from a seminar on collective responsibility, and I'd rather test it on someone who won't just nod along. I call it the Inert Support Principle. The idea is that complicity doesn't require making a causal difference. What matters is whether you deliberately throw your will in with the wrongdoer's. Picture a mob beating a man; you join the edge of the crowd and shout for them to get on with it. As it happens your shouting changes nothing at all—the beating would have happened exactly the same without you. On my principle you're still complicit, not in the beating itself but in the wrong of it, because you aligned yourself with what the mob were doing and you would have done more if more had been needed. Same, I think, for the getaway driver who never gets called, or the board member who votes for a decision he knows is purely symbolic because the chair controls everything. I'm not saying any of these people caused the wrong or bare the same blame as the main actors; I'm saying there's a real, distinct thing—complicity—that they're answerable for.

So the principle, stated baldly: an action that makes no causal contribution to a wrong can still make you complicit in it, provided you knowingly adopt the wrongdoer's end and do something whose point is to further it. Tell me straight whether this is coherent, whether it already exists under a better name (and where I've mangled it), and what the three strongest objections are—do they show it's wrong, or just that it needs trimming round the edges? Please write your answer in British English.
