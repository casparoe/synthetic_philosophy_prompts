one of those arguments i keep coming back to is the performance legitimacy case for authoritarian rule: the idea that a regime can be legitimate, or at least worth having, if it delivers prosperity and order, and that China and especially Singapore are the standard exhibit A, competent authoritarian states that get growth and stability which messier democracies struggle to match. usually it gets treated as a straight trade, liberty for performance. i want to stress-test one specific premise: that the good outcomes are the regime's achievement, caused by its competence, rather than just stuff that happened on its watch.

suppose over the next few decades the machinery that actually produces prosperity and order — planning, logistics, permitting, forecasting, enforcement — becomes mostly AI systems that are basically commodity products. a democracy and an autocracy can buy the same capacity, and the regime's vaunted competence stops being the thing that makes the difference. in that world the argument looses its causal story: the outcomes are no longer something the autocracy did.

does the performance legitimacy argument survive this, and if so what does it have to become? maybe it has to turn into a claim about who can be trusted with the AI levers, or who can correct the system when it fails. or maybe it just dies, because the one thing it was pointed at — that this regime, not the alternative, produces the prosperity and order — is no longer true. say which premise is doing the work, and whether a repaired version is still an argument for authoritarian rule or has quietly turned into an argument for something else.
