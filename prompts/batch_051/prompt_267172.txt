I am a novelist, and the element of worldbuilding I most often get wrong—or leave arbitrary—is the legal order. Across projects I invent city-states, empires, and revolutionary communes, and I need a reusable procedure, grounded in the rule-of-law debate running through Fuller, Raz, and Waldron, for deciding which features of a fictional legal system should be lawlike and which should defect from the rule of law, and for rendering each defect as the specific failure it is rather than as authorial carelessness. Do not give me a bibliography or reading list; I need the theory translated into a working method.

I have enough theoretical literacy to follow a demanding procedure, but I am not a legal scholar, and I will not be stopping to read while I draft. The procedure must be runnable in one afternoon per polity, produce something like a one-page defect map I can pin above the desk, and be reusable across projects and legible enough that my editor can see from the map why each feature of a legal system stands where it does. It should begin by letting me identify the kind of legal order I have sketched and the domain of conduct it claims to govern.

Do not let the procedure collapse into the advice to make my legal systems just. I need it to keep at least these things separate: failures of lawmaking in Fuller's sense, such as rule by ad hoc decree or by secret, retroactive, contradictory, impossible, or violently unstable rules; failures of congruence between declared rules and what officials actually do; and the further dimension Waldron emphasizes, namely whether those subject to coercion are given notice, a hearing, reasons, and a chance to contest. It should also preserve Raz's point that conformity to the rule of law is a virtue of form, not a guarantee of justice: a fictional regime may be procedurally immaculate and substantively monstrous, and the procedure should be able to mark that combination without confusing legality with decency.

For each step, state the question I ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, when the step terminates in a no, and when it must stop and hand the decision to me because the matter is one of values rather than theory. For every defect the procedure identifies, force me to say whether it is intended for plot or theme, and if so what kind of textual or structural signal will make it legible to a reader as intended; otherwise, the defect should be marked for deletion before drafting. The procedure should also tell me which defects make law in a domain simply absent, as opposed to defects that make law present but bad.

Then run the procedure on three of my current polities. (1) A port city whose customs rules are unwritten, change without notice, and are applied retroactively to goods already landed. (2) An empire with a public, clear, stable code whose magistrates nevertheless decide cases by secret edicts that contradict the code. (3) A revolutionary commune that refuses general rules and permanent officials on principle, resolving each dispute in its particular circumstances. The third is a setting I intend to keep, so if the procedure returns an answer I will not like, do not soften it; say what the theory says and then show exactly where the value judgment takes over.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where it would misfire when a fictional world departs from real-world assumptions about enforcement or human motivation, where it would confuse a society's substantive values with its legality, and what kind of revision pressure it can and cannot resolve once a draft has already committed me to a particular plot.
