Please describe the most important implications of expected value fanaticism for how a small philanthropic foundation should reason about a grant whose expected value rests almost entirely on a tiny probability of an astronomically large good. By "expected value fanaticism" I mean the commitment to letting an option's expected value guide what one chooses even when that expected value is generated almost entirely by a tiny probability of an enormously large good. Take the following as the working case. The foundation must allocate a fixed budget. One proposal would fund a speculative project that, on its authors' own estimate, has a 10^{-8} probability of averting a catastrophe that would otherwise destroy the lives of 10^{12} people across the long-term future; that yields an expected value of 10,000 lives. A competing proposal would reliably save 2,000 present-day lives for the same budget. A board member argues that the first proposal has five times the expected value of the second and that the foundation should therefore fund it. Lay out the strongest considerations for treating that arithmetic as decisive, the strongest considerations for refusing to let an expected-value calculation of this kind settle the decision, and then state which implications of expected value fanaticism you regard as most important for a foundation actually making this choice. In particular, address how much confidence a foundation should place in a unique-event probability estimate such as 10^{-8}, whether there is any stake large enough that a sufficiently small probability of it should dominate finite certain benefits, and what would have to be true for the fanatical grant genuinely to deserve the money.
