As a retired natural scientist who has recently turned to philosophical questions, I have been working through the literature on hedonism, and I find myself puzzled by what is apparently called the heterogeneity problem. Benthamite quantitative hedonism seems to require that all pleasant experiences—solving a proof, eating a ripe pear, hearing a child laugh, taking morphine, resting a sore muscle—share a single felt quality, and that this quality is what makes them good. But introspection does not reveal any one common thread. The experiences seem good for quite different reasons, and they do not feel like instances of a single thing at all.

The passage that makes me wonder whether the problem is fatal occurs early in the second chapter of Mill's Utilitarianism. There, Mill answers the charge that hedonism is a doctrine fit for swine by denying that pleasures differ only in quantity. He holds that some pleasures are higher than others, that competent judges acquainted with both kinds always prefer the higher kind even when it brings more discontent, and that they would not surrender it for any amount of the lower. He grounds the distinction in the actual preferences of those who have experienced both.

I would like your actual credence that the move Mill makes in that passage, suitably cleaned up, solves the heterogeneity problem for hedonism—that is, that distinguishing qualities or kinds of pleasure is enough to explain what all prudentially good experiences have in common without collapsing qualitative hedonism into a disguised preferences-based or perfectionist theory.

Please give me a number between zero and one. Then decompose your credence into at least four components, with a rough probability and a one- or two-sentence warrant for each. At least these should be included: (1) that the heterogeneity problem is a genuine challenge for Benthamite quantitative hedonism in the first place; (2) that Mill's competent-judge preference test tracks a real qualitative difference in the pleasures themselves, rather than tracking status, identity, or aversion; (3) that the notion of higher and lower pleasure can be made non-circular without already invoking an independent theory of value; and (4) that a residual common element of pleasantness can still do the unifying work hedonism requires. Add any further terms you think belong in the calculation. Show how you combine them. Then tell me which term your final credence is most sensitive to, and what evidence, argument, or introspective experiment would most move that term.
