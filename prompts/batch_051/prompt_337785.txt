I live in a townhouse assocation, and someone has proposed a governance rule we're half-seriously calling the fence clause. I've been reading about Chesterton's fence (the idea, roughly, that you shouldn't tear down a fence until you know why someone put it up) and about Burke's version of the same instinct — that settled rules and institutions carry accumulated experience that no individual can fully state. The proposal: before any bylaw or longstanding practice can be repealed or substantially altered, the member proposing the change has to file a fence statement. It has to give the strongest honest account they can construct of what problem the old rule solved, even if they themselves think that account is wrong, and it has to specify a measurable indicator of that problem. If the change passes, it takes effect only as a two-year trial, with the indicator measured at the end. If the indicator has worsened past a preset threshold, the old rule snaps back into place automatically and the proposer pays a fine into the reserve fund, scaled to the age of the rule they removed. If the indicator holds up, the repeal becomes permanent and the proposer gets a small dues credit.

I'm not asking whether this is a good idea in the abstract; I want it treated as a mechanism. Walk me through what it does to which bylaws actually get challenged, to how the fence statements get written, to how people game the choice of indicator and the trial period, and to what kind of person ends up running the association under this system. Where does it break first, and can the worst failure modes be patched without turning the thing into a flat status quo bias? Give me a committed answer — take a definite position on what the dominant effect would be and defend it, rather than laying out all the possibilities. And write in American English.
