I head the policy staff of a national bioethics commission. My staff draft advisory opinions and legislative testimony on end-of-life care, human-subjects research, genetic technologies, and markets in organs and tissues. Nearly every submission we receive invokes human dignity as a decisive reason, often for opposite conclusions: assisted dying is demanded in the name of dignity and forbidden in its name; a kidney market is condemned as an offense to dignity and defended as respecting the dignity of poor people whose choices would otherwise be paternalized. We need a reusable procedure, runnable in under an hour by staff with no formal philosophical training, for deciding whether an appeal to dignity in a submission is doing genuine argumentative work or is redundant, circular, or merely rhetorical. Our outputs must be defensible before courts, legislators, and the public.

Write the procedure. Ground it in what the philosophical debate between Kantian and deflationary conceptions of dignity actually establishes, without asking the staff to read the literature. By Kantian I mean the idea that dignity is not one value to be weighed against others but a status of unconditional, non-negotiable worth that places absolute limits on how a person may be treated regardless of aggregate benefit. By deflationary I mean the position, pressed in bioethics by Ruth Macklin among others, that dignity adds nothing to respect for persons, autonomy, and the avoidance of harm, and that its apparent force comes from vagueness and rhetorical weight. The procedure should ask a fixed sequence of questions; for each, state what counts as a sufficient answer; give rules for when an appeal to dignity should be set aside as redundant or circular; identify the points where the procedure stops and flags the issue as one for the commissioners to settle on values grounds; and mark which steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of a procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a submission opposing a regulated market in kidneys because selling a body part offends human dignity; (2) a submission supporting assisted dying because keeping a competent terminally ill person in total dependence violates their dignity; and (3) a submission opposing the creation of embryos for research because it treats human life as a mere means. For each, show the questions, the answers they force, and the outcome. Ensure at least one outcome is one the commission will not like, and say which and why. End by stating what the procedure cannot do when two sides each make a coherent but incompatible appeal to dignity.
