I've been trying to settle a running argument with myself about the regulative principle of worship. The structural case for it seems genuinely hard to beat: if God has instituted how he is to be worshipped, then adding uncommanded elements is just making the service about human preferences, and there is no obvious stopping point once you allow one. The Westminster Confession states the point in chapter 21, roughly, by saying that the acceptable way of worshipping God is instituted by himself and limited by his revealed will, so that he may not be worshipped according to human devices or any way not prescribed in Scripture. Whenever I think about golden calves or people inventing new sacraments, that reasoning feels unassailable.

But then I hit a wall. I can't shake the intuition that a congregation singing uninspired hymns, or using an organ, is in fact worshipping God in an acceptable way even though I can't point to a New Testament command for exacly that. And when I try to hold the regulative principle strictly, I get pulled toward exclusive psalmody, no instruments, no holidays, and a bunch of positions I don't have any independent grip on. I don't want the usual pat answer about "elements versus circumstances" unless you can show it isn't just special pleading. Give me the strongest version of both sides: what the regulative principle really buys if applied consistently, and what the intuition is tracking if you refuse to debunk it. Then tell me what it would cost to give up the principle, what it would cost to give up the intuition, and whether there is a distinction that can hold both without turning the principle into a formality.
