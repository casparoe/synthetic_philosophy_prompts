I lead the model-validation group at a firm that builds climate-risk simulations for insurers and infrastructure lenders. Four times a year our group reviews candidate relationships that analysts want promoted into the core model. Some candidates are fitted correlations mined from satellite or financial data; others are derivations from long-established physical theory. The promotion decision matters enormously: a relationship that is promoted is treated as invariant, held fixed even when the model is run under scenarios far outside the historical record, such as three degrees of warming or a rapid transition away from fossil fuels. A relationship that is not promoted is treated as a removable artifact of its estimation sample: refitted each cycle, damped when extrapolating, and never allowed to drive the tail of a loss distribution. Our current process is a mix of statistical significance tests and seniority, and it has produced two embarrassing failures: we held fixed a tight correlation that vanished out of sample, and we repeatedly refitted a thermodynamically derived constraint because it looked like a black box. The validation committee must sign off on every promotion, and it has asked us to replace the present ad hoc practice with something defensible.

I know there is a substantial philosophical literature on what distinguishes a law of nature from a merely accurate or useful empirical generalization, but my group is not equipped to translate that literature into practice. Please write a checklist my team can actually run in a two-hour meeting, with no assumption that the reviewers know the philosophical vocabulary. You may refer to positions and arguments informally, and I will fill in any scholarly citations myself. The checklist should guide us through the question of what makes a candidate lawlike rather than merely well-supported: what a Humean best-system view would demand, what a governing-account or dispositionalist view would demand, and where the two traditions agree operationally. It should specify which questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and which steps are unavoidably judgment calls rather than facts. Please be explicit about where the procedure will misfire or silently import a substantive metaphysical commitment.

Then run the procedure on the following cases and report what it outputs, including the reasoning in a form my committee could read. Case A: a relationship between temperature and the amount of water vapor air can hold, derivable from basic thermodynamics and already assumed in several submodels, which some group members want to relax because it conflicts with a recent satellite reanalysis over the Southern Ocean. Case B: a strong fitted relationship between a regional drought index and wildfire burned area, estimated over 2000 to 2020; the fit is excellent, but there is no agreed causal mechanism and land-management practices changed substantially during the period. Case C: a cross-sectional power-law relationship between a firm's revenue and its reported emissions, estimated from the last five years of disclosures, which the underwriting team wants promoted because it would sharply improve the model's tractability in carbon-transition scenarios. For each case, tell me clearly whether the checklist recommends promotion, demotion, or handoff to committee judgment, and flag specifically any recommendation the team will find unwelcome and why the procedure forces it anyway.
