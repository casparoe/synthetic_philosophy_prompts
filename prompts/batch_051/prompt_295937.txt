I'm in a Bayesian decision theory seminar and we spent way too long on Good's theorem: for an expected-utility maximizer who conditionalizes, a costless piece of information can never have negative expected value; at worst it's worth zero. A classmate argued that every alleged counterexample cheats in a boring way — someone else finds out what you know and uses it against you, the information isn't really free, your preferences shift after you see it, or you're getting utility from knowing or not knowing itself (dread, curiosity, etc.). I want a case that doesn't lean on any of those and still gives a negative value of information.

Construct a one-paragraph decision problem with a single agent: no strategic interaction, no disclosure duties, no reputation effects, no one else observing what she knows. Her preferences over final outcomes stay fixed, she maximizes expected utility, and she conditionalizes normally. The information is costless and decision-relevant (after at least one possible signal she would choose a different act than she would without it), yet ex ante she rationally prefers not to recieve it. I suspect the only room left is in one of the theorem's quiet assumptions about observation being inert — that getting the signal doesn't change how well, or whether, she can carry out an act. If that's the right diagnosis, make it fail in a mundane, ordinary-life way. If you think the theorem has to break somewhere else instead, make that work, but the case has to stay realistic.

Then do the accounting: what she does without the signal and why, what she does after each possible signal, the ex ante expected utility with and without the signal, and exactly which premise of the proof fails. Finish with the minimal condition you would add to the theorem to rule out your case, whether that condition is ad hoc or a distinction good Bayesians should have been drawing all along, and one line on how a defender of the theorem would try to dismiss the example.
