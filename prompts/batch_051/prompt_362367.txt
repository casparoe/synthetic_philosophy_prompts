I'd read a bit of Bentham on this a while ago and remember thinking he'd already shredded the whole move: he treats "against nature" as a disguised appeal to disgust or to majority taste, and he argues that consensual acts people call "irregular" don't harm anyone except by way of other people's offended sensibilities, which the law shouldn't be in the business of protecting. But when I tried to reconstruct the argument I couldn't get past the slogan.

So can you take the one-liner "it's unnatural, so it's wrong" and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making? For instance: the act is statistically rare; it goes against the apparent biological function of parts or drives; it isn't observed in other animals; it violates natural law or a created order; it's a misuse or waste of a faculty; or it's just not what most people are inclined to do. For each reading, tell me (a) how plausible the underlying claim is when applied to a consensual act like same-sex sex, (b) whether that reading actually gets you from a fact about nature to a moral or legal prohibition, and (c) how Bentham's line of attack — the appeal-to-disgust point, the harm point, and the sliding between different senses of "nature" — fares against it. Then tell me which of these readings my cousin's conclusion actually needs, and what extra premise they'd have to supply before the argument stops being a disguised appeal to there own stomach.
