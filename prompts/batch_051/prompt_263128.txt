Before I read further in the literature on biological function, I want the specification that any adequate theory has to meet. Write me the desiderata for a theory of the function of a biological trait, mechanism, or process: the full list of what an account of "the function of X is to Y" must deliver, with the case or consideration that forces each requirement, and a judgment about which requirements are non-negotiable and which are refinements a theory could reasonably drop. Where two requirements sit uneasily together, flag the pair and say what it would cost to want them both anyway.

I care about the function talk biologists and philosophers actually use — in explanations, in ascriptions of malfunction, in comparative anatomy — not just about regimenting one corner of it.

The list should at least be forced to take a position on these kinds of cases (add more if they earn their place):

- A heart with a malformed valve that cannot currently pump blood. It still has a function, doesn't it? So a theory has to make room for function without current performance, and for malfunction.
- The thumping sound a heart makes. A heart does many things; not all of them are its function. A theory must draw a non-arbitrary line between function and accidental effect.
- A vestigial structure, such as the degenerate eye of a cave-dwelling fish, that once did something and now does little or nothing.
- A structure coopted for a use it was not originally selected for — the standard example is feathers, apparently first for thermoregulation and only later for flight. A theory must say whether, and when, the new use becomes a function.
- A new mutation that happens to improve survival in the present but has no history of selection behind it.
- A molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a dog that arose by cosmic accident, with no ancestors and no evolutionary past at all. Does its heart have a function? A theory must answer, and not by shrugging.
- A brain that can read, even though reading is far too recent to have shaped the selection of the brain.
- The hierarchy problem: a heart valve has a function relative to the heart, and the heart has a function relative to the organism. A theory must say whether these are the same sense of "function" or two senses, and must not equivocate between them.

For each desideratum, explain what goes wrong if a theory ignores it, and whether ignoring it is a fatal failing or merely a cost. Then flag any pair of desiderata that you think no single account can jointly satisfy, and say what that tension tells us before we start choosing sides.

Finally, score the two leading accounts briefly against the list: the selected-effects (etiological) account, on which a function is roughly what a trait was selected for, and the causal-role account, on which a function is roughly how a component contributes to the capacities of a containing system. Keep the scoring short; it exists only to check that the desiderata actually discriminate. And tell me which single requirement does the most to force revision in one of the two accounts.

A table is fine for part of the answer, but the rationale for each item needs to be in prose.
