I have been trying to work out what actually matters in my own survival, and my verdicts about particular cases are pulling me in different directions. I suspect that numerical identity is not what matters, but I cannot state what else does. I want you to do reflective equilibrium on my behalf: state the best principle, or a short list of candidate principles, that captures as much of my pattern of verdicts as possible, test it against some further cases, and tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Here are the cases.

1. The standard teletransporter. I am scanned; this destroys my body immediately and painlessly; the information is sent to Mars, where a single new body is assembled that is molecule-for-molecule like my old one and has all my memories, projects, quirks, and dispositions. The person who wakes on Mars seems to remember stepping into the machine moments ago. My verdict: this is as good as ordinary survival. I would step in without special dread, and I would not complain that I had been killed and replaced.

2. The delayed teletransporter. The scan is the same, but my body is not destroyed until the Mars body has been built, awakened, and observed for an hour. I watch a video of my counterpart on Mars talking and joking exactly as I would. The technician then says that everything is working and that my original body will now be painlessly destroyed. My verdict: I would refuse, and I would regard the upcoming event as my being killed and replaced. The fact that the counterpart is flourishing on Mars does not console me, because I am here, about to die, and he is there, a different person. This verdict feels as strong as the first, and I know the two sit badly together.

3. Hemisphere fission. A surgeon removes my brain and places each intact hemisphere into a separate healthy, emptied body; my original body is discarded. Suppose, for the sake of the case, that each hemisphere was a full psychological duplicate, so each resulting person wakes with my memories, traits, projects, and sense of having been me. My verdict: I am not numerically identical with either successor, but I would not regard this as death. In the sense that matters, I survive in both; the fact that there are two successors neither halves nor dilutes what matters, and if the alternative were certain death I would consent. I also think it makes no difference whether there are two successors or many.

4. Non-destructive duplication. A scanner reads my brain while I sit awake and unchanged, and produces an awake duplicate in the next room who has all my memories, projects, quirks, and dispositions. I am then told that one of the two of us, selected at random, will shortly be killed, and that I cannot influence which. My verdict: which body survives is the only thing I ultimately care about; the duplicate is not me. If this body is killed, I am dead, and the fact that a distinct person continues to live my life is not a form of my survival. Yet I care far more about the duplicate than about a stranger, and I would accept the random selection as giving me a fair chance, not as making either outcome lossless.

5. Gradual whole-brain replacement. Over a year, each of my neurons is replaced one at a time by a functionally equivalent artificial unit, while I remain conscious or sleep normally, my memories and character are unchanged, and every test shows a seamless continuation of my mental life. At the end the resulting person is made entirely of artificial neurons. My verdict: I survive throughout this process; there is no point at which I cease and another person begins, any more than there is with ordinary metabolic turnover.

I can feel that these verdicts are not obviously jointly satisfiable. In particular, I seem to think that a one-to-one teletransport is survivable, that creating a successor before destroying the original is not survivable, that fission with the original gone is survivable in all successors, and that duplication with the original still present does not extend my survival into the duplicate. I want you to state precisely the best principle, or small set of candidate principles, that captures as much of this pattern as possible. For each candidate principle, say which case motivates each of its clauses, give its verdicts on the further cases below, and state honestly which of my five verdicts it cannot accommodate. If the only principles that preserve all five verdicts are contrived, say so, and tell me which verdicts the cleanest principle would have me abandon.

Further cases: (a) a scanner makes ten accurate copies of me and destroys the original; (b) the delayed teletransportation case, except that before the technician can destroy my body it is destroyed by an unrelated accident, while the Mars counterpart remains healthy; (c) the gradual replacement of case 5, except that it is compressed into a single one-hour operation during which I am fully conscious and experience no gap in my mental life; (d) a scan-and-transfer procedure that copies my psychology into a new body and leaves the original brain in a permanent dreamless coma rather than destroying it.
