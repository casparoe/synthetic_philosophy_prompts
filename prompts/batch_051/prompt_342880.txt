In a criminal law seminar I'm taking, the discussion keeps collapsing into a slogan. We were considering a defendant convicted under a fairly obscure administrative regulation — as far as anyone could establish, the rule was never published in any form a conscientious layperson would encounter — and when someone pointed out that the defendant seems to have had no realistic way of knowing he was violating it, another student cut the discussion off with the maxim "ignorance of the law is no excuse." The implication was that that settles it: the state may bind people to rules regardless of whether those people could have known about them.

I find the maxim doing much more work than it can plausibly do in one sentence, and I suspect it compresses several different claims into a single slogan. I'd like to go to the next meeting with a map of possible readings rather than an argument I'll lose for lack of distinctions. Can you lay out the distinct precise principles that "ignorance of the law is no excuse" might be taken to assert? In particular, I'd like the readings separated along a few fault lines:

- actual knowledge ("the defendant in fact knew the act was illegal") versus constructive knowledge or fair notice ("a reasonable or diligent person could have found out, whether or not this defendant did");
- ignorance of the existence of a prohibition versus ignorance that one's conduct falls within its scope, versus a normative mistake about whether the conduct is really wrong;
- whether the maxim applies uniformly to prohibitions on conduct wrong in itself (mala in se) and to purely regulatory offenses with no pre-legal moral valence (mala prohibita);
- and whether it is best read as a claim about the criminal law's rules of liability, about moral blameworthiness, about what citizens owe the state, or about the state's authority to issue binding directives irrespective of individual epistemic circumstances.

For each candidate reading, I'd like a verdict: trivial, plausible but in need of qualification, or false. And then the underlying practical question: which of those readings, if any, would actually support the student's use of the maxim to brush aside the objection in a case where the rule was genuinely inaccessible to a diligent person? I keep wanting to say the maxim is only defensible for rules that are publicly promulgated and for conduct the defendant could have recognized as wrong without legal scholarship, but I want the full menu before I commit.
