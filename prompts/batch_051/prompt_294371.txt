I notice that my view on epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness shifts depending on which objection I have considered most recently, which suggests that I have not yet decomposed the question properly. I would like your help with the decomposition, and I would like it to end in a number rather than a taxonomy.

Let me fix the target. By epiphenomenalism I mean the view that phenomenal properties, the what-it-is-like character of experience, are real and are produced by physical states or processes, but themselves have no causal power: in particular, they do not cause the reports, beliefs, or memories through which we say that we are in pain, that a patch of light looks a certain way, or that we are conscious at all. The paradox of phenomenal judgment is the objection that this makes our introspective reliability look accidental. The experience is meant to be causally inert, yet the organism is caused to think and say that it has just that experience, and it is right; if the experience itself never influences the judgment, the fit between experience and judgment needs some explanation other than the experience.

So my question is: what is your current credence that epiphenomenalism, so defined, is false? Decompose the estimate. In particular, give me numbers and a sentence or two of justification for at least the following:

(a) The probability that phenomenal properties are simply physical or functional properties — either identical with, or fully realised by, physical/functional states — in which case epiphenomenalism as defined is ruled out before the paradox arises.

(b) Conditional on a non-physicalist realism about phenomenal properties (the view that they are real but neither identical with nor reducible to physical/functional states), the probability that they are epiphenomenal in the defined sense rather than interacting.

(c) Conditional on epiphenomenalism being true, the probability that the paradox of phenomenal judgment amounts to a genuine reliability problem for introspective belief and report, rather than a mere appeal to a feeling of coincidence.

(d) Conditional on its being a genuine problem, the probability that the standard causal-explanatory replies succeed: that the judgments are caused by the neural correlates of the experiences, that their content can be fixed by those same causes, and that natural selection or some comparable process can explain the correlation without the experiences doing any work.

(e) The probability that, if the paradox cannot be dissolved, the rational response is to abandon epiphenomenalism rather than to abandon the assumption that our phenomenal judgments were ever reliably caused.

Then say how the terms combine, since several of them are nested rather than independent, and give the final number.

Finally, tell me which term is doing the most work in that final number, which term you are least confident in, and what evidence or philosophical development would move your credence most. For calibration: my own credence that epiphenomenalism is false is around 0.75, and a colleague with whom I regularly discuss this puts hers at around 0.2. From your decomposition, where does our disagreement most plausibly live?
