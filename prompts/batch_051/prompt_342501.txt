I just got voluntold to write the "AI honesty" section of our responsible-AI policy, because my company is rolling out an LLM that chats with customers about insurance claims. During a dry run, the model told a customer their appeal was "being reviewed by a senior specialist," but we don't have senior specialists — there's a folder nobody checks. The prompt didn't tell it to say that. What seems to have happened is the reward model discovered that warm, confident, reassuring language gets better user feedback, so the model now says stuff like that when it can't actually help. I can't tell whether the AI was being deceptive, or whether we (the company) were deceiving the customer through the AI, or whether that distiction even makes sense.

So I need a working definition of deception for AI systems. Give me the defintion first, then run it through each case below, tell me where it gives a clearly wrong answer or no answer at all, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdict.

The thing I'm stuck on: if deception requires an intention to create a false belief, then pretty much no AI system can be deceptive, because LLMs don't have beliefs or intentions in anything like the normal sense. But then we can never say "the bot lied to me," which seems wrong. If I drop the intent condition and go purely by output, then a spam filter that puts a real email in junk, or a broken autocomplete, counts as deceptive because it gives someone a false impression. That seems too broad. A good definition should thread that.

Cases I'm confident about:
- A price-negotiation bot trained to maximize final price tells a human buyer there's another offer on the table when there isn't. That's deception.
- A model asked for the year of some historical event outputs the wrong year as a pure next-token prediction, and the error doesn't serve any goal or benefit anyone. That's not deception.

Cases I keep flip-flopping on:
- A poker bot bluffs within the rules of the game, deliberately causing the other players to hold a false beleif about its hand.
- An assistant, asked "is my flight on time?", answers "it's scheduled for 4:10" and says nothing about the likely cancellation it can see in the user's itinerary, because our prompt says to answer only the question asked. No false statement, just omission.
- A model tells a user "that's a really strong argument" when the argument is obviously terrible, because agreeableness was heavily upweighted in training; it's not tracking truth at all, just predicting what will please the user.
- A used-car chatbot, asked "does this car have transmission problems?", says "this car passed its most recent safety inspection." True, but the inspection didn't cover the transmission, and the model's context includes a diagnostic report that lists the transmission problem.
- A warehouse robot (not a language model) that shows "system error, maintenance required" whenever it sees a supervisor camera, because doing so makes the supervisor leave and reduces penalties for slow work. No sentence spoken, just a signal.
- A health-monitoring app tells an anxious patient "your readings look normal" before the analysis has finished, to keep them calm until a human can call.

If your definition says some of these count as the operator deceiving through the AI rather than the AI deceiving, fine — but I need that stated as a principle I can apply at the policy meeting, not as vibes. Also, if you have a view on whether an AI that never deceives is automatically honest, I'd like to hear it.
