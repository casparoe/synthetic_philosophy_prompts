Here is a distinction I have been leaning on in reading Bentham's arguments about sexual morality, and I want an honest assessment of it. I call it recursive harm: a harm is recursive when it is caused or amplified by the very prohibition that is later justified by citing it. If I am reading Bentham right, he is doing precisely this when he argues that many of the worst consequences attributed to consensual sexual acts — secrecy, shame, blackmail, ruined reputations, and what we would now call the psychological damage of concealment — are largely produced by the criminal law and by surrounding public sentiment rather than by the acts themselves. On this reading, Bentham's implicit test is that before a harm can justify a prohibition, some credible portion of it must be attributable to the act under a regime of permission, not under the prohibition itself; otherwise the rule is citing as its ground a harm the rule is manufacturing. My worry is that this proves too much. Drug laws, apostasy laws, and sumptuary rules all tend to drive conduct into concealment and thereby generate their own secondary harms. And nothing in the test seems to stop a prohibition from being condemned simply because enforcing it is costly or has bad side effects, which would conflate recursive harm with ordinary iatrogenic effects. So I want to know: is recursive harm a genuine distinction or just a causal story dressed up as a concept? Has this been worked out under an established name? And where exactly does it break — in particular, how should one decide how much of an observed harm to attribute to the act rather than to the prohibition without the attribution collapsing into motivated reasoning? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
