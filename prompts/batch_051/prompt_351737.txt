I have been thinking about fairness in terms of a single distinction, and I want to know how an institution built on that distinction would actually behave, not whether the distinction is morally appealing.

The distinction is between the losses people bring on themselves and the losses that merely befall them. If someone freely makes a wager and loses, the loss is the price of their choice, and society has no duty to undo it. If someone is hurt by circumstances they did not choose and could not realistically have controlled — an unexplained illness, an accident, the collapse of the industry they trained for — the loss is something a fair society should repair. Call these chosen losses and unchosen losses.

Now the institution. The Public Chance Fund is financed by a fixed percentage of everyone's income. Any person may file a claim for a material setback: lost income, disability, medical expenses, destroyed property. A three-member panel rules on each claim within two months, using three tests.

1. If the setback arose from an activity the claimant chose, and the ordinary risks of that activity were ones a reasonable person in the claimant's situation would have understood, the setback is a chosen loss and the claim is denied.

2. If the setback arose from circumstances the claimant neither chose nor could reasonably have foreseen or controlled, it is an unchosen loss and the claim is paid in full, up to a cap.

3. If a chosen activity failed because of an external factor that nobody could reasonably have foreseen — a business destroyed by a once-in-a-century pandemic, a skill made worthless by an unexpected technology — the panel pays the portion of the loss attributable to that external factor, judged against how similar activities ordinarily turn out.

There is an appeals board, and claimants may refile after a year with new evidence about what they could and could not have foreseen.

I want a detailed account of how this system would run in practice. Start with the incentives it creates: which risks people would take, how they would describe their own lives when filing, and how employers and insurers would write contracts around the boundary between what counts as chosen and what counts as unchosen. Then tell me where the system gets gamed first, and by whom — claimants, employers, doctors, the panel itself — and how the gaming would interact with the appeals process. Finally, separate the failure modes that could be fixed by amending the rules from the ones that are intrinsic to trying to classify human lives this way, and say whether the repairs would make the system more or less faithful to its own underlying principle.
