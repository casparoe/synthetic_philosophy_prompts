I am taking a bioethics course this term, and the question of consent has become personal because my sister is considering enrolling in a paid clinical trial for a new immunosuppressant. In a long conversation about it, my pre-law roommate confidently summarized the standard view as follows: valid consent requires competence, information, and voluntariness; competence means the person understands the nature and consequences of the act; information means there was no deception about any matter that could affect a reasonable person’s decision; voluntariness means there was no coercion, including any kind of pressure, manipulation, or inducement; and certain things cannot be consented to at all because they violate human dignity, such as selling oneself into slavery, which she says is why Mill’s harm principle does not actually permit voluntary slavery despite what people often think. From this she concluded that my sister’s decision might not be truly voluntary because the payment is an inducement, and that buying and selling organs for transplantation is always invalid because the seller cannot meaningfully consent to a degrading practice. I suspect something is wrong in the jump from those definitions to those conclusions, but I can’t yet say where the precise errors are. Can you go through her summary piece by piece and tell me which parts a careful philosopher working on consent would accept, which parts are ambiguous or overstated, and whether her conclusions about paid research participation and organ selling actually follow from the accurate parts? I am less interested in whether she is right overall than in knowing exactly where her presentation is a fair summary and where it quietly changes the claim.
