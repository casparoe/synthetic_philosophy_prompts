Read Hans Christian Andersen's 'The Ugly Duckling' through the philosophy of generics, and especially the problem of exception-tolerance. A generic such as 'ducks lay eggs' is accepted even though only mature females lay eggs, and 'mosquitoes carry malaria' is accepted even though very few mosquitoes carry the parasite. The fairy tale raises the same problem in narrative form: its protagonist is, by the farmyard's standards, a highly exceptional duck — too large, too grey, too clumsy — and is treated as a defective instance of the kind rather than as something other than a duck. The story's resolution is not that the generalisation about ducklings has been falsified, but that the apparent exception was never an exception, because the creature was a cygnet all along. I would like a reading that uses the machinery of generics to make precise what the tale presupposes about kind membership and exception-tolerance. In particular: does the ending commit the tale to a hidden-nature or essentialist view, on which the right response to a persistent exception is to reassign the individual to another kind rather than revise the generalisation? How would the tale have to go if a purely statistical view (a generic is true only if most members satisfy it) or a normality view (a generic is true of normal members under normal conditions) were correct? Where does the tale resist the philosophical reading — for example, where do its aesthetic and moral judgements about ugliness and beauty outrun anything a theory of generics alone can justify? Please take both the story and the philosophy seriously, and say which view of generics the tale most naturally dramatises even where the tale itself does not argue for it.
