Two friends of mine ended up in an argument the other night after someone said quantum mechanics "disproves determinism," so free will isn't really in trouble. Dana said that's basically right. If determinism were true, every choice would just be the working out of earlier conditions plus the laws, so there's no sense in which you could have done otherwise than you did. Quantum randomness at least ensures there are points where more than one continuation is physically open, so it leaves room for a will to get a word in. Marcus said that's a cheat: randomness is not freedom. If your choice hangs on some subatomic event that might have gone either way, then a coin flip made it, not you. Free will needs authorship, not just an uncaused gap. Indeterminism removes the lock but dosen't put a driver in the car.

Then Dana read this from Lucretius, De Rerum Natura, Book II, in the middle of the atomic swerve argument:

Dost thou not see, when, at a point of time,
The bars are opened, how the eager strength
Of horses cannot forward break as soon
As pants their mind to do? For it behooves
That all the stock of matter, through the frame,
Be roused, in order that, through every joint,
Aroused, it press and follow mind's desire;
So thus thou seest initial motion's gendered
From out the heart, aye, verily, proceeds
First from the spirit's will, whence at the last
'Tis given forth through joints and body entire.

After reading it, Dana said: that's the old point, and it's better than people give it credit for. The swerve isn't supposed to replace the will. It's supposed to make it possible for a chain of motion to start from the animal's own spirit instead of being pushed along from outside forever. The horses want to run before their limbs will follow, and the passage says the start is gendered from out the heart, from the will, and then diffuses through the joints. The swerve only has to be the gap that lets that inner chain get going; after that the will does the steering through ordinary bodily causes. So indeterminism isn't the choice—it's what lets there be a self-initiated chain at all.

Marcus said the horse image is exactly where her argument falls over. Lucretius describes the will having to rouse the stock of matter through the frame and the joints, and he even points out that the horse's body can't burst forward as fast as its mind wants to. That's a causal process with stages, not a single undetermined swerve. And the lag between wanting to run and running shows that voluntary action is just more pushing and pulling inside the body. The swerve itself is supposed to be a blind little sideways motion at no fixed time or place. How does a blind swerve turn into the mind's act instead of a twitch the mind has to manage? Either the indeterminism is down in the atoms, in which case it's just noise, or it's up at the level of the will, in which case it isn't atomic randomness anymore. You can't have it both ways.

Dana said: that's why the word "initial" matters. The swerve doesn't have to steer every act like some little homunculus. It only has to secure that the causal chain has its source in the animal, in the will, rather than in anything outside or before it. After that the will steers through ordinary causes. And if Marcus demands that the will itself be fully caused by prior stuff in order to count as ours, he's quietly signing up for the compatibilist picture he was trying to argue past—which never does justice to the could-have-done-otherwise intuition anyway.

Marcus said: deliberation is a process of causes working themselves out in real time, so the fact that you "have to decide" is perfectly compatible with the decision being caused. And could-have-done-otherwise is doing all the work again. If the swerve had gone the other way, the horse might have flinched or stumbled instead of running—but that wouldn't be the horse choosing. To get authorship out of the swerve, the will has to be able to control a blind swerve, and Lucretius says nothing about how that could work. So either the will controls it, in which case it isn't indeterministic, or it doesn't, in which case it isn't free.

They went around like that until people started leaving, and nobody landed it. I said I'd ask someone. Can you go through the exchange move by move—which claims hold up, which are shaky, who's right about what specifically—and say what, if anything, both of them are missing? The Lucretius passage should weigh in too, not just sit there as decoration.
