I run an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Bayesian decision theory (about 18 students, one 75-minute meeting). Design an in-class game that makes students feel why Good's theorem on the value of information says a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer can never be worse off, in ex ante expectation, by accepting cost-free new information—even though in a particular round the information may reverse their choice and leave them with a worse outcome than if they had never seen it. Base the game on a decision problem with two possible states and two acts, with a signal whose false-positive and false-negative rates are posted so participants can update from the same numbers. Include rules, materials, a timeline, a no-money payoff scheme, a debrief that maps the rounds to the assumptions of Good's theorem as argued in his 1967 paper On the Principle of Total Evidence, and an honest note on which assumption of the theorem the activity cannot make literal and must be supplied in discussion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it.
