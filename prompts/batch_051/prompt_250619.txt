While I was drafting my last novel, a reader and I fell out over a scene I believed I had written plainly. I said the scene meant what I intended it to mean; she replied that my intention could not do that much work. I tried to turn her reply into an argument, and the argument now unsettles me because it seems to prove too much. It runs as follows.

(1) What a text means is fixed by what its author intended to convey in writing it.
(2) An author's intention is a private mental event, available to the author only through recollection and to everyone else only through the text and whatever evidence surrounds its making.
(3) If an intention is available only in that way, then for any reading of a text that fits the text and the surrounding evidence, there is some possible intention the author could have had that would make that reading the correct one.
(4) So for any reading that fits the text and the evidence, it is possible that the reading is correct.
(5) A reading that is possibly correct cannot be ruled out as a misreading.
(6) Therefore no reading that fits the text and the evidence can be ruled out as a misreading.

I find (6) impossible to accept, yet I do not see how to stop the argument short of giving up (1), which I would much rather keep. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Identify the step that fails and say what the correct version of it would be.
