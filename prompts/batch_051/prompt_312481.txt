I'm a PhD student working on causal discovery, and I've got a nagging worry about something I keep hearing around the department. There's a view floating around — in seminars, and honestly in a lot of the papers I read — that the minimal Bayesian network that fits the independencies in your data is, all else equal, your best guide to the true causal structure. The thought is that faithfulness and minimality do a lot of quiet work: unfaithful parameterisations are measure zero anyway, so we can safely assume them away for finite samples, and minimality just encodes a kind of Ockhamist preference for fewer edges. Everything from PC to GES seems to lean on this, and nobody on my floor seems to loose much sleep over it.

I'd like you to play it straight and tell me what would have to be true for this position to be wrong, or at least badly misleading. Which assumptions are doing the real work? What would a world where minimal Bayes nets systematically fail to track causation look like — is it enough that deterministic mechanisms or exact cancellations are common, or do you need something stranger, like stable near-unfaithfulness or parameter spaces that aren't smooth in the way the measure-zero argument assumes? I'm less intetested in the textbook point that faithfulness can fail in principle, and more in which escape routes are genuinely live as opposed to merely logically possible. Are there cases where a defender of the view would themselves admit the method has failed, rather than just been unlucky? And is the view actually risking anything, or has it been insulated by treating every counterexample as a finite-sample artefact or a coding error? I'd like an honest assessment of how exposed the standard conclusion really is.
