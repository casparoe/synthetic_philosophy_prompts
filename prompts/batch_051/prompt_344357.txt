An acquaintance who works on early modern philosophy recently gave me what he called the standard reconstruction of Spinoza's argument for substance monism. His summary was this:

"Spinoza's monism is forced by his account of what it takes for two things to be distinct. Two substances cannot share an attribute, because if they did there would be nothing by which to distinguish them — no qualitative difference — and substances that cannot be distinguished must be identical. If two substances had different attributes they would have nothing in common, so neither could act on or limit the other, and a substance could not be produced by another either. Each substance would therefore have to be self-caused, with an essence that involves existence; but once that is granted there is no reason for the universe to contain more than one such being. Hence there is exactly one substance, God or Nature, and everything else is a mode of it. The crucial first step — the ban on two substances sharing an attribute — is proved at Ethics I, Proposition 5."

I then read the passage, in R. H. M. Elwes's translation:

"PROP. V. There cannot exist in the universe two or more substances having the same nature or attribute.

Proof.—If several distinct substances be granted, they must be distinguished one from the other, either by the difference of their attributes, or by the difference of their modifications (Prop. iv.). If only by the difference of their attributes, it will be granted that there cannot be more than one with an identical attribute. If by the difference of their modifications—as substance is naturally prior to its modifications (Prop. i.),—it follows that setting the modifications aside, and considering substance in itself, that is truly, (Deff. iii. and vi.), there cannot be conceived one substance different from another,—that is (by Prop. iv.), there cannot be granted several substances, but one substance only. Q.E.D."

I now suspect the summary is not as faithful as it sounded. The proof does not plainly say that two substances sharing an attribute would be indistinguishable in the way my acquaintance describes, and its final sentence seems to assert something stronger than the proposition itself states: not merely that no two substances share an attribute, but that only one substance can be granted. I do not know how much of his version is a fair reconstruction, how much is a Leibnizian import, and whether the stronger-looking conclusion is genuinely available in the text. Please audit the summary in light of the passage. For each element of the summary, indicate whether it tracks a specific step of the proof; identify where the summary slides from "cannot be conceived as distinct" to "cannot be distinct", and whether Spinoza is entitled to that slide; say what work the priority of substance to its modifications is doing in the proof, and whether the summary omits or distorts it; and, once the summary is corrected, assess whether the monist conclusion still goes through, or whether it must wait for later propositions.
