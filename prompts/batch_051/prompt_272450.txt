I am the principal of a secondary school of about six hundred students, and over the past two years my staff has split into two camps on a question that now reaches my desk several times a month: how much struggle should we deliberately preserve or build into students' work? One camp treats difficulty as a pure cost; scaffolding, worked examples, revision windows, and flexible deadlines get students to the same learning with less pointless friction. The other camp argues that we have been quietly removing something that matters in itself: the experience of being genuinely stuck, working through frustration, and arriving at an answer that feels earned. The argument has stopped being abstract and is now producing concrete disputes: a chemistry problem set that a third of the class stays up struggling with, a proposal to allow unlimited re-dos of major essays, and a disagreement about whether the final-year independent project should remain deliberately unscripted.

I have read enough of the philosophical debate to hold a working position, but not enough to turn it into policy. My working position is this: effort is not valuable merely as an unpleasant sensation or as struggle for its own sake; but certain goods, such as achievement, mastery, ownership of what one knows, and a kind of self-respect grounded in having overcome a difficulty, are partly constituted by striving, so that removing effort does not just lower a cost but can quietly dissolve the good itself. I also suspect, without knowing how to weight them, that several distinctions have to survive in any usable procedure: productive struggle, where the effort is internally related to eventual success, versus wasted or Sisyphean effort, where success is out of reach, the task is mis-designed, or the student lacks prerequisites; struggle a student has chosen versus struggle imposed without consent; and the fact that the burden of discretionary struggle tends to fall unevenly on students whose lives outside school already contain more of it.

Please write a decision procedure that my deputy or heads of department could actually run whenever a proposal arises to preserve, introduce, reduce, or remove struggle in a course, unit, task, or assessment policy. It should be a checklist of questions in a fixed order, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no and where it stops with a yes, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. It must be runnable in under thirty minutes by an intelligent teacher with no philosophical training, and it should leave a paper trail that a teacher could show to a parent or a skeptical colleague and that I could sign off on. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where the procedure depends on my working position rather than on neutral facts. Then run it on three cases: the chemistry problem set, the unlimited essay re-do policy, and the final-year independent project. At least one of these should come out against my natural leaning, since I entered this debate wanting to minimize avoidable distress, and I want you to say plainly which case that is and why the procedure is right to overrule me. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which questions it cannot settle, including whether effort actually has the value my working position assigns to it, and under what conditions the procedure itself would need to be revised.
