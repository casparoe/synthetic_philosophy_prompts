In our course on Kant's moral philosophy we have spent weeks on the formula of humanity, and I keep running into a tension in how people—including people who accept that framework—actually judge cruelty to animals. The theory seems to say that only rational beings can be ends in themselves, so a dog cannot itself be wronged; our duties regarding it are indirect, owed to ourselves or to other persons. The standard move is to add that cruelty to animals erodes the sympathetic dispositions we need for our duties to human beings, so the real damage is to the agent's dispositions. But when someone tortures a stray dog for amusement, the ordinary condemnation is not merely that the agent has damaged his own character; it is that he wronged the dog, and people treat this as among the clearest moral condemnations there are. Are the two commitments jointly defensible, or must one give way? Give the strongest Kantian account you can, state precisely where it locates the wrong, and then give an honest verdict on whether it captures the ordinary judgment or should revise it. Answer in exactly five numbered points, each of one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
