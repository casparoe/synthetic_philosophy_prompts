Please give me a working definition of "organism." I want a definition that counts a single human being and a free-living bacterium as organisms and counts a virus and a mitochondrion as not organisms. It should also give a definite answer, one way or the other, for each of these harder cases: a Portuguese man o' war (a siphonophore made of functionally specialized zooids that develop from one fertilized egg); a eusocial insect colony, such as a honeybee or leafcutter ant colony, with a queen and sterile workers; a quaking aspen grove whose trees are connected underground and are genetically identical clones; a lichen, made of a fungus and photosynthetic partners; a coral colony together with its zooxanthellae and resident microbes; and a cellular slime mold such as Dictyostelium at two stages—the free-living amoebae and the aggregate slug or fruiting body, in which some cells form the stalk and die without reproducing. Propose the definition, run it through all the cases, and say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer; then revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. I suspect the word "organism" is doing at least two jobs—a physiological one, picking out an integrated living whole, and an evolutionary one, picking out the unit that reproduction and natural selection act on—and that these jobs come apart on several of the cases above. If you agree, say which cases the two readings classify differently; if you disagree, defend a single unified definition. Finally, say which of your verdicts is least secure. Throughout, clearly distinguish what is settled description of the biology from what is genuinely contested among philosophers and biologists, and do not present a contested criterion as if it were the consensus view.
