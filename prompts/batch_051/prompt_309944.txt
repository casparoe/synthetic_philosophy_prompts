I've been working through the dogmatism paradox, and I keep getting stuck on what happens to it when you trade knowledge for graded confidence. The version I have in mind runs like this: if you know that p, then p is true; and if p is true, then any evidence against p is misleading, since it points away from the truth. So from knowing p you seem able to conclude, of any evidence against p, that it is misleading — and then to set that evidence aside without weighing it. That conclusion looks plainly wrong, but the step where the argument goes bad is surprisingly hard to locate.

Here is the variation I want worked through. Suppose an agent does not know p outright but has a rational credence of 0.95 in p on the basis of her total evidence. She then observes a new piece of evidence E, which is exactly eight times more likely to be produced if not-p than if p: genuinely adverse, but not decisive. The agent reasons in the style of the paradox: she is 0.95 confident in p, so she is 0.95 confident that any evidence against p is misleading, so she should barely move. Please work out, with the numbers, what a correct Bayesian update actually gives — prior odds, likelihood ratio, posterior odds, posterior probability — and compare that with what the agent's dogmatist reasoning recommends.

Then commit to a diagnosis: does the paradox survive the change from knowledge to credence, does it collapse, or does some weakened version of it remain? If it collapses, say exactly which step of the original argument has no graded analogue; if a weakened version remains, state it precisely and say under what conditions, if any, a high prior does license setting adverse evidence aside. Wherever you land, tell me where you think a defender of the opposite verdict would push back, and why your answer survives that push.
