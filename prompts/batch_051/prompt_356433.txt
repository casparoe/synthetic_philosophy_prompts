In a debate about whether cryptocurrency really is money, a colleague waved off my doubts by citing Aristotle's line that money exists "not by nature but by custom," as if that settled the matter. I looked up the passage, in the Nicomachean Ethics, Book V, chapter 5 (1133a), and the fuller context seems more complicated than the one-liner. Here it is:

"Hence all commodities exchanged must be able to be compared in some way. It is to meet this requirement that men have introduced money; money constitutes in a manner a middle term, for it is a measure of all things, and so of their superior or inferior value, that is to say, how many shoes are equivalent to a house or to a given quantity of food. It is therefore necessary that all commodities shall be measured by some one standard, as was said before. And this standard is in reality demand, which is what holds everything together, since if men cease to have wants or if their wants alter, exchange will go on no longer, or will be on different lines. But demand has come to be conventionally represented by money; this is why money is called nomisma (customary currency), because it does not exist by nature but by custom (nomos), and can be altered and rendered useless at will."

I want to understand what the slogan "money does not exist by nature but by custom" could actually be claiming. Lay out the distinct precise theses that line might express — about what money is, where its value comes from, who can change it, and whether any real standard stands behind the convention. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, plausible but contestable, or false as stated. Then say which reading my colleague's crypto argument actually needs, and whether Aristotle's fuller passage gives him that reading or complicates it.
