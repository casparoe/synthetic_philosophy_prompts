I work in interpretability, and the workhorse argument I was taught for why networks genuinely have representations is the interventionist one: if you can intervene on a unit or a direction—ablate it, clamp it, patch it in from a different prompt—and the model's behavior changes in just the ways you'd predict if that thing encoded, say, "the recipient of an action," then it isn't just a convenient summary; it's a real representational unit inside the network. The test is supposed to be causal: real features are the things you can manipulate with predictable downstream effects.

Lately I've been reading about sparse autoencoders and dictionary learning, and the part that bothers me is that there's no unique decomposition of a layer's activations. Different dictionary sizes, sparsity penalties, and random seeds give you quite different sets of "features," all of which reconstruct the activations about equally well and all of which pass the intervention test. So the test seems to certify every decomposition, which means it can't single out one of them as the real one. My hunch is that the premise under pressure is uniqueness—the original argument quietly assumed there's exactly one correct carving of the network into features—but I'm not sure wether that premise was load-bearing. Does the interventionist argument for realism survive this? Does it collapse into "representations are real relative to a decomposition," which sounds like instrumentalism with extra steps, or is there a repaired version that's weaker but still substantive—maybe one that says the demand for a unique set of features was the wrong demand in the first place? I care about what happens to the argument itself, not a survey of interpretability methods.
