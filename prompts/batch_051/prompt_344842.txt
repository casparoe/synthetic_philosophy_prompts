I'm the collections manager at a county historical society in a rural county, and we make two or three accession decisions a month about whether to take in a family's papers, an institution's records, or a proposal for an oral history project we'd run ourselves. We're two full-time staff plus a volunteer board that has to approve anything large, and every accepted collection is basically irreversable because storage is tight and our processing backlog is measured in years, so the old attitude of just collect it all and sort it out later is not available to us. I've been reading about archives as instruments of power and about how what is absent from a collection is itself a kind of historical artifact, and I want our intake process to actually reflect that instead of just nodding at it. The stated mission is to document the whole county, but our shelves overwhelmingly hold the records of white, property-owning families and local government, while migrant farmworkers, renters, people held in the county jail, and the women who did unpaid domestic labor mostly appear in other people's papers as entries, complaints, or silences. The board is well-meaning but gets uncomfortable when I talk about structural exclusion, so the procedure has to be written in plain language they will sign off on, and it should not require a volunteer to know the theory.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure for deciding whether to take in a collection or start a documentation project, one a new volunteer could follow. I want the questions in the order they should be asked, a clear statement of what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it explicitly hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Then run the procedure on three examples: (1) a prominent local family's diaries and farm ledgers from 1880 to 1960 that record, among other things, the names, wages, injuries, and dismissals of domestic and farm workers; (2) a tenant association asking us to co-run an oral history project with Spanish-speaking residents of a trailer park that is being demolished for a warehouse, with interviews to be recorded by the residents themselves; and (3) the county sheriff's office offering us a batch of closed case files, mostly arrests for loitering and petty theft during the 1970s. Make sure at least one of the worked examples reaches an answer the board is not going to like, and in the final part say plainly what this procedure cannot decide.
