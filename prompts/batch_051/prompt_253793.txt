In my psychology class we learned that the heritability of IQ is often estimated at around 0.5 to 0.7 in adults in wealthy countries. I have started using this as a rule for evaluating policy claims: when someone says that an educational program, a change in family circumstances, or some other environmental intervention could meaningfully raise IQ or close achievement gaps, I treat the claim with suspicion. My reasoning is that if genes account for more than half of the variation in IQ in the population, then the environment probably cannot matter all that much, so environmental explanations are likely overstated. I apply this rule especially when I don't have time to read the original study behind a claim. My psychology teacher says this is a textbook misunderstanding of heritability. Is my rule defensible as an evidence-management shortcut, or is it the old "heritability means fixed" mistake dressed up in numbers?
