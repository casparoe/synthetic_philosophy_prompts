I have been trying to decide whether an analogy I keep drawing between inflectional morphology and moral theory is more than a suggestive metaphor, and I would like a careful structural verdict. In morphology, there is a familiar division of labor between productive rules and stored exceptions. A regular past tense like walk/walked applies to novel items, and the productive rule does real work; an irregular pattern like sing/sang is subregular, following constraints that cover a class rather than a single item; and a suppletive alternation like go/went involves forms with no common material, where no rule derives the one from the other and the stored form simply blocks the expected regular output. Suppletion is normally treated as a historical accident of high-frequency items, not as part of the productive system. I have been struck by how closely this maps onto the dispute between generalism and particularism in ethics. The generalist treats moral principles as productive rules that yield verdicts in new cases; the particularist replies that what look like rule applications are really summaries of particular judgments, with each case decided on its own terms. Generalists concede exceptions, but their standard exceptions—self-defense, deception to protect the innocent—look like subregularities: principled, class-based, and themselves rule-governed. The contentious slot is the third one: whether ethics contains anything analogous to suppletion, a brute exception to an otherwise productive principle, not derived from any further principle and admitted only because it is on the list of cases where the principle is blocked. I can see how the analogy structures the debate, but I do not trust it yet. Set out the mapping precisely, say what would have to be true of both domains for results to transfer from morphology to ethics, and name at least one thing the analogy predicts that is plausibly false. If you think the analogy is nevertheless deep, explain why the apparent failures are not fatal to it.
