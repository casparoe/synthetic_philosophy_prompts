The exam question was: "Explain Ian Hacking's notion of a looping effect for classifications of people, using one psychiatric example. The student wrote: "Hacking's looping effect is the way classifications of people feed back into the people classified. With multiple personality, late twentieth-century clinicians built up a diagnostic category with expectations about alters and childhood trauma; once patients had access to that script, their presentations changed—more alters, more elaborate narratives—and clinicians then revised the category to match, so the kind never stayed put. Hacking's point is that the category helps produce a new way of being a person rather than merely sorting people who were already there, and he notes that classified people can also resist or appropriate their labels, which leads to still more looping. At bottom, though, this is a self-fulfilling prophecy: the label brings about the behavior it was supposed to describe, which is why the mechanism is fundamentally the same as labeling theory. Hacking also thinks the same loop can happen with indifferent kinds—once a species is listed as endangered, the classification changes how people treat it, and that feeds back into what the species becomes—so in the end the interactive/indifferent distinction is less sharp than it looks."
