I keep having this argument with a friend who teaches ethics, and I genuinely can't tell where the empirical bit ends and the conceptual bit begins. His line is that guilt is the better moral emotion: guilt attaches to the act, so it motivates apology, repair, making amends; shame attaches to the self, so it motivates hiding, denial, or snapping at whoever saw you screw up. He also thinks a culture can, over a few generations, choose to be a guilt culture rather than a shame culture — he points at societies that supposedly lean one way or the other as if they just picked a setting. My worry is that "better" is smuggling in several different goods at once (better for the offender's mental health, better for the victim, better at preventing repeat offenses, better at tracking what the person actually deserves) and those could come apart. I'm also not convinced a whole emotional style is the sort of thing a culture can choose any more than it can choose it's grammar.

So here's the task. If you had to actually test his two claims — guilt beats shame, and a culture can choose between them — what would the strongest realistic test look like? Give me the concrete design: what you'd compare, what you'd measure, which result would favor him and which would favor me, and where the operationalization of "guilt" versus "shame" is quietly doing the heavy lifting, especially in societies whose emotion words don't map neatly onto the English pair. There's also a wrinkle I keep tripping on: people sometimes distinguish shame about having violated your own standards from shame about how you look to others; if that split is real, does it wreck the comparison or just refine it? And at the end, be explicit about what even a clean positive result would not settle — for example, whether we've shown that guilt is better, or just better at the particular things we happened to measure.
