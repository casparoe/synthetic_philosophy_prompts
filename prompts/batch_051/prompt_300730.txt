I'm a novelist and I'm stuck in a book about a regional bank that spent a decade selling a product that wiped out a lot of its customers; I keep getting notes from my editor saying the moral blame in the book sloshes around — sometimes it's the bank as a single villain, sometimes it's the CEO, sometimes it evaporates into 'everyone followed the process.' I want a reusable procedure I can run on any incident in a draft to decide how blame should attach between a company and the people inside it, grounded in whatever the philosophy of corporate moral agency actually says, but translated into a checklist I can actually use while drafting — I wont stop to read theory, and I dont want citations. I just want to know which distinctions I can't afford to collapse.

The procedure should have the questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and it should say where it stops with a 'no' and where it hands the decision to me because the question is one of values rather than anything a theory can settle. Make sure it actually handles the hard cases: a company that knows something no single employee knows, a single bad actor inside an otherwise healthy firm, a small family business where the owner's decision and the company's decision seem like the same thing, a culture that produces harm without anyone deciding to do it, and omissions. I mostly care about blame, not legal liability, though if the law has a distinction I'm stealing, mark it.

Then run the procedure on three incidents from the novel or close to it: (1) the bank where thousands of low-level employees mis-sold over years because scripts, quotas, and bonuses made the harm the predictable output, though no one person intended the full wrong; (2) a three-person bakery where the owner personally and knowingly skipped a safety step and someone got hurt; (3) a software company whose old product turns out, years later, to have caused a harm no one foresaw and no process would have caught. I want at least one of these to come out in a way I'm not going to like, and I want you to say so plainly.

Also tell me where the procedure will misfire, what it can't do — for example if I'm using it to justify a narrative choice rather than actually ask a moral question — and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as checks.

If we're honest about it this might all be that a company can be a genuine agent and blameworthy in some cases and not others, but I need the working procedure, not the slogan.
