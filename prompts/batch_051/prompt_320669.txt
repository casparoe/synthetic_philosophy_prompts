Two classmates in my philosophy of technology seminar fell into an argument after a class on whether material things can be political, and it has been nagging at me ever since. The exchange began over a park bench with a central armrest that makes it impossible to lie down flat, and it unfolded roughly as follows. (1) Maya said the bench is itself a political artefact: the armrest's purpose is to stop rough sleepers, and the object issues a rule that no sign or by-law does, because it works even when no warden or police officer is present. The value judgement that this public space is not for sleeping has been built into the metal. (2) Dan replied that a bench forbids nothing; only people and policies can do that. What is political is the council's decision to install it, the housing system that leaves people without beds, and the unequal enforcement of public order. Calling the object political lets officials shrug and say the bench made the decision. (3) Maya answered that a decision is not lost when it is built into an object; the object carries it forward, like a speed bump that slows every driver without a constable on the corner. If we count only human intentions as political, then politics evaporates once the committee meeting ends, even though the built environment keeps enforcing the decision for decades. (4) Dan said a cliff also structures behaviour, but nobody calls the cliff political. The bench differs from a cliff only because of the surrounding system of property law, policing, and social norms, not because of anything in the metal itself. (5) Maya then shifted ground: some technologies are political regardless of their designers' intentions, because they can operate only under a certain kind of authority. A large centralised power grid or nuclear plant seems to require hierarchical management and expert control, so adopting it quietly shifts power towards centralised, technocratic institutions. (6) Dan replied that this is technological determinism: it mistakes one historical implementation for an unavoidable essence. Electricity can equally be supplied by decentralised generation under local control, so nothing in the technology demands hierarchy; the politics lies in who owns and governs the system, not in the electrons.

I have been asked to referee and find I cannot: each countermove seemed to land without settling the matter.
