I am a retired natural scientist who has only recently begun reading philosophy, and I would value an impartial adjudication of a dispute I witnessed in my reading group. Two members—I shall call them A and B—fell into a sustained argument about vagueness and the sorites paradox. A defended what I have since learned is called an epistemicist view; B defended a supervaluationist view. Neither has formal training in philosophy, and I reproduce the exchange as faithfully as memory allows.

(1) A argued as follows. Consider ten thousand grains, plainly a heap, and the stepwise sorites argument: if ten thousand grains make a heap, then nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine make a heap; if that, then nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight; and so on down to one grain. Each conditional seems irresistible, the argument is classically valid, and the conclusion is plainly false; so at least one conditional must be false. At some point, then, a heap becomes a non-heap by the removal of a single grain. We cannot identify that point, but our failure to identify it does not show there is no such point. Vagueness is therefore a matter of ignorance: there is a sharp, real boundary, and our knowledge simply fails to reach it.

(2) B replied that the step from "one conditional in the series is false" to "there is a sharp boundary in the meaning or in the world" is too quick. A boundary that no speaker could in principle locate, and that no one could have learned when acquiring the word, is hard to credit as part of what "heap" means. The better diagnosis is that the meaning of "heap" does not choose among a range of admissible ways of drawing the line; borderline predications are neither determinately true nor determinately false. On this view the universal induction premise—every instance of "if n grains make a heap, then n−1 grains make a heap"—is false on every admissible sharpening, and therefore false, but no single cutoff is privileged. The sorites argument lapses without our having to posit an unknowable knife-edge in the language itself.

(3) A came back that this proposal avoids one sharp line only by underwriting many. On the supervaluationist account, "there is a number n such that n grains make a heap and n−1 grains do not" comes out true, because every admissible sharpening contains some cutoff; yet no particular number serves as a true witness for the claim—an existential truth with no true instance. Likewise, "either these grains make a heap or they do not" comes out true for a borderline case, while neither disjunct is true. Whatever the technical merits, A added, this is a queer account of what truth does, and the notion of an admissible sharpening seems to be explained by appeal to the very borderline cases the theory was supposed to explain.

(4) B responded that the existential-without-an-instance is not an embarrassment but the correct description of vagueness. There is no single boundary in the meaning of "heap," but every admissible sharpening introduces one, and truth for a vague sentence just is truth on all admissible sharpenings; hence "there is a boundary" is true even though no boundary is the boundary. Similarly, the validity of excluded middle reflects only that logic is indifferent among sharpenings; it was never a given of natural language that every sentence must be determinately true or determinately false. B added that the true liability lies with A's view: epistemicism posits a semantic fact—the exact cutoff—that no competent speaker can state, has ever learned, or uses in communication, and it owes us an account of how such an inaccessible fact enters the meaning of a word at all.

I should like you to adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify that rating. Then tell me which of the two, on the recorded exchange, has the better-supported position on each specific point raised, and what both are missing or underweighting. Throughout, please separate what is settled among philosophers of language from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as if it were the settled consensus.
