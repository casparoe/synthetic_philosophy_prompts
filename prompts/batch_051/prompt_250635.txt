I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I keep running into the same unresolved dispute in decisions where payoffs compound: how much to allocate to A/B test variants when budget and learnings get reinvested, how to size a risky project, and what "expected value" even means for quantities that multiply rather than add. The dispute is between two positions I'll name and then ask you to work with.

Position 1: Expected-utility orthodoxy. Rational choice means maximizing expected utility under a single, well-defined utility function over outcomes. Risk attitude is encoded entirely in the curvature of the utility function, and the Kelly criterion is nothing over and above the special case in which utility is logarithmic in wealth. Nothing in the arithmetic of expected value needs to be replaced.

Position 2: Geometric rationality (ergodicity). For decisions that repeat and compound, the operationally meaningful quantity is the long-run time-average growth rate, not the arithmetic ensemble average. Under multiplicative dynamics, maximizing expected log growth in the manner of Kelly is the general rule, and arithmetic expected value is a special case carried over unthinkingly from one-shot, additive decisions. Treating the ensemble average as the default is a category error, not a neutral starting point.

I would like you to draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between these two positions could produce. It should contain a numbered agreed-claims section whose wording is precise and slants toward neither camp, followed by a disagreements table. In the table, each row should give the precise residual disagreement, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own view; the crux, understood as the sub-question whose resolution would move at least one side; the test, case, or argument both sides accept as bearing on that crux; and each side's prediction of how the test would come out, stated in advance.

Make sure the statement works through at least these points: (i) whether the standard arguments for expected utility implicitly assume a single, unrepeatable decision and whether that assumption matters normatively; (ii) whether apparent risk aversion in repeated, reinvested settings is often arithmetic averaging misdiagnosing the time average; (iii) what fractional Kelly, small-stakes rebalancing, and the ability to adjust position size mid-stream do to each camp's claim; and (iv) whether the geometric view generalizes beyond money to less fungible but still compounding resources such as reputation, attention, or compute capacity, or whether it is over-exporting a financial metaphor. Where the two camps turn out to differ only verbally, the statement should say so explicitly rather than manufacturing a disagreement.
