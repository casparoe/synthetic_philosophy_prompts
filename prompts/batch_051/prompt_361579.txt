I run a monthly philosophy discussion group for adults with no formal background in philosophy, and next month we are working through the opening sections of Kant’s Critique of Pure Reason. I want to spend about thirty minutes teaching the distinction between intuitions and concepts, because the rest of the meeting will fall apart if people are fuzzy about it. I am not looking for a lecture script; I want a teaching plan. The group is sharp but untrained: they will challenge evasions, but they also come in with the ordinary meanings of words like intuition and concept, and they will keep slipping back into those meanings unless I actively untangle them.

Please plan the explanation: the order the pieces must be introduced in and why, with attention to what depends on what; the one concrete example or running case that can carry the distinction, and what that example must have and must lack; the misconceptions I should expect—for example people treating intuition as a mere hunch, or treating concepts as just words, or thinking Kant is saying the mind invents reality—and where in the sequence each should be headed off. End with a short diagnostic question I can ask to check whether the distinction has actually landed, and say explicitly what I should leave out at this level. Act as a professional philosopher who teaches Kant to non-specialists. Think carefully about the possible sequences before settling on one, and when you give interpretive claims, separate what is settled in Kant scholarship from what is genuinely contested rather than presenting one reading as the consensus.
