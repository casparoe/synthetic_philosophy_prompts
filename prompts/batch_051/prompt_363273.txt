I am a doctoral student in political and legal theory, and for a chapter on punishment I have been collecting my own verdicts about a set of capital cases in the hope of discovering whether they are controlled by a single principle or are merely a set of reactions I happen to have. I would like your help working out what they commit me to. I will describe several cases and what I think about each, as concretely as I can, and then ask you to state the principle, say which of my verdicts it cannot keep, and apply it to a case I have not settled.

In the first case, a man abducts, tortures, and kills several people over a period of months, keeps trophies, and records in his journals that he took pleasure in their fear. He is sane, and the evidence is overwhelming; there is no question of mistaken identity. I find that I have no objection to his being executed, and I do not mean only that I could accept it: the death sentence strikes me as the fitting response.

In the second, a young man agrees to drive a getaway car after being assured that no one will be hurt, waits outside unarmed, and an accomplice unexpectedly shoots and kills a security guard. Under a statute that treats any death occurring during a robbery as murder by everyone involved, he is convicted of capital murder. I do not think he should be executed, and I doubt he should be treated as a murderer at all.

In the third, a seventeen-year-old shoots and kills a shopkeeper during a robbery. By the time of sentencing he is thirty-five, has completed an education in prison, expresses settled remorse, and has shown no violence while incarcerated. I do not think he should be executed.

In the fourth, an adult with a serious intellectual disability takes part in a murder, but his capacity to formulate long-term plans, to appreciate the consequences of his actions, or to resist the urging of others is severely limited. I do not think he should be executed.

In the fifth, a man plans and finances a bombing that kills a dozen people, but remains hundreds of miles away and never sees the site or the victims. The evidence of his control over the operation is overwhelming. I think he should be executed, and I notice that I feel no more hesitation about him than about the man who tortured with his own hands.

In the sixth, a man is convicted of setting a fire that killed a family. The case against him rests on a single eyewitness who saw him near the building, weak circumstantial evidence, and no forensic link. I do not think he should be executed, because the chance that he is innocent feels intolerable in a penalty that cannot be undone.

In the seventh, a woman kills her husband after years of severe abuse, not during an attack but after planning the killing, and she says she saw no other way out. The abuse is thoroughly documented. I do not think she should be executed, though I do think she should be punished.

In the eighth, a man commits a murder as cruel as the one in the first case, is sentenced to death, and then spends thirty years on death row. By now he is, by all accounts, gentle and repentant and psychologically almost unrecognizable as the person who committed the murder. I find that I do not think he should be executed now, but I cannot tell whether my reason is mercy, the fact that the present man is not the one who killed, or the cruelty of the decades of waiting.

Two features of this set stand out to me. My verdicts seem mostly to track how much the actor deserves death, and yet the eighth case appears to make desert depend on the person who exists now rather than on the act committed decades earlier, which the first case did not. And the sixth case suggests a threshold of certainty below which desert cannot operate at all, though I cannot tell whether that threshold is a side-constraint on a retributive view, a separate principle about the ethics of risk, or an ad hoc patch.

What principle, or small family of principles, best captures these verdicts? Please state the principle in its strongest form, show how it handles as many of the cases as possible, and then tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which I would have to revise. Then apply the account to the case I remain undecided about: a man commits a murder as cruel as the one in the first case, is never caught, lives a blameless and generous life for the next thirty years, and is then identified through new evidence. Assume that, like the man in the eighth case, he has become gentle and psychologically unrecognizable from his younger self, but there has been no death-row waiting and no state-induced suffering. Should he be executed on the account you have given? Finally, say where deterrence belongs in the principle you propose. My gut says it does none of the work, but I suspect that is because I do not know how to weigh it rather than because it is irrelevant. Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if writing a careful letter.
