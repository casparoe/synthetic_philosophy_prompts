In my global studies class we've been talking about official apologies, and the whole thing kind of doesnt make sense to me once you dig into it. Germany has apologized a bunch for the Holocaust, Canada and Australia apologized for taking Indigenous kids away from their families and putting them in residential schools, the US apologized for the internment of Japanese Americans in WWII, and there's a whole fight about whether countries like the US or Britain should apologize for slavery and colonial stuff. But the politicians doing the apologizing didn't actually do any of it, and the people they're apologizing to are mostly descendents, not the direct victims. At the same time, governments clearly care a lot about wording: they'll say they "regret" something instead of "apologize" for it, or hold a big apology ceremony but then fight compensation claims in court, so it looks like both a moral act and a legal maneuver. I can't tell what a state apology is even doing philosophically.

What I want to know is: what view of responsibility — or of what a country even is over time — does the practice of state apologies presuppose? Does it quietly assume that countries are like persons that persist across generations, that living people inherit guilt or at least obligations, or that an apology to descendants can make sense even after the direct victims are dead? Or is it not really about responsibility at all, but about something like political signaling or reputation? Reconstruct the implicit philosophy behind these apologies, say which assumptions the practice genuinely needs and which ones it just happens to fit with, and tell me whether the practice is coherent or whether it's philosophically confused but still worth doing.
