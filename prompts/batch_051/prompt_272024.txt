I've been reading rationalist and econ-adjacent blogs for years, and somewhere along the way I picked up a rule I now use almost automatically. It came up again last week when I refused to sell a ticket for twice what I paid, because I'd already imagined being at the show; my friend said I was being mug-irrational. When someone cites the classic endowment effect result, where people demand much more to sell a mug they were just given than they would ever pay to buy one, I immediately discount the conclusion that their preference is irrational. My default is that ownership legitimately changes the value of an option: selling means losing the known object, taking on search and transaction costs, maybe taking a hit to identity or expected regret, and the old self may have had reasons to keep the object that I can't fully introspect. So I treat loss aversion and the WTA/WTP gap as a legitimate feature of preferences until I see fairly strong evidence otherwise, and I ignore most policy arguments that lean on people being irrational in the classic mug experiment. My friend, who is more into the heuristics-and-biases side of things, says I've built a rule that can explain away any bias as a hidden preference, and that at some point it stops being empiricism and starts being motivated reasoning.

His charge is that a bias isn't defeated by pointing out that a hidden cost could exist; you need independent evidence that the cost is real and roughly the right size. Otherwise I'm just letting a theory of rational ownership do all the work, and every bias becomes a legitimate preference by fiat. I genuinely don't know how to settle the cases where attachment and bias make the same prediction. Is my habit defensible? Can it be restated in a form that keeps what's right about it—say, requiring concrete transaction costs or testing whether the preference survives an outside view—without becoming an unfalsifiable shield? Where does it fail, and what should I do when I can't tell a real preference from irrational loss aversion? Hard limit: 700 words.
