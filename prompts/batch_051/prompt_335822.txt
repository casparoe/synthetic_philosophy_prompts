I'm a retired neuroscientist—spent thirty years on startle responses, reaction times, that sort of thing—and Ive recently fallen into philosophy, mostly the Stoics. The dichotomy of control as I keep seeing it spelled out goes something like this: something is up to you iff your choice alone is enough to bring it about, nothing outside you can block it, and it can't be taken away from you once you have it. Epictetus's list puts judgments, impulses, desires, and aversions in the up-to-us box, and body, property, reputation, and office in the not-up-to-us box. Modern writers sometimes add a third box for partial control.

Now here's a case from the lab that keeps bugging me. You're sitting in a chair and a starting pistol goes off behind your head. You flinch—eyes shut, shoulders up—at maybe 30 to 50 milliseconds, long before any sort of deliberative judgment. Now consider whether you're annoyed by the noise. The Stoic wants to say the annoyance is a judgment, so its up to you; the flinch is a bit of body, a reflex, so it's not up to you. But I've spent decades watching people train away flinches: soldiers, bomb techs, drummers. With enough practice the flinch shrinks or disappears or turns into something else. So your choices do seem to reach down into the reflex, but not all at once and not completely. And the annoyance can also outrun you: you can find yourself already annoyed before you've caught it, and then you judge the annoyance, which is a second thing.

So: does the trained flinch qualify as up to you? What about whatever part of it won't train away, if there is such a part? Work through the criteria one by one and tell me where the Stoic should draw the line. Then tell me whether the dichotomy itself needs a third box, or a continuum, or whether it's fine and I'm just confusing timescales.
