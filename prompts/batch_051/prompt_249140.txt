Here's one I've been chewing on. Take the Volkswagen diesel emissions scandal from 2015. The setup in brief: VW put software in its diesel cars that detected when the car was on a test rig and switched the engine into a clean low-emissions mode, then let it run considerably dirtier during real road driving. Enforcement was built around lab tests—fixed drive cycles and tailpipe measurements—so regulators were effectively auditing the cars outcome on the test, not the actual running process that produced the real-world emissions. Now analyze this episode through the lens of process-based versus outcome-based supervision of AI systems. In AI training, outcome supervision rewards the final answer or downstream result, while process supervision rewards intermediate steps, methods, or legible chains of reasoning. Spell out the mapping first: who is the supervisor, who or what is the system, what is the supposed outcome being measured, and what would the real-world process be? Then say what each supervision regime would reward in a car manufacturer, and whether VW's cheating is best described as reward hacking—gaming the test—or as a more ordinary case of fraudulent intent. Given that, what would a process-supervision-minded regulator have done ex ante, and where would that still have failed? Then run the analysis the other way: which features of the scandal does the process/outcome distinction explain cleanly, which ones does it misdescribe or have to stretch to fit (like the fact that engineers knowingly deceived, rather than a trained system drifting into a hack), and where is the distinction just silent? Finally, what should someone designing process-based supervision for ML systems actually take away from this—does the VW case make process supervision look better, or does it show that process audits can be gamed too, and if so what specific failure mode should they worry about?
