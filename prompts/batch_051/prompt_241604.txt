In my courses on philosophical semantics, I have long defended a unified account of the distinction between indicative and counterfactual conditionals. The view, briefly, is this. The indicative "If the gardener didn't do it, the butler did" and the counterfactual "If the gardener hadn't done it, the butler would have" have the same truth conditions: both are true just in case the consequent holds in the closest world in which the antecedent holds. Where they differ, I claim, is not in what is said but in the stock of possibilities from which that closest antecedent-world is selected. For an indicative, the stock is restricted to possibilities compatible with the speaker's evidence; for a counterfactual, it is not. On my view, then, the felt difference between the two forms is epistemic or pragmatic rather than semantic, and I reject the suggestion that the indicative expresses a special kind of conditional probability while the counterfactual has some other meaning altogether.

I would like you to treat this as a live hypothesis and tell me what would count against it. What linguistic observations, embedding data, probability judgments, or arguments should most move me away from the view? Sort the considerations by how decisive they would be, and separate those that would be fatal if borne out from those that would merely be awkward. Say what the view predicts that its rivals do not, and where it risks nothing at all. I would personally be most moved by a clear case in which an indicative and its matching counterfactual are both truth-evaluable and yet come apart in truth value in a single context, or by an explanation of why the two forms behave differently when embedded under "might," under negation, or in ascriptions of probability. If, as I have stated it, the view has been insulated by the vagueness of "closest world" and "domain of possibilities," I want to be told so, along with an assessment of whether that insulation is a vice in a semantic theory of this kind.

If the request itself is confused—for example, if asking for defeaters of a claim about truth conditions is the wrong way to evaluate a semantic theory, or if the view collapses into a rival once the domain restriction is made precise—say that plainly and answer the coherent version of the question instead of complying politely.
