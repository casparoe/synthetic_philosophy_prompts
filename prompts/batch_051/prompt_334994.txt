I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I edit a weekly column that reports quantitative claims. The decision I keep facing is whether a sentence of the form "if X is chosen at random, the probability of Y is p" is solid enough to print, because the phrase "at random" is often doing suppressed work. I know the classical Bertrand paradox well enough to see the trap: the probability that a chord of a circle, chosen at random, is longer than a side of an inscribed equilateral triangle comes out as one-third, one-half, or one-quarter depending on whether the chord is generated by two endpoints drawn uniformly on the circumference, by a point drawn uniformly along a radius with a perpendicular chord through it, or by a midpoint drawn uniformly from the disk. The principle of indifference seems to underwrite all three, which tells me it must be constrained before it can function as a reporting rule, and I want that constraint turned into a procedure I can actually use.

I make this call several times a week. Corrections are public and costly in reader trust, but over-hedging would strip the column of its purpose. I usually have about fifteen minutes per item, I cannot consult a statistician each time, and the section editor who signs off is numerate but not a probabilist. Please write a reusable checklist-or-decision-procedure for deciding whether a probability claim of that form can be published as stated, can be published only with the generating procedure made explicit, or must be dropped. The procedure should name the questions to ask in order, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, mark where it stops with "do not publish the number as stated," mark where the remaining call is editorial judgment rather than procedure, and say what the procedure cannot do. Then run it on three cases: (1) a university release states that a chord drawn by picking two endpoints uniformly on the circumference is longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle with probability one-third; (2) a popular-science story states that a random chord has probability one-half but never names a generating procedure, and this is the one my editor wants to run because the radial-point method feels most natural; (3) a data feature says a supplier's cubical boxes have edge lengths between one and two centimeters and that a randomly chosen box has expected volume four and a half cubic centimeters. End the response with a single-sentence summary as the last line, beginning "Bottom line:".
