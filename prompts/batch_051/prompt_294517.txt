In AP Stats we did the unit on independence, and it honestly made me more confused about something I used to think was obvious. My friend is really into true crime, and whenever two rare things happen to the same person -- like someone gets struck by lightning and then wins the lottery, or their same number comes up twice -- he multiplies the probabilities together and concludes it basically can't be luck. But I keep thinking: that multiplication is only allowed if the two events are independent, and whether they're independent is a claim about the world, not something you get for free from the math. He says independence is just the sensible default, but I have no idea what makes it the default.

I know there's a standard stats move where you compare how often two things co-occur in a sample to how often they'd co-occur if they were independent, and if the gap is big enough you reject independence. But that feels like it only tells you whether your data fit an independence model; it doesn't tell you whether the initial presumption of independence was reasonable, and it especially doesn't tell you what to do with a single weird coincidence you'll never see repeated. So here's the actual thing I want to get at: suppose I had a large dataset where two kinds of rare events could both happen to the same person -- like a bunch of people who report an unusual dream and then something matching it happens that week. What would a real test of the "independence is the default" rule look like? What pattern in the data would count as "independent was a fine heuristic" and what pattern would count as "independence is leading us astray"?

And then, after that test comes back either way, what would it still not settle? If the events turn out not to be independent, have we learned anything about what the actual connection is, or only that the simple multiplication was giving us the wrong number? I'm trying to figure out whether presuming independence is a testable claim, a harmless bookkeeping rule, or just a lazy way to make coincidences sound more damning then they actually are.
