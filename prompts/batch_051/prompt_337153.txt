I'm taking a seminar on argumentation and social epistemology, and the prof keeps returning to whether scored, ranked arguement — like debate as a literal sport — would improve our reasoning or just make us better at point-scoring. To keep us from talking past each other she gave the two sides names: the 'gym' view and the 'casino' view. The gym view says competition works like chess: the drive to win makes you find better moves, so over time the best arguments float to the top. The casino view says competition optimizes for whatever the judges will reward, and if the scoring doesn't explicitly pay for truth, you should expect crowd-pleasing sophistry, defensive point-mongering, and polarization.

I want a case built to order to stress-test the gym view. Here's the spec. Construct a single competitive format for arguing about factual questions — the rules, the scoring, and the judge instructions — that has these three properties:

(1) A rational debater whose only goal is maximizing expected score, and who has been randomly assigned one side of a factual question, has the same optimal strategy as a rational debater whose only goal is to settle on the true answer.

(2) The judges do not know the correct answer, and the points for a match are based only on things the judge can observe during or immediately after the debate — no delayed fact-checks, no hidden answer key.

(3) The rules and scoring never mention 'truth', 'accuracy', 'correct', 'evidence', or close synonyms, and there is no fact-checker or oracle anywhere in the loop. No perfect lie detectors, no truth serum, no 'read the debater's mind' technology either.

It has to work for ordinary, biased, strategic debaters, not just honest ones who already want the truth.

This is for a short paper on whether you can get the benefits of scored competition without writing truth into the payoff. Please dont cheat by letting the judge see the answer, by adding a fact-checking stage, or by stipulating that the score-maximizer cares about truth; the alignment should come out of the payoffs. If you become convinced the exact spec can't be met, build the closest format that meets (1) and (2), say which part of (3) you had to bend, and explain why the exact version fails.
