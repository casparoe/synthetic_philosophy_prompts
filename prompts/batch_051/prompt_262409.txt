So I spent way too long last night arguing with a friend about evolutionary debunking arguments in ethics. The basic back-and-forth was the usual one: he argued that our moral intuitions were shaped by natural selection to keep our ancestors alive and cooperating, and that this gives us know reason to think they track any real moral truth. I kept saying that's too quick, and that some moral judgments (like about fairness or consistency) have to be presupposed even to run the debunking argument in the first place.

But here's the thing I walked away bothered about. In my own life I actually follow a lopsided version of his argument, and I'm not sure it's principled. My policy is this: when one of my strong moral intuitions looks like the sort of thing evolution would have wired into me regardless of whether it's true — my revulsion at incest, my gut feeling that loyalty to my own group matters more than loyalty to strangers, my sense that unfairness stings harder when I'm the victim — I refuse to count that intuition as evidence for the moral claim. But I don't treat my other intuitions, like the pull toward consistency or impartiality, that suspiciously; I just use them. My friend says that's the cherry-picking version of his argument, and that if I treated every intuition by my own standard I'd have nothing left, because even the demand for consistency is just another useful mental tool evolution gave me.

I want a committed verdict. Is my selective debunking habit defensible, or is it just a bias dressed up as evolutionary skepticism? If there's a defensible version, tell me exactly where the line should fall, or why the version I use is already right. Don't give me a balanced survey; take a side and defend it.
