So I keep going back and forth on a debate between a conservationist and a wild-animal-welfare person I know. The conservationist says that when you clear a patch of forest you're not just killing animals, your replacing the whole life-history profile of the area: mature, slower-breeding species with long-ish lives get swapped for dense, fast-breeding early-successional species that die young and often, so total suffering probably goes up. The welfare guy says the reverse — a logged patch might hold more small animals living short, pain-dense lives, but a mature forest is full of animals that starve slowly over winters, get eaten alive, or die of injuries, and in any case he doubts "total suffering in an ecosystem" is even a well-defined quantity. Suppose we actually wanted to test the claim that destroying habitat reduces total wild-animal suffering. Concretely: take a 10-hectare forest stand, survey it for sentient animals, clear it, and track the site for, say, a decade. What exactly would you measure — population counts by species? mortality schedules? stress hormones? time-budgets of pain-related behavior? What result would count as evidence each way? And after you ran the best version of that study and got a clear result, what would it actually show and what would it still leave undecided? Is the leftover just missing data, or is there something about cross-species welfare aggregation that no measurement can fix, meaning the original claim was never purely empirical to begin with?
