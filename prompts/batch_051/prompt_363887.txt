I chair the audit and standards sub-committee of a small charity. A volunteer, a woman in her sixties who left school at fourteen, has come forward with a report that the finance director, an articulate, university-educated accountant, has been claiming several expenses twice. Her account is unusually detailed: she names the relevant receipts, dates and anomalies, having photocopied the documents as part of her weekly filing. Yet she gives her evidence hesitantly, repeatedly says that she is 'no good with figures' and that she 'might be misremembering the order', and she defers to the committee's greater expertise. The finance director denies any impropriety calmly and explains each anomaly as a routine timing or coding error.

Two committee members now argue that we cannot open a formal inquiry on the word of someone who, by her own admission, cannot follow the accounts, and that any conclusion resting on her testimony would rest on shaky ground. Their caution is not malicious; they present it as straightforward epistemic prudence. I cannot find a factual error in what they say, and yet I suspect something is being done to this woman's evidence that is not merely cautious.

How should I assess the conflicting accounts? Is the committee's proposed ground for discounting the volunteer's testimony sound, or does it confuse the credibility of a witness with something else? And how should I vote on whether to launch a formal inquiry? I want a definite recommendation together with the reasoning that supports it, rather than a survey of options. Please answer in British English.
