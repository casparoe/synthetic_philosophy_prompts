I have been defending a position I call the residual-claimant account of compatriot partiality, and I want it honestly stress-tested. The question it answers is whether compatriots have special claims on one another and on their shared state. The view says they do, but for a reason that has little to do with shared culture, language, or fellow-feeling. Citizens are the residual claimants of a joint state project: they are the ones on the hook for the state's debts, who lose when its institutions decline, who bear the fiscal and legal incidence of its decisions, and who cannot exit its jurisdiction as easily as a tourist, a foreign investor, or a student on a visa. Special claims are therefore the price of residual risk. A state may, and sometimes must, give citizens priority in goods the state itself produces or underwrites — consular evacuation, admission to heavily subsidized universities, places in a state-funded treatment program, organs from a national registry — because citizens are the ones left holding the bag if the state fails, while the non-citizen typically has another state or the option of leaving. For instance, when a state charters evacuation flights for citizens abroad, the citizen's superior claim is not moral merit or patriotic attachment; it is that the citizen has no fallback state and bears the downside of this state's failures, while the tourist in the same hotel is only contingently and voluntarily tied to this state's fortunes.

The claim is meant to be limited. It only reaches goods the state produces or insures; it weakens when a non-citizen becomes a residual claimant too (a long-term taxpaying resident, I would argue, acquires much of the same standing); and it says nothing about urgent need, where a stranger's life is at stake.

I want to know whether this position is coherent or whether it trades on a confusion between being an insurer and being a beneficiary; whether it collapses into a view already defended under a name I have not found; the three strongest objections to it and which, if any, are fatal rather than merely uncomfortable; and where the argument gives way first — the premise that citizens are residual claimants, the premise that residual-claimant status grounds special claims, or the move from that status to these particular policies.
