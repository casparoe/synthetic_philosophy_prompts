I'm a philosophy PhD student working on epistemology. The other night I watched two friends from my cohort go in circles about assertion, and I honestly couldn't sort out who was right at each step while it was happening.

We were talking about Priya's housemate, Dan. Dan told a visitor that their landlord is selling the building. It was true. But Dan's only basis was that he'd overheard two neighbors say it in a café. He hadn't seen a listing, hadn't gotten any notice from the landlord, nothing.

Priya said Dan didn't do anything wrong: he believed it, he had some evidence, and it was true. Marcus said that's not enough — Dan didn't know it, so he shouldn't have asserted it flat-out.

Priya: So you're saying every time someone passes along something they heard, they've violated a rule? That can't be how conversation works.

Marcus: Not every time. If he'd said "I heard the landlord is selling," that would've been fine. The problem was the flat-out "the landlord is selling." Flat-out assertion signals that you know.

Priya: That's just a verbal move. The fact that he happened to be right shows the signal was fine. If the neighbors had been wrong, then sure, blame him. But they weren't.

Marcus: That's backwards. Whether he was blameworthy can't depend on a fact he had no access to at the time. Same action, same evidence — if the landlord wasn't selling, you'd say he spoke out of turn. So you can't say he was fine just because the world cooperated.

Priya: Okay, but then you're saying that two people with the exact same evidence, one of whom says something true and one says something false, are equally okay or equally not okay. That means truth doesn't matter for assertion at all. But the whole point of assertion is to tell people the truth.

Marcus: It's not that truth doesn't matter. It's that the standard you have to meet is knowledge. True belief that falls short of knowledge is like winning a scratch-off: you don't get to claim you were justified in buying it.

Priya: Wait, so what about a doctor who says "your test came back negative" based on a test that's 99% reliable. By your view, she doesn't know, so she shouldn't say it flat-out? She should say "I believe the test came back negative"? That would terrify patients and it's not how medicine works.

Marcus: The doctor does know, in the ordinary way, because the test result is her evidence and it's good enough. The difference is that Dan's gossip is not a reliable method. Reliable methods can get you knowledge; lucky overhearing can't.

Priya: But the neighbors were reliable, as far as Dan could tell. And in fact they were right. So his method, if you want to call it that, was reliable on this occasion. You're just smuggling in "knowledge" as a label for whatever you've already decided to count.

Marcus: No, I'm saying knowledge is the norm because assertion is a kind of guarantee. When you assert, you take responsibility for the truth of what you said in a way you don't when you hedge. And you can only take that responsibility if you're in a position to know, not if you're just hoping your gossip is good.

Priya: Fine, but responsibility comes in degrees. If Dan had hedged, less responsibility. But the visitor was just making small talk about the building. Asserting on decent secondhand evidence is taking exactly the right amount of responsibility for the context. Your norm is too demanding for real conversation.

Marcus: Then we're not disagreeing about assertion; we're disagreeing about whether "the landlord is selling" in that context was meant as a flat-out assertion or as small talk. I think it was flat-out, and the visitor took it that way.

Priya: Hmm. That's actually a different point than the one you started with. You started with "he didn't know." Now you're saying the context makes it more or less of an assertion. Which is it?

Marcus: Both. The context determines what speech act he performed, and the speech act determines the standard. Flat-out assertions require knowledge; loose conversational moves don't. He performed a flat-out one.

Priya: But now we're just back to whether the visitor was entitled to take it as flat-out. Dan didn't flag it as gossip, but he also didn't present himself as having checked the records. I still don't see why the default standard should be knowledge rather than reasonable belief.

And at that point the conversation moved on. I didn't want to referee at the table, but I'm genuinely curious how an outsider would score this. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say whose right on which specific point, where each of them overreaches or shifts ground, and what distinction, if any, would have untangled them? I'd rather have a point-by-point adjudication of the arguments as actually made than a general essay on the norm of assertion.
