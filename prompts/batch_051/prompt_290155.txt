My friend has gotten really into conceptual engineering lately, and his whole argument now runs on one analogy: he says concepts are basically tools. On his view, people who go around asking what something "really" is—whether addiction is really a disease, whether intelligence is really one thing or many—are making the same mistake as someone who asks what a hammer really is instead of asking whether it drives the nail you need driven. He leans on this hard: because tools are things we build for our purposes, he says, we should stop analyzing concepts as if they have hidden essences and just fix or replace the ones that aren't doing the job we want, the way you'd throw out a hammer with a loose head. The other day he applied it to "addiction," saying there's no fact of the matter about whether it picks out a brain disease; the only real question is which concept would best serve the purposes we're trying to advance (medical treatment, assigning responsibility, designing policy, whatever), so we should just engineer it for the job.

I can't tell whether the analogy is doing real work or is just a vivid way of re-stating the conclusion, so I want you to audit it. I especially want you to separate the disanalogies that actually matter for his revisionist conclusion from the ones that are only cosmetic. One thing I'm suspicious about is that the tool picture quietly imports the idea that a concept has a single stable purpose and a fairly clear answer to who gets to decide that purpose; I want to know whether the conclusion is being smuggled in there. Give me a bottom line: does the conclusion—that we should stop analyzing and just fix concepts to serve our chosen purposes—survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if not, what is the strongest version of it that does survive?
