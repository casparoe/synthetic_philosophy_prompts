Next month I'm defending a paper at a workshop on family ethics. The position is basically Jane English's: adult children have no filial duties as such — whatever they owe their parents, they owe in virtue of an actual, ongoing friendship, and past parental sacrifice can't by itself ground a duty to maintain the relationship. My argument, in three moves: (1) sacrifice-based accounts overgenerate, since any large unrequested sacrifice could then create a duty; (2) friendship explains the cases where we really do think grown children owe their parents something; (3) the bare biological relation is symmetrical while the duties we actually attribute aren't, so biology can't be doing the grounding. The audience is mostly people working on special obligations — associative duties, nonvoluntarist accounts — plus a couple of care ethicists who think dependency rather than choice is where obligation starts. They're already convinced family relationships matter morally; they're suspicious of grounding any of it in chosen friendship, because so much of family life is unchosen. Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the six or seven objections I'll actually get from this crowd, phrased the way they'd phrase them, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. And include the question I should be praying nobody asks.
