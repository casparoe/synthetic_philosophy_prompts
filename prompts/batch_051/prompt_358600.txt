Last night I had a long argument with a friend about a rule I realize I have been using whenever I reason from correlational data to causal structure. The rule is this: when several Bayes nets are compatible with the observed conditional independencies, I adopt a sparsest one that satisfies the causal Markov condition as my working model, and I treat any additional edge as something to be added only when new data positively force it. My friend argued that this is just an aesthetic preference for simple graphs. The world, he said, might be dense and heavily connected; alternatively, a real causal pathway might leave no trace in the independence structure because its influence is cancelled along another pathway. My rule, he concluded, would systematically underfit the true structure while allowing me to feel principled about it. I replied that one has to stop somewhere, and that treating every uneliminated edge as live would leave causal structure effectively unlearnable. But I left the argument unsure whether my rule is defensible as it stands, and whether I have been helping myself to a substantive assumption about nature that I cannot justify.

Please evaluate the rule as an epistemic policy. In particular, distinguish minimality from faithfulness: does my sparse-graph rule amount to assuming the true graph is faithful to the distribution, or to something strictly weaker? Under what conditions on the data-generating process would the rule tend to recover the true causal structure, and under what conditions would it systematically mislead? Finally, restate the rule, if it can be restated, in a way that keeps whatever is worth keeping while being honest about its failure cases.
