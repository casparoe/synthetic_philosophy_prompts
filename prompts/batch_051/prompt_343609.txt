I'm giving a talk next month to our university's joint workshop on machine learning and evolutionary biology, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than more slides. The thesis I'm defending is that the charge that natural selection is a stupid optimizer rests on an unfair comparison: selection gets graded against a full-gradient, fixed-objective, batch optimizer, when the honest comparison is to gradient-based learners placed under the conditions selection actually faces—noisy updates, indirect and delayed credit assignment, and a continuously moving objective. The interesting divide, I'll argue, isn't between smart and stupid optimizers; it's between processes that optimize a fixed objective and processes that merely track a shifting one, and neither natural selection nor stochastic gradient descent falls cleanly on one side. The talk's spine is three moves: (i) fitness landscapes are non-stationary, because populations alter their own environments through frequency-dependent selection, niche construction, and coevolution, so selection rarely gets to optimize anything fixed; (ii) the properties usually cited as selection's defects—high variance, no gradient signal, credit assignment spread over generations—also show up in SGD and in REINFORCE-style updates, and some of them are now deliberately introduced (minibatch noise as a regularizer, data augmentation as environmental variation); so (iii) the defensible lesson from evolution for machine learning is not "choose better optimizers" but robustness under objective drift—standing genetic variation, bet-hedging, modularity.

The audience matters here. It will be mostly ML theorists who work on the optimization dynamics of SGD and who tend to treat evolution as a cautionary tale about local optima; a smaller group of quantitative geneticists and evolutionary biologists who find the "stupid optimizer" framing either illuminating or insulting in roughly equal measure; and two philosophers of science who will press on whether the word "optimization" is doing any explanatory work at all. I expect the ML people to challenge move (ii) and demand that I state my comparison formally rather than by analogy; the biologists to push back on my fitness-landscape idealization, possibly harder than I do; and the philosophers to ask what has been shown at all if both processes turn out to be "not really optimizing."

Please build the prep sheet with these four sections in this order and roughly these lengths: (1) "The camps and their stakes" (150 words)—identify the distinct commitments in the room and what each camp would count as a decisive objection. (2) "Objections in order of arrival" (1,000 words)—the eight or so objections I should actually expect, phrased in the idiom of the camp most likely to raise each, ordered by combined likelihood and damage, and tagged with their probable source. (3) "Replies, concessions, and soft spots" (1,000 words)—for each objection, a two-minute reply, what I should concede rather than defend, and a one-line note on where the reply is weakest. (4) "The question I'm hoping nobody asks" (150 words)—the most dangerous version of an objection I have not answered, and what I should say if it comes.
