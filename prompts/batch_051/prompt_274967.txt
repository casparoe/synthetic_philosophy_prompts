In my course on rational choice, we have just worked through Quinn's self-torturer, and I am finding it hard to say what a "rational preference" even amounts to once the puzzle gets going. I would like a working definition of a rational preference (or of the relation "is rationally preferred to") that sorts the following cases correctly: (1) a free, perceptible improvement, such as an unsolicited $10 with no strings attached, is rationally preferred to the status quo; (2) preferring an option that is worse in every respect one cares about and better in none is not rational; (3) the self-torturer's pairwise judgment that advancing to the next imperceptibly different setting is worth $10,000, at every step; (4) the self-torturer's standing preference for the initial painless setting over the final excruciating one; and (5) an intransitive cycle of pairwise preferences over three options, where A is preferred to B, B to C, and C to A. Please run your definition through each case, note any case where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and say how you would revise or defend it in light of that. Finally, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on the self-torturer and intransitive preferences, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
