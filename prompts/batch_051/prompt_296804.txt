A state privacy statute, enacted in 1971 and never amended, provides:

"Any person who, without the consent of all parties thereto, uses any instrument, apparatus, or contrivance to overhear, intercept, or record any oral communication uttered under circumstances in which the parties have a reasonable expectation of privacy shall be guilty of a felony."

The drafters were thinking of hidden tape recorders, spliced telephone wires, and bugged rooms. I have been charged under it for conduct they did not anticipate. From a public street, I aimed a high-speed camera at the outside of a window. The camera took silent video of the windowpane vibrating; no sound itself was recorded. Later, software used those vibrations to reconstruct the sound waves passing through the glass, and from them the words of a private conversation inside the room, word for word. No microphone, recorder, or other sensing device was placed in or near the room.

Does the statute reach me? Argue it from the words, from the evident purpose of the provision, and from what the drafters would have said if asked about this technique, and then tell me which approach should win and why. Then address the vagueness question separately: if the prosecution has to stretch "overhear" or "intercept" to cover reconstructing speech from a windowpane, is the statute void for vagueness as applied to this conduct? I am less interested in my own case than in the principle: how precise must a criminal statute be when it uses a broad term meant to catch future tricks it cannot yet imagine, and how does the fair-notice requirement ever catch up with that drafting strategy?
