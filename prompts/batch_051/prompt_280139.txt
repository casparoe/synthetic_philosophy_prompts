Philosophers, computer scientists, and engineers routinely slide from the claim that a problem is solvable in polynomial time to the conclusion that it is solvable in practice, and the word "tractable" usually covers the slide. I want a working definition of "tractable" — the notion people are actually relying on when they call a problem practically solvable, not merely solvable in principle — that does not simply paper over the gap.

Here are seven cases the definition should handle. Treat the descriptions as stipulated facts, not points to argue with.

1. Sorting a list of 1,000 names with one of the standard comparison-based methods, regardless of constant factor.
2. An exact algorithm for a scheduling problem that runs in n^100 steps on inputs of size n, with constant factor 1.
3. An algorithm that runs in 2^(n/100) steps on inputs of size n, with constant factor 1.
4. An algorithm that runs in (10^30)·(n^2) steps on inputs of size n.
5. A Monte Carlo algorithm that always halts in n log n steps, is wrong with probability at most 2^-100 on any input, and whose individual outputs cannot be efficiently checked afterward.
6. An algorithm for a combinatorial problem with no proven running-time bound that has solved a million benchmark instances in under a second each, although inputs deliberately constructed to defeat it take hours.
7. A quantum algorithm that solves a problem in n^3 steps on a fault-tolerant quantum computer with enough qubits — a machine that does not currently exist.

For what it is worth, I am confident that 1 is tractable and that 2 is not, even though 2 is polynomial. I have no settled view about 5 and 6, and 3, 4, and 7 look to me like the places where the word comes apart.

Propose the definition, run all seven cases through it, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then either revise it or defend the verdicts it forces.

Format your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the definition; (2) its verdicts on cases 1–3; (3) its verdicts on cases 4–7; (4) where the definition breaks down or stays silent; (5) the revision or the defense.
