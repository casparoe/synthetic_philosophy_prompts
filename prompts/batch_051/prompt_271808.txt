I recently got an inconclusive lab result (nothing scary, just annoying) and fell down a rabbit hole about how you're supposed to revise your credences when the evidence itself is uncertain. That led me to Jeffrey conditionalization, and I ended up with an argument that seems to show the rule is broken. I half-suspect step (3) or (4) is doing something sneaky, but I can't say exactly where. Which step, if any, fails?

(1) If you learn E for certain, your new credence in H should be your old credence in H conditional on E: P_new(H) = P_old(H | E).

(2) If your evidence is uncertain, and all it does is shift your credence in E from q to q* while leaving P_old(H | E) and P_old(H | not-E) unchanged, then Jeffrey's rule says P_new(H) = q*·P_old(H | E) + (1 − q*)·P_old(H | not-E). I gather this "leaving the conditionals unchanged" part is called rigidity.

(3) The order in which you take in two pieces of uncertain evidence should be irrelevent. Updating first on a shift in E1 and then on a shift in E2, or first on E2 and then on E1, means you're taking in the same total evidence, so you should end up with the same credence in H either way.

(4) But when I actually apply (2) twice in sequence — updating on E1, then updating on E2 using the conditional probabilities in the posterior after the first update, as (2) seems to require — the final credence in H depends on the order, even when both orders end up assigning exactly the same credence to E1 and to E2. In a toy case I set up, I get about 0.553 for E1-then-E2 and about 0.570 for E2-then-E1.

(5) Since (3) is non-negociable, Jeffrey conditionalization must be wrong.

Is the argument valid as stated, and if not, exactly which step fails? If (3) is the problem, what's the correct version of it?
