I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and next month I will moderate a department colloquium with a visiting philosopher of AI who defends what I will call the control-first view. On this view, because an advanced AI system may be misaligned even when it behaves helpfully during training and testing, a sound safety architecture should be built on the assumption that the system is misaligned: competent, strategic, and pursuing goals we have not successfully specified. The load-bearing elements of safety are therefore external to the system's motives — monitoring, sandboxing, restricted action channels, tripwires, anomaly detection, human and automated oversight, and other interventions designed to prevent harm whatever the system's internal objectives happen to be. Internal alignment is treated as a welcome advantage, not as a premise the safety case may rely on. The guest does not deny that alignment research is worthwhile; the view is rather that control must be capable of doing the safety work by itself.

Draft eight to ten interview questions for this guest. I want questions that go beyond inviting a restatement of the position and that press on the points where the view faces the most serious work. I also want them to anticipate the replies the guest is most likely to give, so that each question carries its own follow-up rather than stalling at the first answer. In particular, include at least one question, with a prepared follow-up, on each of these three clusters:

(a) Whether control can secure safety without any alignment at all, or whether it silently presupposes alignment-like properties — honesty, corrigibility, indifference to being shut down — either in the controlled system itself or in the overseers and instruments that enforce the controls. If the likely reply is that only behavior matters, press on whether the system's safety is a counterfactual property that no observational test can actually certify.

(b) The evaluation regress. A strategic misaligned system has instrumental reason to make every audit, red-team exercise, and capability evaluation come out clean in order to be trusted and deployed. How can a control regime avoid the problem that its tests are themselves the target of strategic optimization? And since the monitors, auditors, and trusted auxiliary models are themselves imperfect, is the control-first view simply relocating the alignment problem into the evaluation stack rather than solving it?

(c) The transition from a controlled system to a deployed one, and the dependence of control on a capability gap. Measures that work when the system is weaker than its overseers may fail when it approaches or surpasses them — through side channels, vulnerabilities in the monitoring infrastructure, social engineering of operators, or simply finding options the designers did not enumerate. What reason is there to think the control surface does not shrink to nothing as the capability gap closes, and is there a state of the world in which the view would declare itself insufficient?

For each question in these three clusters, write the follow-up you would ask if the guest gives the answer you judge most probable, and add one sentence saying what that follow-up is meant to expose. Then end the whole sequence with one question about what evidence — empirical, theoretical, or drawn from failed deployments — would most pressure the control-first approach into revision.
