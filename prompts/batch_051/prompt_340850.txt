I'm marking a take-home essay for an intermediate philosophy course and I'd like a second opinion on one of the answers. The question I set was:

Explain the difference between Hume's is-ought gap and Moore's naturalistic fallacy. Give an example of each, and say whether it's possible to commit one of them without committing the other.

The student wrote:

Both Hume and Moore are reacting to the idea that moral claims can simply be read off from the facts. Hume's is-ought gap is about inference: if all your premises are 'is' statements and your conclusion is an 'ought' statement, the argument doesn't go through unless you add some evaluative premise that connects them. Moore's naturalistic fallacy is something different: it's the mistake of trying to define 'good' in terms of a natural property such as pleasure. His open-question argument shows why any such definition fails, because for any natural property N it remains an open question whether a thing that is N is good, whereas a correct definition would make that question closed. So far the two ideas are distinct: Hume is concerned with the validity of arguments, Moore with the meaning of 'good'.

One could commit the naturalistic fallacy without committing the is-ought fallacy: someone who defines 'good' as 'what maximises pleasure' has already committed Moore's fallacy, regardless of whether they draw any inference at all. And one could commit the is-ought fallacy without the naturalistic fallacy: the argument from 'human beings are naturally capable of compassion' to 'we ought to be compassionate' has a purely descriptive premise and an evaluative conclusion, so it's invalid as it stands, but it doesn't attempt to define 'good' in natural terms.

Having said that, I think Moore's argument ultimately gives a deeper explanation of Hume's point. Once we accept that 'good' cannot be defined by any descriptive property, it follows that no set of purely descriptive premises can ever entail an evaluative conclusion; for any proposed inference you could always ask of the conclusion 'but is that good?' and the question would remain open. So the is-ought gap is really a consequence of Moore's open-question argument, and in that broader sense deriving an ought from an is just is the naturalistic fallacy. The distinction is therefore more about emphasis than substance.
