In my faculty, a proposal to allow undergraduates to use large language models on graded assignments is being defended by an analogy with calculators. The argument goes like this. We let students use calculators for arithmetic without worrying that this prevents them from understanding mathematics; the calculator merely automates a mechanical step so they can attend to the mathematics above it. By parity, the argument continues, we should permit language models to handle the mechanical parts of producing essays, proofs, or code, because this frees students for higher-level understanding, and a policy that treats such use as a threat to learning would be as misguided as forbidding calculators.

I suspect this analogy is doing a lot of work and want it audited carefully. Please give me an explicit mapping: in each case, what plays the role of the tool, the skill being offloaded, the higher-level work that remains, and the surrounding educational context. Then identify where the analogy breaks. For each disanalogy, tell me whether it is incidental or whether it affects the conclusion the analogy is being used to support. I am especially interested in three contrasts: the kind of cognitive work each tool automates (procedural and checkable versus interpretive and open-ended), the transparency and reliability of the tool's outputs, and what a student must still understand in order to use the tool well and to detect its errors. Finally, say whether the calculator analogy can carry a permissive language-model policy, and if not, what analogy better captures what is at stake when students outsource this kind of intellectual work to a model.
