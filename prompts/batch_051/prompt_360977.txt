I am a retired research chemist, and having spent my career inferring unobservable processes from physical traces, I have recently begun reading philosophy of mind and philosophy of science. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher specialising in the philosophy of the social sciences and the philosophy of mind. Please think carefully rather than quickly: begin by brainstorming two or three candidate reconstructions of the practice's implicit commitments before settling on the one you think is best, and please write your answer in British English.

Here is the practice that puzzles me. A team excavates a prehistoric burial: a body in a flexed position, strewn with red ochre, accompanied by perforated shell beads, several flint blades, and a small carved animal figurine. In the excavation report and in the museum display, two distinguishable claims are typically made. The first is a claim about social structure: the person buried there was of high status, because such objects are prestige goods. The second is a claim about the community's beliefs: the people who performed the burial believed in some form of afterlife, because the objects were provisions for the dead. The same objects are routinely used to support both claims at once: the shell beads are cited as evidence of rank and as evidence of beliefs about post-mortem existence.

To me, trained as a scientist, this looks like a single set of measurements being used to support two quite different hypotheses. In chemistry, if one spectrum were cited as evidence both that a catalyst was present and that a particular reaction mechanism was operating, I would object that the datum could not support both claims unless some further principle connected them. Yet the archaeological report moves between claims about social hierarchy and claims about belief without stating the principle that warrants this move. The practice also seems to assume that collective beliefs can be read off from objects at all — that what people did with objects is a reliable trace of what they thought, and not merely of what they did.

What I want is not a survey of archaeological theory, and not an assessment of whether any particular reconstruction in my example is correct. I want you to reconstruct the implicit philosophy of the practice. What view of the relation between objects and minds, and of evidence more generally, does this practice only make sense under? Which rival views does it quietly rule out? Are the two inferences — about social status and about belief — presupposing consistent things, or are they in tension? What does the practice strictly presuppose, as opposed to what it merely fits well with, and where is it philosophically underdetermined? Finally, what would have to change in how excavation reports and museum labels are written if the presupposed view were replaced with a deflationary alternative, on which the objects are not traces of shared belief but only the material residue of practical choices within social competition and ritual routine?
