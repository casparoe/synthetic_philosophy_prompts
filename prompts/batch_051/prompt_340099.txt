A friend and I went back and forth for something like two hours last night about whether majority rule is secretly a truth-tracker, and he left me with this arguement that I cant shake. It goes:

(1) Pick any yes/no question with an objective answer. You can always find a group of people such that each member is more likely than not to get it right — not experts, just people better than a coin flip.

(2) The Condorcet jury theorem says: if each voter is more likely right than wrong and their votes are independent, then the chance that the majority is right goes up with group size and approaches certainty as the group grows.

(3) So for any factual yes/no question, we can, at least approximately, assemble a big group of people who each clear the better-than-chance bar and whose answers are roughly independent, and then the majority will be right with probability arbitrarily close to 1.

(4) But that means any factual question whatsoever can be settled by polling enough people: whether a defendant is guilty, whether some dam will hold, whether the number of planets with life is even or odd — whatever.

(5) This cant be right. Large groups are confidently wrong all the time: panics, witch trials, elections where a majority backs a false claim. Polling a million people does not turn a crowd into an oracle.

I want to keep the theorem itself — it's a theorem, you can't argue with the math — and I also want to keep the weaker thought that groups can be smarter than their average member. But I won't accept that a big enough poll is an oracle. So which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me: which uses of the theorem still stand, and which were quietly depending on the step I gave up?

Give me a proper diagnosis in prose first. Then, after the prose, give me a short JSON object with these fields:

- "flawed_step": the number of the step (or the premise within a step) you'd reject first
- "diagnosis": one sentence on exactly what goes wrong
- "cost": what giving up that step costs — which conclusions or uses of the theorem no longer go through
- "repaired_theorem": the theorem restated with the missing or weakened condition made explicit
- "verdict": one of "the argument is repairable", "the argument is fatal to this use of the theorem", or "it is valid and which premise to give up is a judgment call"

And American English, please.
