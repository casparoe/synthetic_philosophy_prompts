I keep getting stuck on a very specific fight between a tidy theory of gratitude and an intuition that won't leave me alone, and I'd rather have someone actually adjudicate it than give me the usual survey of options. The theory I'm drawn to says gratitude is a response to benevolence — what makes an act a benefaction, and what grounds the debt-like response, is that the benefactor acted for my sake, with some concern for my good rather than their own. That view does a lot of work for me: it explains why I don't owe the weather anything even when it saves my crops, why I don't owe anything to the rival who accidentally gets me hired while trying to ruin me, and why the cashier's courtesy doesn't leave me in anyone's debt. The intuition that threatens it is this: a stranger slips on the pavement, crashes into me, and knocks me out of the path of a bus. They never saw me, never formed a single thought about my welfare. And yet it feels false, almost callous, to say I owe them nothing at all — and false in a way that isn't just 'I'm glad the outcome happened,' but something with a normative shape, the same kind of thing real gratitude has. So the theory says the benefactor's good will is necessary; the intuition says the benefit alone can sometimes do the job, at least in a minor key. What I want is a negotiated verdict. Lay out the strongest version of the case for each of these moves: (a) the intuition is a misfiring of a generally reliable 'warmth toward the source of benefits' mechanism and should be discounted; (b) the benevolence view should be amended so the lucky benefactor comes out mattering, and what that amendment costs elsewhere; (c) there's a reading on wich both survive, maybe by distinguishing gratitude proper from something thinner like appreciation, or by widening 'benevolence' enough to capture the lucky case without cheating. I'd also want the verdict to generalize to gratitude for past help I can't remember receiving, so don't let it hang on the bystander's dramatics. Then tell me which move you'd actually make and what you'd have to give up to make it.
