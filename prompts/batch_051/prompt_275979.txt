Two members of my reading group got into an argument about Wolfram's A New Kind of Science that none of the rest of us could settle. Miriam said the book is not really science, because its central claims — the principle of computational equivalence and the alleged ubiquity of complex (class 4) behavior among simple rules — are framed so loosely that no empirical finding could count against them. Tom replied that this judges an entire research program by one over-stated principle: the book also reports a large body of reproducible work about what simple computational rules actually do, and that work generated concrete heuristics, for instance that searching small rule spaces can replace elaborate hand-built models of natural systems. Miriam answered that cataloguing cellular automata is natural history rather than a theory of nature, and that when people have tried to sharpen the principle of computational equivalence it has either become false or collapsed into tautology. Tom said established science includes a great deal of exploratory work that does not issue in falsifiable predictions, and that the principle does have empirical content: it predicts that no naturally occurring or evolved system should exhibit computational behavior beyond a universal system, and it licenses a method — enumerate simple programs before constructing complex models — whose success can be assessed. Miriam replied that this ceiling prediction only works if one has an independent way of ranking computational sophistication, and in practice the ranking is gerrymandered to accommodate whatever turns up. I could not referee it. Please go through their exchange move by move: which claims are right, which are wrong, what both of them are missing, and who is more right overall. Write your adjudication as continuous prose, in the style of an email or a letter, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
