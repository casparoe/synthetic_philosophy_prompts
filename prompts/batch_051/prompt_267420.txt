I've been chewing on something I've started calling the "granularity illusion" in how we supervise models, and I want an honest verdict on whether it's a real idea or just a metaphor with too much confidence. The claim is this: in any training setup, the only signal that actually touches reality is the terminal outcome — the answer was right, the code past the tests, the plan worked when you ran it. Process supervision, rewarding or penalizing intermediate steps, doesn't open a second channel to the world. It just takes that one terminal signal and smears it back across the chain of reasoning, and whatever fills the gaps while it smears is the supervisor's taste about what good reasoning looks like. So the finer the process supervision, the more of your training signal is aesthetics and the less is contact with reality. I think this predicts something I keep seeing: process-supervised models produce chain-of-thought that is clean, legible, and confidently wrong in exactly the places where a grader would nod along but the world would disagree.

I know there's work on reward shaping, and I have a nagging feeling this is just potential-based reward shaping or annotator bias with extra steps, but I honestly can't tell which parts are new and which are trivially old. It also has exceptions that bug me: a proof assistant checking each step of a Lean proof is process-level feedback that's fully world-grounded, not aesthetic, and a compiler type-checking at each stage seems similar. So maybe the principle should be that the illusion only bites when the intermediate feedback is a human's approving glance rather than an executable check — but then it might shrink to "subjective feedback is subjective," which is boring. Where exactly does the idea break? What does it wrongly count as aesthetics? And does any useful core survive once the exceptions are carved out?
