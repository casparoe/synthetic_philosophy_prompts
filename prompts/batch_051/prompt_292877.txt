I'm a novelist, and last week at a dinner party two of my writer friends got into it over a third friend's novel. The book ends with its protagonist deciding not to leave her marriage, and when her sister asks why, she can't do better than something like: 'I can't explain it — it's simply true that I should keep my vow.' Adam said the ending was empty, because by Peirce's pragmatic maxim a belief with no conceivable practical effects is just noise, and 'simply true' here carries none. Priya said that can't be right: the belief has obvious practical effects — it keeps her married, changes how she treats her husband, makes her turn down an affair. Adam came back that those effects belong to the ordinary practical side of the belief, not to the 'simply true' part; if you deleted the metaphysical claim and kept only the behaivoral dispositions, nothing in her life would differ, so the ending is smuggling in a phrase with no meaning. Priya said the 'simply true' part does differ: it makes her treat the belief as unconditional, so she doesn't re-litigate the marriage every time the pay-offs shift — and treating the maxim as requiring a behavioral difference indistinguishable from a rule of thumb begs the question against beliefs that present themselves as demands. Adam replied that this saves the maxim only by making it unfalsifiable, since you can always discover some disposition. Priya said so what — as a writer's discipline it still forces you to show, not tell. I could see both sides and didn't know how to referee. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — say who is right about which specific point, where each overreaches, and who is more right overall.
