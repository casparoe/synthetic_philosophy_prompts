I’m a university student in a political philosophy course on Mill and the limits of free speech, and I keep having the same uncomfortable thought in discussion. Our professor presents the harm principle as a threshold: a person’s liberty may be restricted only to prevent harm to others, not to protect the person from themselves or to enforce what others regard as better or wiser. But the more I look at Mill’s argument in On Liberty, the less it looks like a factual claim about where harm begins, and the more it looks like a decision-theoretic claim about which kind of error a liberal society should prefer.

Specifically, I see the structure of an asymmetric loss function. Mill seems to treat the error of suppressing speech that would not in fact have caused harm as much more serious than the error of permitting speech that does cause harm. That is, he does not seem to argue that regulators will rarely be right when they intervene; he argues that the cost of being wrong in one direction is not symmetrical with the cost of being wrong in the other. The harm principle, on this reading, isn’t primarily a line between harmful and harmless conduct. It’s a standing instruction to set the burden of proof so high for those who would restrict speech because false positives are treated as worse than false negatives. My classmates say this makes Mill sound like a cost–benefit analyst and misses what is distinctive about his liberalism. I can’t decide whether I’ve noticed a deep structural point or just pattern-matched the words false positive onto a moral principle.
