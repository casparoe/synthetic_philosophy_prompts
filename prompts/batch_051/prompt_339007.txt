I've been writing down my gut verdicts on refugee and asylum cases, and I want to know whether there is a single principle behind them, or how near to one I can get before something has to give. I keep noticing that I treat four questions as separable even though public debate runs them together: who counts as a refugee; whether a state may send someone away; whether a state must actually settle someone; and how the burden of settling should be distributed among states. I'd like you to answer at the level of normative principle, not by citing treaties or case law. Here are my cases, numbered for reference.

1. Hana — a journalist imprisoned and tortured for her reporting, released only after signing a confession, and now threatened with re-arrest — fled directly here. Verdict: a textbook refugee. Returning her would be indefensible, and we must grant her asylum, not merely leave her in a legal limbo.

2. Carlos — his brother was killed by a cartel after refusing to work as a lookout; Carlos refused too and was threatened; the police are unwilling or unable to protect him. He passed through a neighbouring country with a functioning asylum system, stayed four days without applying, and then travelled on to us. Verdict: he counts as a refugee, because persecution by non-state actors counts when the state will not or cannot protect its citizens. But I do not think our state specifically must settle him: returning him to the safe country, or paying for his protection there, seems acceptable.

3. Mariam — fled a husband who beat her and threatened to kill her; the police dismissed it as a family matter and refused to take a report. Verdict: she counts. The state's failure to protect is exactly why this is persecution even though the immediate persecutor is private.

4. Omar — an engineer whose country's economy collapsed. No one has threatened him and the government does not target him, but he cannot feed or educate his children. Verdict: a serious humanitarian problem, but not a refugee claim as I understand it. We may choose to admit him through immigration or labour channels, but I do not think the obligation not to return him flows from the same source as in Hana's case.

5. Grace — from a low-lying island state. Saltwater intrusion has destroyed the soil, two floods have destroyed her house, and her government lacks the resources to maintain the seawalls, let alone relocate everyone. Her country has contributed almost nothing to global emissions; the countries she might go to have, collectively, contributed a great deal. Verdict: I want to say she has a claim to admission and durable support at least as strong as a classical refugee's. But I cannot ground it in persecution, and I cannot point to any discrete act by the receiving state that wronged her. This is one of two verdicts I keep going back and forth on.

6. Tomas — for two years he was a guard at an abusive detention facility and took part in beatings of detainees. He fled after refusing an order to kill a prisoner, and he now fears both the state, for refusing, and opposition groups, for his past. Verdict: he has a well-founded fear, but he is not entitled to protection. Complicity in serious wrongs excludes him, even though the refusal that put him at risk was the right act.

7. Nadia — a lesbian whose family in the capital has sworn to kill her; the police will not intervene. She could move alone to a distant city, cut off contact with everyone, and never disclose her orientation, and she would likely be safe. Verdict: that is not an acceptable internal flight alternative. She is a refugee even though she could avoid the harm by hiding who she is.

8. Allocation — a country has already admitted 900,000 displaced people in five years and its schools and hospitals are at capacity; another, equally wealthy country has admitted 9,000. A new group of 40 refugees arrives at the first country's border. Verdict: the first country still may not turn them back — the duty not to return binds each state individually — but the fair cost of settlement should fall mainly on the second country. I do not think the second country's free-riding cancels the first country's duty to the people in front of it, but I do think it gives the first country a claim to burden-sharing or compensation. This is the other verdict I keep going back and forth on.

I can already see two tensions in myself. One is between Omar and Grace: both face severe deprivation caused by something other than persecution, yet I exclude Omar and want strongly to include Grace. If my principle says deprivation without persecution is not enough, it loses Grace; if it says severe deprivation plus a state unable to remedy it is enough, it seems to take in Omar too, and perhaps very many others. The other tension is between Carlos and Nadia: in both cases the person could be safe if they accepted a heavy constraint on how and where they live, yet I treat remaining in a safe third country as a legitimate requirement and concealment of one's identity as not. I want to know whether those two lines can be drawn in a principled way, or whether one verdict in each pair has to go.

What I would like from you, in prose first: state the best principle you can find behind these verdicts, or the best two or three candidate principles if no single one fits. For each principle, make it precise enough to decide cases; say which of my cases motivates each clause; tell me flatly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate; and say what it predicts for two further cases I have not listed:

(a) Aisha, fleeing a forced marriage arranged to settle a dispute between families, where the police refuse to intervene.
(b) Samuel, from a country whose state has collapsed, at risk from indiscriminate shelling between armed factions. No one has singled him out, and there is no functioning state to protect him or to be asked for protection.

If the best principle forces me to overturn some of my verdicts, rank the ones I should give up, in order, and say what each concession costs or buys. If I am forced to choose, I would most like to keep the verdicts on Hana, Mariam, Nadia, and Grace; I would surrender the Carlos settlement-hedge before I would give up the Tomas exclusion.

Then, after the prose, give me a table with exactly these columns:

- Case (my number and name)
- My stated verdict
- What the best candidate principle delivers for the case
- Match? (Yes/No; if No, name the verdict that must be overturned)
- Clause or motivating case doing the work

If you end up defending more than one candidate principle, add a second table comparing them, with these columns: Candidate principle; Core formulation in one sentence; Verdicts it captures (by case number); Verdicts it rejects or cannot accommodate; Prediction for Aisha and Samuel.
