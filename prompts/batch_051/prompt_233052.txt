So I keep going back and forth with my roommate about the absurdity doctrine, and I want someone to check my criteria against a case instead of just arguing at me. My criteria for when a court may ignore what a statute plainly says are: (1) the literal reading has to produce a result that's absurd in a strong sense, not just bad policy or probably not what the drafters would have wanted; (2) the absurdity has to be traceable to something inside the statute itself — its definitions, its structure, its stated purpose — not just the judge's own sense that the result is too harsh; (3) the court should depart from the text only as far as needed to avoid the absurdity, and no further.

Now consider this. A city ordinance says: 'No person shall bring any animal into a public market.' The definitions section says 'animal' means every living creature other than a human being. A parent brings their kid's goldfish in a small bowl into the market, and the city actually prosecutes. The parent says this is absurd: the whole point of the rule is sanitation, and a goldfish in a sealed bowl isn't a sanitation problem, while the city says the definition is clear and the judge shouldn't write in an exception the council could have added.

Go through my criteria one by one and tell me whether the goldfish case clears them. Where they underdetermine the verdict, say so. Then tell me whether the case shows one of the criteria needs changing — and in particular whether the difference between prosecuting the goldfish and prosecuting someone with, say, a pet rat in a market is a difference the word 'absurd' can actually do, or whether the doctrine is just covertly grading how sympathetic the defendant is.
