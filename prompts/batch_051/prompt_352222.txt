I've been working through the Epicurean puzzle — the argument that death cannot harm the one who dies, because there is no subject present to bear the harm once death has arrived — and I've found the standard reply unsatisfying in a way I want pressure-tested. The usual move is to say that death harms us by depriving us of the goods we would otherwise have enjoyed, so the badness isn't located in the state of being dead but in the life that was prevented. I accept something like that, but it keeps treating the lost goods as if they were external objects stripped away. I've ended up with a different picture, and I've been calling it the open-position account.

Here is the account. A person at a time is holding a large portfolio of forward-directed dispositions: plans, skills in the middle of being built, relationships being tended, preferences whose point presupposes a future, small expectations about tomorrow and next year. I call these open positions. They are "positions" in the sense that their value is partly conditional on how later states of the world turn out, and they are "open" in the sense that the person is still invested in their resolution; they don't have to be consciously entertained, because they are mostly structural facts about what the person is up to right now. My claim is that death is bad for the one who dies exactly insofar as it settles open positions before they can be resolved on the merits — it unilaterally closes a book the person was still keeping open. What we call deprivation is downstream of that. We aren't primarily losing future experiences; we are losing the chance to see the positions we had already opened get their outcomes.

This handles, at least neatly, several things that otherwise get treated as add-ons: why death in dreamless sleep can still be bad (the positions persist through sleep even when nothing is felt), why the time before birth isn't bad in the parallel way (no positions had been opened yet), and why a quick death is a mercy for someone who has genuinely closed out every position but a catastrophe for someone with a full book. But I can already see the stress points, and I want an honest assessment. It seems to make death's badness hostage to the person's own psychology: a self-deceived person with doomed positions might come out less harmed than a clear-eyed person in the same circumstances, and an infant — who has barely opened any positions — might count as barely harmed, which feels wrong. I also can't tell whether this is just the standard deprivation account wearing a finance metaphor, or whether it commits me to something stronger. Is there an existing view this collapses into, and under what name? What are the three or four strongest objections, and which ones are fatal rather than merely uncomfortable? And is the core idea — that the harm of death sits in the foreclosing of already-open positions rather than in the loss of future goods — actually distinct, or should I drop it as a relabeling?
