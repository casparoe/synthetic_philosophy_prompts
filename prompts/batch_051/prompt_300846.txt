I've been going back and forth with a friend in a defense-tech policy class about whether meaningful human control over autonomous weapons is a real requirement or just a slogan, and I keep coming back to a modified trolley problem that I can't quite get straight. Take the standard case: a trolley is heading toward five people, and a bystander can pull a lever to send it down a side track where it will kill one person. Everyone knows the two big answers: minimize deaths and pull, or refuse because you'd be using the one person as a means. Now change one thing. The lever is no longer pulled by a human being. It is operated by an autonomous targeting system whose visual classifier correctly counts the people on each track 95% of the time, and the system is programmed to pull the lever whenever its expected-death calculation favors pulling. A human operator sits at a console with a slightly delayed, low-resolution feed of the tracks and has a three-second window to press a hold button before the system pulls; if she does nothing, it pulls by default. So the default has flipped: in the original case, inaction kills the five and you have to actively choose to kill the one, but here inaction kills the one and you have to actively intervene to let the five die.

Here is what I want an actual analysis of, not just hand-wringing. Do the standard utilitarian and deontological arguments about the original trolley survive this change, and where do they land — does the deontologist now have to press the hold button, or is three seconds too quick for that to count as a refusal? Is the person at the console meaningfully in control, or is the veto just a rubber stamp for an algorithm that has already encoded one side of the debate? And if I stretch the override window to twenty seconds and give the operator a clear, real-time feed, has anything philosophically important changed, or is the problem not the timing and the picture quality but the fact that the default is machine action?
