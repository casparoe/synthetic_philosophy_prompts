In debates about immigration, integration is treated as the success term: people ask whether migrants are integrating, whether a policy promotes integration, and whether a generous welfare state requires integration as a condition of admission. The word is confident in ordinary use and elusive under pressure. I want a working definition of integration — precise enough to tell whether a particular person is integrated, and whether an integration program has succeeded — that handles the cases below.

I take these to be clear positives. (1) A woman who arrived as an adult twenty years ago, has citizenship, works as a nurse, speaks the language fluently, sits on her neighbourhood council, and says she feels fully at ease in the country. (2) The adult daughter of immigrants, born and schooled in the country, who works for a local firm, votes, and moves easily through the wider society while continuing to speak her parents' language at home and visit their country of origin.

I take these to be clear negatives. (3) A seasonal agricultural worker on a temporary visa, housed by his employer, who speaks no local language, has no contacts outside the worksite, sends his pay home, and will leave after the harvest. (4) A lawful permanent resident of thirty years who lives and works entirely within a co-ethnic enclave, speaks almost no local language, and has no social or civic ties to the wider society, though he is law-abiding and content.

The following I cannot settle. (5) An affluent software engineer who has held permanent residence for a decade and pays taxes, but works remotely for a foreign employer in a foreign language, socializes almost entirely with other expats, likes the country, and says he does not identify with it. (6) A migrant woman who has learned the language, adopted local customs, joined local clubs, and very much wants to belong, but is long-term unemployed, receives benefits, and reports that locals still treat her as a foreigner. (7) A seasonal worker who returns to the same harvest five months every year, stays legally, speaks the local language conversationally, and has local friendships, but has no permanent residence and no intention to settle. (8) A woman who came through marriage, is a full-time homemaker with young children, speaks the local language only basically, but whose daily life is densely woven into a local extended family and neighbourhood.

Propose a working definition of integration, run it against each case, and say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer; then either revise the definition or defend the verdict it yields. I also suspect the word is doing two jobs at once — one descriptive, tracking social facts about an immigrant's position, and one normative, tracking a standard that immigrants or receiving societies are supposed to meet. If you think so, give a definition for each job and say which of my cases change classification between them; if you think one definition can do both, argue for that. If you think the request as framed rests on a confusion — for instance, that integration is not something that admits of a definition, or that my case descriptions already presuppose the answer — say so plainly rather than forcing a definition. And end by telling me which of your verdicts you find least secure.
