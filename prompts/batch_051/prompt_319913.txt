Please answer as an expert in early modern philosophy with a strong secondary interest in the philosophy of animal minds. Take your time: before settling on an answer, brainstorm several candidate objections and discard any that turn out, on inspection, to be restatements or special cases of the four given below.

I am putting together a teaching file on Descartes's doctrine that non-human animals are mindless automata — that is, that they lack thought and consciousness, and that their behaviour is wholly produced by the disposition of their organs, much as a clock's movements are produced by its mechanism. The file already contains the four objections I encounter most often:

(1) The argument from analogy: where an animal resembles a human being in behaviour and bodily structure, and I explain the human's behaviour by thoughts and feelings, the best explanation of the animal's similar behaviour is a similar inner state; refusing that inference in the animal case, while making it in the human case, looks arbitrary.

(2) The evolutionary-continuity objection: after Darwin, human mental capacities are products of the same historical process as everything else about us; mindedness did not arrive in a single step, so a species-wide gulf that makes animals wholly mindless and humans wholly minded is biologically arbitrary.

(3) The marginal-cases objection: Descartes's public test for the presence of thought is flexible, compositional language — the ability to arrange signs to answer the sense of what is said. But prelinguistic infants, some humans with severe cognitive or linguistic impairments, and people who have lost language through brain damage fail that test, and we do not conclude that they lack minds. So the test cannot by itself settle the animal case either.

(4) The neurophysiological-similarity objection: the brain structures most implicated in pain and affect in human beings are present, in homologous form, in other mammals; if a physicalist allows that those structures realise conscious states in us, then denying anything similar to an animal with the same structures requires a further principled difference, and that difference is never supplied.

I know these four are related, and I know a Cartesian has replies to each (for example, distinguishing thought from mere sensation, or insisting that an animal may behave exactly as if it felt pain without feeling anything). I am not asking for those replies; nor am I asking for the moral-cost objection that treating animals as machines historically licensed vivisection, since that objects to the use of the doctrine rather than to its truth.

What I want is one further objection to the truth of the doctrine itself that is genuinely different from all four: not a restatement, not a special case, and not a combination of them. Then tell me honestly whether the objection is any good — does it damage the doctrine independently of the four already listed, or does it only work if one of those has already landed?

Please write your answer in British English.
