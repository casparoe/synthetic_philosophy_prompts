In our political economy reading group, one member defended the claim that collective wage bargaining should be treated as presumptively anticompetitive, on the grounds that a union is, at bottom, a cartel for the sale of labor. His reasoning was that a group of otherwise competing sellers who agree on a price for their product and withhold supply to enforce it are engaged in exactly the kind of price-fixing antitrust law exists to stop, so a union is simply that structure applied to labor, and its defenders owe us an explanation of why this one collusive arrangement deserves immunity. I would like a written audit of how far the analogy really holds. Produce a correspondence table with at least the product, the sellers, the fixed price, the withheld supply, and the consumer, then work through the places where the mapping strains or fails. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the antitrust conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic. End by saying whether the argument survives once those disanalogies are on the table, whether it was being carried by the analogy, and whether some other comparison does better.
