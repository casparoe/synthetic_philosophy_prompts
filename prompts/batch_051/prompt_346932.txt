I run a small reading group on AI interpretability—about a dozen grad students, mostly philosophy with a few CS people who keep the rest of us honest—and we've been chewing on chain-of-thought faithfulness. The group's vibe is less "read and discuss the paper" and more "try to run into the problem ourselves." I want a 90-minute session that makes people feel why a language model's stated chain of thought might not report its actual reasoning. The subtle version of the point isn't that the model is lying; it's that the chain of thought is itself just more generated text, produced under training and reward pressures, and there may be no privileged inner state that the report could be a faithful readout of. I'd rather the participants discover that difference than have me assert it.

So here's the kind of thing I have in mind, though I want you to design it properly from scratch. For 12–15 people. Split the room into "models" and "auditors." The models solve some structured task under a time limit and must narrate their reasoning out loud, step by step, as they go; the auditors see only the narration and the final answers (maybe plus timings or scratch work, your call). The setup should be tuned so that a narration can be fluent, plausible, and causally irrelevant all at once. I guess the cleanest way is an incentive mismatch: the actual payoff rewards something the narration doesn't mention—speed, confidence, matching the crowd, whatever—while the narration keeps sounding like sober problem-solving. But I don't want it to collapse into the boring lesson that people sometimes fib when there's an audience. It should land closer to: a plausible self-report need not be a faithful one, even when the speaker is trying to be honest. And ideally the "models" themselves should leave genuinely unsure how much of their own narration was real reasoning versus post-hoc gloss. Give me roles, rules, materials that take under ten minutes to set up, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the faithfulness/plausibility distinction and why that matters for whether we should trust a model's chain of thought. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about what this can't reproduce about real chain-of-thought: I worry that human players do have introspective access, so the game might capture the incentive side while missing the no-ground-truth side—if so, say how the debrief should patch that.
