I work in the climate office of a small island government — not a scientist or an academic, mostly I draft position papers and talking points for my boss. Lately I keep having to take a position on solar geoengineering: putting reflective particles high in the atmosphere to cool the planet. Every few months a university consortium, a foundation, or a foreign ministry floats a "small field trail" or asks us to co-sign a declaration, and my boss wants a one-page recommendation from me by the end of the week. What makes it hard is that my gut keeps flipping. On one hand, every time the option gets treated as live, the big emitters in the room get more relaxed about their own cuts — they talk like they have a backup plan, so our push for faster emissions cuts loses what little steam it had. I think that is what people call moral hazard, but here it is not a bank being bailed out, it is the whole planet. On the other hand, say we back a deployment and then the spraying stops — a war, a funding fight, or the government running the program collapses — the cooling unmakes itself in a couple of years and temperatures snap up faster than ecosystems or farms can adjust; I've heard it called termination shock. That would be far worse for us than the warming we were trying to offset. And then there is the question of who decides: we are among the most exposed and the least responsible for the emissions, we have almost no veto, but our sign-off gets used as legitimacy. I know no procedure can make the call for me, but I want something that stops me hiding behind one-liners.

Could you write me a reusable decision procedure — a checklist in words — for deciding whether we should oppose, support, or support-with-conditions a given solar geoengineering proposal (a field trial, a deployment, or a joint statement)? It should walk through the moral-hazard worry, the termination-shock worry, and the who-decides worry in some deliberate order, and it should be usable by a non-expert with about two days to produce a recommendation. For each worry, use the strongest version you know, not the slogan version; I can write the slogan version myself. Please answer in American English, organized in exactly these sections, with rough lengths: "Bottom line" (one or two sentences); "The procedure" (about 400 words — a numbered checklist, each step with a short yes/no or scoring test and a note on what would have to be true to move on); "Worked cases" (about 300 words — apply it once to a small outdoor field trial and once to a full deployment, showing where the two part ways); "Where it will misfire" (about 150 words, including at least one case where the procedure tells us something we won't want to hear); "What the procedure can't settle" (about 100 words).
