I took a philosophy of mind course a couple years ago and keep coming back to the Clark and Chalmers Otto/Inga case. Otto is the guy with mild memory loss who writes everything in a notebook he always carries and consults without hesitation, and the claim is that the notebook is literally part of his memory, not just a tool. The parity principle, as I remember it, says that if something external plays the role an internal process would play, then it should count as part of the cognitive system. I get that argument and honestly find it pretty convincing for Otto's notebook.

But Ive been thinking that my actual "external memory" is nothing like Otto's notebook. My notes live in a cloud app; the app reorders them, surfaces old ones to me unprompted, sometimes autocorrects my own past words, and the company could change the product or delete my data tomorrow. If I ask the app for a memory, the answer is filtered by a recommendation algorithm before it reaches me. Does the extended mind argument survive this kind of artifact, or did it only go through because Otto owned and controlled his notebook? If it breaks, which premise exactly is doing the work to break it: control, reliability, transparency, the fact that the system has its own interests, or something else? And is there a plausible repair, like saying the extended mind reaches into the parts a user doesn't control, or does that stretch the thesis too far?
