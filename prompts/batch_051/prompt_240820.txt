I'm organizing a reading group on science fiction, and this month we're doing Kurt Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five. Most of the regulars come from rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums, so there's some shared background to build on: they know the standard claims about special relativity undermining a privileged cosmic present, and a few have name-dropped the Rietdijk–Putnam argument — the line of thought that, because simultaneity is frame-relative, any event one wants to call past or future is simultaneous with some observer's now, so the universe is best pictured as a four-dimensional block rather than a flowing river of time. The novel looks like an almost suspiciously perfect dramatization of that picture. The Tralfamadorians speak as if every moment simply exists, and Billy Pilgrim drifts loose from chronological time, experiencing his life as a set of co-existing moments rather than a sequence. I'd like you to read the novel against the block universe view: where does Vonnegut's eternalism track what the physics actually licenses, and where does he quietly trade it in for something stronger?

Specifically, I want to know whether the novel's slide into fatalism follows from the block universe or is an illicit extra step. The Tralfamadorians take the existence of all moments to imply that free will is a specifically human illusion and that one should meet every death with the same even-toned acceptance. Is that a licensed inference, or does it conflate the claim that the future is already real with the claim that the future would be unchanged no matter what anyone did? And what does the Rietdijk–Putnam argument actually establish, as against what Vonnegut needs for his existential-pacifist conclusion? I'd value an honest critic's accounting: a reading that takes both the physics and the novel seriously, that identifies where the narrative devices illustrate the philosophy well and where they warp it, and that says what Slaughterhouse-Five adds to the philosophical debate that the argument alone doesn't. No plot summary and no biographical criticism; I want the ideas.
