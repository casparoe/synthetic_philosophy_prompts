I have been developing a refinement of the Stoic dichotomy of control and would like an honest assessment of it, including where it collapses or quietly changes the point of the original view.

As I understand the standard position, Epictetus divides everything into what is up to us — judgment, impulse, desire, aversion, the activity of the will itself — and what is not up to us — the body, reputation, property, office, and in general everything external to the will. The therapeutic instruction is to concern ourselves only with the first class and to regard the second as indifferent. I think this second class conceals a distinction that matters for how the instruction should be applied.

My proposal is to split the not-up-to-us class in two. Some things are external to the will and also outside any causal purchase of my choices: the weather over another continent tomorrow, whether a passerby happens to notice me, the fact that a historical event occurred one way rather than another. Call these the mere externals. Other things are external in the sense that their occurrence is not determined by my will, but my deliberate activity can shift their probability in a reasonably stable way: my health, which my habits influence but never guarantee; my chances at a position, which I can improve by preparing and applying widely; a friend's forgiveness, which a sincere and undefended apology can make more likely but cannot secure. Call these the influenceables.

The central claim is that the dichotomy's practical instruction — withdraw concern from everything outside the will — should take a different form for each subclass. For mere externals, the standard instruction stands: they are indifferent without qualification, and no rational striving can take them as its object. For influenceables, the correct discipline is not bare detachment but a two-part stance I call invested equanimity: pursue the preferred outcome through one's own excellent activity, while having already accepted in advance that the outcome itself will be what it is and is not one's own.

I recognize that this may simply be the Stoic doctrine of preferred indifferents pursued with the reserve clause, or the modern trichotomy of control under a new name, and that the original partition may have been deliberately exhaustive in a way my refinement obscures. So I want to know: is the distinction coherent, and does it add anything to the standard view once one separates the activity of choosing from the outcome that the choice aims at? Does treating influenceables as something other than sheer indifferents quietly reintroduce the attachment the dichotomy exists to dissolve, or does it correct an overly ascetic reading of the tradition? Give me the strongest version of my proposal first, and then say exactly where it fails or stops being distinctive.
