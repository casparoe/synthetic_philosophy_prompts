You are asked to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a virtue ethicist in the Aristotelian tradition and a situationist in the tradition of John Doris and Gilbert Harman. After a year of arguing, the two parties have accepted that the collaboration must produce a document that both could sign. The virtue ethicist maintains that the virtues are robust, causally effective dispositions: a compassionate person can be relied on to notice and respond to need across a broad range of trait-relevant situations, and such dispositions are cultivable by practice. The situationist maintains that the experimental record — helping and honesty studies, obedience experiments, manipulations of mood, cost, and social pressure, together with the modest size of cross-situational consistency coefficients in personality research — shows that behavior is dominated by features of the situation and that global traits of the kind virtue ethics requires are empirically unsupported.

The document is to have four parts. First, a numbered list of claims that both sides can sign, phrased so that neither side is conceding anything it would later need to retract; these should cover what the classic experiments do and do not show. Second, a table of the residual disagreements, with each side's own position stated in wording that side accepts. Third, a list of cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. At least three cruxes should be identified, and candidates include whether the virtues must be broad global dispositions or may be narrow, situation-indexed ones; whether modest but nonzero cross-situational consistency is compatible with virtue, and if so how much is enough; and whether the experiments show that virtues do not exist or only that they are rarer and harder to cultivate than ordinary moral praise assumes. Fourth, for each crux, state a concrete longitudinal or experience-sampling study, or a specifically described behavioral case, that both sides agree would bear on the crux, and record each side's prediction in advance.

The hardest part is the drafting itself. Where a disagreement is partly or wholly verbal — for instance, because the two sides have used "compassion" or "trait" with different scopes — repair the wording and say explicitly which part of the dispute dissolves. End with a short note identifying the question whose resolution would do most to advance the collaboration, even if the two sides predict different answers to it.
