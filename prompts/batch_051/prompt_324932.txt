I’m taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of language, and we’re reading Russell’s “On Denoting” closely. We spent most of a session on the following passage from Russell:

“Take as an instance ‘the father of Charles II was executed’. This asserts that there was an x who was the father of Charles II and was executed. Now the, when it is strictly used, involves uniqueness; we do, it is true, speak of ‘the son of So-and-so’ even when So-and-so has several sons, but it would be more correct to say ‘a son of So-and-so’.”

The discussion got stuck on a point I’d like you to adjudicate. Russell says that when “the” is strictly used, it involves uniqueness, and that a phrase like “the son of So-and-so” is loose and would more correctly be “a son.” But consider an ordinary exchange: a neighbor has two sons, and I say, “I gave a ride to the son of our neighbor yesterday.” My interlocutor does not typically respond, “That’s false, since there is more than one”; she responds, “Which one?” The definite description seems to be doing useful work even though the strict uniqueness condition fails. Please argue that Russell’s theory of descriptions, as stated in this passage, should be amended so that the uniqueness condition for definite descriptions is evaluated relative to a contextually salient domain rather than absolute uniqueness. Make clear whether this is best understood as a repair of Russell’s theory or as a concession that ordinary language is not fully captured by the formal analysis. Also apply your proposal to Russell’s own example, “the father of Charles II was executed,” and say whether the amended theory changes its analysis.
