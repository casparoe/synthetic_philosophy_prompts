Cox's theorem is usually waved around as a proof that probability is the unique logic of plausible reasoning: if your degrees of belief obey a few commonsense rules about consistency with logic, they have to be probabilities. But the derivation quietly assumes that each plausibility is an exact real number, and that any two plausibilities can always be compared. Now suppose the thing we actually care about is bounded reasoners—humans or ML systems that work with imprecise credences, like intervals or sets of probabilities, or with partial orderings where A and B sometimes just aren't comparable. Does Cox's theorem survive this change? In particular: which premise does the imprecision bear on, and is the natural fix to say probability is the unique exact-and-totally-ordered limit case, or does that quietly give up the uniqueness claim?
