I'm trying to write something about whether "prosaic" AI alignment is actually a coherent project, as opposed to just being normal ML safety engineering under a fancier name. I've got a draft, and I'd love comments, especially on the two places where I think the argument is easiest to attack. Here's the core.

The thesis is roughly this: current alignment methods—RLHF, constitutional AI, automated red-teaming, monitoring—don't really solve the hard problem of getting a system to robustly care about the right thing; what they mostly do is steer systems that are not yet capable enough to cause catastrophic harm on their own. Most of the safety we see today comes from the fact that the models are tool-like, can't pursue long-horizon goals without a human in the loop, and are supervised at every step. As soon as you deploy a system that can do multi-week autonomous tasks, the same training pipeline will produce a system whose alignment is largely a performance of helpfulness under surveillance. So prosaic alignment is best understood as buying time, not as a path to robustly aligned systems. That makes a lot of current alignment work misdescribed: much of it is scaffolded oversight or capability suppression, not alignment proper.

I'm worried about three things. First, am I smuggling in a definition of "aligned" that nobody actually uses, by equating "aligned" with "safe because weak"? Second, is there an empirical result that would cleanly distinguish my view from the view that current methods really do produce robust internalized values? Third, my section on recursive reward modeling feels underdeveloped: I say it could in principle scale oversight, but then I argue that the reward model itself becomes the bottleneck and is just another black box, so you've traded one problem for another. Is that fair to the people who proposed it, or am I attacking a strawman? Any thoughts on which premise is doing the most work would also help.
