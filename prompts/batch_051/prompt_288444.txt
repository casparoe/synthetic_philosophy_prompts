I'm a retired geologist who's gotten into epistemology over the last few years, mostly through a reading group, and I've somehow ended up teaching a short intro course on rationality and belief at the community college. I'm out of practice at grading and one answer has me wanting a second opinion.

The question I set was: "What do the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, taken together, tell us about rational belief? In particular, identify which principle or principles each paradox puts pressure on, and say whether the two paradoxes press in the same place or in different places."

Here's the student's answer, word for word (I havn't corrected anything):

"The lottery paradox is about a fair lottery with, say, 1000 tickets. Each ticket has only a 1/1000 chance of winning, so it is rational to believe, of each ticket, that it will lose. But if rational belief is closed under conjunction, then from believing each ticket will lose you get the belief that all 1000 tickets lose, which you know is false. So the lottery paradox shows that either high probability isn't enough for rational belief, or rational belief isn't closed under conjunction, or maybe both. The preface paradox is the same sort of problem but with an author. She has checked each sentence of her book carefully and rationally believes each one, but she also knows from experience that long books almost always contain some error, so she rationally believes at least one sentence in the book is false. Since no rational person can believe an inconsistent set of propositions, and her beliefs are inconsistent anyway, we must reject closure under conjunction in her case as well. So the two paradoxes put pressure on exactly the same principle, namely closure. The moral of both is that you can rationally have lots of individual beliefs even when you can't rationally believe the big conjunction."
