I cover artificial intelligence for a science-and-ideas magazine, and I have become interested in a methodological fault line among researchers who work on making advanced AI systems safe. On one side are people who argue that average-case performance is almost meaningless for safety, since what matters is how a system behaves in rare, high-stakes situations; for them, a safety or alignment technique is intellectually respectable only if it offers a worst-case guarantee, or at least if its behavior can be bounded under severe assumptions. On the other side are people who regard worst-case guarantees as a false friend: often vacuous, usually unavailable for real systems, and sometimes so conservative that they would rule out systems we have good average-case reason to believe are safe. I want to write an article that asks whether this disagreement is, even in principle, the kind of thing evidence could settle.

The claim I most want to interrogate is this: all else equal, selecting and developing safety techniques by worst-case criteria will produce systems that are safer or more aligned than selecting and developing them by average-case criteria. Please design the strongest concrete test of that claim that could plausibly be run in the near term. Specify the system or simulation, the exact measurement, and the pre-registered criteria for a result that favors the worst-case camp versus one that favors the average-case camp. Then be rigorous about the residue: say what a clean result would still not show about transfer from the test bed to frontier systems, about whether the measured outcome is a valid operationalization of safety or alignment, and about whether the two camps might merely differ in tolerable risk rather than in factual prediction. Where the necessary concepts can be constructed from scratch, prefer that over reliance on obscure technical facts.
