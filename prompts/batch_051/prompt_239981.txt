I am a high school student, and I have a policy about my own reasoning that I would like evaluated. The policy is this: during any timed performance that depends on reasoning I have already practiced — a debate round, a math contest, an in-class essay — I refuse to monitor my thought process while it is happening. I do not ask, in the moment, whether my argument is going off track, whether I am falling for a known bias, or whether my confidence is justified by the evidence. I used to ask exactly those questions, and I found that it reliably made my performance worse: I slowed down, second-guessed correct answers into wrong ones, and sometimes talked myself out of good first impressions. In a debate round last year, I paused during cross-examination to check whether I was merely being overconfident; by the time I decided my confidence was probably fine, I had lost the thread and dropped a response I had practiced for weeks. So now I deliberately run the performance on autopilot and reserve the thinking-about-my-thinking for afterwards, when I debrief alone. I apply this policy only to time-pressured performances, not to unhurried decisions like choosing a class, forming a political view, or deciding whether to trust a source.

Is this policy a defensible division of labor — confining metacognitive scrutiny to after the task because in-the-moment monitoring is reliably harmful — or is it a self-serving way of ensuring that my beliefs never get challenged at the moment when it matters most? How could I tell the difference from the inside, and is there a revision of the policy that would keep the performance benefits while recovering what in-the-moment metacognition is supposed to provide?
