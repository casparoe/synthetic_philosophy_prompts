I am a novelist working on a book and have stalled on a character study that I believe has a precise philosophical structure I cannot yet name. The protagonist enters a clinical trial for a non-invasive stimulation device designed to treat depersonalization; the device is meant to make the ordinary process by which the brain constructs a sense of being a self available to introspection. The intended effect is that she should be able to observe, rather than simply use, the mechanisms behind her own feeling of being an I. But the effect overshoots: she begins to experience the self as a translucent, contingent construction generated moment by moment, something she normally looks through without noticing. She can no longer recover the naive, prereflective sense of being a solid agent behind her eyes. This destabilizes her marriage, her work, and her narration of her own body; by the middle of the novel she insists to her husband that there is no one inside her having her experiences, only the experiences themselves plus a felt but empty impression that they belong to someone.

My difficulty is that I cannot tell whether I should write this as a loss of an illusion, as a pathological opacity in a normally unnoticed process, or as some third thing, and each option would shape the prose differently. I also do not know what her new state should imply for her practical life: whether she should be expected to become more detached from her commitments, or strangely freer, or whether the ordinary sense of self can be replaced by a more accurate but still serviceable way of acting. What is the underlying structure of what she has lost, and how should that structure guide both the interior voice and the character's decisions over the following months? I want psychological credibility rather than a mystical awakening or a slide into madness, so I am less interested in plot suggestions than in a precise account of what kind of experience this is.
