You are grading a midterm answer in an undergraduate philosophy of language unit on writing systems. The question was:

'In what sense might a writing system be described as a theory of the spoken language it represents? Evaluate the claim that an alphabetic writing system is better to the extent that it approaches a one-to-one correspondence between letters and phonemes. Draw on English and at least one other writing system.'

Student answer:

'A writing system embodies a theory because it selects a level of linguistic structure to represent: logography takes the word or morpheme as basic, syllabaries take the syllable, and alphabets take the phoneme. An alphabet therefore embodies the theoretical claim that speech decomposes into a small inventory of contrastive segments, phonemes, and that these are the units worth writing down. Once this is granted, the obvious standard of adequacy is phonetic transparency: an alphabet approximates a one-letter-per-phoneme mapping, and falls short only where the correspondence breaks down. English is a clear case of failure, since one letter sequence can have many pronunciations ("ough") and one sound can have many spellings (/f/ as "f", "ph", or "gh"); a reformed, purely phonemic English orthography would therefore be a strictly better writing system. Hangul, by contrast, comes close to the ideal, because its letters correspond regularly to Korean phonemes. This shows scripts can be ranked along a single scale of adequacy, from logography through syllabary to alphabet, with a fully phonemic alphabet at the top.'
