Question for people who've thought about risk and consent more carefully than I have. My housemate just bought a used e-bike and insists on charging the battery overnight in the hall right under the smoke detector. The problem is that those batteries occasionally go into thermal runaway, and it's the kind of fire a normal extinguisher wont put out. I told him I wasn't okay with it, and he hit me with the full rationalist toolkit: the base rate is tiny, my expected years-lost are smaller than the risk I impose on him whenever I drive him to the airport, and if we both had to get explicit sign-off for every one-in-a-million hazard we'd never do anything. I buy most of that, actually. But it still feels like the fact that the risk was chosen for me, rather than by me, is doing real work that his expected-value framing can't capture. There are risks I'd happily accept in exchange for the benefit that I now resent having taken on my behalf. He says that's just status-quo bias with deontological branding, and that consent talk has to bottom out in expected disvalue or it doesn't bottom out at all. I keep going in circles.

So: is there a coherent principle that says I get a veto over small risks imposed on me without my consent, even when the expected value to me is only slightly negative? And what does that principle say about the 'everything imposes risk, so requiring consent is a non-starter' move? Ignore the practical dispute-resolution stuff; I want to know whether his argument actually dissolves my objection or just changes the subject.
