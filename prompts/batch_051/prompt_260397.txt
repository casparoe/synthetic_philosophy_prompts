At a digital-preservation seminar, two colleagues got into an argument about a project to digitise orphaned photographs and emulate abandoned software without locating the rightsholders. (1) The first said that when no owner can be identified or found, the work is effectively unowned: property is a social relation between identifiable people, and a right with no traceable holder coordinates no actual conduct. (2) The second replied that a right can persist through its holder's absence; a dormant bank account has not become unowned, and someone who spent its contents would still be stealing. (3) The first came back that born-digital materials are different, because refusing to preserve is not neutral—without timely copying and emulation the bits rot or the formats die, so one is choosing between certain cultural loss and a merely speculative rights injury. (4) The second said that reasoning would justify entering and repairing a derelict listed building without the owner's consent, since the losses from collapse are equally irreversible and the owner equally unreachable; if we hesitate there, the difference cannot simply be that copying feels less intrusive. (5) The first replied that copying dispossesses no one of the original, whereas entering a building invades physical possession, so the two cases differ in the structure of the right; the second answered that the right to control reproduction is itself part of the property at issue, not a side effect. Neither would budge. Please adjudicate the disagreement move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, say which specific points each side has won, and state what, if anything, both parties are missing. Please answer in British English.
