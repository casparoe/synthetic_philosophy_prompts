I'm putting together a session for an upper-level undergrad seminar on Foucault, and I want the students to actually feel the thing he describes in Discipline and Punish about the examination — not just get it as a concept. The class is 16 students, 80 minutes, and I don't want to lecture at all before the activity. I want the rules of the game itself to make them run into the normalizing gaze, then we debrief.

Here's the passage from Foucault, Discipline and Punish, in the Sheridan translation, that I want the session built around:

"The examination combines the techniques of an observing hierarchy and those of a normalizing judgement. It is a normalizing gaze, a surveillance that makes it possible to qualify, to classify and to punish."

You are an experienced philosophy teacher and someone who has read Foucault carefully. Before you settle on a design, brainstorm two or three candidate mechanics (whole-class observation? paired case notes? a small committee? a simulated intake?) and pick the one that would make the experience closest to what the passage describes, not just a metaphor. Then write it up as a runnable activity.

The design should meet these specs:
- The group must generate classifications of people from thin observations, and those classifications should then have a small but real consequence in the room.
- Every participant should end up both observing and being observed, although not necessarily at the same moment.
- The observing hierarchy should be produced by the rules, not by me playing the authority.
- No real humiliation, no disclosure of private material, no actual punishment. Use small stakes only.
- The activity should fit in 80 minutes, including the debrief.

Format your response in these sections, in order, with rough lengths:
1. The setup (one short paragraph): what the room is arranged like and how you frame the task to the students.
2. Rules and materials (about 150 words): the exact instructions, roles, timing, and materials.
3. Run of session (about 200 words): minute-by-minute or phase-by-phase, including when the classifications get used.
4. Debrief (about 300 words): map what happened in the activity onto the passage's terms — observing hierarchy, normalizing judgement, qualifying, classifying, punishing — and say which moments you would point to.
5. What will predictably go wrong (about 150 words): the two or three most likely failure modes and how to recover from them.
6. What this can't reproduce (about 100 words): the feature of Foucault's account that a classroom game cannot fully recreate and that the facilitator has to supply by comment.

One more constraint: the activity should not use any external texts and shouldn't require students to have done the reading beforehand.
