So here's a thing I can't get straight about how medicine handles causation, and I'd love the underlying philosophy pulled apart. Randomized controlled trials are the gold standard for establishing that a drug *causes* an improvement — nobody disputes that. But the machinery is all about group averages: you randomize, blind everyone, give the control arm a placebo, compare mean outcomes, and report an average treatment effect. The statisticians meanwhile are very insistent that you can't infer what the drug did to any *individual* participant, becuase you never observe their counterfactual — what would have happened without the drug. Fine. But then the very same evidence gets used to say the drug causes lower blood pressure, full stop, and a doctor prescribes it to you as if the causal relation holds for you, the individual. So the practice officially denies individual causal inference, then quietly makes it all the time. What theory of causation does this practice presuppose? Does putting randomization on a pedestal commit medicine to something like an interventionist account — a cause is what a controlled manipulation changes — or does it actually presuppose a counterfactual/potential-outcomes picture, where the trial is just an unbiased way of estimating the never-observed counterfactual? Are those even rival theories here, or does interventionism just collapse into counterfactuals? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say where the practice is genuinely committed and where it could be licensed by several theories, and tell me what would change in trial design and reporting if we replaced the operative view with a regularity or mechanism-based account.
