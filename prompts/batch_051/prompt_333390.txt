Consider the claim that the meaning of a text is fixed by what its author intended, such that the correct interpretation of an ambiguous or figurative passage is the one that recovers that intention. The thesis is usually defended with armchair examples and appeals to intuition, not with data. Yet part of it looks empirically tractable. Design a concrete study that would put real pressure on that part. You might, for example, use short literary passages whose wording underdetermines the meaning, vary what interpreters are told about the author's avowed intention (including a condition in which the author disavows the reading most readers naturally arrive at), and have judges blind to condition rate each offered interpretation on textual faithfulness, internal coherence, and plausibility. Specify your stimuli, conditions, and measures. Then say what pattern of results would count as support for an intentionalist account and what pattern would count against it. Finally, state what even a clean positive result would not settle — in particular, whether showing that interpreters converge on intended meanings shows that intention constitutes correct meaning rather than merely guiding interpretive practice, and whether a disavowed or unintended meaning can ever be the right one in its own terms.
