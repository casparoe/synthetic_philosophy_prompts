I'm preparing a handout for an upper-level epistemology seminar that has just finished a unit on higher-order knowledge, and I'd like you to write the diagnostic instrument for it. The seminar has read Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument and a couple of defenses of the KK principle, but the handout should test students' commitments rather than their recall, so every node needs to be a concrete case or claim that a student can assess without knowing the literature. For the purposes of the handout, take the KK principle to say that whenever a subject knows that p, she is in a position to know that she knows that p; take a condition to be luminous if, whenever it obtains, the subject is in a position to know that it obtains. The interesting question for the seminar is where these two theses come apart, and which failures of iteration are really failures of luminosity.

Please write a decision tree built from the logical space of positions rather than from a list of names. The leaves should include at least: full KK; KK restricted to phenomenal or self-presenting states (one is always in a position to know that one is in pain when one is, but not always in a position to iterate ordinary factual knowledge); a Williamson-style view on which no non-trivial condition is luminous and KK fails as a special case; and a safety/margin-for-error view that denies KK but explains why knowledge often feels iterable. Your branch questions should separate these views on their actual commitments — e.g., whether a subject can be wrong about her own current phenomenal state; whether a subject who gives the correct answer on an exam but has misleading evidence that she was guessing nevertheless knows the answer; whether a gradual series of cases from clearly cold to clearly warm can force a subject to know she is warm before the change actually happens; and whether the subject's reliability at a margin matters for what she knows. Phrase each node as a question answerable by someone who has never heard the terms "KK" or "luminosity." Aim for roughly eight to twelve questions. Where the logical space contains combinations no one occupies, mark them as incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored; and flag any two leaves that your tree cannot separate, saying what further question, if any, would split them.
