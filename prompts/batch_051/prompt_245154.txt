For a feature I am writing on how platform rules shape the moral content of games, I want to think through a counterfactual rather than report an existing case. Suppose that, starting next year, the two dominant console platform holders and the largest PC game storefront jointly adopt and enforce a rule: any game whose primary activity is combat in a recognizable real-world armed conflict from the past hundred years may be sold on their platforms only if it includes an unskippable interactive segment, at least twenty minutes long, in which the player controls a civilian in the immediate aftermath of a specific battle the game depicts; the segment cannot award points, achievements, or unlockable content. New releases must comply immediately, existing titles receive a two-year grace period, and noncompliant games are delisted. Predict, with reasoning, how the mainstream war-game genre changes over the following decade. I am less interested in whether the policy is good or bad than in the causal chain: how developers respond — shifting to fictional or older conflicts, restructuring the core loop, designing minimal-compliance segments, relabeling genres — how players and modding communities respond, how the rule interacts with age ratings and regional regulation, and what happens to the experience of fun itself, whether it is suppressed, redirected, or redefined as tension and dread rather than power fantasy. Be explicit about which links in your prediction are robust, which are guesses, and what would have to be true for the prediction to fail.
