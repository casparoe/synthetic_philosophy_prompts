Construct a two-envelope case to the following specifications. The case is for a graduate decision-theory seminar on the two-envelope paradox, and its purpose is to isolate exactly which premise of the familiar switching argument fails; the verdicts must be generated by the specified prior, not stipulated by the story. The scenario itself should be no more than a short paragraph and involve nothing more exotic than two sealed envelopes and an honest friend who has placed money in them.

Specifications:

1. The setup is an otherwise standard two-envelope problem. The friend places money in the two envelopes by a method that is fully specified and known to the player, with one envelope containing exactly twice the other. The player picks one envelope at random, opens it, sees the amount, and must decide whether to keep it or switch.

2. Everything is finite and ordinary. Amounts are whole dollars; the larger amount never exceeds $10,000; and all expected values that arise are finite. No improper priors, no unbounded ranges, no exotic mathematics.

3. Build the case around the observed amount of $256, and make both of the following true at once:
   (a) After the player sees $256, both hypotheses — that the other envelope contains $512 and that it contains $128 — remain open on the specified prior, so the observation does not by itself settle whether the player holds the smaller or the larger envelope.
   (b) The familiar switching argument — that the other envelope is equally likely to contain double or half, so its expected value is 1.25 times the observed amount — recommends switching, while a correct risk-neutral Bayesian expected-value calculation using the full specified prior says that switching at $256 has expected value strictly below $256, and is therefore worse than keeping.

Then verify the construction:

- State the prior explicitly, confirm that it is normalized over the stated bounds, and derive the posterior probabilities of 'the other envelope has $512' and 'the other envelope has $128' conditional on seeing $256.
- Compute the exact expected dollar value of switching versus keeping at $256, and confirm that it satisfies (b).
- Identify which premise of the familiar switching argument is false in this case, and isolate the structural feature of the prior that is doing the work.
- Test that isolation with two perturbations: first, keep the prior's shape fixed while the upper bound grows without limit; second, keep the same finite bounds but replace the prior with a uniform distribution over them. Which perturbation restores the switching verdict at $256, and what does that show about whether the original paradox is driven by unboundedness or by the equiprobability premise?
- Finish with the strongest objection a defender of the switching argument could give — for example, that the case changes the subject by imposing a known prior on a puzzle that is posed under ignorance about the generating process — and say honestly whether the case dissolves the two-envelope paradox or only disarms one premise of one version of it.
