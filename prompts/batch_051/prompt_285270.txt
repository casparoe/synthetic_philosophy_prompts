I run a small philosophy reading group that got accidentally split down the middle by something that happened in our city last spring: a publicly funded gallery showed a piece that used pages from a Qur'an and a Torah under a walkway, and the ensuing arguement about whether to pull the funding made it clear that two of our regulars have been quietly fighting past each other about sacrilege for a year. One, a former human-rights lawyer, holds that "blasphemy" names no distinct wrong at all — the only real wrongs are downstream: threats, incitement, discrimination, maybe targeted harassment, and the sacred is just a status a community projects onto objects and texts. The other, an anthropologist who works on ritual and sacred space, says that's artificially flat: intentionally degrading something a community treats as sacred is a distinctive kind of symbolic wrong that doesn't decompose into hurt feelings plus harm, the way defiling a grave or a war memorial seems wrong for reasons that are not just "someone is upset." It got personal for me when my sister-in-law, who is religious, said she'd pull her kids out of a public event our group co-sponsors because she doesn't trust secular harm-talk to notice what was actually damaged. I'd like to run this as a formal adversarial-collaboration exercise and get out of them the document they should have produced.

You are a professional philosopher who has mediated between opposing schools before and is good at drafting texts both sides can put their names to. Before you write, spend real effort brainstorming the strongest possible version of each position, including the cases each side finds most embarrassing and the concessions each would privately admit; use that to make both sides in the final document as non-vacuous as possible. Then write the joint statement with a numbered "agreed claims" section and a "disagreements" table. I want: the claims both accept, about the gallery incident and about sacrilege generally; the exact disagreements, stated in neutral wording, with each side's own view phrased so that side would accept it; the cruxes — I'd guess one is whether the sacred is constituted by group attitudes or tracks something not exhausted by those attitudes, another is whether symbolic degradation can wrong a community in a way that isn't reducible to harmed or offended individuals, and maybe a third about whether "offence against the sacred" is really just insult with better branding. For each crux, give one or two concrete cases or kinds of evidence both sides would agree bear on it, with each side's prediction about the case recorded in advance. If you conclude part of the dispute is purely verbal, say exactly which part and why.
