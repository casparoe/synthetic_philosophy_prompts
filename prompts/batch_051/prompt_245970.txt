So I got into this two-hour argument with a friend last night about Bernard Williams's integrity objection, and she kept saying I was being way too generous to the utilitarians. At one point she laid out her whole reading of Williams, more or less like this: Williams's real target is the idea that you should ever step back and think about the impartial good when someone you personally love is on the line. She brought up the famous example where a man has to choose, in an emergency, between pulling his wife out of danger and pulling out a stranger. She said Williams thinks utilitarianism gives the obviously wrong answer there, because it tells the man to save whichever person would produce more total utility — possibly the stranger, if the stranger happens to be a brilliant surgeon. But the deeper problem, on her telling, is that the husband would even pause to do that calculation in the first place; that pause is the one-thought-too-many thing. So on her reading, Williams is saying that any impartial reasoning inside a personal relationship is already a failure of character, that love is supposed to be a bit blind like that, and that utilitarianism is therefore incompatible with having character, friendships, or integrity at all. She ended with something like, a consistent utilitarian is just a calculator with a pulse.

I pushed back that this sounded like a cartoon verison of Williams — more like a slogan than what he actually argued — but when she asked me to be precise about what she got wrong, I realized I couldn't trust my memory of the actual essays. So how fair is her summary, really? Go through it piece by piece and tell me what a philosopher who defends Williams would accept, what they'd think she's conflating, and which of her conclusions actually follow from the parts of the summary that are true. I especially want to know whether the emergency-rescue example does the work she says it does, and whether Williams is against pausing to reason at all, or against something narrower than that.
