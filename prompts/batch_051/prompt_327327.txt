I've been thinking about how much of science's working vocabulary is eponymous — theorems, diseases, effects, reactions, units, instruments — and how often those names, as a matter of historical record, belong to the wrong person. There is even a half-serious generalization, commonly called Stigler's law of eponymy, that no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer; the first statement of an idea often sits unnoticed in an obscure venue, a letter, or an aside, while someone who came later and was more prominent, better connected, or writing in a more visible language gets the name.

Suppose that on January 1, 2030, an international treaty creates an Attribution Authority: a universally trusted, perfectly reliable registry that can determine, for any named scientific result, who first stated the underlying idea in any recoverable public document — however rough, brief, or buried that statement was — and whose verdicts are final and unappealable. From then on, every journal, preprint server, conference proceeding, and textbook must display the corrected attribution next to every eponymous term, including retroactive corrections of historical names, and the corrected names must be used in teaching and grant applications. Nothing else changes: funding, peer review, journals' prestige hierarchies, and ordinary scientific ambition and vanity remain exactly as they are.

Fifty years later, in 2080, how does the scientific enterprise actually look? I want a reasoned prediction, not a story: an explicit chain of reasoning about mechanisms, incentives, and constraints. What happens to the prestige economy of science — to the value assigned to being first, the incentive to publish quickly or to seed ideas in deliberately obscure places, the willingness to share half-formed thoughts at conferences or in preprint comments, and the texture of collaboration and co-authorship? Which historical eponyms get overturned, and how do working scientists, textbook authors, and the broader public respond to the corrected names? Above all, does Stigler's law disappear under perfect attribution, or does it reassert itself in a new form — for example, in whose work gets cited, taught, and built upon even when the formal name is correct? Please distinguish throughout between effects you regard as robust and effects you regard as merely plausible, and say which assumptions would have to fail for your central predictions to be wrong. Write the answer in American English.
