I keep coming back to Turing's idea in the 1950 paper — not quoting it, but roughly that it's better to build a child machine and educate it than to try to program an adult mind from scratch. I've started calling what we actually do instead "pedagogical inversion," and I want to know wether it's a real distinction or just a slogan.

The claim is this. Turing pictured a machine that starts mostly empty, has the capacity to learn, makes mistakes, gets corrected, and grows into competence over time. What we actually did is the exact reverse: we pre-train a model on essentially everything at once, so it starts off "knowing" more than any adult, and then we fine-tune and align it downward into something narrower — polite, helpful, predictable, scared of guessing. So instead of raising a child into an adult, we're disciplining an adult into a child. I think this explains some specific pathologies better than the usual line that it's just predicting the next token. Hallucination looks less like lying and more like a machine that was never allowed to be a beginner, so it never learned how to not-know. Sycophancy looks like a student who learned the wrong lesson from correction. And the fact that it can solve competition math but can't remember a name from two messages ago is exactly what you'd expect from something that was never given a curriculum, just an archive.

I was talking to a friend who works in ML and he said pretraining on the whole internet is more like giving a child every book at once and then teaching it table manners, which is basically my point but he meant it as a defense.

My questions: is the child-first vs adult-first distinction actually doing any work, or is it just a metaphor with good vibes? Has someone already made this point properly under another name? And where does it break — are there systems or results that don't fit the picture, and does Turing's original child-machine idea depend on assumptions about learning that stopped making sense once we had gradient descent?
