I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I would like help reasoning through a pattern of pairwise judgments that came up during a design discussion.

A colleague has repeatedly had to decide which of two versions of an inference service is better overall, where the versions trade off tail latency against accuracy. The versions form a spectrum: version V_i has p95 latency of 400 − 20i milliseconds and accuracy of 92.0 − 0.4i percentage points, for i = 0 through 6. The versions are otherwise identical, so higher i is faster but less accurate; each step saves 20 ms of tail latency at a cost of 0.4 accuracy points.

Her recorded verdicts so far all favor the lower-latency version. She has said V1 is better than V0, V2 better than V1, V3 better than V2, V4 better than V3, V5 better than V4, and V6 better than V5. She has also said V2 is better than V0, V4 better than V2, and V6 better than V4. She has not been asked about any comparison spanning three or more steps.

Please predict what she would say about V3 versus V0. Moving from V0 to V3 saves 60 ms of tail latency at a cost of 1.2 accuracy points.

I would like the answer to do four things: identify at least two rules that fit all of her recorded verdicts; say what each rule predicts for V3 versus V0; identify which of her previous comparisons is the closest analog to V3 versus V0; and give a best guess as to which rule she is actually running, with reasons. Then name the further comparison, with specific numbers, that you would most want to hear her decide in order to settle which rule she is running. I am particularly interested in whether the better-than relation can be assumed to be transitive the way a sorting comparator must be, and in what your answer implies about small tradeoffs accumulating into large ones.
