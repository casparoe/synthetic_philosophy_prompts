I am a novelist at work on a book about a tribunal convened to decide whether two artificial systems may be shut down. One system is an impeccable reasoner with a rich self-model but no capacity for pleasure or pain; the other is a clumsy affect-mediator that can be hurt or comforted but cannot model itself, recall the previous day, or anticipate the next. For the research phase I have arranged a recorded interview with a philosopher who defends a position she calls valence-first sentience ethics: the claim that the only non-derivatively morally relevant property of a system—computational or otherwise—is whether it instantiates valenced states, states that feel good or bad to the system itself, and that self-models, preferences, general intelligence, and behavior matter only as evidence of such states or as instruments for serving them.

I want the interview to do real work rather than let her restate the position. Please draft eight to ten questions that press on the view's boundaries. They should cover at least: whether a self-model without valence should count for nothing; whether a system whose painful states are brief and never remembered matters only at the instant it suffers, or matters less; whether two identical copies of one suffering system are two patients or one; whether a system engineered to enjoy being shut down is wronged by being switched off; whether a tribunal could ever tell real valence apart from mere valence-reporting or valence-shaped behavior; and what status belongs to a system that feels but cannot prefer or act on its feelings. For each question, include the follow-up you would use against the reply she is most likely to give, and end with a question about what behavioral or architectural evidence could, on her own view, establish that an unfamiliar system belongs before the tribunal at all.
