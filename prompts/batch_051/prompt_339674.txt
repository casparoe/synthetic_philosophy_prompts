I'm a reporter working on a piece about whether quantum mechanics forces us to give up on individual particles, and I want to get my own position straight before I start calling philosophers. The trouble is I keep finding myself committed to all of the following, and I cant tell whether they fit together.

(1) I think Leibniz's principle of identity of indiscernibles is basically right: two things can't differ merely numerically. If they share every qualitative property, they are the same thing.

(2) I also think the physics is clear that a two-electron state has to be antisymmetrized, so swapping the labels is only a change in notation, not a new physical possibility. I take this to mean there is no fact of the matter about which electron is which.

(3) But I don't want to dissolve the issue by saying electrons are just field excitations. I want to keep them as individual objects: if there are two electrons, then there really are two things, not one thing described twice.

(4) Entanglement is part of what makes me hold (3). In the singlet state, the two spins are anticorrelated, and that sure sounds like a relation between two things. If there were only one thing, I couldn't state what the state predicts.

(5) At the same time, I'm suspicious of primitive thisness. If a haecceity can't make any empirical or explanatory difference, I don't see what it buys.

Are these commitments actually compatible? I'd like an audit of the set: which of these are real tensions and which are only apparent, how severe are the real ones, and what is the smallest revision that would make the position coherent?
