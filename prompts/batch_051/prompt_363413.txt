Among novelists and screenwriters in my circle, a common defense of restraint in biographical fiction is the executor analogy: the writer is an executor of the dead, the real person's reputation is an estate, and the writer's task is to administer that estate as the deceased would have wished. The analogy is usually invoked to conclude that invented scenes, words put into the subject's mouth, or exposure of what the subject deliberately kept private amount to a breach of a quasi-fiduciary duty. I want an audit of how far this holds. Give the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the testator, the estate, the beneficiaries, the will, the executor's discretion, and the supervising court. Then identify where the mapping breaks down. In particular, can a dead person have the kind of interests the analogy requires; does a novelist actually stand in anything like a fiduciary relation to the historical subject; and how should we handle the fact that an estate is finite and meant to be wound up, whereas a reputation is remade every time it is represented? Tell me which disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion, whether some chastened duty of fidelity to the real dead survives, and, if the executor analogy was carrying the conclusion illicitly, what comparison would do better.
