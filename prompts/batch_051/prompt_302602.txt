I spent most of my career in an experimental science, and since retiring I have come late to philosophy and have been trying to think more carefully about a pattern I only half-noticed while I was working. When two independent groups reach the same successful result at nearly the same time, the community tends to treat the duplication as evidence that the discovery was bound to arrive: the field was ready, the methods were ready, the result was in the air. The same doubling is then used to dilute credit—no one person was indispensable, so no one person may claim to be the lone genius. The logic appears to be that if something happened twice independently, it was overdetermined.

I have rarely seen the same reasoning applied to simultaneous error. If two independent groups publish substantially the same incorrect interpretation in the same year, and the mistake is later exposed, the error is not described as having been in the air. Each group is faulted more or less separately, as though the convergence were an unfortunate coincidence with no common cause. The independence and the simultaneity are the same; only the sign of the outcome differs. I am trying to determine whether the community is entitled to this asymmetry. Is there a principle that justifies treating a shared success but not a shared failure as evidence of inevitability—perhaps something about the sources of true and false belief in a mature science—or is this a flattering story science tells itself when it wishes to spread the credit for a triumph and concentrate the blame for a blunder? I would like the strongest justification you can find, tested against the hard case in which two genuinely independent lines of work arrive at the same error rather than sharing a sloppy method, and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept or given up.
