Came up in faculty meeting last week and I can't stop turning it over. Someone proposed that before any of us may voice blame of a colleague's conduct during a departmental meeting, we have to file a public "clean hands" statement listing the kinds of wrongdoing we swear we have not comitted in the past five years. If you later want to complain that someone, say, poached a grad student or tanked a junior colleague's tenure letter, your statement has to cover that category. Anybody in the room can challenge your standing by producing evidence that you've done the same sort of thing yourself; if the challenge succeeds, your complaint is struck from the minutes and you're barred from voicing further complaints for the rest of the year. The proposer says this filters out hypocrites and leaves only blame that's worth hearing. Treat it as a mechanism, not as a position in the standing-to-blame literature. Walk through how it actually plays out over a few years: what gets complained about and by whom, what the clean-hands statements become once people realize they're weapons, how the challenge process gets used, and where the whole thing gets gamed first. Then say which failure modes are patchable with small amendments and which ones come from the very idea that a blamer's record should gate their standing.
