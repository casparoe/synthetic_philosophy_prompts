For several years I have been collecting a friend's verdicts about a single question: when, if ever, is a visitor to a place morally obliged to follow its everyday conventions — tipping, queueing, haggling, holding tables, and the like? She never states the principle behind her views, but her verdicts are strikingly consistent. Here are the cases, with the facts as she accepted them.

1. A visitor from a no-tipping country eats at a restaurant where no service charge is included and the waiting staff are paid very little apart from tips. She leaves nothing, explaining that tipping is not something people do where she comes from. My friend says she is obliged to tip.

2. A visitor pushes in at the front of a long queue for the last bus home at a crowded station, and the passengers behind him miss the bus as a result. My friend says he is obliged to wait his turn rather than push in.

3. A visitor is in a region where bargaining is the local convention at market stalls. She pays the first price the stallholder names, politely and without complaint, and the stallholder is plainly content. My friend says she is not obliged to bargain.

4. In a busy café with a well-understood local practice that leaving one's coat at a table holds it while one orders at the counter, a visitor takes a table where a regular has left her coat. The regular returns and loses the table. My friend says the visitor is obliged to leave the table alone.

5. A visitor crosses an empty road against a red signal at two in the morning; there is no traffic in either direction. My friend says she is not obliged to wait for the signal to change.

Now here is the case I want to put to her. A visitor is dining in a country where restaurant staff are paid an adequate living wage by law, but where tipping is nevertheless an entrenched local custom: an extra ten per cent or so is normal, and the staff reasonably rely on this custom when deciding which shifts to take, counting the tips as part of their expected income. The visitor does not tip. She is courteous, shows no contempt, and explains that in her home country tipping is not the practice and that she prefers to pay the listed price. The server is surprised, but is not left short of a living wage, and there is no insulting gesture or scene.

What should my friend say, if she wants to cohere with her earlier verdicts: is the visitor morally obliged to tip, or not? Which earlier verdict is the closest analogue? If her earlier judgements genuinely underdetermine the new case, set out the strongest candidate principles that fit them, say what each principle predicts about the visitor, state which principle each available verdict would commit her to, propose the further case that would discriminate between the surviving principles, and give your best guess as to what she would conclude and why.
