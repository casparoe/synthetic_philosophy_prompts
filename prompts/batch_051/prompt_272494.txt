I've been drafting a paper on whether the passage of time has any moral significance in its own right, and I keep returning to an asymmetry I'd like you to help me think through. Take two cases identical except for the calendar: a manager defrauds a client of a hundred thousand dollars and is found out a month later; another manager commits the same fraud, against the same kind of victim, under the same circumstances, but is found out twenty years later. In many legal systems the first will be prosecuted and the second will not, because the limitation period has run. Yet if you ask people whether the twenty-year-old fraud was less wrong, few will say yes, and if the offender is identified with certainty they generally won't say he has become less answerable as a moral matter either. So the practice seems to treat time as decisive for the legal response while ordinary judgment denies that time changed anything about the wrong itself: time extinguishes the consequence but apparently not the debt, and it does so in only one direction, since an expired case never revives no matter what happens afterward. The pattern gets stranger when you notice that time extinguishes some wrongs entirely and others not at all — in many systems an old fraud is unprosecutable while an old murder is not, as though the law itself doesn't believe time has any uniform moral effect.

The standard defenses I've collected — evidence decays, witnesses die, defendants need repose, the deterrent point fades with delay, people change over decades — all strike me as contingent features of ordinary cases rather than a defense of the rule as such. If the offender confesses today, or if a complete and reliable record leaves no evidential doubt, the bar still operates; if the offender is demonstrably the same person, unrepentant and still enjoying the proceeds, the "he has changed" rationale does no work. So what is left doing the justificatory work once those prudential rationales are stripped away? Is there a defensible principle by which time itself can remove the entitlement to call a wrongdoer to account without in the least removing the wrongness — or is the practice an inconsistency we have simply agreed to live with, and should be prepared to revise?

What I'd like is the strongest philosophical defense of the asymmetry you can construct, tested against the confession and perfect-evidence versions of the old-fraud case, followed by your own verdict on whether the practice should be kept, narrowed, expanded, or abandoned. Please be explicit about your confidence: tell me which argument or counterexample would most likely change your verdict, and where you think your reasoning is most liable to fail.
