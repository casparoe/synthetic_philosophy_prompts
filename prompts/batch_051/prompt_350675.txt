I have been reading around a long-running dispute between defenders of a lexical threshold in negative utilitarianism and those who maintain that pleasure and pain are fully commensurable on a single scale of well-being, and the exchange has settled into a stable deadlock. I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is, not a verdict on which side is right.

The two positions, as I understand them, are these. The continuity theorist holds that all pleasures and pains are values on one continuous scale: for any episode of suffering, however severe, there exists some quantity of pleasure, or some amount of equal suffering prevented, that would outweigh it; trade-offs are therefore defined in principle, even when the required quantities are impracticably large. The lexical threshold theorist denies this. Beyond a certain intensity, or for certain kinds of pain, no amount of pleasure below the threshold can compensate: such suffering is lexically prior to every lower-order good, so that a small amount of it counts for more than any amount of what lies beneath. Both sides accept the same intuitive data. A pinprick can be outweighed by a sufficiently large pleasure, while no plausible aggregate of mild pleasures, however widely distributed, seems to justify allowing someone to be tortured. Yet the two sides draw opposite conclusions from these shared cases. The continuity theorist treats the second case as evidence only that the exchange rate is extremely steep, still finite, even if the required surplus is impossible to realise in practice. The threshold theorist treats it as evidence that the scale itself breaks: the pain and the pleasure are not merely far apart on one scale but belong to different ranks of value.

My working hypothesis is that the deadlock turns on an ambiguity in what is being claimed when one says that two values are commensurable. It may be that one side is making a claim about the formal representability of orderings, in particular about whether the relevant ordering can be captured by a single real-valued utility function, which lexical orderings resist; while the other side is making a claim about the normative authority of certain experiences, for which the demand that there be some finite exchange rate is itself the mistake. But I am not confident in this. Please diagnose the dispute concretely, engaging with what each side actually says: is it a verbal disagreement about the word commensurable, a substantive difference about which formal properties of value may be given up, a difference about which cases are probative, or a bedrock difference in intuitions that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a formal result, an experiment, a sharper question — or should we expect the impasse to persist?
