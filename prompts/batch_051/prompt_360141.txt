I am trying to determine what I actually think intelligence is by working from considered judgments about particular cases rather than from a definition. Below are my verdicts; I want to know whether any single principle captures all of them and, if not, which verdicts a defensible principle would force me to revise.

1. A four-function calculator dividing 19,847 by 37 by running a fixed arithmetic algorithm: not intelligent.
2. A thermostat switching a heater on and off at a single threshold: not intelligent.
3. A slime mold that extends and retracts pseudopodia until it settles on the shortest path through a maze to an oat flake: intelligent, though only minimally.
4. An octopus that has seen a jar closed from the outside and watched a human twist it open once, and then opens it from the inside: intelligent.
5. An ELIZA-style chatbot that responds by matching keywords to scripted templates, with no representation of what is being discussed: not intelligent.
6. A large language model that correctly solves novel geometry problems from a recent olympiad and explains its reasoning: intelligent, though I am unsettled about it in ways I cannot quite state.
7. A person performing long division by mechanically following an algorithm they were taught, without understanding why it works: the person is intelligent, but the act of carrying out the algorithm does not seem to be what makes them so.
8. A single worker ant following pheromone trails: not intelligent.
9. The colony of such ants, over weeks, regulating temperature, humidity, and traffic flow in the nest: intelligent, at least as a system.
10. A person with advanced dementia who can no longer solve novel problems but responds appropriately to affect and familiar routines: intelligent, but I am unsure whether it is the same sense of the word as in the octopus and language-model cases.

I have a working principle, but it does not sit well with all of this: intelligence is flexible, goal-directed adjustment of means to ends under novelty. That principle handles the octopus and the language model cleanly, but it strains to explain the slime mold, the colony, and the person with dementia, and it leaves the single-skill robot below undecided.

Please tell me what principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures the pattern in these verdicts. State it precisely, identify which cases motivate each of its clauses, say what it predicts about a case I have not listed — a simple mobile robot that learns through trial and error to open one particular kind of door and then cannot adapt that skill to a door that opens outward rather than inward — and give an honest statement of which of my verdicts, if any, it cannot accommodate.
