I am assembling a two-week unit on moral worth for an advanced undergraduate ethics seminar, and I want the students to begin from a precise specification of the thing the competing theories are after rather than from the theories themselves. Please write out the desiderata that an adequate account of moral worth must satisfy: the phenomena it needs to explain, the distinctions it is not permitted to collapse, and the pressures from ordinary moral appraisal it must absorb. For each desideratum, give the particular case or consideration that earns it a place on the list, and then mark it as non-negotiable, such that any view that violates it has stopped being an account of moral worth, or as a camp-specific demand that one side of the debate treats as compulsory while another side reasonably treats as optional.

Among the pressure points the list must address are the following. A shopkeeper gives correct change solely because honesty is good for business. A passerby helps a stranger out of immediate sympathy, with no thought of duty. An agent does what is in fact right while believing on the basis of a mistaken moral theory that it is wrong. An agent is moved at once by duty and by self-interest and would have acted the same way from either motive alone. An agent attempts a rescue for the right reason but fails through bad luck. An adequate theory has to return a principled verdict in each of these cases rather than treating them as peripheral, and where the verdict clashes with a standing intuition, the theory should explain the intuition away instead of ignoring it. The list should also keep visible the distinctions no theory may legislate out of existence: between an act's being right and its having worth; between the worth of an action and the praiseworthiness of the agent; between a motive's being good-hearted and an action's being worthy; between being moved by the fact that the act is right and being moved by the particular features that make it right; and between worth as an all-or-nothing status and worth as something that comes in degrees, including what would explain its degree.

Finally, I want two higher-order judgments about the list itself. First, say whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, and if so, locate the tension precisely; that would itself be a result worth teaching. Second, identify the single desideratum that does the most work in excluding the standard families of view, such as duty-based accounts, good-motive accounts, and accounts on which worth consists in acting for the features that make the act right. Do not turn this into a scorecard; keep the weight on the criteria and their rationale, since that is where I want the students to do their thinking.
