The Nyāya school defended reliable verbal testimony (śabda) as an independent pramāṇa—a means of knowledge—not reducible to perception (pratyakṣa) or inference (anumāna). The core argument, roughly, is this: when someone who knows a matter and is disposed to tell the truth about it—an āpta—tells me something, my resulting belief is warranted by the speaker's competence and honesty, not by my having verified the claim for myself. Most of what any of us knows about geography, history, law, or the natural sciences arrives this way, and we could not check it all by our own perception or inference. Testimony, therefore, must be a distinct source of knowledge whose warrant is tied to the speaker's trustworthiness rather than to evidence I independently possess.

Now consider a development the Nyāya theorists did not face: an enormous share of the beliefs we form from the word of others now reaches us through anonymous, aggregated, or machine-mediated channels. A claim may come from an unknown, pseudonymous, or dead author, from a Wikipedia edit history, from a review aggregate, from forum consensus, from a recommender system's selection, or from an LLM's paraphrase of other people's words. Often there is no identifiable speaker whose competence and honesty a hearer can assess; the text's standing depends instead on processes—editing norms, voting, correction, aggregation, moderation, statistical summarization—rather than on any individual person's trustworthiness.

Does the Nyāya argument for testimony as an independent pramāṇa survive this shift from person-to-person speech to networked, sometimes effectively speakerless text? Which premise is really under pressure—that testimonial warrant requires a trustworthy speaker, that testimony is irreducible to inference, or something else? Is there a repaired version that relocates trust to a system or collective without silently turning testimony into induction from pooled data, or should online testimony be understood as exactly that kind of induction, with the original argument applying only to direct assertion by an assessable person? If the argument needs repair, give the strongest version of the repair and say what it concedes.
