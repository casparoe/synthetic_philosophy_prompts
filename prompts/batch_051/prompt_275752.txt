For a seminar I'm teaching on moral psychology, I've been trying to pin down when a moral response counts as a bias rather than just a preference. My students keep using "bias" for any moral stance they don't like and "preference" for the ones they do, which is intellectually unsatisfying but hard to fix. Could you give me a working definition of a moral bias, one that separates it from a mere preference and from a substantive moral commitment, and then test it against these cases? (a) a man who is repulsed by the idea of eating dog and says doing so is morally wrong, though he can't articulate a principle beyond "it feels like eating a friend"; (b) a vegetarian who simply dislikes the taste and texture of meat but thinks eating meat is morally unobjectionable; (c) a judge who treats defendants more harshly when they resemble someone who bullied him in childhood; (d) a parent who says she would save her own child over three strangers in a fire and calls that just the kind of partiality love demands; (e) someone whose view about pushing a large man in front of a trolley flips depending on whether the case is described as killing him or as saving five people; (f) a person who suspects factory farming is wrong but keeps buying cheap meat and reports that facts about suffering feel less vivid to him than facts about prices.

I think (c) is a clear bias and (b) is a clear preference, and I honestly have no stable view about the other four. Apply the definition to all six, flag any place where the verdict feels wrong, and revise the definition until it stops simply tracking your own initial hunches. I'm less interested in a final dictionary sentence than in seeing where the concept starts to break.
