I am writing a novel set in a near-future city that has made high-fidelity experience machines available to all residents as a public utility. The city council proposes the following regulatory scheme. Every adult receives a weekly allotment of twenty machine-hours, which may be banked but not sold. Before a resident may draw any hours in a given week, they must log four hours of what the ordinance calls unmediated practice: waking activity performed outside the machine, chosen primarily for a reason other than pleasant experience, with caregiving, physical training, craft, repair, and study among the scheme's examples. A municipal Bureau of Unmediated Practice audits these logs. It flags residents whose stated motives it doubts, drawing on physiological and behavioral data and on inconsistencies between a resident's claimed motive and their other logged activities that week. A resident flagged twice in one year goes before a hearing officer; if the officer finds they inflated the unpleasantness or disinterestedness of their unmediated hours, they lose their machine allotment for the following month.

I am less interested in whether the scheme is just than in how it would actually behave, because I want the novel's plot about enforcement and corruption to grow out of the mechanism rather than out of cartoon villainy. Walk through what this policy would incentivize among residents, among Bureau staff, and among the politicians who set the Bureau's budget. How would it be gamed first, and how would the gaming change in the second year? What happens to the social meaning of unmediated activity once it becomes an audited precondition for machine access? And, since the city's debates draw on the standard theories of well-being, which of hedonism, desire-satisfaction, or objective-list theory does the auditing logic implicitly endorse, and where does that implicit theory strain against the mechanism?
