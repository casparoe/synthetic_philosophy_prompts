I am preparing a unit on the ethics of human extinction for an advanced undergraduate seminar in population ethics, and I want to close the unit with a tier-list exercise that forces students to disentangle several different strands of the intuition that extinction is bad. The guiding question is: what, if anything, makes the permanent end of the human species bad over and above the ordinary badness of the deaths it would involve? The question is deliberately framed to include cases such as humanity's gradual dying out through universal infertility, in which there need be no additional premature deaths at all, so the exercise should not reduce everything to the harm of dying.

I have seven candidate answers in mind, and I would like you to sort them into three tiers. Tier A should contain views that identify a genuinely distinct and defensible source of the additional badness; Tier B should contain views that are true as far as they go but are incomplete, or that largely restate the problem rather than explain the distinctive badness; Tier C should contain views that are false, confused, or collapse into one of the others under pressure. The candidates are: (1) the loss of all the worthwhile lives that could have been lived, on a straightforward aggregative picture; (2) a wrong done to the last generation, whose members could reasonably have expected humanity to continue after them and whose plans depend on that continuation; (3) the destruction of accumulated value, the art, knowledge, institutions, and ways of life that would be left with no inheritors; (4) Scheffler's afterlife conjecture, on which much of what we now do and value is sustained by an implicit background confidence in the ongoing human enterprise, so that a coming extinction would make much of our present activity lose its point; (5) the permanent failure of collective projects, which would be left unfinished in a way that an ordinary death merely interrupts; (6) the elimination of the only known locus of moral agency, so that after extinction there would be no valuers left to value anything at all; and (7) a deflationary view on which there is no further badness beyond the deaths, because once the last person is gone there is no subject left to be wronged. Place each candidate in a tier and give one or two sentences of reasoning for each; where candidates overlap, say explicitly which one you take to be doing the real explanatory work and which is borrowing from it.
