Please act as a professional philosopher who specializes in decision theory and is skilled at explaining subtle distinctions to advanced high-school students. Before you present anything final, think carefully and brainstorm the logical commitments that can separate positions on Newcomb's problem; include a short paragraph on which candidate distinctions are genuinely diagnostic and which look promising but actually produce false contrasts.

I am a high-school student preparing a handout for my school's philosophy club on why reasonable people disagree about Newcomb's problem. I would like you to build a decision tree that sorts someone into the position they actually hold, rather than the position they would call themselves by. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of received names: first present the branching questions and the combinations of answers they allow; then locate the actual named positions within that space; and finally note which answer combinations appear to be unoccupied and say, for each, whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or probably just unexplored.

Your branching questions must use the following five variants of the problem, and each question must be phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing labels such as evidential decision theory or causal decision theory:

1. The standard case: Box A is transparent and contains $1,000. Box B is opaque and contains $1,000,000 or nothing. Yesterday a predictor with 99% accuracy placed the million dollars in Box B exactly when it predicted that you would take only Box B. You may take only Box B or both boxes.
2. A near-chance case: identical to the standard case, except that the predictor is only 55% accurate.
3. A transparency case: identical to the standard case, except that Box B is transparent, so you can see whether the million dollars is there before you choose.
4. A commitment case: before the predictor makes its prediction, you may take a pill that will cause you to take only Box B, and the predictor observes whether you swallow it.
5. A disposition case: the predictor does not predict your act; it predicts, from a brain measurement taken the day before, whether you have the standing disposition to take only Box B, and your later choice is made in the normal way.

After the tree, say in one line for each question which underlying claim it is really testing. Each leaf should carry a short name for the position — use a standard name where one exists, and write unnamed if nobody defends that combination — plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Aim for four to seven leaves. Make sure at least the following positions appear as leaves or are explicitly flagged as too close to separate: one-boxing, understood as the evidentialist response; two-boxing, understood as the causalist response; the view that the problem is ill-posed because a predictor of the required accuracy is impossible; and a view on which the standard decision theories both neglect the role of precommitment. If two positions cannot be separated by your questions, say so and explain why rather than pretending that a branch sorts them.

Because this is for a discussion handout, please do not include a bibliography, citations, or reading recommendations.
