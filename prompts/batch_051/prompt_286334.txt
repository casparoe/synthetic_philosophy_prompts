After teaching a seminar on the adaptationism controversy in philosophy of biology for several years, I have become convinced that the two positions that persist in the room are not as far apart as their rhetoric suggests, but also that their residual disagreement is not merely verbal. One position, the adaptationist, holds that natural selection is the only known process capable of producing complex, function-like fit, and that it is therefore rationally privileged as the default explanation for a trait's presence, shape, and maintenance; apparent failures of optimality are problems to be refined against, not refutations. The other, the pluralist in the spirit of Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique, holds that adaptation is far too often asserted on the strength of plausible-sounding stories, and that byproducts, allometry, drift, and architectural constraints should stand on equal footing until selection is positively evidenced rather than assumed.

Write the document these two parties should have produced after a year of good-faith adversarial collaboration. Begin with a numbered list of claims both can sign, including claims about what optimality models and reverse-engineering arguments do and do not establish, about the difference between a trait's current utility and its origin, and about what a minimally adequate test of an adaptive hypothesis requires. Then give a disagreements table in which each row isolates one issue, states each side's view in wording that side would accept as its own, and marks the row substantive or merely verbal. Finally, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions whose settlement would move at least one side—and, for each, record in advance what each party predicts and describe a concrete case or class of evidence both would agree bears on it. Two cases worth making precise are a trait that persists unchanged after its putative function has been experimentally removed, and a trait whose apparent design can be produced by a known developmental constraint. Conclude by stating, for each side, what observation or argument would most force it to revise its central claim, and avoid a vacuous compromise to the effect that some traits are adaptations while others are not.
