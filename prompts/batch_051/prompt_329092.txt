Here are the criteria I was taught for something's being a code in the information-theoretic sense: (1) it employs a finite set of discrete symbols; (2) there is a systematic, at least partly arbitrary mapping from symbols or symbol combinations to distinct meanings or outputs; (3) an encoder produces the symbols and a decoder reads them by way of that mapping; and (4) misreading is possible, because decoding is a rule-governed act rather than mere causal interaction. Now consider the histone code: the combinatorial patterns of covalent modifications, such as acetylation, methylation, and phosphorylation, on the N-terminal tails of histone proteins. These marks are written and erased by dedicated enzymes, bound by reader proteins with specialized recognition domains, and in combination appear to regulate whether a stretch of chromatin is transcriptionally active or silent. Defenders call this a code; skeptics reply that the marks often act as graded, additive binding surfaces or through direct effects on chromatin's physical chemistry, and that their effect depends so heavily on context that there is no fixed decoding key. Work through the criteria one by one. Does the histone code qualify as a code, or does it sit at the border in a way that forces revision of the criteria, or of the claim that DNA is itself a code?
