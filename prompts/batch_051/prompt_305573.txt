I'm a junior in high school and one of the organizers of our school's philosophy club. Last week two seniors, Maya and Daniel, got into a long argument about moral language that nobody could settle, and I'd like a careful referee. Maya was defending what she called an expressivist picture: she said a sentence like "stealing is wrong" isn't in the business of reporting a fact, the way "the pen is on the table" is; it's closer to booing stealing, or refusing to go along with it, because it expresses an attitude rather than describing how the world is.

Daniel pushed back with a modus ponens example, which he said was what people mean by the Frege–Geach problem. He asked us to consider the argument whose first premise is that if stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong; whose second premise is that stealing is wrong; and whose conclusion is that getting your little brother to steal is wrong. That argument is plainly valid, he said. But in the second premise, "stealing is wrong" expresses disapproval of stealing, while in the first premise the same words sit inside an "if" clause where the speaker is not disapproving of stealing at all. So either the phrase has two different meanings in the two occurrences, in which case the argument equivocates, or it has one meaning, in which case that meaning can't be just an attitude. Maya replied that Daniel was assuming all validity has to be preservation of truth. Moral arguments, she said, are valid because the attitudes involved fit together: accepting the premises while denying the conclusion would be a rationally incoherent combination of attitudes, and that kind of incoherence, not truth-preservation, is all the logic morality needs. Daniel answered that this replaces logic with psychology unless Maya can supply a real formal account of which combinations of attitudes are incoherent — one that handles negation, disjunction, quantification, and belief reports like "John believes stealing is wrong" — and he said no expressivist account does that in a way that matches ordinary moral reasoning.

Maya then changed tactics. She said moral terms might be like pejoratives, which both describe and vent an attitude: "wrong" could have a descriptive core that does all the logical work in embedded contexts, while the sentence still expresses a non-cognitive attitude toward the thing described. That would let conditionals behave classically while preserving the expressivist thought that moral disagreement is partly a clash of attitudes rather than only a clash of factual beliefs. Daniel's last move was that this is too good to be true: once "wrong" is given a descriptive core that behaves like an ordinary predicate, the view starts to look like a fact-stating theory in disguise, and it becomes unclear what the extra attitude component is actually buying. I honestly can't tell who has the better case. Could you go through their exchange step by step and say who is right about which specific point, and who is more right overall? Please finish with a final line beginning with "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase giving your overall verdict.
