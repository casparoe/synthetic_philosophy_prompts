I'm a software engineer, so I tend to think about political systems the way I think about a legacy distributed system: a bunch of agents with misaligned incentives, no test suite, and everyone patching code they don't really understand. That's probably why I care more about mechanism design than about ideology, and why I keep coming back to one question about elections. Lately I've been wondering what happens to the career politician problem if you solve it by force.

Imagine a wealthy parliamentary democracy of about 17 million people — call it a Netherlands clone — decides in 2025 to abolish elections for its lower house. The new lower house has 150 seats filled by random selection from the adult citizenry, sortition-style. Each selected member serves one four-year term with no re-entry for ten years afterwards, and a quarter of the seats turn over every year so the whole body is never fresh at once. It keeps the permanent civil service, an independent judiciary, a free press, and a small indirectly elected upper house that can delay but not kill legislation. Parties aren't banned, but they have no formal role in selecting lower-house members, and campaign finance basically becomes irrelevant because there are no campaigns. Assume the draw gets a high acceptance rate because the salary is meaningful and there are legal protections for you're old job.

Where does this system actually end up after about thirty years? I'm not asking for a story; I want a failure analysis and a prediction with the reasoning visible. In particular: do policy decisions get better or worse when the median seated member has no electoral accountibility and no incentive to build a reputation for the next election? Where does the actual political expertise go — into the civil service, the upper house, lobbyists, the parties operating as shadow organizations, or the staffers who brief the citizen-legislators? Does the assembly get captured by whoever controls the agenda and the flow of briefings, and if so by whom? Some people say the point of elections isn't only picking good people but letting voters punish incumbents — what happens once that release valve is gone? And once career politicians are banned, does the problem they were meant to solve just relocate to a new class of unaccountable professionals, or does it actually disappear?

For each step of your chain of reasoning, tell me how confident you are, and single out the one point in the whole prediction you're least sure about — and say what fact, if you had it, would most change your mind.
