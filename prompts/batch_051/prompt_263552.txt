Two people in a reading group I'm in got into a long argument about Émilie du Châtelet's Discours sur le bonheur (I guess the title is often translated as Discourse on Happiness). I'm the only one there who isn't in grad school for something; I'm a backend engineer and mostly just lurk. The bit that set them off is near the end, where she says, roughly, that you need passions to be happy, and that you should not always go around stripping away the illusions that attach you to things, because those illusions are often what make life tolerable and interesting. One of them, Dana, said the passage is either banal or dangerous. If illusions just means "engagement" or "selective focus," it's banal; if it literally means false beliefs, then she's telling us to be less accurate about our own lives for the sake of feeling good, which Dana thinks is self-deception and unstable anyway, because you can't knowingly decide to believe what's false. The other, Sam, said that's too fast. Sam's take is that du Châtelet is talking about a real phenomenon: there are things—love, long projects, political hopes—that you can only care about properly if you don't keep them at arm's length and audit every detail, and that "illusion" is her word for the forward-leaning part of attention, not for lying to yourself. Dana came back with: but she says the person who has no illusions is miserable, and that sounds like she's saying happiness requires being wrong about something, at least sometimes. Sam replied: she says that because the person who insists on total accuracy about their own prospects and relationships ends up living in a kind of cold storage, and that's an observation, not a recommendation to reason badly. Dana said Sam is just softening the text so it doesn't sound bad. They asked me to referee, and I ended up with nothing smarter than "I think you're both onto something." Could you go through this exchange and say who's right at each step, what the passage is actually best read as saying, and whether the two readings can be made to fit together?
