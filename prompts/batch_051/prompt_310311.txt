I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about Molyneux's problem—the question whether a person born blind who can tell a cube from a sphere by touch would, upon gaining sight, recognize which is which before touching them. My friend leaned toward the negative, on the strength of what he took to be the messy and generally disappointing reports from restored-sight cases; I kept resisting, because I do not see how any empirical result could settle the question until one has decided what counts as recognizing a shape rather than merely noticing its visible features and slowly working out an answer. By the end we had arrived at a familiar diagnosis—that we were not really disagreeing about the data but about what the question was asking—and I am not sure that diagnosis is right.

Afterward I noticed a parallel with something I have been reading about in machine learning: models trained on images and captions in a shared vector space can retrieve an image from a caption they were never paired with, and practitioners then argue about whether this shows that content is genuinely represented in a modality-neutral format or merely that two separate codes have been learned to align without sharing a common currency. The strong version of the parallel says Molyneux's problem is exactly about whether shape information is stored in a modality-specific or modality-neutral format, which is the same question one asks about a joint embedding space. The weak version says the analogy collapses at the crucial point: the model receives enormous cross-modal supervision, while the man born blind has no paired sight-and-touch experience of the objects to learn from, so the engineering case cannot illuminate the philosophical one.

Which is it? I want a precise diagnosis rather than a verdict on either side. State the mapping between the two cases explicitly, say what would have to be true of both sides for a result to transfer in either direction, and name something concrete the parallel predicts that is probably false—for example, something we should expect to observe in a person gaining sight if the machine-learning reading were the right way to frame Molyneux's question. Could such engineering work ever sharpen the philosophical problem, or does it only restate it in a new vocabulary?
