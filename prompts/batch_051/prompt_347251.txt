Please compare the doomsday argument and the simulation argument as exercises in anthropic reasoning, with the aim of determining how deep the similarity between them runs.

Understand the doomsday argument roughly as follows. Treat your position in the birth order of all humans who will ever live—say, roughly one hundred billionth—as a random draw from the entire sequence of humans who will ever exist. A random draw is more likely to fall near the middle of a sequence than in its initial small fraction, so the fact that you are roughly one hundred billionth in the birth order is more probable on the hypothesis that the total human population will be in the low hundreds of billions than on the hypothesis that it will run to many trillions. Unless something independently explains why you are early, the observation therefore shifts probability toward a smaller total and a sooner human extinction.

Understand the simulation argument roughly as follows. If a technologically mature civilization comes to run vast numbers of high-fidelity simulations of its own ancestors, then most minds with experiences like yours would be simulated minds rather than original biological ones. Unless there is independent reason to think such simulations are rare or almost never run, the fact that you are having experiences of this kind therefore counts as evidence that you yourself are simulated.

Compare the two arguments. In particular: Is the simulation argument best understood as the doomsday argument with the reference class widened and a different empirical premise inserted, or do the two arguments differ in kind—in what each treats as its evidence, in the role each assigns to assumptions about the future, and in the conclusion each targets? Do they share the same underlying principle about random location among possible observers, and are their standard vulnerabilities the same ones, such as the choice of reference class, the legitimacy of self-locating probabilities, and the role of prior probabilities, or does each have distinctive weaknesses? Finally, does accepting or rejecting one of them commit you to accepting or rejecting the other, or are they logically independent? Justify your comparison.
