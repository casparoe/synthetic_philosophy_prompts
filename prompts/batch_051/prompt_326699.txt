I spent four decades in the experimental sciences, and now that I have time I have been reading my way through the philosophy of mathematics. The debate that most puzzles me is the one over computer-assisted proof—the kind of proof used for the four-colour theorem and, later, for the Kepler conjecture. It has been running since at least the 1970s, and although the tools have changed enormously, the basic positions have not. I am less interested in who is right than in why the debate has not moved; I would like a diagnosis of the disagreement itself.

Let me set out the sides as I understand them. One side treats a proof as, at bottom, a certificate: a finite object that can in principle be checked mechanically. On this view Appel and Haken's proof of the four-colour theorem, with its exhaustive case analysis delegated to a computer, is no less a proof than a humanly surveyable argument; indeed it may be more reliable, because the checking is mechanical and the enumeration is complete. The later formal verification of Hales's proof of the Kepler conjecture only strengthens the position: the proof is, in effect, a long formal derivation, and the fact that no human can survey it is a fact about human limitation, not about the proof's status.

The other side treats a proof as a human epistemological artifact: its job is not merely to certify a theorem but to make the theorem's truth intelligible to a competent mathematician. On this view a computation that no one can follow step by step is closer to measurement or experiment than to proof. It may give excellent grounds for belief, but it does not count as mathematical proof in the full sense, because it does not provide the surveyability, generality, or understanding that proof is supposed to provide. The first side replies, as I have met the reply, that this confuses proof with explanation: a proof can be perfectly valid without being explanatory, and demanding surveyability is nostalgia for a proof technology that has been outgrown.

What strikes me is that this exchange has stayed structurally the same for decades, even as formal verification has become routine. I would like you to diagnose the impasse. Is this a verbal disagreement about the word 'proof', in which the term has quietly shifted between two senses—a formal certificate and a humanly surveyable argument—and the parties are simply not using it the same way? Is it a substantive disagreement about the epistemology of mathematics, for example about whether one can be said to know a theorem whose proof one cannot survey? Is it a difference about standards of evidence: what counts as an acceptable means of verification? Are the two sides answering different questions, one about certainty and one about understanding, under a single heading? Or is it a difference in bedrock intuitions about what mathematics is, of the sort neither side can argue for?

Let me add a suspicion from my background as an experimentalist. In much of modern science, no single person has personally checked all the evidence for a result; knowledge rests on distributed trust, calibrated instruments, and statistical conventions, and I have accepted such results as knowledge all my life. It is not obvious to me how someone who accepts that can refuse to accept an unsurveyable proof, unless there is something special about mathematics—for instance, that its knowledge is supposed to be a priori, or that understanding is internal to what a mathematical proof is for. I also notice that surveyability seems to come in degrees: a several-hundred-page human proof strains it too, and mathematicians often cite such results without reading every line. If surveyability can be distributed across a community, the contrast with computer proof may be one of degree rather than kind.

Given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress here: a distinction that dissolves the dispute, a formal or empirical result that would settle it, a change of question that would make it tractable—or, if your view is that no progress should be expected, an argument for why this is a reasonable place for philosophical disagreement to remain.
