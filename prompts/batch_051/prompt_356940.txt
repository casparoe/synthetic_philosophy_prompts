Work with the following account of what it is for a game about war to glorify war. On this account, a war game glorifies war to the degree that (i) it frames military violence as noble, heroic, or redemptive; (ii) its mechanics make killing systematically rewarding while suppressing or abstracting away the costs of killing; (iii) it presents the conflict as morally clean—a righteous side against a villainous side, with no real moral remainder; and (iv) its audiovisual and reward design makes violence feel exhilarating, beautiful, or triumphant rather than merely intelligible.

Now consider a game constructed as follows. It is an online, squad-based shooter set in a real historical siege, played from the position of ordinary soldiers. There is no campaign, no cutscenes, and no named hero, and the game itself never editorializes about the war. Missions are procedurally generated and deliberately mundane: repair a bridge, hold a checkpoint, resupply a forward position. Killing is not scored directly, but the objectives usually cannot be completed without firefights, and the combat is tuned to feel fluid, suspenseful, and deeply satisfying; players habitually describe matches as fun in an unforced way. After each match, a screen lists every named enemy and civilian death for which the player's side was responsible, but the next match begins automatically after a short pause. The game awards medals for effective combat and for saving teammates, yet it never praises the cause for which the soldiers are fighting.

Is this game a glorification of war? Work through the four criteria in turn, and say where they underdetermine the verdict or pull against one another. Then say whether the case reveals that any criterion is badly drawn—for instance, whether criterion (iv) is doing too much or too little work—and finish by stating what would change your mind: which criterion you would be most willing to revise, what consideration would most plausibly flip your overall verdict, and where your reasoning is least secure.
