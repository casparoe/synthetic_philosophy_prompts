Act as an expert in applied ethics and political philosophy, particularly on partiality, special obligations, and the ethics of philanthropy. Think the issue through carefully; begin by mapping the strongest case on each side and then move toward a concrete recommendation rather than a survey.

I sit on the board of a mid-sized private foundation with an annual grant budget of about $4 million. Our charter directs us to improve the well-being of people in our country, while permitting a minority of spending on urgent humanitarian needs abroad. We have historically funded domestic food banks, youth programs, and college-access initiatives. A newer board member has now pressed the effective-altruist case that the same money spent on global health in low-income countries — malaria prevention, deworming, and direct cash transfers to very poor households — would save or improve far more lives per dollar than our domestic grantees do. She proposes moving 60 percent of our grant budget to these global programs over three years. Two board members oppose her: the foundation was built from domestic wealth, enjoys tax-exempt status here, and exists to serve our fellow citizens; compatriots, they say, have a special claim on these resources, and it is not acceptable to treat a compatriot child's future as worth a fiftieth of a distant child's simply because of where she was born. The other two reply that nationality is morally arbitrary, that need and expected impact should determine grants, and that partiality toward compatriots is just a dressed-up failure of impartiality.

Draft the decision memo I would circulate before next month's meeting. It should recommend a concrete allocation policy — roughly what share of the budget goes domestic versus global, and on what schedule — give the board a principled criterion for revisiting the split, and engage the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously rather than caricaturing it. The board will act on the memo, so the bottom line and the reasoning must be tight.
