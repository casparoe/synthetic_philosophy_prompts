I am a doctoral student in social and political psychology, preparing a chapter on the normative presuppositions of resilience policy, and I keep running into the same image in the character-education and intervention literature: the claim, treated almost as self-evident, that resilience is like the elasticity of a physical material — a bent reed that straightens, a ball that bounces back to its original shape. The image is made to do real work. From it, authors infer that resilience is a general, trainable disposition of individuals; that the appropriate response to adversity is to return to one's previous level of functioning; and that programmes teaching people to bounce back are therefore an unqualified good.

I suspect the analogy is doing a good deal of quiet normative work — above all in treating recovery as a return to an earlier state, in locating the relevant capacity inside the person rather than in the environment, and in converting harms that are unevenly distributed into personal deficits. But before I press that suspicion, I want the analogy audited properly. Could you set out in detail what corresponds to what between a deformed elastic object and a person under adversity; where the mapping breaks; which disanalogies matter for the conclusions usually drawn and which are merely superficial; what the elasticity picture silently imports about reversibility, the value of the original shape, and individual versus structural determinants; and whether the conclusions commonly derived from it — that grit, fortitude, and resilience are individually teachable virtues and that restoration is the goal of intervention — really survive once those disanalogies are made explicit. If they do not, say what remains in a more restricted form, and whether some other analogy would carry the useful residue better. Please answer in British English.
