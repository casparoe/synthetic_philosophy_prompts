I recently reread Flowers for Algernon, and it kept pulling me back to a distinction I have seen people draw between therapy and enhancement. The rough idea is that medicine is on solid ground when it repairs a defect or restores someone to normal functioning, but becomes morally uneasy when it pushes an already healthy person beyond the normal range. Charlie's operation seems designed to sit exactly on that line: he begins with an intellectual disability, so the surgery is presented as a treatment, yet it does not stop at average—it carries him far past the people around him, which makes the very same procedure look like an enhancement.

I have no academic background here; I am just a reader trying to think this through, and I would like a careful reading of the novel through this lens rather than a general essay on whether the distinction is blurry. A few things puzzle me especially. First, Charlie is paired with Algernon, a mouse who had the same operation but did not start out disabled. Does the novel treat those two cases as morally different, or as one kind of intervention that differs only in the starting baseline? Second, at the height of his abilities Charlie becomes isolated and often impatient with the people around him. Is the book suggesting that something is wrong with the enhancement itself, or with how he and others respond to the change? Third, the gains reverse. If they had lasted, would the therapy/enhancement line look more or less important—and does the fact that they fade change whether we should call the operation an enhancement at all?

What I really want to know is whether the story leaves any version of the therapy/enhancement distinction worth keeping, or whether it shows that the real issue lies elsewhere—in who gets to decide what counts as normal, or in what the change does to a person over time. And I would like the reading to say what the novel adds to this debate that a purely conceptual discussion of therapy versus enhancement misses.
