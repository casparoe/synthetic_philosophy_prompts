In my philosophy club we've been discussing the nonidentity problem, and I tried to reconstruct the core argument with a case of my own. Here's what I ended up with:

(1) An action is wrong only if it wrongs at least one particular person, and an action wrongs a person only if it makes that person worse off than she would otherwise have been.
(2) A couple have a harmless, easily treatable medical condition. If they conceive now, before treating it, their child (Ada) will be born with a chronic but manageable illness, and her life will still be clearly worth living. If they take a cheap six-month treatment and then conceive, they will have a different child (Beth), who will be born healthy. Waiting costs the couple nothing.
(3) If they conceive now, the only individual who could be made worse off is Ada; Beth never exists under that choice.
(4) Ada is not made worse off by their conceiving now, because if they had waited, Ada would never have existed at all.
(5) A life that is worth living is better for the person living it than never having existed, so Ada's actual life is not worse than never existing.
(6) Therefore, the couple's conceiving now makes no particular person worse off.
(7) Therefore, by (1), the couple's conceiving now is not wrong.

I can't accept the conclusion, but I also can't find the step where the argument goes wrong. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails? If it is valid, which premise should someone who thinks the couple's choice is wrong reject, and under what reading of that premise? Be specific about the step and the reading involved.
