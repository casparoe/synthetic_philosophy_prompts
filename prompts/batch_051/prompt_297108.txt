Two friends of mine, Dev and Mina, got into a long argument at dinner about whether society should keep publicly funding cosmology. Dev is a cosmology PhD student and Mina works in science policy, but neither was just arguing from their job description, and honestly I couldnt tell who was getting the better of it. I mostly listened and kept changing my mind. I'll reconstruct the back-and-forth as best I remember.

Mina started it. She said public money for science is justified by what the science eventually gives back—technology, medicine, useful prediction—and cosmology has no foreseeable applications at all. So, she said, it's not that cosmology is worthless; it's that tax money is a forced contribution, and forcing people to pay for something with no practical return is hard to justify. If private donors want to fund it, great, but that's a different question.

Dev said that's much too narrow. Knowledge isn't only valuable when you can plug it into a product. Understanding what the universe is made of, how it began, whether it will end—that's worth having for its own sake, in the same loose way that knowing history or literature is. He said a society that refuses to spend anything on questions that big has a tin ear for values that aren't priced in dollars.

Mina came back that "valuable for its own sake" is exactly the problem, because any field can say that. A city government could justify counting every pigeon in the city on the grounds that knowledge is intrinsically good. What's missing, she said, is a limiting principle, and in a pluralist society you can't tax people on the strength of your own sense of profoundness. Either the knowledge pays its way, or you're asking other people to subsidize your taste.

Dev said we already accept taxing people for things not everyone values: national parks, opera, pure math. Unless she wants to defund all of those, she needs an argument for why cosmology is specially bad. And, he added, "no foreseeable application" is a temporary verdict, not a fact about the universe. General relativity looked useless in 1916, and now GPS would not work without its corrections. He said betting against the long-term usefulness of an entire basic field is itself unscientific.

Mina said that's a survivorship-bias story. For every piece of pure research that later paid off, there are whole graveyards of it that didn't, and you can't run a funding agency by buying retrospective lottery tickets. She also said Dev had dodged the point about forced contributions: saying "we do it for opera too" just moves the problem to procedure. Either way, it's a majority taxing a minority for stuff the minority finds pointless, and for something as optional as cosmology the burden should be higher.

Dev said that if her real objection is that any majority-funded taste is illegitimate, then she is no longer arguing about cosmology at all; she's arguing against public funding of art and parks and archaeology. If her objection is specifically that cosmology has no applications, then she has to answer the point that its real product is not a widget but a changed self-understanding—and she hasn't. He also said the actual sums are so small next to the rest of the science budget that the "forced contribution" is more like a rounding error than a tax on anyone's conscience.

Mina said the small-sums point cuts both ways: if the funding is so small, then its loss would also be small, so the budget line can't settle it—it's a question of principle. And on the principle, she said Dev's "self-understanding" argument needs a rule that funds cosmology but not counting pigeon feathers, and until he gives one, "we find it profound" is doing all the work.

That's roughly where it ended. I want you to adjudicate this as it happened, move by move. You're a philosopher who works on science policy and the value of knowledge; think carefully, and start by brainstorming the distinct issues before you rule. For each move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say who (if anyone) had the better of each exchange, who has the better overall arguement, what each side's strongest point is, and name the strongest consideration that neither of them made.
