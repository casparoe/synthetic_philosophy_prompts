I'm a high school student working on a project about how people reason from base rates and past cases, and I'd like you to work through a modified version of a classic problem for me. Please keep the reasoning precise and quantitative, but don't assume I know graduate-level terminology.

In the standard taxicab problem, 85% of the cabs in a city are Green and 15% are Blue. A cab is involved in a hit-and-run at night, and a witness identifies the cab as Blue. Under the relevant conditions, the witness identifies cab color correctly 80% of the time and incorrectly 20% of the time. The usual answer is that the probability the cab really was Blue is only about 0.41, because the lopsided base rate overwhelms the witness report.

Now here is the variation I haven't seen discussed. Suppose the 15% Blue figure is a citywide average over all hours and all neighborhoods. But the accident happened at 2 a.m. on a particular street, and taxi log data show that among cabs actually driving on that street between 1 a.m. and 3 a.m., 60% are Blue. The witness's 80% accuracy was measured in a testing booth where half of the cabs shown were Blue and half were Green, so the accuracy test itself conveys no information about the mix of cabs on that street.

Please do the following. First, work through the posterior probability that the cab was Blue using each base rate: 15% and 60%. Second, address the conceptual question: is one of these base rates the right one, or is the correct answer genuinely underdetermined? Third, explain what principle, if anything, could justify choosing a reference class when the data can be sorted into finer or coarser classes, and whether the ordinary Bayesian calculation still has a determinate meaning when the base rate depends on where we draw the boundary around "similar" cases. I especially want to know whether a more specific reference class is automatically better just because it is more specific. Please write your answer in American English.
