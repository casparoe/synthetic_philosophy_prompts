I work in the emerging-technology unit of a small country's government. Every quarter we get a briefing pack on frontier AI progress: training runs, algorithmic efficiency numbers, eval results, deployment incidents, the lot. Based on that briefing we have to set a 'takeoff risk level' on a 1–5 scale. The number isn't decorative: it changes compute-reporting thresholds, decides whether new models need pre-deployment audits, and at level 4 or above lets us ask for emergency powers. We're three policy staff, none of us are actually researchers, we have about two weeks per quarter, and the minister signs off on whatever level we set — so false alarms and false comfort are both career-ending.

Right now we mostly just go to level 3 because it feels safe, and that's making the whole scale meaningless.

Please write a decision procedure we can actually run on a quarterly briefing pack. I want it grounded in the real conceptual distinctions from the AI takeoff debate — fast vs slow takeoff, soft vs hard takeoff, recursive self-improvement, compute overhang, algorithmic progress vs hardware progress, discontinuous capability jumps vs smooth extrapolation — but translated into questions an non-expert can ask. Give me: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because it's a values or judgment call rather than a fact, and how to map the answers onto the 1–5 level. Then run the procedure on three made-up quarterly briefings: (1) a quarter where most progress is just scaling compute on settled architectures, (2) a quarter with a sharp jump on a hard benchmark plus credible signs of AIs contributing to AI research, and (3) a quarter where headline numbers look flat but models are being used in the wild in ways that suggest a lot of latent capability is just sitting there (compute overhang). Make sure at least one of the three comes out at a level we won't like, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle.
