Ive been trying to sort out what it actually means when a country "joins" something bigger, and I keep going in circles. Take the EU's customs union. As I understand it, member countries can't set their own tariffs on goods coming from outside the bloc, and they negotiate trade deals with outside countries together, not one by one. They also follow the same rulebook for product standards and customs procedures at the border. But those same countries still have their own parliaments, their own armies, their own tax systems, their own courts, their own citizenship and immigration rules (I think?), and their leaders still say the country is sovereign. That seems inconsistent to me: either you have the final say over your own affairs or you dont. And then there are confederations, which look even looser — members keep more powers, maybe even foreign policy and defense, but they still set up common institutions for some things. So whats actually going on underneath these arrangements, philosophically?

Act as an expert political philosopher and think through this carefully, maybe brainstorm a few interpretations before settling on an answer. What view of sovereignty and membership does a customs union presuppose? Does joining one only make sense if you think sovereignty can be divided or pooled, or can you make sense of it while still holding that sovereignty is indivisible? What does the practice strictly presuppose versus merely fit well with? And if we swapped in a different view — say sovereignty is all-or-nothing, or it's just a legal label with no moral weight — what would actually have to change in how customs unions or confederations are designed? Also, what does joining do to the "we" that is supposed to be sovereign — does the country stay the same political entity that now shares a decision, or does membership change what it is? I want the hidden philosophical commitments dug out, not a description of how the EU works.
