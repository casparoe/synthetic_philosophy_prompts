I'm a PhD student and I've been living inside Machiavelli long enough that I cant tell any more whether my central claim is a reading or just a clever anachronism. I'm giving a paper in a couple of weeks to a mixed research seminar, and I'd like to be prepared for the question period rather than blindsided by it. The thesis, roughly, is this: the usual fortune-versus-virtù framing is misleading. Fortune in Machiavelli is better read not as a force that opposes individual virtù, but as a measure of a state's exposure to shocks that no ruler can fully anticipate; virtù, correspondingly, is the quality — mostly of institutions rather than of persons — that lowers that exposure. So I want to argue that the real unit in Machiavelli isn't the prince but the constitution: laws, armies, civic religion, the habits that let a state survive a mediocre successor.

The audience is a mix of political theorists who read Machiavelli as a republican and put citizenly virtù at the centre of his thought, contextualist intellectual historians who will probably say I'm importing a modern concept of 'institutions' into a sixteenth-century advice-book writer, and a couple of IR people who read him as a realist about what founders actually have to do. The theorists are mostly worried about whether my account leaves any place for civic virtue; the historians care about anachronism and genre; the IR people want to know whether I can still underwrite a usable theory of statecraft. I want a prep sheet, not a defence. What will I actually get asked? I'd like the objections in their own vocabulary, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, with a note on what I should concede up front, where my replies are weakest, and the one question I should be terrified of but probably won't see coming. If the thesis is just muddled, don't hedge: tell me which bit collapses first.
