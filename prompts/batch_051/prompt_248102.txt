Okay so I'm a PhD student in political economy and I'm trying to get a clearer grip on a specific thing in the institutions-versus-geography debate about why some countries are rich. I get the basic identification problem: you can't just regress income on instutional quality and claim institutions cause development, because of reverse causality (rich countries can afford better institutions) and a million omitted variables. I also get the settler-mortality instrument as a story: Europeans who settled in large numbers built more inclusive institutions, while Europeans who mostly died of disease built extractive ones, so settler mortality acts as a kind of quasi-experimental shock to institutional type. The part I keep tripping over is the exclusion restriction. For the instrument to identify the effect of institutions, settler mortality has to affect today's incomes only through institutions. But the same disease environment that killed settlers would presumably also have shaped the health, human capital, and even agricultural possibilities of local populations in ways that persist independently of institutions. How do people defend the exclusion restriction against that, and what exactly would a critic have to show to actually break the design? I don't need a literature review or citations, I want the intuition for where the load-bearing assumptions are and which challenges are genuinely damaging as opposed to just noise.
