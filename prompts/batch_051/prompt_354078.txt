I would be grateful for a technical analysis of the following scheme, which is meant to address the information problem at the heart of the socialist calculation debate. Consider a planned economy in which the means of production are socially owned and all prices for producer goods are set by a Central Board. Each quarter the Board publishes its prices. The distinctive rule is this: any employee, manager, engineer, or consumer may propose a new price for a producer good, and the Board is obliged to implement the proposal at once, provided the proposer deposits a bond proportional to the good's previous quarter's turnover. At the end of the quarter the bond is repaid with interest if the good's inventory falls within the target band announced in advance by the Board's statistical office; otherwise it is forfeited to a public investment fund. In addition, the proposer receives a bonus proportional to the reduction, if any, in measured shortages and queuing time for that good over the quarter. Please walk through how this institution would actually behave. Who would propose changes, on the strength of what knowledge, and who would be deterred? Where would it be gamed first, and how would the choice of bond size, interest rate, and target band alter the outcome? Would the scheme elicit the dispersed, often tacit local knowledge that Hayek argued cannot be transmitted to a central planner, or would it merely recreate the calculation problem in the form of a bond market? Finally, which of these failure modes could be corrected without dismantling the mechanism, and what would the corrections themselves do?
