I recently spent an embarrassing amount of time dithering over whether to sell a second-hand car I no longer need. The car runs perfectly well, but I kept thinking about what I had originally paid for it, and I felt that selling it at the current price would be "losing" money even though the money is already gone. When I mentioned this at lunch, two colleagues — let me call them Miriam and James — began to argue about whether my hesitation was simply loss aversion, and the argument quickly became more interesting than my car problem. I followed the individual moves but could not tell who was actually winning.

Miriam said loss aversion and the endowment effect are straightforward failures of rationality. Losing £100 and gaining £100 are symmetrical changes in wealth, so a rational agent should care only about where they end up; yet people reliably demand far more to sell something than they would pay for an identical item, merely because they happen to own it. The classic classroom experiments, she said, prove the point: students randomly given a mug and then allowed to trade behave as though the mug's value changed the moment it became "theirs", even though there are no transaction costs and no information asymmetries. She concluded that the bias is real, and that marketers exploit it with free trials and opt-out defaults.

James said this was much too quick. Ownership, he argued, normally carries information and familiarity: you know the quirks of the thing you own, it fits your routines, and selling it involves search costs and the risk of a poor replacement. Even in the mug experiments, he said, the gap mostly disappears once people gain trading experience, which suggests inexperience rather than a deep bias. More fundamentally, he said, Miriam was smuggling in the assumption that only final states matter: a person can rationally care about changes from their current situation, because a loss may push them below a threshold that a same-sized gain would merely add slack above. Miriam answered that learning to suppress the gap does not show the original valuation was unbiased, and that if two equivalent framings of a single choice produce different behaviour, at least one behaviour must be mistaken. James replied that framing effects show only that reference points matter to people, not that reference points are illegitimate.

Could you go through their exchange point by point and say who is right about which specific point, what both of them are missing, and who has the better case overall on the question of whether loss aversion and the endowment effect are biases or legitimate preferences?
