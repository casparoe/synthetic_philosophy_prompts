I keep coming back to the spot in Mill's A System of Logic, in his discussion of the logic of the moral sciences, where he says social phenomena exhibit what he calls the composition of causes. Paraphrasing: when lots of circumstances act together in society, the joint effect is often not just the mechanical sum of the effects each cause would have on its own. It is more like a chemical combination, where the compound has properties that none of the separate ingredients had, and you cannot predict the compound's behaviour just by knowing the ingredients individually. Mill uses this to push back against the idea that you can start from a few simple laws of human nature and deduce what a whole society will do by simple addition.

I'm less interested in a summary of Mill than in the implications of that point for the old individualism-versus-holism dispute in social science. If social outcomes routinely have this chemical character, does that give a principled reason to say there are social-level properties or forces that individualistic explanations can't capture? Or does it just show that individual-level explanations need to include interactions, contexts, and feedbacks, which is still a claim about individuals? For a model trying to explain something like a bank run, a moral panic, or the rapid spread of a new word, where should the reductive story stop: at the people, at the group, or at something like the network state? And does Mill's chemical analogy maybe cut the other way, making social science look less lawlike, because you can no longer derive large social patterns from stable psychological laws?
