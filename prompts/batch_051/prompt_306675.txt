The repugnant conclusion in population ethics says that for any population of hugely happy people, say ten billion people at welfare +100, there's some much larger population—say ten trillion people at welfare +1—with more total welfare, so total utilitarians are stuck calling the second, barely-worth-living world better. Now change the setting: suppose the second world isn't made of ten trillion separate biological lives but of ten trillion copies of a single brain emulation—one mind uploaded and run as software, copyable like a file—each copy living the same just-barely-positive life. Does the repugnant conclusion survive this, or does the copy structure change the argument? I'm interested in whether the total view is still forced to say the same thing, and whether person-affecting views (ones that say an outcome is better only if it's better for some particular person) get any new resources from the fact that the extra lives are exact copies rather than new people. Try to isolate which premise of the original argument does the real work under emulation.
