I have been trying to make the distinction between history and collective memory precise, and I keep coming back to the question of what should give way when the two conflict. After some argument with friends, I settled on four criteria for an account of the past to count as history, where by "history" I mean a public account that may claim the same kind of authority as the documentary record when they diverge: (1) independent grounding—the account's assertions can be traced to sources existing apart from the community's present retelling, such as documents, artifacts, or testimony recorded close in time; (2) corrigibility—the community treats the account as revisable in light of new evidence, rather than treating doubt as disloyalty; (3) outsider assessability—a suitably trained person who is not a member of the community can in principle check the account against its sources; and (4) fact–meaning separation—the account distinguishes the question of what happened from the question of what the event must mean for the community now.

As a borderline case, take a village I will call Kelshaven, settled after the passenger ship Corbel broke up on its rocks in 1888. The local commemorative tradition holds that the lighthouse keeper, Ewan Reeve, had been denied his wages and deliberately extinguished the light, and that the first settlers were working people who built the rescue boats themselves while the shipping company stood by. For more than a century the story has been passed down, taught in the school, and recited at the annual memorial. Last year, marine archaeologists recovered part of Reeve's log and a section of the ship's manifest. The log reports that the light failed during a sudden squall and was relit before dawn; it mentions a wage dispute but dates it three months earlier, and it reads as a self-justifying document. The manifest shows that the rescue boats were owned and crewed by the company. The historical society accepts both documents as authentic and concedes the boats point, but votes to continue presenting the commemorative account as "the town's own history" in the school curriculum, on the grounds that the log is one surviving entry written by an interested party and that collective memory is itself evidence of how the community came to be. Does the commemorative account qualify as history under my four criteria? Work criterion by criterion, say where the criteria leave the verdict underdetermined, and then tell me which should give way on the specific point about the light—the archival record or the collective memory—and whether my four criteria have already stacked the deck.
