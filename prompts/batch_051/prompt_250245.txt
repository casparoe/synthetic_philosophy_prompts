Okay so ive been chewing on this bit of Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.7 (Rackham translation), where he's trying to separate sophia from phronesis. He writes:

"The wise man therefore must not only know the conclusions that follow from his first principles, but also have a true conception of those principles themselves. Hence Wisdom must be a combination of Intelligence and Scientific Knowledge: it must be a consummated knowledge of the most exalted objects. For it is absurd to think that Political Science or Prudence is the loftiest kind of knowledge, inasmuch as man is not the highest thing in the world. And as ‘wholesome’ and ‘good’ mean one thing for men and another for fishes, whereas ‘white’ and ‘straight’ mean the same thing always, so everybody would denote the same thing by ‘wise,’ but not by ‘prudent’; for each kind of beings will describe as prudent, and will entrust itself to, one who can discern its own particular welfare; hence even some of the lower animals are said to be prudent, namely those which display a capacity for forethought as regards their own lives."

The part I keep tripping on is that last contrast: he's willing to grant prudence to some lower animals, but 'wise' is supposed to mean the same thing for everybody the way 'white' does. So my question is about the sophia he's defining here, not our everyday word "wisdom." What's your actual credence that this thing — scientific knowledge plus intuitive intelligence of first principles, aimed at the most exalted objects — can be taught to a reasonably intelligent adult who doesn't already have it, by a well-designed multi-year course of study, in roughly the way we can teach real analysis or thermodynamics? By "taught" I mean that at the end a normal capable student demonstrably has it, not just has read about it.

Dont hide behind "it depends what you mean by wisdom." Use his definition, and flag only the places where the translation or your reading of the passage is doing real work. I want a number, then the decomposition. The sub-questions I can see are something like: (a) how likely is it that the "scientific knowledge" component is teachable in the ordinary way; (b) how likely is it that the "intuitive intelligence of first principles" component (nous) is teachable at all, rather than a native knack or something that only shows up after long experience; (c) how likely is it that the best reading of "combination" is conjunctive, so that missing the nous part means you flatly don't have sophia, which makes (b) the bottleneck; (d) how likely is it that the "most exalted objects" bit, on the best reading, just collapses sophia into theoretical physics, math, or theology, and how much that should discount the whole question for aiming at the wrong target. Give each a rough probability and a one-line justification, show how they combine into the final number, and then tell me which sub-question moves the final number most and what evidence or argument would shift it.
