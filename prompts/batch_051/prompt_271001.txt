Me and my friend just spent the whole afternoon arguing about whether guilt can actually be transferred from one person (or animal) to another, and I came out with a pile of verdicts but no principle. I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, is behind them — and which of my verdicts it forces me to drop.

- A company president resigns because of a scandal she didn't personally cause. I think that's basically theater, and a little unjust even if she agreed to be the face of it.
- Paying someone else's fine: fully fine.
- A mother volunteering to take a whipping meant for her son (in some old story): fine, maybe even noble.
- The ancient Israelite scapegoat, where the priest lays hands on a goat, confesses the people's sins over it, and drives it into the wilderness: fine as a ritual, but the goat isn't now guilty.
- An animal killed as a sacrifice to atone for someone's sin: I can't see the death as doing any moral cleaning.
- Christ being punished in place of sinners: I can't except it, even though it looks a lot like the mother case.
- A whole class kept in at recess because one kid broke the projector: not fine.
- Burning a politician in effigy after a lost election: not fine, and a bit creepy.

My friend kept poking the same spot: I accept one person paying another's fine, but not Christ standing in for sinners; I accept a scapegoat ritual, but not that the goat's death cleanses anyone. So what separates the substitutions I accept from the ones I don't?

Give me the best principle you can, stated precisely, tell me which of the above it can't accommodate, and apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on: someone confesses to a crime he didn't commit so the real culprit, who has young kids, won't go to prison.
