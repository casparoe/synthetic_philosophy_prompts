I'm in a linguistics-adjacent class where we've spent a week arguing about what counts as a grammatical rule without actually settling on a definition, and I'm hoping for something I can actually use. People throw around the word "rule" in very different cases, and I want one working definition that sorts them, not a dictionary definition.

The cases I want it to get right. (1) Regular English plurals: add "s," "z," or "iz" depending on the final sound (cats, dogs, horses). I'd be genuinely shocked if this isn't a rule. (2) go/went: the past and present don't share a stem at all. To me this is a memorized suppletive pair, not a rule—but I can't say what makes it different from a rule whose domain has exactly one item. (3) The i-before-e-except-after-c mnemonic. This seems clearly not a grammatical rule; it's a spelling heuristic with messy exceptions (weird, seize, species). But if rules are allowed to have exceptions, I need a principled reason this one doesn't count. (4) The -en plural in oxen, children, brethren. Is a pattern that covers three nouns a rule? It isnt productive (nobody says boxen except as a joke), but it's not a one-off either. (5) dived vs dove (or sneaked vs snuck). There's a regular past and a competing form that looks like it got built by analogy with drove/rode. Do two competing forms mean two rules, one rule with a listed exception, or no rule at all for this verb? (6) A historical sound change: Grimm's law turns Proto-Indo-European voiceless stops like p, t, k into fricatives in Germanic, and Verner's law adds that those fricatives voice instead when the old accent didn't fall on the immediately preceding syllable. Is that one rule with a principled exception, two rules, or something other than the kind of rule a speaker is supposed to have in their head?

Propose a working definition of "grammatical rule," run it through all six cases (or revise it as you go), and tell me which of my judgments you'd overturn and which of your own verdicts feels least secure. If the right answer is that the term is doing several different jobs, I'm open to that, but I'd still want to know which of the cases are using it honestly.
