I'm a software engineer who works on a small multiplayer game, and over the last few years I've ended up with a bunch of strong feelings about when something "in a game" is a real loss or a real wrong. They feel pretty coherent to me but I can't state the rule behind them, and a couple of my co-workers just say "it's all just data," which I don't buy. I'm hoping you can find the principle behind these verdicts, or tell me which ones I have to give up. No need for citations or anything, I can fill that in later; I just want the reasoning.

Here are the cases:

1. An old-school MMO has a mount that only drops once every few months. A guildmate spent the better part of a year farming the spawn and finally got it. A botched dev rollback reverted his account to before the drop, and support said in-game items aren't property and they don't restore luck-based drops. To me this is a real loss. Not just "he lost the hours" -- the mount itself mattered, and losing it after having it is different from never getting it.

2. Another friend bought the exact same mount from a gold-farming site with real money. A month later his account got banned over a billing mix-up and he lost everything. I feel like he lost money, but I don't feel like he lost the mount in the same way. That seems inconsistent even to me.

3. On a survival server I used to run, a griefer blew up a bridge that a small group of builders had spent about 60 combined hours on. The bridge was in a shared world and people used it constantly. I think the builders were genuinely harmed, even though the game's rules allowed the griefing and the bridge could in principle be rebuilt. The harm isn't just the hours -- it's the destruction of the thing in the world.

4. In a single-player game, I was genuinely attached to a horse I'd named and ridden for a hundred hours. A patch accidentally deleted all saves with horses acquired before a certain quest. I was sad, but I wouldn't say the developers harmed me in any serious way. I can't tell why this is so different from the bridge case, since the hours are similar.

5. In a social VR space, someone groped my partner's avatar while she was afk. Her actual body was never touched. I think this is a real harm, full stop -- and not because the avatar has intrinsic value; a real thing was done to a real person. But that move seems like it would make case 4 a harm too, which I don't want to say.

6. A private server owner wiped a world with no warning. The ToS said he could. A friend lost a castle he'd build over six months. I think he was harmed even though the owner was within his rights. So "legal" and "real" come apart for me.

Is there one principle about when virtual objects or virtual actions are real that gives all six verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts is doing the most damage and should be dropped? And run the principle on a case I haven't listed: someone uses a dupe bug to copy a rare item so the original still exists but is now worthless. What would it say there?
