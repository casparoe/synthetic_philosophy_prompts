I teach an upper-level undergraduate metaethics seminar and am setting the timed final essay (50 minutes, 20 marks). I want one question on the relationship between Hume's is-ought gap and Moore's naturalistic fallacy that cannot be answered by reciting the slogan that no ought can be derived from an is, or that Moore held good to be indefinable. The question should force students to work through whether the two theses stand or fall together. In particular, it should make them assess an inference of this shape: from the claim that good cannot be defined in natural terms, to the conclusion that no set of purely descriptive premises entails a moral conclusion. It should also force them to face the converse: if Moore's indefinability claim were dropped, would the is-ought gap automatically close?

Please produce three things:

(1) The exam question itself, worth 20 marks, suitable for 50 minutes, phrased so that a candidate cannot pass by summarizing Hume and Moore separately.

(2) A model answer of roughly 700–800 words.

(3) A marking scheme that names five must-hit points, the marks each is worth (out of 20), and a one-line note on how weaker candidates typically get each point wrong.

The crux the marking scheme should reward is this. The is-ought gap is an inference-barrier thesis: no valid derivation of a normative conclusion from wholly non-normative premises goes through without an already normative premise or a bridge principle. Moore's naturalistic fallacy is, by contrast, a definability thesis about good: any proposed reduction of good to a natural (or, for that version of the argument, any other) property leaves the question whether the thing so defined is really good open. These are distinct claims, and accepting or rejecting one does not by itself settle the other. The model answer should map at least three positions in the resulting logical space: a Moorean non-naturalist who accepts both; an expressivist who accepts the gap but rejects Moore's non-naturalist explanation of it; and a reductive naturalist who rejects Moore's indefinability claim and either closes the gap or accepts only a trivial logical gap while denying a metaphysical one. It should also note that even if good were definable in natural terms, the definition or property identity would still have to do inferential work among the premises—so the derivability question is about whether the right premise is available, not merely about whether a definition is available. Weaker answers will treat the two theses as synonyms, credit Moore with having proved Hume's point, or run together indefinable, non-natural, and nonderivable. The marking scheme should therefore separate answers that merely reproduce the two slogans from answers that can map the logical space.
