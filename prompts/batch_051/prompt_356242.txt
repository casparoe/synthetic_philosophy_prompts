I'm helping a friend design a typeface and we keep having the same fight about what makes every 'a' an 'a.' She points at a single-storey 'a' (like in many sans-serifs) and a double-storey 'a' (like in a lot of serif faces) and says no definition captures both unless it's so vague it also captures 'd' in some weird display font. I used to try to fix this by listing necessary-and-sufficient features, but lately I've been applyng a rule I picked up from Peirce's How to Make Our Ideas Clear. Peirce writes, "Nothing new can ever be learned by analyzing definitions." So now, when a glyph gets disputed, I refuse to start with a definition of the letter. Instead I ask: what difference could it make, for someone learning to read, copy, or render the character, if we classify this mark as an 'a' rather than as something else? If no difference, I say the question is empty; if there is one, we test those practices.

My friend says this is just a bias against definitions dressed up as a principle. She thinks letterforms have an identity you can see directly, and that my Peirce rule would make category membership too dependent on practical context—like whether the shape is an 'a' in a logo, in a dyslexia-friendly face, or in handwriting. She also says Peirce didn't mean that about ordinary objects like letters, since practical effects only work for "concepts" used in inquiry, not for perceptual kinds.

Act as a philosopher of categories and a Peirce scholar. Before giving your verdict, briefly brainstorm two or three ways one might distinguish definitional analysis from the practical-effect test I am using. Then tell me whether my rule is a defensible epistemic policy for letterform categories, or whether I am using Peirce to avoid the hard part. If the rule is too crude, is there a restated version that keeps what is useful about it without making letter identity purely context-dependent? And does the quote even support my use of it, or am I ripping it out of context?
