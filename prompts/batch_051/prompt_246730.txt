I keep coming back to a parallel between evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) and Kant's categorical imperative, and I can't decide wether it's a deep structural identity or just two ideas that both gesture at "act as if everyone relevant did what you're doing." You're a philosopher comfortable with both Kantian ethics and formal decision theory—please think carefully, start by stating the mapping as precisely as you can, and then stress-test it instead of being polite about it.

The ECL idea, as I understand it: in a big enough universe there will be lots of agents whose decision procedures are correlated with yours—copies, near-copies, or just agents who reason in a sufficiently similar way. Under evidential decision theory, your act is evidence about what those similar agents do. So in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with a copy, you cooperate because your cooperating is evidence that the copy cooperates, and the usual off-the-shelf logic for "defect if you can't affect the other player" stops applying. Kant's formula of universal law, on the surface, runs the same move: evaluate a maxim by imagining it generalized across all rational beings in your situation, and act only on the maxim you could will in that generalized form.

The mapping I have in mind: the correlated similar agents in ECL play the role of all rational beings under the universalization test; EDT's conditioning on your own choice plays the role of willing the maxim as universal law; and the criterion of what you would want the copies to do plays the role of what you can will without contradiction.

So here's the question. Is the categorical imperative basically evidential cooperation with the class of rational agents, where the "payoff" being maximized is the internal coherence of the will? Or does the analogy come apart because Kant's test is about contradictions in conception and in will rather than about expected utility, and about acting from duty rather than from self-interest? If it's a real identity of structure, what does the decision-theoretic version add—a proof that the correlation is rational, a measure over "similar agents," a way of handling degrees of similarity? If it's superficial, can you name something specific the parallel predicts on one side that comes out false on the other? The one I keep chewing on: ECL predicts that what a rational agent ought to do depends on how many agents are actually correlated with her and how strongly, whereas Kant's test is supposed to be insensitive to any such census of the universe. Is that the crack, or is that just ECL doing what any application of a formal moral theory has to do—bringing the ideal down into a world that has actual populations?
