I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on a fight with my editor about a house style rule. The press uses a style guide from the late 1970s, and one clause reads:

"In all copy, a pronoun that refers to a person of unspecified sex shall be masculine: he, him, his. The locutions 'he or she' and 'he/she' are not permitted, as they are clumsy and to be avoided."

My current novel has a character, Alix, whose sex is never given. Its a deliberate formal choice: the narrator refers to Alix as "they" for the whole book, and the reader is supposed to notice their own assumptions about which details they fill in. My editor says the clause covers Alix, because on the page Alix is a person of unspecified sex, so the rule requires "he." I think the rule was written for generic, hypothetical persons -- "a student should hand in his paper" -- not for a particular fictional person with a name and a story; Alix is a specific person whose pronoun is "they." Ive also been rereading Hofstadter's Person Paper, and it's making me suspicious of the editor's main move. The editor keeps saying "the masculine is purely generic; nobody actually pictures a man." The satire works by making you sit with exactly that move and notice how much it loads in by default, so I don't trust myself to separate the concept from the reflexive defensiveness.

What I'd like from you is to apply the standard ways of reading a rule to this case and say which one wins. Structure the response in this order, with rough lengths:

1. Plain meaning (about 150 words): does the text of the clause literally reach Alix, or not?
2. Original purpose (about 150 words): what problem was this rule aimed at, and does that purpose genuinely extend to a character whose gender is deliberately withheld?
3. The drafters teleported (about 100 words): if I could put this manuscript in front of them in 1979, what would they most likely say, and is that the same as the plain meaning?
4. Your ruling (about 150 words): which reading should govern here? Does anything change if we notice the rule rests on a default assumption it never states?

I'm less interested in a legal answer than in the conceptual mechanics: how far does wording carry a rule when the case is one its drafters couldn't have pictured?
