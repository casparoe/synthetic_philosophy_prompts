I've ended up holding a position in the suffering-focused family — call it a lexical-threshold negative consequentialism, though I don't love the label. It has a lexical threshold: below the threshold, trade-offs between reducing pain and promoting pleasure are handled the ordinary consequentialist way, maybe with pain weighted somewhat more heavily; but once the suffering is extreme enough, preventing even a small amount of it outweighs any amount of promoting happiness or averting milder bads. And I accept something like the Benatar-style asymmetry about creating people: a possible child's future suffering counts as a reason not to bring them into existence, but the happiness they would have had does not count as a reason to bring them into existence — that absence isn't a loss in the relevant sense. So the view is negative in its foundations without being merely "pain weighs a lot."

I know the standard objections, so I don't need them listed. What I want is a map of what would count against this position: what would have to be true, or what case would have to be described, for me to actually revise it. Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and distinguish what a committed defender of the view would themselves admit is pressure from what only critics say. At minimum I want: the world-exploder conclusion, the pinprick argument relocated to the threshold (why should one person's brief extreme pain outweigh an astronomical amount of mild happiness, and can the threshold be drawn non-arbitrarily?), the worry that the view has its own repugnant conclusion (a joyless, nearly pain-free world outranking a flourishing world with slightly worse but still not-extreme suffering), and whether the creation asymmetry is doing independent work or is just a patch on the axiology. Then be honest about whether the view, as I've stated it, still risks anything, or whether its been insulated so much that no possible case or observation could really move it — and if so, whether that's a problem for a view like this. I'd be most moved by concrete places where it makes a surprising call: large-scale intervention in wild-animal suffering, or whether to bring a happy child into a world where they'll eventually suffer badly. Tell me what the rival view says there, and which way the pressure should push.
