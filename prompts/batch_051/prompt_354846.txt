I sit on the editorial committee of an ancient history journal, and over the years our committee has accumulated a set of de facto precedents about where legitimate revisionism ends and pseudohistory begins. We have classified as pseudohistory the following: a manuscript claiming that Atlantis was a real, technologically sophisticated civilization whose destruction is accurately recorded in Plato and whose archaeological traces are absent because the catastrophe eliminated them and later institutions concealed the remainder; a manuscript claiming that the Great Pyramid and comparable monuments must have been built under extraterrestrial instruction, since no known human engineering of the relevant period could account for them, and that mainstream Egyptology systematically explains this evidence away; and a work in the style of Fomenko's new chronology, which maintains that several centuries of the standard historical timeline are phantom fabrications imposed by a conspiracy of later scholars, and which absorbs every apparent inconsistency by recalibrating the proposed scheme. In the same period, we declined to classify as pseudohistory: a manuscript arguing for pre-Columbian contact between Polynesia and South America on the strength of the distribution of the sweet potato and a few disputed linguistic correspondences, which we called speculative but legitimate because the author specified which discoveries would settle the question and conceded that the current evidence is thin; and a proposal that the Library of Alexandria declined through piecemeal neglect rather than a single catastrophic fire, which we treated as ordinary scholarly disagreement because it argued from the documentary record and could be wrong on ordinary historical grounds.

We now have a new submission to rule on. It argues that a sophisticated maritime civilization existed before the end of the last ice age, that it was destroyed by the abrupt cooling of the Younger Dryas, and that its survivors seeded architectural and astronomical knowledge across the globe, which would explain recurring similarities among widely separated ancient monuments. It points to Göbekli Tepe as a surviving trace of that world, and it explains the scarcity of direct evidence by noting that the proposed settlements would now lie on submerged continental shelves that remain largely unsurveyed; it also complains that funding for underwater survey has been hard to obtain. Unlike the earlier Atlantis submission, the author identifies specific findings that would refute the migration scenario — for example, certain results from excavation of submerged coastal sites or from genetic analysis of certain early populations — and explicitly allows that some of the cross-cultural monument similarities may be coincidental.

What should we decide if we want to remain consistent with our own track record, and which of our earlier rulings is the closest analog? If our precedents can be read as reflecting more than one defensible principle, say what principle we would be committing ourselves to under each available verdict, and describe the sort of future submission that would most cleanly force us to choose between those principles.
