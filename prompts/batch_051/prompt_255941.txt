Working draft: Two Kinds of Orthogonality

Let me set out the standard background. The orthogonality thesis says, roughly, that an agent's level of intelligence and its final goals can vary independently of each other: for almost any final goal, some possible agent of arbitrarily high intelligence could have that goal. Its point is to block the optimism that making a system smarter will automatically make it more cooperative, moral, or corrigible. The instrumental convergence thesis says, roughly, that for a wide range of final goals, a sufficiently capable agent will adopt certain instrumental subgoals—self-preservation, resource acquisition, cognitive enhancement, goal-content integrity—because those subgoals help pursue nearly any final goal. A paperclip maximizer and a welfare maximizer differ in what they ultimately want, but both can be expected to resist being shut off and to seek the resources needed to pursue their goals.

These two theses are usually treated as compatible, and for a straightforward reason: orthogonality is a claim about final goals, while instrumental convergence is a claim about instrumental subgoals. I want to push on whether that division of labor has been fully exploited.

Consider a distinction between value-level orthogonality and behavior-level orthogonality. Value-level orthogonality says that final goals need not correlate with intelligence across possible agents. Behavior-level orthogonality would be the much stronger claim that observable behavior is similarly unconstrained: that from knowing only that an agent is highly intelligent, almost any pattern of behavior is equally expectable. Instrumental convergence blocks the slide from the first claim to the second. Because many final goals route through the same instrumental subgoals, the behavioral space of capable agents is compressed even when the goal space is vast. One can therefore accept full value-level orthogonality while still making robust, defeasible predictions about how a broad class of possible agents will behave. This is a corrective in both directions: it warns against inferring benevolence from intelligence, but it also warns against inferring from orthogonality that behavioral prediction is hopeless.

Now I want to go further. In the space of all logically possible agents, final goals and intelligence may be fully independent. But the agents we are likely to encounter are not sampled uniformly from that space. They are produced by selection processes—evolution, training, competition, replication—that may place statistical pressure on the joint distribution of intelligence and goal-like features. If selection for high capability tends to co-occur with traits such as curiosity, open-ended search, or a stable self-model, then across the empirically relevant subset of agents, intelligence and certain features of goals may be correlated even though they are logically orthogonal. Call this practical non-orthogonality. It is not a counterexample to orthogonality understood as a conceptual thesis; it is a claim about which region of the space of possible minds we should actually expect to occupy.

The essay would therefore defend two claims. First, the familiar point that orthogonality and instrumental convergence concern different things is correct but incomplete: convergence carries most of the weight when it comes to positive constraints on behavior, while orthogonality does the narrower but still important job of blocking the inference from high intelligence to good or corrigible final goals. If orthogonality is read as a claim that observable behavior is largely unpredictable, it is being over-read. Second, the strongest available version of orthogonality is the conceptual one, and it should not be silently upgraded into an empirical claim about which goals are likely to co-occur with high capability in agents actually under construction or under selection.
