I'm on the board of a small family foundation, and this year we're stuck on a decision that has made the far future weirdly personal: whether to keep the money in near-term grants (malaria, education, that sort of thing) or start directing a real chunk toward causes that mostly pay off only if the long-run future goes well. The thing that keeps disrupting our conversations is the simulation argument. If we might be living in a simulation, does that actually dampen far-future fanaticism, the tendency to let astronomically large numbers of possible future beings swamp all the near-term considerations? I've collected the standard reasons for thinking it does dampen it, and I'd like one more. First, the truncated-simulation point: conditional on being simulated, we shouldn't assume this world contains an astronomical far future at all, since many simulations plausibly cover only a narrow window, a single life, or one historical period, so the expected future population inside the simulation may be much smaller than longtermist back-of-the-envelope numbers assume. Second, the steering point: the simulators are agents with their own aims, and they can tweak, reset, or terminate the run, so the long-run trajectory may be insensitive to my marginal choices from inside. Third, the value point: even where a simulated far future exists, there's real uncertainty about whether simulated welfare and base-reality welfare belong in the same moral currency, or about whether the simulators' criteria track maximizing the wellbeing of simulated descendants, so the value at stake is less determinate than the naive calculation suggests. I want a fourth dampener that isn't on that list and isn't a rewording, special case, or combination of those three, something I probably havent thought of. Then tell me honestly whether it's any good, and whether it should actually shift how we allocate.
