Ordinary states treat presence within their borders as legally decisive: if you enter, reside, own property, or even pass through on a public highway, you become subject to the state's criminal law, many of its taxes, and its administrative requirements, regardless of whether you ever announced any intention to join. In section 119 of the Second Treatise of Government, John Locke gives an early philosophical statement of this practice: he contends that whoever enjoys any part of a government's dominions—whether by holding land, renting a room for a week, or merely traveling on the highway—thereby gives tacit consent and is obliged to obey its laws for as long as that enjoyment continues. Private cities, charter cities, and seasteading projects seem to invert this logic: they are designed around choice among competing providers of law and services, with membership, contract, or the ability to exit doing the work that territorial presence does in an ordinary state. What theory of consent and political obligation is presupposed by the ordinary practice that Locke describes and that states still use? Is section 119 best read as articulating that theory, testing it, or revealing an uncomfortable consequence in it? And what would a private city or seastead have to assume, institutionally and philosophically, if it wanted to escape Locke's principle rather than merely relocate it? Take a definite position: reconstruct the implicit theory, judge whether it is coherent, and say which institutional changes would follow from accepting or rejecting it.
