I'm a science journalist working on a longread about how the Hiroshima and Nagasaki decisions look if you start not from the usual invasion-versus-bomb counterfactual but from the Franck Report — the June 1945 memo from scientists at the University of Chicago's Metallurgical Lab, mostly nuclear physicists and chemists, urging the government to stage a demonstration of the weapon in an uninhabited or barren place before dropping it without warning on Japan. Their core prediction, in my paraphrase, is that a surprise attack would produce a wave of global revulsion, wreck the credibility needed to create international control of atomic energy, and set off an avoidable arms race; the report treats that as a moral and political cost that must be counted alongside the lives a quick end to the war would save.

I'd like you to act as a philosopher who specializes in just war theory and the ethics of emerging technology, and think through this slowly, brainstorming before you commit. Use the Franck Report's prediction as a lever to pull apart the just war criteria of last resort and proportionality, and also the doctrine of double effect. Specifically: does the report offer a genuine third option between invasion and the bomb, and what does last resort even mean when one of the options is an unprecedented weapon whose effects are uncertain? Analyze the actual bombings through the distinction between killing civilians as a means versus as a foreseen side effect — where does that distinction do real work here, and where does it break? Then bring in the "supreme emergency" line of thinking often associated with Michael Walzer and a stricter noncombatant absolutism, and say what each would make of the scientists' claim that using the bomb would undermine the moral foundations of future arms control. Finally, tell me what this episode exposes about just war theory itself: is it better at retrospective judgment, prospective advice, or neither? Don't paper over the places where the theory has to strain to fit.
