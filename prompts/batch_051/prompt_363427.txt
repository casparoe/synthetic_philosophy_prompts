I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I've gotten myself into the weeds for a character. My protagonist is a law student who starts applying "what if everyone did that?" to everything—lying to a neighbor, not voting, buying fast fashion, calling in sick for a mental-health day—and I want the book to show why the test felt so powerful to her and where it starts to mislead. In my research I keep seeing people explain universalizability through a physical-law analogy: you test a maxim by asking what the world would look like if the maxim were a law of nature, the way you'd check whether a proposed law of physics is consistent with the rest of the universe. The analogy is doing real work in the arguments I'm reading: people use it to say lying or promise-breaking fails because the corresponding "laws" undermine themselves, while something like everyone staying home from an election just makes a shabbier world but isn't self-contradictory. I need an audit of that analogy. What corresponds to what, where the mapping breaks, what the law-of-nature image quietly imports that the moral test doesn't actually have, and whether the conclusions people license with it survive once the disanalogies are on the table. I'm especially interested in whether Hare's merely formal universalizability and Singer's generalization argument treat the analogy differently than Kant does in the Groundwork. Also, please tell me where your verdict is most likely to fail: which consideration about the difference between Kant's law-of-nature formula and an actual law of nature, or about Hare and Singer, would change your mind if it went the other way.
