I hold a broadly Dennettian view about the ontology of the special sciences. The things that psychology, biology, and economics quantify over — beliefs and desires, selection pressures, functions, market equilibria, centers of gravity — are real in the way a pattern in noisy data is real: they exist where a shorter description of the data is worth having, where the compression pays for itself, and they are not less real for being partly a matter of what we are trying to predict. The three stances — physical, design, and intentional — are, on this picture, three instruments for detecting such patterns at different levels of abstraction, and none of them gives a more real account of the world than the others; they differ in cost, precision, and predictive leverage, not in ontological rank.

I am drawn to this view, but I am also aware that it has been accused of being instrumentalism with the word real attached, and I am not sure that accusation is empty. So I would like an honest assessment of how exposed the position actually is. What would have to be true for it to be wrong, or to collapse into a rival it claims to improve on?

I want the answer organized as a ranked survey of defeaters, from fatal to merely awkward, marking where the view takes genuine risks — what it predicts about particular cases that rival positions predict differently — as against where it can be adjusted afterward to accommodate anything. I would like you to address at least these questions: whether there are cases in which a stance reliably predicts but the pattern is nonetheless not real in any sense worth wanting; whether the compressibility criterion smuggles in the very notion of real it was meant to explain; and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that is a defect here or a defensible modesty about what an ontological claim can be. I also want the objections a committed real-patterns theorist would themselves concede count against the position separated from the ones they would dismiss as question-begging, such as objections that simply help themselves to a richer, intrinsic notion of what it is to be real. Finally, if the strongest challenge comes not from outright eliminativism but from a nearby position — a realism about patterns combined with a fictionalism about the content the intentional stance ascribes, say — I would like to know why that position is or is not genuinely distinct.
