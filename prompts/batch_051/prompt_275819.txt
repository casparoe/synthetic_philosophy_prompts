I'm on the elections commission of a mid-sized charter city in California, and we've spent three meetings stuck on a ballot-measure provision our new clerk wants to modernize. Section 41 of the charter, adopted in 1932, reads: "Before any measure shall be submitted to the electors of the City, the Clerk shall cause to be delivered to each registered elector a pamphlet containing the full text of such measure and a statement of the arguments for and against the same, presented with equal prominence." The clerk has contracted with a vendor to replace the single mailed pamphlet with a web portal for the March bond measure. Every voter who logs in sees the full text, but the "arguments for and against" are generated on the fly by a model that picks examples, framing, and which arguments to feature based on the voter's ZIP code, party registration, and what they clicked on the city's website. The for and against sections are always the same length, and the vendor says that's all "equal prominence" can mean once you stop printing paper. I tested the portal with two dummy voters from different neighborhoods; one got a "for" case built around street repaving and an "against" case built around "some residents have raised concerns about borrowing costs," while the other got roughly the reverse. No two voters get the same statement, and no single human-reviewed version of it exists anywhere. Half the commission thinks this guts the clause; the other half and the clerk think it's fulfilling it better than the 1932 authors could have imagined, since almost nobody actually read the old pamphlet.

I've been asked to draft the commission's opinion on wether this violates Section 41 and, if so, what the remedy should be (back to one fixed mailed or PDF document, a human-approved common "arguments for and against" with only harmless personalization at the edges, or something else). Walk me through what the textual, purposivist, and original-intent readings each yield, plus what a reasonable voter reading the charter in 1932 would have thought they were promised and what a reasonable voter today would understand it to require, then tell me which approach should control and why. Steelman the losing side rather than the weakest version of it. And keep it in continuous prose only, like an actual memo: no headings, bullets, bold, or tables.
