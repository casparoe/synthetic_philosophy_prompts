Consider the following pair of events.

A mine's ventilation fan stops during the night. In the first version, the mine employs a safety inspector whose only task is to check that fan before the workers enter each morning. On the fatal morning she skips the check without excuse, although a routine inspection would have found the failure and a five-minute repair would have prevented the explosion that kills several miners later that day. We call her omission a cause of the deaths, and we blame her.

In the second version, everything is the same, except that the person who notices the silent fan is a hiker passing the mine. The hiker understands mine ventilation, sees that the fan has stopped, knows this is dangerous, and could phone the mine office at no risk or cost to herself. She does nothing, and the miners die. We may find her callous, but most people would not say her silence caused the deaths, and few would blame her anywhere near as much as the inspector.

The two failures are physically matched: in both cases, had the person acted, the deaths would have been prevented. The only evident difference is that one omission breaks an undertaken role or duty, while the other does not. Yet our causal language—not merely our moral language—treats the two omissions very differently.

Is that asymmetry justified? Or is "causation by omission" really a moral or practical judgment wearing causal clothing: we call an absence a cause when we already hold someone to a standard, and withhold the word "cause" when we do not?

Please structure your answer exactly as follows.

1. The asymmetry (about 150 words). State precisely where the two cases come apart in ordinary causal judgment, and note which facts are held constant across them—especially the counterfactual facts.

2. The strongest justifications (about 500 words). Present the two or three most plausible principles that would make the inspector's omission a cause while denying that the hiker's was: for example, that the omission breached an undertaken role, that it deviated from a "normal conditions" baseline, or something else you think stronger. State the hidden costs of each principle.

3. Stress tests (about 400 words). Apply each candidate principle to the variants below, and to at least one variant of your own construction, then say which principle best tracks the judgments you would actually defend.

- The hiker is a mining engineer who was employed by the mine until being fired the previous day; she knows exactly what the inspector knows.
- The inspector is off duty and visiting a relative at the mine; she notices the silent fan, knows the risk, and could call at no cost.
- The fan is monitored by an automated system whose software silently skips the alert; engineers later describe the software failure as a cause of the explosion, though the system had no duties in the moral sense.
- Two inspectors share the morning check and each independently skips it. Do both omissions count as causes, or the pair as a single cause, or neither?

4. Verdict (about 250 words). Say whether this asymmetry in causal talk about omissions should be kept, revised, or abandoned, and sharply distinguish the causal question from the question of moral and legal responsibility. Do not introduce real statutes, case names, or citations; the argument should stand on the cases themselves.
