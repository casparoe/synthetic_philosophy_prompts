Right, so I've just spent the whole evening arguing with a mate about moral responsibility and free will, and we kept circling round a case that neither of us would settle, so I want a proper take.

My starting position was this: you can only be morally responsible for something you did if, at the time, you could have done otherwise. That seems almost tautalogical to me — if the world was set up so you literally had no alternative, blaming you feels wrong.

The case my mate threw at me (he'd read about this kind of thing) goes like this. Jones is going to vote in a by-election. A neuroscientist, Black, has secretly implanted a chip in Jones's brain that can detect what he is about to decide and, if it reads that Jones is going to vote Green, will override him and make him vote Labour instead. In the actual run of events, Jones decides completely on his own to vote Labour, and the chip never activates. So his vote is the product of his own ordinary deliberation. But the one alternative that seems morally relevant — choosing to vote Green — was not genuinely open to him, because the chip would have shut it down.

My friend then made it worse by pointing out that Jones could still have spoiled his ballot or stayed home, since the chip only blocks the Green option. So in a thin sense he had alternatives, just not the one that matters. Does that save my principle, or does it just show that "could have done otherwise" only works if you say which alternatives count?

So really three questions. Is Jones morally responsible for voting Labour? If he is, does that show that "could have done otherwise" isn't actually neccessary for responsibility? And if I have to give it up or restrict it, what should replace it — what is it about the actual sequence of events that makes him responsible, if indeed he is?
