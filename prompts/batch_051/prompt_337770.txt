I am in a course on practical rationality, and we have just finished a unit on Buridan's ass and the problem of acting without preference. I have been recording my own verdicts about particular cases, and I would like to know what principle, if any, captures them.

(i) Before me are two glasses of water identical in all relevant respects, and I take the left one without deliberating. This seems fine.

(ii) I cannot decide between two equally appealing restaurants, so I flip a coin and am content with the result. Fine.

(iii) A donor has settled on two charities of exactly equal cost-effectiveness, yet spends weeks unable to give because no reason favors either. I want to say this is a rational failure, not merely a quirk; she should simply pick one and give.

(iv) A voter finds that the two candidates on the ballot are, as far as she can determine, identical on every matter she cares about, and she abstains. I am inclined to say abstaining is perfectly fine, perhaps even the appropriate response.

(v) Offered a higher-paying job in a dull town and a lower-paying job in a city I love, I take the second because of the city. This feels like a genuine choice for a reason, not an arbitrary selection among equals.

(vi) Offered chocolate and vanilla and liking both equally, I simply pick chocolate. Fine; but someone who refused dessert altogether because there was no reason to prefer either would seem to me to be making a small rational mistake.

I have been working with the tentative principle that picking and choosing are different responses: choosing answers to a reason that favors one option, while picking is the permitted way of resolving a tie where no such reason exists. But (iii) and (vi) make me wonder whether picking can itself be required, and (iv) makes me wonder whether declining to pick can itself be a permissible response to the tie. Can you offer a precise principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures these verdicts, say what it implies about a further case—a donor who asks someone else to decide which of the two equally good charities should receive her money so that she need not pick—and tell me plainly which of my verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to give up?
