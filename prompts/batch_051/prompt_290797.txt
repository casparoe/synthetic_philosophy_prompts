I would like help finding out what set of commitments about future truth my own verdicts are pointing toward. Here are some cases with my intuitive reactions; I am not sure they are consistent with one another.

1. The Aristotelian sea battle. Tomorrow there either will be a naval battle in the strait, or there won't be. I accept the disjunction as true today, and as true purely from logic. But I am uneasy saying that one of its two disjuncts is already true today. The particular claim "There will be a sea battle tomorrow" seems to lack the kind of mooring that would make it true or false now.

2. The past hidden coin. A coin was flipped by a machine last night and now lies hidden under a cup. I think "The coin came up heads" is determinately true or false right now, even though nobody knows which. The coin's having landed is, to me, sufficient for there to be a fact about how it landed.

3. After-the-fact backdating. Suppose I say in the morning, "It will rain here this afternoon," and then rain comes. In the evening I am willing to say, "What I said this morning was true." But I am reluctant to spell that as "There was already, at the moment I said it, a fact about the future that made my sentence true." The first statement seems natural; the second seems to smuggle in a false picture of the future as already existing.

4. Stability over time. If "There will be rain tomorrow" was true yesterday, I do not think that truth can later have been "made" true by today's rain. Truth should not change retrospectively merely because the world catches up. But I do think the world can, so to speak, settle things.

5. A yet-unknown measurement. For the exact depth of water at a harbor at noon tomorrow, I think "At noon tomorrow, the depth will be greater than 10 meters" is not just unknown but not yet settled. I am happy attributing a probability, but unhappy attributing truth or falsity to it in a God's-eye sense.

6. Free action. If a reliable predictor says "You will choose tea, not coffee," and I later freely choose tea, I do not want to say that the prediction was true all along in a way that removed my alternative possibility. I can call it a successful prediction, but I resist saying the truth of my future action pre-existed the choice.

My provisional principle is something like this: propositions about the future have truth values only when, and to the extent that, present facts suffice to make them true, or the proposition is a logical consequence of something already settled; otherwise they are neither true nor false. But this principle seems to break on at least cases 1 and 3: logic alone gives truth to the disjunction, and ordinary retrospective ways of speaking like "It was true this morning that it would rain" do not require present facts.

So my question is: is there a single, well-motivated theory of future-contingent truth that preserves as many of my verdicts as possible? State the leading candidate principles precisely; indicate which of my verdicts each one must reject or reinterpret; and work out what each principle would say about two further cases: (a) a future-tensed sentence about an event 100 years from now, under deterministic laws but with no one around to utter it; and (b) the past-tensed statement "It was already true last week that this coin would land heads," made after a genuinely chancy coin toss. Finally, separate what is settled in the debate over future contingents from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
