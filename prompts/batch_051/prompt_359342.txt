You are a philosopher specialising in anthropic reasoning and formal epistemology, engaged as an advisor to a foundation that funds conceptual research on decision-making under observation selection effects. The foundation can support three researchers for two years. The trustees want a research agenda: not a summary of positions in the field and not a verdict on which positions are true, but a ranking of the open questions most worth trying to settle with those resources.

Before you commit to a ranking, spend a brainstorming pass over the candidate questions. Your pass should include, though it need not stop at, the following:

- The choice between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption as the default rule for updating on indexical information, especially the fact that you exist as a particular observer at all. On the former, roughly, you should reason as if you were a random sample from all actual observers in a suitable reference class; on the latter, roughly, you should reason as if you were a random sample from all possible observers, so that your own existence counts as evidence favouring hypotheses on which observers are more numerous.
- What the presumptuous philosopher thought experiment actually shows: when two otherwise equally well-supported theories differ only in how many observers they imply, is there any legitimate basis for confidently favouring the theory with vastly more observers, and is that conclusion best read as a reductio of the self-indication assumption, an acceptable consequence, or something else?
- How to choose a reference class, whether that choice is a normative stance or an empirical claim, and whether the reference-class problem can be settled independently of the two assumptions above.
- Whether observation selection effects matter only in exotic cosmological settings or also in everyday forecasting and in cause prioritisation, where the number of morally relevant beings might depend on one's choices.
- How anthropic reasoning should be integrated with decision theory once the number of future agents is itself choice-dependent.

Then select three or four items, from this list or from your wider pass, and rank them. For each, explain why it matters for the foundation's downstream aims, how tractable it is within two years, how neglected it is relative to its importance, and which other questions depend on its answer. Also identify at least one tempting item you would deliberately set aside for now, and say what would have to change for you to place it first. The audience wants the reasoning behind the ordering: the criteria you are using, the trade-offs you are making, and what new evidence or results would rearrange the list. Please write your answer in British English.
