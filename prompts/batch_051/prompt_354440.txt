A forecasting outfit does long-horizon predictions about whether particular regimes and empires will survive. It's sick of analysts telling elaborate inside-view stories about the present situation, so it imposes this rule. Before each forecast, you must first pick a reference class from a shared menu of historical cases — contiguous land empires since 1200, revolutionary regimes that came to power by coup, hereditary oil monarchies, that sort of thing — and state the base rate of the outcome in that class over the relevant horizon. Then you give your own inside-view probability. The number you're actually scored on is a fixed blend of the two, say half and half. Separately, there's a second leaderboard for "class picking": you gain points when the base rate of the class you picked ended up closer to the truth than your inside-view number, and lose points in the reverse case. I want to know how this thing actually behaves, not whether it's a good idea in the abstract. What does it do to how forecasters choose reference classes, to what they report as their inside-view number, and to the final forecasts? Where does it get gamed first? Does it push people toward genuine outside-view reasoning, or toward optimizing the blend and the class-picking contest, or something else entirely? As you go, please seperate what is actually settled among forecasting researchers from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as if it were the field's consensus.

Structure it as: "The incentives in brief", "Reference-class selection", "The inside-view number", "Gaming and failure modes", "Settled vs. contested", then "Bottom line". Give each section a short paragraph or two — the whole answer maybe 700 to 1000 words.
