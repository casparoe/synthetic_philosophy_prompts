After a year of adversarial collaboration, a constitutional precommitment theorist and a present-majority democrat sit down to write a joint statement they can both sign about whether entrenched amendment rules are a legitimate collective Ulysses contract or an illegitimate dead hand. Write that statement for them. Make it textured enough that nobody who skipped the collaboration could have produced it, but keep both sides as strong as possible and fair to themselves.

Open with a numbered list of agreed claims. These should include, among other things: what both sides accept about the original Ulysses story as a model; which features a constitutional analogue would have to have before the analogy is even in play; and what both now think is off the table, such as reading the binding of future majorities as literally the same act of consent as the original vote. Each agreed claim has to say something substantive, not just paper over a lingering disagreement.

After that, produce a disagreements table. The columns should be: the exact issue; the precommitment camp's preferred statement of its own position; the present-majority camp's preferred statement of its own position; the crux (the narrower question that, if settled, would shift at least one side); and a test, case, or argument both sides accept as genuinely bearing on that crux. Somewhere in the table or just below it, record each side's prediction for each test in advance.

Anchor the whole document in a running example they both worked through. In 1990 a country adopts a constitution with an escalator clause: any amendment must pass two successive parliaments, each by at least 58%, and the escalator clause itself can only be amended through that same procedure. In 2024 a stable 61% majority wants to lower the threshold to 50% in a single parliament but cant get around the waiting period. The precommitment camp says this is the polity refusing to be untied from the mast. The present-majority camp says it is Ulysses tying his grandchildren to it.

Make the cruxes go past the standard talking points. Include at least these three: whether a later majority can revoke a precommitment without thereby showing it was never actually bound; whether the gradual replacement of the citizenry over time breaks the premise that the earlier and later majorities are the same agent; and whether the distinctive problem here comes from an amendment rule protecting itself, as opposed to a substantive clause being protected.
