I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have spent the past few weeks trying to make sense of the strange afterlife of Thomas Malthus. In debates about population, food, and planetary limits, I keep hearing him claimed by both sides. His defenders say he has been vindicated — that the past two centuries were a postponement rather than a refutation and that the underlying pressure of population on resources is now reasserting itself in a form he would recognize. His critics say the specific claim that population multiplies faster than the food supply can be made to grow was simply falsified, and that calling him "right but early" is a trick for keeping a failed long-run prediction alive forever. I would like to stop coasting on a vague hunch and assign an actual probability to a precise version of the dispute, decomposed enough to defend to an editor.

Here is the proposition I want a credence in. By "right but early" I mean the following: Malthus's central dynamic — that a human population tends to press against its means of subsistence and is held in check by rising mortality or by deliberate restraint of reproduction — describes a genuine and recurring tendency of human societies that has so far been postponed by technology and social change, rather than a failed generalization that is now surrounded by after-the-fact qualifications. Decompose your judgment into the sub-questions your number turns on. I would expect at least these to appear: what the central dynamic actually commits Malthus to and what it leaves open; whether a tendency can be real even if it never wins the historical contests; how much of the past two centuries of escape is explained by mechanisms internal to his account, such as delayed marriage and restraint, rather than by exogenous developments such as sustained technical change and falling fertility; and what would have counted as clean disconfirmation of the core claim. Give each sub-question a rough probability and a line or two of justification, then show how they combine into a final number. Tell me which component you are least confident in and what evidence would move it most. For calibration: my own private estimate is around 0.6, and a demographer I interviewed puts it at 0.2; I would also like you to say which sub-question most likely houses that gap.
