I am a novelist. I am drafting a story in which a character must decide whether it is permissible to delete an emulated animal, and I want the characters in the book to hold defensible, mutually inconsistent views about which processes matter morally. Over the past year I have been corresponding with a friend, Sasha, who is a philosopher of mind. You are a professional philosopher working on moral status and the boundaries of sentience. Please think carefully before answering; start by brainstorming the principles that could lie behind the verdicts, and resist settling on an obvious principle too early.

I have asked Sasha, for each case below, whether what is going on in the system matters for its own sake — that is, whether the system has morally relevant experience or can be wronged, rather than merely simulating experience or reacting. Her recorded verdicts:

1. A healthy adult human with a broken arm: yes.
2. A dog with a broken leg: yes.
3. A chicken confined in a battery cage: yes.
4. An octopus exploring its tank: yes.
5. A honeybee performing its waggle dance: no.
6. A jellyfish whose diffuse nerve net responds to touch: no.
7. A thermostat using a feedback loop to control a furnace: no.
8. A giant finite lookup table that, for every possible English input under one hundred words, returns exactly the sentence a particular human would utter, with no processing beyond table lookup: no.
9. A coarse software simulation of a mouse brain, built from fifty thousand simplified integrate-and-fire neurons, that learns to avoid noxious stimuli in a minimal virtual maze by reinforcement: no.
10. A human body kept metabolically alive after total brain death: no.

The new case I must extrapolate to is this: a whole-brain emulation of an adult dog, neuron-for-neuron and synapse-for-synapse, running in real time, connected to a detailed virtual body in a detailed virtual environment, with the dog's nociceptive, affective, and memory systems intact, behaving indistinguishably from the biological dog it was copied from.

What would Sasha say about the dog emulation?

In your answer, please do the following:

(a) State the two or three most plausible principles that, singly or in alternation, fit all of Sasha's recorded verdicts.
(b) Say which earlier cases each principle accounts for well and where each is strained.
(c) State what each principle predicts about the dog emulation.
(d) Give your best prediction of Sasha's verdict, and explain why you favor that principle over the others.
(e) Describe one further case, not already on the list, whose result would most cleanly discriminate between the leading principles, and explain why it would.

Where the record underdetermines Sasha's principle, say exactly where the underdetermination lies and what each resolution would imply.
