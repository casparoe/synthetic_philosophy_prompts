I am a novelist, and I am in the early stages of a book set in the early seventeenth century: a merchant in Amsterdam, a moneylender in Surat, a silver broker in Potosí, a minor official in Ming China. I want the book to make the connectedness of that world visible without quietly making any one place the center from which the others become legible. The more I read in the scholarship critical of Eurocentric history, the more I suspect that the interesting difficulty is not factual but conceptual. It seems to me that two different demands are being run together under the heading of decentering Europe. The first is to multiply standpoints: add more places, more actors, more sources, more points of view. The second is to alter the categories of narration themselves—the periodization, the background assumptions about what is an event, what counts as economic or religious or political, what counts as an explanation. These two demands may pull in different directions, because one can add many standpoints and still keep the frame fixed, or change the frame and leave a single standpoint in place.

The phrase "telling the past from everywhere at once" seems to me to hide the confusion. A history told from everywhere at once is not the sum of many located views; it is a view from no place, or from the impossible position of a narrator outside history. But if I settle for a history told from some particular place, have I not simply reinstated a center—arbitrary, residual, perhaps European after all? I am trying to work out whether this dilemma is genuine or whether it dissolves once we distinguish, for instance, between the origin of a question and the standpoint of a narrative, or between a center of narration and a center of explanation. It also seems to me that the very act of treating my four lives as contemporaries presupposes a single shared timeline, and I do not know whether that assumption is already a Eurocentric leftover or simply an unavoidable condition of writing narrative at all.

Do not lay out the positions and leave me to choose. I want you to commit: tell me what you think the real conceptual issue is, and then argue for a definite view of whether "the past told from everywhere at once" is a coherent ideal, an incoherent slogan, or a coherent but misleading way of naming something else. Give your reasons, and be explicit about where you think the alternative view goes wrong.
