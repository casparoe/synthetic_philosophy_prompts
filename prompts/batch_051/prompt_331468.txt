I am a machine-learning researcher who works on reward modeling and RLHF. I think comfortably in terms of objective functions, loss terms, reward components, Pareto frontiers, scalarization weights, preference data, KL-regularization, learned optimizers, and related constructs; I am fluent in that vocabulary and much less fluent in moral philosophy. My exposure to Sidgwick is roughly this: in The Methods of Ethics he treats both rational egoism — the principle that one has most reason to pursue one's own good on the whole — and the utilitarian principle of impartial benevolence — the principle that one has most reason to pursue the good of all from a standpoint that counts every individual's good equally — as first principles of practical reason; he finds that they can issue conflicting directives and that no further principle can adjudicate between them, a deadlock he calls the dualism of practical reason. Restate that problem in my vocabulary, argumentatively rather than metaphorically, and then tell me what does not survive the restatement.

Be specific rather than gestural. Map each element — the two principles, their conflict, their apparent self-evidence, and the failure of adjudication — onto the closest constructs in my vocabulary: two reward components or loss terms that must be combined, a Pareto frontier on which no point is canonical, a weighting that must be supplied from outside the system, an optimizer that still has to act, and preference data that underdetermine a single reward model. For each mapping, say where it is faithful and where it begins to distort.

Then give a residue report. What in Sidgwick's problem has no precise counterpart in my vocabulary, or can be carried over only with distortion? In particular, address these candidates: that each principle makes a claim on the agent rather than merely contributing a component to a vector-valued objective; that the failure is a failure of reason itself rather than the absence of an engineer willing to choose weights; and whether a social-choice or preference-aggregation theorem under impartiality could perform the scalarization, and what such a theorem would have to assume if it were to count as a solution rather than a relabeling of the problem.

End with a verdict on the residue: is it a defect in my vocabulary — something it would have to add before it could even see the problem correctly — or is it a sign that the dualism is partly an artifact of Sidgwick's framework, and that once the need for scalarization is made explicit the problem is less mysterious than he took it to be? Keep the translation honest: do not smuggle normative assumptions into the technical mapping, and do not let either verdict win by default.
