William P. Thurston, in his 1994 essay On Proof and Progress in Mathematics, writes: "For instance, when Appel and Haken completed a proof of the 4-color map theorem using a massive automatic computation, it evoked much controversy. I interpret the controversy as having little to do with doubt people had as to the veracity of the theorem or the correctness of the proof. Rather, it reflected a continuing desire for human understanding of a proof, in addition to knowledge that the theorem is true."

I want you to treat the claim compressed in that passage as a testable claim about mathematical cognition: that there is a real cognitive possession, human understanding of a proof, which can be present or absent independently of the ability to verify each inferential step and independently of the knowledge that the theorem is true. Design the strongest concrete test you can of that claim. Say exactly what comparison you would set up—for instance, among mathematicians who have only stepwise-verified a computer proof, mathematicians who have worked through a human proof, and mathematicians who have done both—and state what results would favor the claim, what results would favor the deflationary reading that the four-color controversy was only sociology or a preference for shorter and more checkable proofs, and what results would favor the reading that so-called understanding is just a feeling of fluency that tracks no real difference in epistemic state. Then say what your test would and would not settle: in particular, whether the extra thing Thurston is pointing to is epistemic or aesthetic, whether it reduces to the ability to transfer, modify, and explain the proof, and whether it is the sort of thing a machine could in principle have. Your answer should be continuous prose only, in the style of an email or a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
