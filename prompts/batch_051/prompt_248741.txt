I keep noticing a weird asymmetry in how people make rescuing choices, and I can't tell if there's a principled line behind it or just an inconsistancy we live with. If one person will die without some scarce resource, most people think it would be monstrous to spend that resource relieving a thousand people's mild headaches; the lesser harms just don't seem to count, however many of them there are. But if the choice is between one death and two other deaths, the numbers suddenly do all the work: we save the two. In between, the same people fudge it — one death versus a hundred broken arms, versus a thousand lost fingers, whatever. I want to know whether there's a defensible principle that tells us when numbers of smaller harms get to outweigh a single greater harm and when they don't. Give me the strongest version of that principle you can, test it against the awkward middle cases, and tell me honestly whether it holds up or whether the only real explanation is psychological. Keep it under 500 words.
