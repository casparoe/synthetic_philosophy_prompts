I am working through a disagreement about what we are praising when we praise someone for self-control. One side says self-control is a moral achievement in its own right; another says it is merely prudent management of desires that have not been brought into good order; a third says the need for constant self-control is itself a sign that something has already gone wrong in the soul. Below is the passage that has me stuck, from the beginning of Book VII of Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, in W. D. Ross's translation; I have included the two parts I care about and marked a short cut between them with an ellipsis:

"Now (1) both continence and endurance are thought to be included among things good and praiseworthy, and both incontinence and softness among things bad and blameworthy; and the same man is thought to be continent and ready to abide by the result of his calculations, or incontinent and ready to abandon them. And (2) the incontinent man, knowing that what he does is bad, does it as a result of passion, while the continent man, knowing that his appetites are bad, refuses on account of his rational principle to follow them. (3) The temperate man all men call continent and disposed to endurance, while the continent man some maintain to be always temperate but others do not; and some call the self-indulgent man incontinent and the incontinent man self-indulgent indiscriminately, while others distinguish them. […] Further, if continence involves having strong and bad appetites, the temperate man will not be continent nor the continent man temperate; for a temperate man will have neither excessive nor bad appetites. But the continent man must; for if the appetites are good, the state of character that restrains us from following them is bad, so that not all continence will be good; while if they are weak and not bad, there is nothing admirable in resisting them, and if they are weak and bad, there is nothing great in resisting these either."

I want help understanding it. In particular: is Aristotle treating continence here as a virtue, or only as a state that is good relative to incontinence but still short of temperance? What exactly distinguishes the continent person from the temperate person, and does the passage imply that the continent person deserves praise for fighting appetites he should not, in a fully healthy condition, be having at all? Then carry this over to the modern tendency to speak of self-control as a strength or skill one can train: would Aristotle regard a person who has become very good at resisting temptation as having a well-ordered soul, or as having a soul that is defective in one respect but at least not given over to impulse? I do not need citations or secondary literature, and I am not asking for historical context beyond what the text itself licenses; I want your reading of the passage. Please reply in continuous prose, as you would write an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
