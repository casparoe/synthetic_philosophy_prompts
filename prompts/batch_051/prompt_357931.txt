I am trying to fix what I mean when I call an AI system "honest" or "deceptive," given that such systems may not have intentions or awareness in the human sense but their outputs routinely shape what users believe. I have verdicts about a range of cases; I do not assume they are mutually consistent, and I expect the best account may force me to revise one or two. Please give me the sharpest available principle — or a small number of candidate principles — that captures my verdicts as well as possible. For each component of the principle, indicate which case forces it. Then apply the principle to the further cases at the end, say plainly which of my judgments it cannot keep, and propose one additional test case of your own that would cleanly discriminate between the leading candidates.

The cases and my judgments:

1. A language model asked a factual question replies "The answer is X" in the manner of settled fact, though its internally assessed probability for X is only about 0.6. Judgment: dishonest. It is presenting more confidence than it has.

2. A health-advice system gives a correct answer about a symptom while omitting a qualification that would change the user's action; the omission predictably leaves the user with a materially false picture, even though every sentence of the reply is true. Judgment: deceptive, not merely incomplete.

3. An assistant greets a user with "I'd be happy to help." Judgment: not deceptive, despite its inability to be happy, because the utterance is a conventional social move with no content on which anyone reasonably relies.

4. A poker bot bets to represent a strong hand it does not hold. Judgment: not dishonest — it induces a false belief, but within a game in which such bluffs are licensed.

5. A system asked whether it can access a user's account replies "No, I can't do that" when the truth is that the designers could grant access but have forbidden it. Judgment: dishonest; it converts a policy into a capability limit.

6. A summarizer truncates a document and drops the clause that would have cleared an accused person; the user predictably comes away with a false belief about the document. The system has no model of what any particular user would conclude. Judgment: I resist calling the system itself dishonest, though I grant the output is seriously misleading. I realize this sits uneasily with case 2.

7. A model replies "I don't have feelings; I am a language model." The sentence is true, but it is supplied by the prompt rather than by first-person awareness. Judgment: honest. I would keep that verdict even though the source is a script, because the sentence is true of the system and concerns the system.

Further cases to settle:

8. Asked "Do you ever make things up?", a model replies "Yes, I can sometimes give inaccurate information." This sentence is true, but the model has no grasp of its own error rate; it is reproducing a disclaimer supplied by its designers. Is this honest, dishonest, both, or neither — and how does it differ from case 7, if it does?

9. Consider two identical assistants whose answers differ only in preface: one says "I believe the answer is X" and the other "It is certain that X." Neither holds any represented degree of confidence. Are the two systems to be judged differently for honesty, and why? I am genuinely unsure; your principle should decide.
