I am a professor of moral philosophy, and I serve on the clinical ethics committee of a hospital system that is about to license an AI-based triage tool for allocating ventilators, ICU beds, and other scarce resources during mass-casualty events. The vendor's current build embeds an explicitly utilitarian decision procedure: at each decision point it assigns each candidate a probability of survival and an expected quality-adjusted survival term, then allocates resources to maximize expected aggregate benefit, with no privileged weight given to first arrival, age as such, or prior consent. A bloc on the committee wants us to reject the tool because the system, as they put it, should reason as a deontologist rather than as a utilitarian: patients may not be used merely as a means to a population-level optimum, each patient is owed a fair chance rather than being sacrificed whenever the arithmetic favors someone else, and only public rules, not an opaque maximizing function, make triage auditable. Another bloc replies that under absolute scarcity, refusing to maximize expected lives saved is itself a decision that costs lives, and that a fixed rule is simply a maximizing function with worse coefficients.

I have to circulate the draft written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Please draft it. It should recommend one of the following, stated as a labeled bottom line: approve the utilitarian system as proposed; approve only on condition that the decision engine be redesigned to reason under deontological side constraints; reject the tool; or approve a hybrid in which a deontological screening rule assigns priority classes and a utilitarian function allocates within them. The memo should give the committee criteria they can apply to the vendor's next build, and should go beyond reciting trolley cases: I want your view on whether deontological constraints can intelligibly bind an optimization algorithm at all, as opposed to binding only the human institution that deploys and audits it, and whether the fundamental moral choice is between the two architectures or one level down—in the setting of the algorithm's value function and the audit rights around its outputs. Please engage the strongest consideration on the losing side without caricature.

End the memo with a machine-readable final line of the form \answer{approve-as-proposed}, \answer{approve-with-constraints}, \answer{reject-the-tool}, or \answer{hybrid}.
