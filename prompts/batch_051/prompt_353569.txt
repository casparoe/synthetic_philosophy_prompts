I'm a political philosopher about to give a workshop presentation to a philosophy department on the ethics of sweatshop labor, and I need to be ready for a question period that will be technically sharp but morally suspicious of my conclusion. The audience is mostly normative ethicists and political philosophers: two Kantians who think of exploitation as using a person as a mere means, one contractualist, two people working on global justice and structural injustice, and one PPE-trained economist who will press on whether my argument survives being restated in terms of workers' actual alternatives rather than an ideal baseline. They care, genuinely, about the welfare of the workers I'm writing about, and they are not going to let a conclusion they find morally uncomfortable pass without making me earn it.

The thesis I'm defending is that the standard anti-sweatshop argument from exploitation is largely unsuccessful as a case for consumer boycotts and for legal bans or mandated above-market wages in poor countries — even granting that much sweatshop labor is really exploitation. My line is that exploitation in the unfair-advantage sense (the gains of trade are divided lopsidedly against a background of unjust inequality) can coexist with mutual benefit: for the workers in question, the factory job is better than their actual next-best alternative, which is usually worse informal-sector work, unemployment, or subsistence agriculture. When an exchange is Pareto-improving over the worker's actual alternatives, the bare charge of exploitation does not yet show the exchange should not happen; refusing to transact harms the person the objection means to protect. So I locate the wrong, where it exists, in adjacent features — deception about hazards, coercion, unsafe conditions, restrictions on organizing — and argue those are better addressed through targeted regulation, enforceable health and safety standards, and worker organization than through exit or bans. I concede from the start that the background institutions that make the factory job the best available option are unjust, that a firm can wrong a worker even while benefiting her, and that my argument is not a complete answer to the question of what an individual consumer should do.

Please prepare me a prep sheet for that question period: the objections as this room will actually voice them, sorted from most to least dangerous, each paired with a reply I can deliver in under a minute and a note on where the reply is weakest. Include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks, handled separately at the end. Return a JSON object with these fields:

- "thesis": string — the one-sentence version of the thesis this audience will repeat back to me.
- "opening_concessions": array of strings — what I should concede up front, in my opening remarks or in the first sentence of an answer, to take the heat out of the strongest objections.
- "objections": array of objects, ordered from most to least dangerous (likelihood weighted by seriousness), each with:
  - "objection": string — the objection phrased the way this audience would actually put it, in their vocabulary.
  - "likely_raiser": string — who in the room is most likely to raise it.
  - "likelihood": "high" | "medium" | "low".
  - "seriousness": "high" | "medium" | "low" — how much damage it does if unanswered.
  - "reply": string — what to say back, deliverable in under a minute.
  - "weakest_point": string — the place where that reply is most exposed, or what I should retreat to if pressed.
  - "verdict": "concede" | "partially concede" | "hold" — what I should ultimately do with the point.
- "hoped_nobody_asks": object with the same fields as one objection — the question I should be most afraid of, separately flagged.
- "survival_priority": string — the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the session.
