In a seminar on obedience and authority, I have repeatedly watched the discussion end the same way: when someone asks why an otherwise ordinary person complied with a harmful or unlawful command, another student shrugs and says "orders are orders," as if that settled something. The sentence is clearly being asked to do real work—partly explanatory, partly exculpatory—but I cannot tell which precise claim is carrying that work. I would like you to treat the slogan as an object of analysis and lay out the distinct claims it could be making. Some readings I can see, though I do not trust my own list: (a) a legal or institutionalist claim that a valid order transfers liability to the one who issued it; (b) an organizational claim that blame belongs to the role, not the occupant, so the subordinate is not the right target of moral inquiry; (c) an epistemic claim that people inside a chain of command are not in a position to reassess the orders they receive; (d) a moral claim that deference to authority is itself a virtue, or is the price required to sustain valuable institutions; (e) a purely psychological claim that the slogan merely describes why people comply rather than justifying it; and perhaps (f) a quietist claim that once we have located the causal path from order to action, asking for further moral explanation is confused. For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, substantive but plausible, or implausible, and more importantly whether it actually supports the explanatory or exculpatory work the slogan is being made to do in the seminar context. I also want an honest diagnosis: which reading is the person who says "orders are orders" most likely relying on, whether they are entitled to it, and where the slogan does its illicit work. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about that diagnosis—which argument or consideration about responsibility, institutional roles, or the epistemic capacities of subordinates would most threaten your reading, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
