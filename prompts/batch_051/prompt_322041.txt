I serve on the Classification Review Working Group of a regional psychiatric health system. We are deciding whether to recommend a proposed new intake diagnosis, persistent avolitional withdrawal (PAW), which would consolidate a cluster of clinical impressions currently recorded only in free text—apathy, anhedonia, prolonged social disengagement—into a single triage and billing category. The proposal came out of a review of two years of intake notes in which clinicians used a dozen different phrases for roughly the same presentation. Its advocates argue that a shared label would improve triage, outcome tracking, and communication with insurers; its critics worry that it would carve a stable disorder out of what is now a diffuse and often situational set of difficulties, and that the category would change the very thing it claims merely to describe.

The working group has been reading Ian Hacking on looping effects: roughly, the phenomenon whereby classifying people does not just sort them but loops back and alters those classified—their self-understanding, behavior, and identity—so that the classification comes to fit them differently, or better, or worse, than it did before. We are split. Some members hold that looping is a downstream side effect, like stigma or self-labeling, to be managed after adoption through clinician training and periodic review. Others hold that for interactive kinds such as psychiatric categories, looping is not downstream of classification but partly constitutive of how the category functions; they argue that validation standards built for indifferent kinds—interrater reliability, temporal stability, biomarker correlates, treatment response—are measuring the wrong thing, because they treat the category as if it named a stable, classifier-independent target.

Please draft a policy memo, addressed to our clinical director, recommending whether and under what conditions we should adopt PAW. The memo should state a clear recommendation and the reasoning for it, treat honestly and forcefully the strongest argument on the losing side, and propose criteria the working group can apply to this and to future classification proposals rather than only pronouncing on this one case. Throughout, flag which claims about looping effects, interactive and indifferent kinds, and psychiatric validity are settled or near-settled, and which are live disputes among people who know the literature, and avoid presenting a contested reading as the consensus.
