I am giving a colloquium paper next month before a philosophy department with strengths in analytic philosophy of religion, moral philosophy, and philosophy of criminal law. The paper defends a broadly Lewisian thesis, inspired by David Lewis's objection to penal substitution: that if the word 'penal' is taken literally, the doctrine requires God to punish Christ for wrongs Christ did not commit, and that this is not merely a harsh or paradoxical consequence but a conceptual mistake, because punishment—unlike a penalty, a debt, or a cost—cannot be detached from the wrongdoing of the person punished.

The argument has three steps. First, I distinguish transferring a penalty from transferring punishment: a fine can be paid by someone other than the offender and remain a fine, but a punishment cannot be undergone by someone other than the offender and remain punishment. Second, I argue that the standard moves available to defenders of penal substitution—imputed guilt, federal headship (Christ as a second Adam representing those he redeems), and Christ's voluntary assumption of our liability—either turn guilt into a legal fiction that no longer tracks agency, so that what is transferred is no longer punishment, or they redescribe the cross as vicarious penance or solidarity in suffering, which may be a defensible account of atonement but is no longer penal substitution. Third, I conclude that the doctrine is unstable: the more literally penal it is, the less coherent it becomes, and the more coherent it is, the less distinguishable it becomes from non-penal accounts of the atonement.

The audience matters, and I want to prepare for this audience rather than for a generic debate. In the room will be two senior philosophers of religion who defend penal substitution and know the imputation and atonement literature far better than I do; one takes a broadly Reformed line and will say I am importing a modern, legalistic concept of punishment into a discourse where confessional usage should govern, while the other works with a liability-transfer model and will say my first premise simply begs the question against it. There will also be a Catholic philosopher sympathetic to Anselmian satisfaction theory, on which the cross offers God a satisfaction rather than inflicting a punishment, who shares my conclusion but worries that my argument is too strong and would embarrass all substitutionary accounts. Two members of the criminal-law group—one retributivist and one expressivist—will press whether the principle that only the guilty may be punished is a conceptual truth or a substantive moral principle, and what follows in either case. The graduate students in moral responsibility and the metaphysics of personhood will likely ask whether my argument depends on a contested view of how wrongs attach to persons and whether anything at all can be transferred across persons.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet for this audience: the objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, and phrased the way the relevant person in the room would actually put it. For each objection, give a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Flag what I should concede rather than defend, identify the one point I should hold no matter what, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
