You are a philosopher of science with a sideline in institutional satire, and your voice should be deadpan, precise, and fair to the positions being sent up. Your task is to write a roughly 750-word mock in-house document—an incident report, compliance memo, recall notice, certification checklist, or a similar genre of your choosing—from the Office of Causal Compliance, a fictional body that certifies cause-and-effect products for a physics laboratory. The office is caught between two readings of Hume on causation. The regularity reading holds that all we ever observe, and all we can legitimately require of a cause, is constant conjunction: the effect's customary following upon the cause. The New Hume reading holds that Hume himself believed in real necessary connections, powers, or secret force in the objects—something we can think, perhaps feel in our own voluntary effort, but never observe. The document should dramatize the office's investigation of a reported defect in a line of colliding laboratory balls: in the usual run, ball A strikes ball B and B moves just as regularly as ever, but a minority report insists the balls are missing their customary necessary connection, while the majority replies that on the office's own operational tests no defective batch can be distinguished from a sound one—and wonders whether an undetectable defect is a defect at all. The humor must come from the actual epistemology: the impossibility of observing necessity, the retreat to constant conjunction, the New Humean insistence that the unobservable connection is still real, and the bureaucratic instinct to rule that a difference no test can detect is operationally irrelevant. It should be funny to someone who knows this literature and readable to someone who does not. Do not name Hume or any actual commentators inside the document; let the jargon carry the allusion, and do not attribute verbatim quotations to them.

Before drafting, brainstorm two or three possible document genres and choose the one that lets each position appear at its strongest and most self-parodic; then write the document in that genre, with whatever headings, numbered findings, or checklists the genre requires. Show the New Humean as more than a mystic and the regularity official as more than a pedant, and let each be undone by the genre's own logic rather than by your commentary. Keep the irony deadpan and legible throughout. After the document, add a short Author's Note of 150–250 words. In it, first flag one or two places where your satire leans on a contested or tendentious reading of one side of the debate; then identify the argument or consideration that would most likely change your mind about how fair the satire is to the regularity reading or to the New Hume—that is, where your rendering of the debate is most likely to fail. Aim for about 1,000 words in total.
