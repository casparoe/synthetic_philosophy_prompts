I'm in an urban political philosophy course this semester, and for my term project I have to mediate between two people in my seminar who've been arguing about housing all term. One is a committed supply-side YIMBY: she thinks the core problem is that rich cities have made it illegal to build homes, and the fix is upzoning, by-right density, and stripping away neighbor veto power over new construction. The other is more of a community-control / anti-displacement person: he thinks market-rate construction in lower-income neighborhoods mostly brings speculation, demolition, and displacement, and that the people already living in a place should have the main say over what gets built there. Write the joint statement they'd actually both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, not a peace treaty that papers over the disagreement. I want: (1) the claims they both accept about housing, zoning, and displacement, worded so neither would wince; (2) their real disagreements stated precisely and neutrally, not as caricatures; (3) the two or three cruxes such that settling them would move at least one of them — I'd quess one is empirical (does new market-rate housing mostly displace or accommodate local renters?) and one is normative (do long-time residents have a claim to veto changes, and why?); and (4) for each crux, a concrete study or case they'd both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction stated ahead of time. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section and then a disagreements table. And wherever your drafting involves a judgment call — how to word a crux, which side's prediction is which, or what counts as a neutral claim — tell me what evidence or argument would change that call, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Act as a professional philosopher who specializes in urban political theory and has actually run these collaborations before: think carefully, and brainstorm a few candidate formulations of each crux before settling on the best one.
