I've been chewing on a comparison since a seminar on markets and bodies, and I can't make it come out clean. Where I live, a surrogacy contract is simply void: no court will enforce it, brokering one is an offence, and an egg donor can be reimbursed for her expenses but not paid. A few thousand miles away in California, those same arrangements are a regular market — agencies post compensation ranges for egg donors, intended parents contract with gestational carriers, and courts enforce the deal. The official rationale here is that paying for reproductive capacity turns a person or her body into a commodity, and that consent you're paid to give isn't the right kind of consent. The rationale there is that refusing to allow payment is just paternalism, since gestation and egg retrieval are demanding and sometimes risky work, and we already pay people for equally intimate labour. The thing that bothers me is that the same country whose California courts enforce surrogacy contracts will prosecute you for buying a kidney, while the same Europe that bans paid surrogacy is perfectly comfortable paying a nurse to do intimate work on strangers for a wage.

So here's the question: what theory of commodification, consent, bodily autonomy, or work does each regime have to be presupposing for its combination of rules to make sense — not which policy is better, but what each one is committed to. Does the European position actually presuppose that money corrupts reproductive acts in a way it doesn't corrupt other risky, intimate work, or is that just a post-hoc story? Does the American position presuppose that any free, informed contract makes a transfer legitimate, and if so, how does the kidney exception survive? And how much of the surface law would actually have to change if we kept the statutes but swapped in the rival's implicit theory? I suspect a lot of this is underdetermined — that several theories could license the same rules — so tell me where the law is doing less philosophical work than it looks like its doing.
