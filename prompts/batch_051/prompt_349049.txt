A friend and I spent last night arguing about whether alienation under capitalism is best treated as one underlying condition or a cluster of distinct deprivations, and the conversation kept circling back to Marx's canonical account in the 1844 Manuscripts. As I understand that account, estranged labor has four aspects. First, the worker is estranged from the product of her labor: the object she produces confronts her as an alien thing, owned and controlled by another. Second, she is estranged from the activity of labor itself: the work is not the free expression of her powers but forced, self-sacrificing toil that belongs to someone else. Third, she is estranged from her species-being: free conscious activity, which should be the distinctively human form of life, is demoted to a mere means of keeping her alive. Fourth, and as a consequence of the first three, she is estranged from other human beings: her relation to others becomes a relation between owners and the propertyless, mediated by things.

I think those four exhaust the standard account, and I am not asking you to recall a well-known fifth one I've forgotten. I want you to propose a genuinely new fifth aspect: a dimension of alienation under capitalism that is not a paraphrase of any of the four, not a special case or application of one of them, and not a combination of two or more of them. If you are tempted by something like "alienation from nature" or "alienation from the body," say precisely what is left in the proposal once everything already covered by the four has been subtracted, and judge honestly whether that remainder deserves to stand as a fifth aspect rather than merely a domain in which the four recur.
