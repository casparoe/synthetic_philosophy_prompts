Two friends of mine got stuck arguing about where to draw the line between inflection and derivation, and they used English -ly adverbs as the test case.

(1) Devin said -ly is obviously derivational: it changes category from adjective to adverb and creates a new lexeme, which is why "quick" and "quickly" get separate dictionary entries while "walk" and "walks" don't.

(2) Mira said that's the wrong criterion. -ly is paradigmatic: it's the default way English makes a manner adverb from an adjective, it's semantically transparent ("in an ADJ way"), and it's effectively obligatory since you can't just drop a bare adjective into adverb position ("he runs quick"). Regular, transparent, syntactically required — that profile is what inflection is for.

(3) Devin replied that productivity and transparency prove nothing, since derivational -ness has both, and "obligatory" just names a missing English construction rather than a paradigm slot — plenty of adjectives do fine without -ly ("fast," "hard"). Worse, -ly fails the one test he thinks matters: inflection is exceptionless (every noun has a plural), but -ly has gaps — there's no "greenly" or "bigly," and "good" takes "well."

(4) Mira answered that the gaps are semantic rather than morphological: color adjectives can't form manner adverbs because colors aren't manners; and "good/well" is just suppletion, which inflectional paradigms show all the time (go/went). So the gaps don't make -ly derivational any more than "went" makes past tense derivational.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Please go through move by move and say who is right about which specific claim, where each argument overreaches, and what the dispute is missing about how the inflection/derivation distinction is standardly drawn.
