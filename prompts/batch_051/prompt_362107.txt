A national literature fund has a mandate to support fiction that will prove lastingly valuable to the public rather than fiction that sells well in its first season. It proposes to abandon peer-review panels and allocate its budget as follows. Each year, any author or publisher may enter one completed but not yet published novel for a small listing fee. During a one-month trading window, anyone may buy or sell shares in each entered novel; each share pays 1 unit at a 25-year review if the novel is then still in print or has been republished by another publisher, and 0 otherwise. When trading closes, the fund divides its annual fiction budget among the entered novels in proportion to their final market prices, but only novels whose final price clears a floor receive anything, and grants carry no royalty obligations. Treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed rather than a policy to endorse or reject. Walk through what the market price would actually come to track, who would trade and with what information, what would get submitted, how authors, publishers, and deep-pocketed outsiders could game it, and whether the scheme would select for literary value or for something else. Identify where it fails first and whether the failure modes can be repaired without abandoning the mechanism.
