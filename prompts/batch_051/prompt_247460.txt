In a political theory seminar, a classmate defended a proposed law that would fine citizens who abstain from voting by saying, roughly, that the general will may force citizens to be free. I know the line originates in Rousseau, but I have encountered versions of it in arguments about seatbelt laws, vaccination mandates, and restrictions on hate speech, and each time the person uses it to do different work. When I pressed my classmate on which claim was doing the argumentative labor, the reasoning slid between several distinct ideas in successive sentences.

I want a careful disambiguation of the slogan forced to be free. Lay out the precise claims it could express, including at least the following possibilities and any others you find. First, that the state may use legal coercion to make someone do something. Second, that what they are forced to do is already prescribed by their own true or rational will. Third, that they would have willed it themselves as a co-author of the law under the general will. Fourth, that it makes them free from dependence on private wills even though it reduces their freedom from interference. Fifth, that it is not really force because the person, as a member of the sovereign, obeys only himself.

For each reading, separate the trivial versions from the substantive ones: which are true by definition but cannot justify the force, which are substantive and defensible but only under specific conditions, and which are simply false. Then say which precise reading or readings would actually support fining people for not voting, and which of the readings would fail to reach that conclusion. I am less interested in a verdict on Rousseau exegesis than in a menu of readings with judgment about what each can actually justify.
