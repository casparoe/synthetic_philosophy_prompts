I've been playing around with a short piece on whether grammatical categories—parts of speech, case, prepositions, that kind of thing—are natural kinds, and I'd value a skeptical read. Here's the rough idea.

The essay starts by seperating two questions that I think keep getting run together. The first is the projection question: if I tell you something is a noun in a language you don't know, how much can you infer about how it'll behave? The second is the essence question: is there some underlying property common to all and only nouns that makes them nouns, over and above those behavioral regularities? My claim is that a lot of the "are these categories real?" fights are just people answering the second question while their evidence only bears on the first.

Then I want to use case as the hard case. Greek and Latin have a tidy-ish five or six case system, but once you look at what "dative" picks out across languages it looks more like a loose cluster of roles—recipient, beneficiary, experiencer, goal—than a kind with a core. I want to say that's not a knockdown against dative being real; it's a sign we've been using a portabilty label (something we need to do typology at all) and mistaking it for an essence claim. As a comparison, I want to say case categories are closer to artifact kinds like "valve" than to chemical kinds: a valve can be a flap, a ball, a gate; what makes it a valve is the job it does in a larger system, and the job is partly specified by us, not by nature. But here's where I'm stuck: artifact kinds are usually intentional—someone made the thing for a purpose—whereas grammatical categories aren't made by anybody in particular. So the analogy may be doing more harm than good, and I'm not sure whether to cut it or try to fix it.

A couple specific things I'd like your take on. First, does my two-question split actually earn its keep, or is the essence question just the projection question in disguise? Second, is the valve analogy misleading enough that the essay is better off without it, and if I keep it, what would I have to say to keep it from collapsing into "these categories are just made up"? Third, am I being unfair to the people who want grammatical categories to be natural kinds—is there a stronger version of their view that I'm not steelmanning?

Don't worry about line-by-line prose; I'm more interested in whether the overall argument gives you anywhere to push.
