Someone floats this for governing stratospheric aerosol injection. Before any country or coalition starts pumping sulfates into the stratosphere, it has to post a continuity bond — say 2% of its GDP for every year it declares it will keep the program running — held in escrow by a trust run by non-deploying states. The interest is paid out annually while injections continue at the declared level; the principal stays locked until the program is wound down through one of the legal exit routes. If injections stop abruptly — war, revolution, a coalition member dropping out, budget cuts, a public panic over side effects, whatever — the remaining principal is seized and paid out to countries that can show they were harmed by the termination shock. The only legal exits are: atmospheric CO2 has fallen below an agreed threshold, another actor formally takes over injections, or a decade-long pre-announced phaseout with compensation to exposed countries. Walk me through how this actually behaves in the world, not whether it's a good idea on paper. Who gets attracted to deploying under this rule and who gets scared off? How do the deployer, the trust, the suppossed victims, and the non-deploying states each game it — the definition of harm, the GDP base, verification of injection levels, the trust board's composition? Does backstopping termination shock with a bond make deployment more or less likely, i.e. does it worsen the moral hazard it's supposed to fix? And where does the whole thing fall apart first?
