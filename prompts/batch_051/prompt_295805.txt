I keep noticing a structural parallel between an argument in Daniel Dennett's Quining Qualia and a routine problem in applied statistics, and I want to know whether the parallel is deep or superficial.

In the essay, Dennett introduces two long-serving coffee tasters. Both have grown dissatisfied with the coffee they professionally evaluate. The first says that the coffee has always produced exactly the same sensory experience in him, but that his standards for what counts as a good cup have risen over the years; he is a harsher judge of an unchanged object. The second says that his standards have not changed at all, but that the coffee now produces a different sensory experience; he is an unchanged judge of a changed object. Dennett points out that every outward fact—their discrimination behavior, their verbal reports, their performance on blind tests—could be held fixed between the two men. The disagreement between them is not empirically resolvable, because it decomposes one observed shift, from satisfaction to dissatisfaction, into two hidden changes: a change in the object as experienced and a change in the evaluative response. The data underdetermine that decomposition. From this he concludes that the quale, the supposed private sensory quality, is an explanatory posit with no work to do.

The same shape appears to me in a different domain. When a deployed machine-learning classifier loses accuracy over time, there are two stories: the model has gone stale relative to a stable world, or the world has drifted under a stable model. The observed accuracy decline can often be compatible with both decompositions; attributing the loss to model age or to data shift requires a background model of how the world is supposed to stay put. Likewise, when a price statistician sees a product's sticker price rise, they must split the increase into pure inflation and quality improvement—a decomposition that is acknowledged to be partly conventional and model-dependent. In both cases, a single observed change is factored into an object-side component and an evaluator-side component, and the factorization is not uniquely determined by the aggregate observation.

Here is what I am asking. Is this a genuine structural similarity—both cases being instances of underdetermination in decomposing a change into two latent variables—or is the resemblance only verbal? If it is genuine, does Dennett's eliminativist conclusion about qualia transfer to the statistical cases? That is, would it be a mistake to treat model staleness or quality change as real although unobservable components? Or is there a feature of the qualia case—perhaps that the private quality is defined as accessible only through the very report being decomposed, while the statistical cases can always in principle collect further data—that breaks the analogy before the conclusion can pass through it? I would like you to state the mapping precisely, say what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer, and identify at least one thing the parallel predicts about one side that turns out false.
