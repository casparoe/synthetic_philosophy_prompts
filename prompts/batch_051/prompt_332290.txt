I'm a retired physicist — spent most of my career in atmospheric optics, which means I'm comfortable with continuous probability densities but fairly new to the philosophical side of things. Ive been trying to get straight on what "possible but probability zero" is supposed to mean, and I stumbled into an argument I can't resolve. It goes like this:

(1) A rational person's degrees of belief should be probabilities in the usual sense: real numbers between 0 and 1 obeying the standard axioms.

(2) Bayes' rule never moves you off a zero prior: if P(H) = 0, then P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H)/P(E) = 0 for any evidence E with P(E) > 0. Zero is a black hole; once a hypothesis is at zero you can never learn it.

(3) A rational person should assign zero only to logical contradictions. Anything that's a genuine possibility should stay learnable in principle. (I think some people call this "regularity.")

(4) Take a continuous quantity — the fine-structure constant, say. For each real number x, the hypothesis "the exact value is x" is a genuine, non-contradictory possibility.

(5) But a probability measure on the reals can assign positive mass to at most countably many points. The argument: for each n, only finitely many points can have probability greater than 1/n, otherwise a finite sum of disjoint events would already exceed 1; and a countable union of finite sets is countable.

(6) Since there are uncountably many real numbers, every probability measure over the exact value of the fine-structure constant assigns zero to all but countably many of those possibilities.

(7) By (3), a rational person cannot have a standard probability distribution over the fine-structure constant at all. But (1) says rational belief is standard probability. Contradiction.

So either "regularity" is too strong a demand, or the probability calculus is the wrong model of rational belief in continuous cases — or the argument has a sleight of hand I'm not seeing.

Tell me: is this valid? If not, which numbered step fails, and under exactly what reading? If it is valid, which premise should I give up, and what happens to the underlying intuition that you shouldn't rule things out ahead of the evidence? I keep half-suspecting step (5), but the counting argument feels airtight; maybe the real problem is that "learnable" in (3) and "positive posterior" in (2) are not quite the same idea.
