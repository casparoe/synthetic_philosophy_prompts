I keep a mental list of people I have actually seen change their mind in public about something political—not just a vague "I was wrong," but a specific reversal with a reason attached. I started it after I realized the people most eager to tell me the other side argues in bad faith were also the people who, in two years of arguing with me, had never once budged on anything. The rule I've slid into is this: when someone tries to convince me that the other side is arguing in bad faith, or that some story ripping through my feed is true, I check the list. If the person has never, at any point in the last two years, admitted in front of other people that a political opinion of theirs was wrong, I discount their argument pretty hard—not to zero, but something like seventy precent.

Last month it backfired in an interesting way. A friend of a friend posted in a group chat about a ballot initiative, claiming the other side was deliberately lying about how it would be funded. She was persuasive, but she wasn't on my list, and I learned later from someone who knows her well that she's probably the most careful fact-checker in the group and just doesn't post corrections because she thinks it makes her look weak in a male-dominated local politics scene. So now I have a clean case where my rule and my local knowledge point in opposite directions.

Is the rule defensible, or is it a personality test dressed up as an epistemic shortcut? Under what assumptions about who is willing to admit error would it actually track good faith, and under what assumptions would it systematically favor people who perform humility or overcorrect? Could it be fixed—by counting private admissions, or by tracking predictions made in advance rather than admissions after the fact—or is the whole practice of judging good faith by public behavior misguided?
