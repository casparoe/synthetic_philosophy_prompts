I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've only encountered this debate through articles and conversations. A friend summarized the Clifford–James dispute for me in the following terms; I want to know how much of it I can trust before I repeat it to anyone else.

Clifford's view, as my friend presented it, is that you should never hold a belief without sufficient evidence, because every belief will eventually show up in your conduct and therefore affect other people. His central example is a ship-owner who convinces himself that his ship is seaworthy because paying for a proper inspection would be expensive; when the ship sinks and passengers die, Clifford says the owner is blameworthy even though he had genuinely convinced himself the ship was safe, because he had no right to that belief given the evidence he had. James is usually read as answering that Clifford optimizes solely for avoiding false beliefs, which also means systematically missing true beliefs. On James's account, when you face a choice between two hypotheses that is live, forced, and momentous, and the available evidence cannot settle it, you are entitled to believe whichever hypothesis makes your life go better. Religious faith, on this reading, is justified because it gives people hope and purpose; Clifford's mistake is demanding proof for claims that cannot be proved.

The two last moves strike me as suspicious: the claim that James entitles you to believe whichever hypothesis makes your life go better sounds like wishful thinking with extra steps, and the claim that faith is justified because it is useful seems to settle the debate by quietly redefining justification as usefulness. But I also suspect that I'm reacting to a caricature and missing distinctions a careful defender would insist on. Which parts of this summary would Clifford and James respectively accept, and which parts are subtle misrepresentations? In particular, does James's notion of a live, forced, and momentous option actually license believing whatever makes one's life go better, and does the conclusion drawn from the accurate parts—that James defends religious belief because of its practical benefits—survive once the misrepresentations are corrected?
