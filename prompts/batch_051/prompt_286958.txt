I have been thinking about the view often called performance legitimacy: the claim that a political order can be legitimate in virtue of its performance — sustained growth, rising living standards, public order, competent administration — even where genuinely competitive elections and full liberal rights are absent. I want to put the position precisely, because the version that interests me is not the weak descriptive claim that people in fact accept or tolerate such regimes, nor the vague claim that good outcomes count for something alongside procedure. The version I am attracted to is a diachronic, normative performance account: the claim that good performance can itself ground a provisional right to rule, that this right is earned and can be lost, and that the main thing that defeats it is a sustained failure to deliver. On this view the theory is not merely a rationalisation of whoever is in power; it makes definite, falsifiable commitments about when a performance state should be recognised as legitimate and when legitimacy lapses.

Tell me what would count against this position — what observations, arguments, cases or conceptual results would have to be true, or would need to be accepted, for the view to be false or seriously weakened. Rank the defeaters from roughly fatal to merely awkward, and separate the objections a committed performance theorist would themselves concede as damaging from those only opponents press. Among the pressure points I am especially interested in: whether performance is too easily reframed through choice of benchmark and timeframe; whether the view needs a substantive account of which gains count — growth, health, order, dignity — and thereby borrows normative premises it pretends to do without; whether it collapses into a blanket licence for any competent state with an appalling human-rights record; and whether the theory risks anything empirically, or has been insulated by qualifications so that no actual outcome could refute it. Be committed throughout: say which single kind of defeater you judge most serious, which objections you would set aside as not really counting, and defend the ordering rather than presenting options. Write in British English. End with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing your ranking of the three strongest defeaters as short comma-separated labels, most damaging first.
