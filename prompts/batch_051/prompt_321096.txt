I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, and I find myself relying on a policy whose epistemic credentials I have never fully examined. When I compare actions by their expected consequences, I assign zero to any consequence whose magnitude and direction I cannot estimate with at least minimal confidence. The effect is not entered as uncertain and possibly large; it is simply dropped from the assessment. I apply this to personal decisions and to moral arguments alike: appeals to diffuse social signaling, long chains of influence, or the claim that some small act might gradually shift a norm are treated as carrying no estimated weight unless someone can offer at least an order-of-magnitude account of their size. Last month, for instance, a friend argued that giving money to a particular person would, through a long chain of local economic effects, probably do more harm than the visible good; I set those downstream effects to zero and acted on the visible consequences. Is this policy defensible, or is it a disguised bias toward short, legible causal chains? Under what assumptions about the distribution of unknown effects does rounding them to zero approximate the expected value I would hold if I could bound them, and under what conditions does it fail in a predictable direction? More practically: is there a version of the rule that keeps the protection against speculative consequentialist narratives while still allowing delayed, diffuse, or tail-risk effects to count when they should?
