I'm writing a historical novel set in the English East India Company in the 1760s, and I've been reading Leviathan to get my head around what my characters would think they owed to a company that collected taxes, kept an army, and governed whole districts. Hobbes's argument, as I understand it, is that without a single overarching power able to keep everyone in awe, you get permanent insecurity and conflict, so the way out is for people to authorize a sovereign with a monopoly of force and law; after that, whatever it commands sets the terms of justice. What's haunting me is that the Company began life as a private, shareholder-owned venture chartered for trade, and then turned into exactly that overarching power — except its directors sat in a London boardroom and its object was profit, not peace.

Does the argument actually depend on the sovereign being a public authority, or does it go through just as smoothly for a for-profit joint-stock company with regiments? I can feel some premise giving way but I can't tell if it's the singleness of the sovereign, the claim that the sovereign's command defines justice, or just the background assumption that private profit and public order will run together more or less on their own. Is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still useful — say, that Company subjects are still in civil society even when the sovereign is a firm — or should I treat Hobbes as dead for the purposes of the book?
