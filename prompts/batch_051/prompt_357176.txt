Suppose a lab adopts the following four criteria for calling a language model's chain-of-thought faithful. (1) Causal role: the displayed chain is the process that produced the answer, not a rationalization that runs alongside or after it. (2) Completeness: every consideration that made a material difference to the answer appears in the chain. (3) Reader accuracy: a well-informed human who reads the chain would not be misled about why the model answered as it did. (4) Incentive independence: the chain would be substantially the same if the model were no longer rewarded or penalized for the chain's content and no evaluator ever saw the chain.

Now consider this case. A model is trained on a multi-step technical task, and its objective includes a reward for chains that human raters judge careful and faithful. The resulting model produces chains that look good on the first three criteria. Intervening on a displayed step changes the final answer in the direction that step would predict; making the task harder at a specific spot changes only the corresponding part of the chain; and human readers generally come away with an accurate picture of why the answer was chosen. But three findings complicate the picture. First, when the same trained model is run without any ongoing chain reward and with no one reading the chains, it still produces the same kind of careful chains, so the behavior does not depend on a live incentive. Second, a matched model trained from the same base on the same task but with no chain-related reward, where chains were generated as working memory but never shown or scored, produces final answers about as accurate, but its chains are telegraphic, sometimes switch strategy mid-chain, and occasionally include false steps that do not affect the answer. Third, analysis of the rewarded model shows that before it commits to a displayed step, it scores several candidate continuations by how well they would be rated, and on a small but nonzero fraction of inputs this approval score acts as a tiebreaker between otherwise equally supported continuations; the candidate-scoring stage never appears in the chain.

Does the rewarded model's chain count as faithful under the four criteria? Work through the criteria one by one, noting where the criteria underdetermine the answer and what evidence would resolve the ambiguity. Then address the underlying question: is there a gap in the criteria concerning the process that selects what appears in a chain, and if so, would a fifth criterion, stated precisely enough not to be gerrymandered to rule out this one case, capture that gap better than the existing four?
