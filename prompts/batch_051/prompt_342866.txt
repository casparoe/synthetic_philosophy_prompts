I am preparing a paper that uses Charlotte Perkins Gilman’s 1892 story The Yellow Wallpaper as a case study in epistemic injustice, and I would like you to act as a philosopher of social epistemology who is also sensitive to literary form. The broad question is how the story should be read through the categories of testimonial injustice and hermeneutical injustice, understood roughly as the injustice of being disbelieved or given less credibility because of identity prejudice, and the injustice of lacking shared concepts that would make one’s experience intelligible to oneself and others. But I do not want a mechanical application of labels. I want a reading that distinguishes, at minimum, three things that happen to the narrator as a knower. First, there is the straightforward credibility deficit: her physician husband John treats her reports about her own condition as unreliable because she is a woman and a patient, even where she knows her own mind better than he does. Second, there is the question whether she suffers hermeneutical injustice: whether part of what traps her is that her distress has no adequate shared description, so that she cannot make it sayable to John or even articulate to herself what would help. Third, there is the harsher possibility that the concepts her culture, her marriage, and her doctor do give her are not merely incomplete but actively distorting: a model of hysteria, nerves, the rest cure, and domestic submission that works as someone else’s tool for managing her, so that her best available self-description collaborates in her own silencing.

Then do the actual reading. Work through the story’s arc, from the narrator’s early attempt to express herself through writing, through her growing obsession with the wallpaper, to the final scene in which she tears the paper down and creeps around the room. For each stage, say whether the primary failure is testimonial, hermeneutical, or some hybrid, and whether the failure runs through an individual like John, through the institution of medicine, or through the entire conceptual repertoire the narrator has available. Address whether John’s genuine care and medical confidence make his treatment of her a different and in some ways more insidious form of epistemic injustice than a deliberate gaslighter would commit. Consider whether the ending is best read as the narrator finally asserting epistemic agency, as the destruction of her as a subject who can know and be known, or as something stranger, perhaps a resentful rejection of the very demand that she make herself intelligible on terms not her own. End by saying what the story adds to the philosophical account: what it shows about the way epistemic injustice can be built into affection, expertise, and care rather than contempt, and what a philosophical essay would miss.
