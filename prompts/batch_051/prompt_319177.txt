I am a novelist, and for the past several months I have been locked in a courteous but apparently irresolvable disagreement with my editor about a manuscript I have just finished. She maintains that it is not a novel but a collection of linked stories; I maintain that it is a novel, albeit one composed in fragments. When we set the word novel aside and simply describe the object before us, our descriptions coincide almost sentence for sentence: the recurring characters, the elliptical joins, the order in which the parts may be read, the absence of any single governing plot. Yet the moment the word is reintroduced, the quarrel returns in full. A philosopher friend, whose work is in metaontology, assures me that ours is a merely verbal dispute, a difference in lexical preference with no fact of the matter behind it and no empirical question left open once the concrete features have been fixed. I resist this conclusion, in part because the dispute plainly has consequences: it would determine how she publishes the book, how I should like readers to approach it, and on which shelf it would sit.

Behind my resistance is a stronger, more general claim that I would like to see tested. I suspect that the line between a substantive disagreement and a merely verbal one is itself at least partly an empirical matter: one can, in principle, bring evidence to bear on whether two people are disputing the world or merely disputing a word, and the boundary is not just a philosopher's convenience. Suppose you were asked to settle the quarrel between my editor and me. What is the strongest test you could devise to determine whether our disagreement is genuinely substantive or merely verbal? Be concrete about what you would elicit from each of us, and say what pattern of answers, predictions, or behaviour would incline you to each verdict. Then say what the test could not settle, even in principle.
