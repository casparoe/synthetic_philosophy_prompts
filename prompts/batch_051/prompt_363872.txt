I am a retired research chemist who has spent the past few years reading philosophy of science, and I have agreed to lead a ninety-minute session of an adult reading group whose members are retired natural scientists — an ecologist, two chemists, a geologist, and a civil engineer. The topic is Popper's argument against historicism: his claim that there cannot be laws of historical development analogous to the laws of the natural sciences. I have done the reading myself, so do not rehearse the argument for me; plan how I should teach it to people who are scientifically literate but philosophically untrained.

What I need from you, in this order.

First, the sequence. I want the session to begin where the group is already at home — their working sense of what a law of nature is and what it would take to falsify a generalisation — and to reach the conclusion that large-scale, long-term historical prediction is impossible, through Popper's argument about the unpredictability of the growth of knowledge rather than through any appeal to human complexity. Give me the dependency structure: which idea must be in place before which, and where in the sequence this particular audience is most likely to slow down or resist.

Second, one carrying example. I want a single case, ideally drawn from the natural sciences themselves, that I can carry through the whole session and that does the real work of distinguishing a law from a trend. State what the example must have and what it must lack. I had assumed I would use Marx's purported stages of history as the foil, but it now seems to me to beg the question for scientists who have no independent grip on nineteenth-century social theory, and to drag the session into political history. Tell me whether I am right to abandon it, and what should replace it, or whether I am misjudging the audience.

Third, the misconceptions. The misconception map matters to me more than the rest. The ones I anticipate are: that Popper is merely saying human affairs are too complex or chaotic for reliable prediction; that a statistical or probabilistic regularity in history is already a law, so Popper has knocked down a straw man; that a claim which is not falsifiable is meaningless rather than merely disqualified as a universal historical law; and that Popper's argument, if accepted, would also rule out retrospective explanation of singular events in evolutionary biology or geology, which this group will find absurd. Tell me whether these are the right misconceptions and whether they will arise in this order, and specify the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off before it hardens.

Fourth, the check. Give me one closing question — not a quiz item — whose answer will show me whether someone has grasped the law/trend distinction in their own words rather than parroting a definition.

Fifth, the exclusions. Say plainly what I should leave out at this level. I expect that includes the finer structure of the demarcation criterion, the logical asymmetry between verification and falsification, and perhaps Popper's positive programme of piecemeal social engineering; tell me whether to set those aside and how to acknowledge openly that I am doing so without making the session evasive.

Write the plan in British English, in a register suitable for me to study while preparing. Do not include a reading list.
