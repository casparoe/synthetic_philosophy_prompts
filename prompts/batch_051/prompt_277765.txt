Next month I'm giving a lunch talk at a sociology deparment where I'm a visiting philosopher. The thesis I'm defending is that generational categories—Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z—aren't real kinds in any interesting sense, not even the way gender or class are sometimes claimed to be social kinds. I want to say they're closer to brands or horoscope categories: classifications that become real mainly through being applied, internalized, and marketed to, but that don't carve any preexisting joint in social reality. I don't deny birth year correlates with attitudes. My argument is that most of what gets attributed to generations is period effects, age effects, or confounds like class and education, and the small residual is a looping effect: we label people Millennials, institutions target the label, people take it up, and the category firms up because it's used, not because it was tracking anything. The audience is mostly quantitative sociologists and demographers who do age-period-cohort analysis, plus two or three cultural sociologists who actually like generation talk and assign Mannheim on generations. They're not hostile to constructivism in general, but they're going to think I've ignored the emprical literature on cohort effects. Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get, roughly in the order they'll come, phrased the way this crowd would put them, with which objections have real force, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own position I should be ready to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the question I'm praying nobody asks.
