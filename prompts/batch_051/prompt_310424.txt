I'm taking an American intellectual history course, and the textbook's Harlem Renaissance chapter has a small sidebar on Du Bois's essay 'Criteria of Negro Art' that I've now read four times. It summarizes his position about like this. Du Bois thought Black artists in a racist society could not afford to fuss over beauty or craft, because art is a weapon; every work either helps or hurts the struggle, so the only serious question to ask about a poem or novel is what it does politically. The sidebar explains that this is what he meant by calling all art propaganda, and that he meant it strictly: even a seemingly apolitical piece is just propaganda for the status quo, so a technically lovely poem that ignores racial injustice is still a failure on his criteria. It then infers that this is why Du Bois wanted Black writers to choose uplifting subjects and avoid handing white readers unflattering material about Black life.

Something about the last step, and the way beauty comes out as a distraction, feels off to me—I'd always though Du Bois tied beauty, truth, and justice together rather than setting them against each other—but it's a published textbook and I'm just an undergrad, so I don't trust my instinct. Is this a fair summary? Walk me through it claim by claim: what would Du Bois have nodded at, what would he have qualified, and what would he have outright rejected, and whether the final inference about avoiding unflattering material really follows from his actual position. And don't hedge: give me the margin comments you'd write if you were grading the sidebar.
