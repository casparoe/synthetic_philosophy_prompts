I’m an undergraduate in an upper-level seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and for my term paper I’ve tried to write out my current position as a set of commitments I’d be willing to defend. Before I commit to it in print, I want an outside audit of whether the set actually hangs together, because I keep getting the feeling that some of these views are pulling against each other, but I can’t tell which ones or how seriously.

Here are the commitments, roughly as I’d defend them:

1. Compatibilism: moral responsibility is compatible with determinism.
2. Sourcehood rather than leeway: whether an agent is responsible for an action depends on whether the action issues from the agent’s own agency in the right way, not on whether she could have done otherwise.
3. In Frankfurt-style cases, the agent who decides for herself is morally responsible even though she could not have done otherwise.
4. In the first case of Pereboom’s four-case manipulation argument, an agent whose behavior is directly produced by the hidden manipulation of neuroscientists is not morally responsible for that behavior.
5. Structuralism about the responsibility-relevant facts: once an agent’s current psychological structure—her reasons-responsiveness, values, and quality of will—is held fixed, facts about how that structure came about, including whether someone else designed or caused it, make no difference to whether she is responsible for what she does from it.
6. Strawsonianism: moral responsibility is best understood in terms of the fairness or aptness of our reactive attitudes, such as resentment and gratitude, and the appropriateness of these attitudes is not threatened by determinism.
7. I accept the central result of the Consequence Argument: if determinism is true, then, given the past and the laws of nature, no one ever has a choice about what they do. I just do not think that result by itself imperils responsibility, given commitment 2.

Please audit this set. I’d like you to identify which pairs or larger subsets of commitments are genuinely in tension and which only look as though they are, order the genuine tensions by severity, and for each genuine tension say what the least costly revision would be and what that revision costs. Don’t assume that every apparent conflict is real; I want the audit to discriminate, not flag everything.

Please give me, first, a short prose audit of around 300 words, and then a table I can refer to. The table should have these columns:

- “Commitments involved” (the numbers of the commitments in the pair or subset)
- “Status” (“genuine tension” or “apparent only”)
- “Severity” (“high,” “moderate,” or “low”)
- “Source of the pull” (for apparent conflicts, why it dissolves on inspection)
- “Least costly revision” (for genuine tensions, which commitment most plausibly gives and what the resulting view loses)
