I am a retired physical chemist who came to philosophy late, and I now sit on the ethics advisory committee of a mid-sized teaching hospital. The committee meets in six days, and I have been asked to draft the short written opinion that will serve as our decision. Please take the role of a professional philosopher working in moral psychology and applied medical ethics; think carefully before composing anything, and begin by brainstorming rather than reasoning straight to a conclusion.

The case is this. A patient admitted for non-urgent but medically serious surgery has already had one request accommodated: citing a documented history of sexual assault, she asked that no male-identifying staff be present for physical examinations, and the hospital granted that as a matter of routine practice. She has now made a second request. She will not allow the phlebotomist assigned to draw her blood, a nurse of East Asian descent, to touch her, and asks that someone else be sent, explaining only that she feels uncomfortable and that there are cultural differences. She says she means no offense and regards this request as no different in kind from her earlier request about male staff, which the hospital has already honored. The hospital has an informal practice of accommodating gender-based requests where clinically feasible and not unduly burdensome, but it has no parallel practice for requests based on race or ancestry.

The committee is split. One group treats the request as a legitimate patient preference, continuous with the accommodation we have already made: patients retain authority over who touches their bodies, and consistency requires honoring the new request unless we can articulate a principled asymmetry. A second group treats it as a bias rather than a preference: accommodating it would make the hospital complicit in racial discrimination against its own staff, and the two requests are not continuous because the first responds to a specific history of harm while the second rests on groundless group animus. A third member, a psychologist, argues that the distinction between preferences and biases does no independent work in ethics: every preference is partly a learned, statistically colored rule of thumb, and calling something a bias is just a way of marking disapproval of its object. She predicts that whichever criterion we adopt will simply ratify the moral verdict we already hold.

My scientific training keeps pulling me toward the same worry in the language of measurement. I spent a career asking whether an instrument measures the construct it claims to measure, and I suspect the constructs here are not clean. If the category of preference must cover both a rape survivor's request and a xenophobic refusal of care, and if bias must cover both groundless animus and the ordinary, often statistically informed generalizations that guide real clinical judgment, then we cannot settle this by sorting the two requests under the labels and being done.

What I need from you is the reasoning behind a memorandum, not a survey of any literature. Work through the items below, and then show how the reasoning compresses into a document the committee can act on.

1. Brainstorm at least three candidate principles that could separate preference from bias in this clinical setting. For each candidate, give one case where it yields the right result and one case where it over- or under-includes, so the committee can see where the principle runs out.

2. Recommend a concrete decision for this patient. State the bottom line within the first two sentences of the memo, specify what the hospital should do in the forty-eight hours after the decision takes effect, and say whether and when the policy should be reviewed.

3. In the reasoning section, present the strongest honest version of the accommodationist position and the strongest honest version of the refusal position. Show where the losing argument fails rather than asserting that it fails; the committee will not be moved by a strawman.

4. Address the psychologist's charge directly. If the preference/bias distinction is epiphenomenal, say what follows for hospital policy: should we stop treating preference as a category that shields a request from scrutiny, or does the distinction still earn its keep on some other ground?

5. Write for the actual committee: physicians, nurses, a chaplain, a hospital attorney, and two community members, none of them professional philosophers. The memo will be circulated before the vote, so the recommendation must survive being summarized in a single sentence during discussion.

Please do not include citations or a reading list; they would be speculative, and the committee will not consult outside literature before the vote.
