I'm giving a philosophy of physics colloquium talk next month, and what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet rather than more argument. The thesis I'm defending is that considerations of elegance in fundamental physics can be genuine evidence, but only on a deflationary, empirically calibrated reading. By elegance I mean a loose family of theoretical features — symmetry, unification, parameter economy, explanatory range per added complication, and what physicists call depth — and the claim is not that these features are intrinsically truth-conducive. The claim is that they have a track record: within a specified reference class of theory transitions since roughly the late nineteenth century, the theory scored higher on these features was the one that later survived novel test regimes at a rate unlikely under the assumption that elegance is purely aesthetic. Elegance therefore functions as a modest, defeasible prior-updater, not a final arbiter; it can be outweighed by direct anomaly, and it never licenses protecting a theory from falsification.

The argument is in four moves. First, the relevant features can be given operational, if not fully formal, scores. Second, within the reference class, high elegance scores predicted eventual empirical success better than low ones. Third, the best explanation of that correlation is that elegance tracks something real about the world — some kinds of order, constraint, or symmetry — rather than merely reflecting which theories physicists chose to remember. Fourth, the licensing is domain-relative and self-correcting: the reference class and the scoring are themselves revisable as new transitions accrue, so the view makes contact with future history rather than standing outside it.

The audience is not inclined to grant any of this. About half are philosophers of science whose default lens is some version of the pessimistic induction, fortified by the usual graveyard of elegant failures — vortex atoms, SU(5) grand unification, supersymmetric partners below the TeV scale — and they suspect appeals to beauty are exactly the kind of a priori theorizing the historical record undermines. Two are theoretical physicists: one string theorist who likes aesthetic arguments but worries my deflationary account will undercut the stronger uses of elegance he actually needs, and one lattice/QCD person who regards elegance talk as seminar-circuit social construction. There is also a historian of physics who will press on the problem of which past physicists counted as elegant and whose judgments I am supposedly correlating with later success, and a philosopher who works on formal epistemology and will worry about scoring flexibility, base rates, and selection artifacts.

Write the prep sheet. I want, first, the eight or nine objections I am most likely to get from this specific room, ordered by expected likelihood weighted by how much damage each would do if unanswered, and phrased roughly as the relevant audience member would put them in that person's vocabulary. For each objection give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft or what the follow-up would be, and a clear statement of what I should concede on the spot and what I should hold. Flag which objections have real force and which I can treat as noise from this particular audience. Also tell me the one objection I should pre-empt in the talk itself instead of waiting for the question period. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks, a sentence on why it would hurt most, and the single reply in the whole sheet that has to land for the talk to survive the room.
