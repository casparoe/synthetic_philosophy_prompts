Question (20 points): Explain Carl Hempel's covering-law model of historical explanation and identify two standard objections to it. Then describe a narrative model of historical explanation: what intellectual work does placing an event in a narrative do beyond merely listing what happened? Finally, say whether narrative explanation is best understood as a rival to the covering-law model or as a different kind of achievement, illustrating your answer with at least one concrete example.

Student answer: Carl Hempel's covering-law model says that an event is explained when a statement describing it is logically deduced from general laws plus statements of initial conditions. The first obvious problem is that historians almost never provide such laws. Hempel's fallback is the explanation sketch, an explanation in which the laws are left unspecified; but this is unsatisfying because it makes the model unfalsifiably elastic—nearly any account can be regarded as a sketch of some unstated law. A second problem is the symmetry between explanation and prediction built into the model. If the model were right, then with the right laws and initial conditions a historian could in principle have predicted, say, the French Revolution from conditions in 1788, and since no one came close, something must be wrong. The narrative alternative, by contrast, explains an event by placing it in chronological order and tracing how it grew out of earlier events. Unlike a covering-law explanation, a narrative does not deduce the event from laws; it simply reports a sequence, so it is really closer to description than to explanation. That said, narrative does something the covering-law model often cannot: it can show how an outcome became possible, or how the assassination at Sarajevo took on significance as the trigger of the First World War only in light of later events, without requiring a strict law. I therefore think the two are not serious rivals. Covering-law explanation addresses why an event had to happen, whereas narrative explanation addresses only how the story hangs together; a narrative is an organizing device, but only the covering-law model actually explains.
