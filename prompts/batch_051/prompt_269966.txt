I'm preparing a lesson on what does and doesn't count as petitionary prayer, and I want to test the working account I've been teaching against a pair of hard cases. The account, adapted from Aquinas, is this: a petitionary prayer must be (i) an asking addressed to God, (ii) for something fitting rather than just anything the asker happens to want, and (iii) a human act that operates as one of the causes through which God has arranged to bring about certain effects — not an attempt to inform an omniscient God or to change an immutable one. The passage we've been studying is Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, II-II, q. 83, a. 2:

"Divine providence disposes not only what effects shall take place, but also from what causes and in what order these effects shall proceed. Now among other causes human acts are the causes of certain effects. Wherefore it must be that men do certain actions, not that thereby they may change the Divine disposition, but that by those actions they may achieve certain effects according to the order of the Divine disposition: and the same is to be said of natural causes. And so is it with regard to prayer. For we pray not that we may change the Divine disposition, but that we may impetrate that which God has disposed to be fulfilled by our prayers."

Here are the hard cases, both reported by a hospital chaplain. One patient, in severe pain, prays only a lament, complaining to God about how long the suffering has gone on, with no stated request. Another prays only that God's will be done, asking for no particular outcome beyond the realization of what an immutable God has already decreed. For each, work through my three criteria and say whether it counts as petitionary prayer, where the account gives a clear answer and where it leaves the question open, and whether either case shows that one of the three criteria is not doing any real work.
