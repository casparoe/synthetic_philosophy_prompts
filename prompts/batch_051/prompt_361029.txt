I am a retired biologist—systematics and evolutionary biology were my trade—and in retirement I have been reading philosophy of mind and cognitive psychology. I keep noticing a parallel that I cannot tell is genuine or merely a trick of vocabulary, and I would value a careful judgment.

In the psychological literature on concepts I have seen three main positions. On the prototype view, a concept is a summary representation of the central tendency of its instances, and category membership is a matter of similarity to that prototype, which makes membership graded. On the exemplar view, there is no summary representation: the concept just is the stored collection of previously encountered instances, and new items are classified by similarity to those remembered exemplars. On the theory-theory, concepts are not similarity structures at all; they are embedded in causal-explanatory knowledge about a domain, and what makes an item a member of a category is the role it plays in that naive theory. I gather the live debate is now more nuanced, with hybrids, but this is the shape of it.

This looks to me strikingly like the older debate in my own discipline about the species concept. There too one finds a similarity-based account—the phenetic or morphological species concept, on which a species is a cluster of organisms that resemble one another. There too one finds a turn toward particularistic and historical accounts: some systematists treat species as spatiotemporally bounded individuals composed of organisms, rather than as classes defined by shared properties, which strikes me as structurally akin to the exemplar view's refusal to posit an abstract summary over instances. And there too one finds accounts that make the category depend on an underlying causal structure—the biological species concept with its reproductive isolation and gene flow, or the phylogenetic species concept with its shared lineage—much as the theory-theory makes category membership depend on causal-explanatory structure rather than raw similarity. Finally, both debates have generated pluralist positions: many philosophers of biology now hold that no single species concept serves every legitimate purpose, and I have the impression that something analogous is now said about concepts—that different representational formats suit different cognitive tasks, or that "concept" is not one kind of thing.

My question is whether this is a deep analogy or a superficial one. If I spell out the mapping as

- prototype view : phenetic or morphological similarity concept;
- exemplar view : species-as-individuals, or particularistic view;
- theory-theory : biological or phylogenetic concept;
- pluralism : pluralism,

where exactly does the mapping hold and where does it break? In particular, species concepts are proposals about how to delimit real entities in the world, whereas the prototype/exemplar/theory debate is about the representational format of a mind; does the analogy survive that difference? Would anything transfer from the species debate to the concepts debate—for example, is the history of pluralism in biology a good reason to expect a corresponding pluralism about concepts, or does that prediction fail? And what is something the parallel would naturally lead me to expect that is in fact false? I am not asking for a literature review, only for a structural verdict on the analogy itself.

Please end with a final line beginning "Verdict:" and giving a short phrase such as "deep", "superficial", or "deep only with respect to X".
