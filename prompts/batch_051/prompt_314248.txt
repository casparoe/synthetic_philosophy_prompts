Next month I'm presenting to our political economy workshop a line I've been developing on charter cities and seasteading. My thesis is that the usual framing — 'can you opt out of the state?' — is misdescribed, because these projects don't exit the state; they renegotiate which state, and on what terms, supplies legal order and enforcement. The difference matters because 'exit' smuggles in the idea that you could get sovereignty out of the picture entirely, which I dont think any serious proposal does. My quick argument runs like this: a charter city needs a host-state legal instrument before it can be anything other than a land grab, and a seastead needs a flag state, an insurer, or ultimately a navy willing to treat it as a legal object. The menu of feasible jurisdictions is itself set by states, so what changes hands is not statehood but residual control over a new entity. The audience is two public-choice economists who think competition between jurisdictions is the main engine of accountability, a maritime law person who knows flag-state jurisdiction inside out, an anarcho-capitalist theorist who thinks polycentric law can work without any monopolist, and a development economist who's skeptical that charter cities have delivered much. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll land, phrased more or less as each of those people would push back, with what I should concede in the moment, what I should hold, and where my own position is weakest. Include the question I'm praying nobody asks. And as you write the replies, keep clear which pieces are settled among people who actually work on this and which are genuinely contested, so I don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
