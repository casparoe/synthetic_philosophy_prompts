I recently spent several weeks trying to help a small organization decide how to allocate a fixed yearly budget across very different interventions, and the process forced me into a corner. I am drawn to expected-value reasoning because it is complete, respects stochastic dominance, and follows from axioms I find individually compelling; I do not know another rule that yields definite, non-arbitrary answers under deep uncertainty. But the same reasoning seems to imply that any ordinary intervention can be beaten by a sufficiently improbable, sufficiently high-value alternative, and that no probability floor or payoff ceiling I set can protect me, because the long shot can always be specified one step beyond whatever limit I choose. I have a firm intuition that a decision procedure that can be hijacked merely by adding one more zero to a promised payoff has lost contact with the world. Yet whenever I try to state that intuition as a principle — discount tiny probabilities, cap value, refuse to compare across orders of magnitude — the proposed fix looks arbitrary and quietly gives up one of the very features that made expected-value reasoning attractive. Rather than a verdict for one side, I want an account of what each resolution would cost. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, that is, for the conviction that some possibilities are too remote to count to be a debunkable bias? What would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes, amended or replaced in a non-ad-hoc way?
