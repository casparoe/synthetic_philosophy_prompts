Consider Putnam's Twin Earth argument for semantic externalism. In its standard form, the argument asks us to imagine that Twin Earth is exactly like Earth except that the clear, drinkable liquid the locals call "water" has the composition XYZ rather than H2O. Before either community knows any chemistry, Oscar and his Twin Earth counterpart are internally identical, molecule for molecule; yet, according to the argument, Oscar's word "water" refers to H2O while Twin Oscar's word "water" refers to XYZ. Since the two speakers are internally identical but their terms refer to different substances, Putnam takes the case to show that what a speaker's words mean is not fixed by the speaker's internal states alone.

Now consider a development the original argument did not anticipate. A large language model acquires the ability to produce and respond to "water" discourse purely from the statistical structure of a text corpus, without ever seeing, drinking, or measuring water or XYZ. Suppose two such systems are internally identical — same architecture, same weights — and are trained on corpora that are token-for-token identical, the only difference being that one corpus was produced by Earth speakers and the other by Twin Earth speakers. The original argument holds internal states fixed while varying the speaker's environment; the language-model case holds internal states fixed while apparently removing the speaker's perceptual and practical contact with any environment at all.

Does the Twin Earth argument survive this development? In either case, say what the Twin Earth case actually established and whether the language-model case changes that.
