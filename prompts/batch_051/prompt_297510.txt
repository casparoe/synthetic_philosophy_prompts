You are a philosopher who works on the ethics of games and aesthetic value. Think the question through carefully; if it helps, begin by laying out the candidate premises of the position under pressure before evaluating it.

Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether a war game can be morally criticized for making war fun. My friend defended the familiar play-frame position. On his view, play is sealed off from ordinary moral assessment: the player brackets the game's violence as make-believe, does not endorse its real-world counterpart, and harms no one, so taking pleasure in a stylized military shooter is no more glorification of war than taking pleasure in a chess capture is glorification of regicide. The defense appears to need three things to be true. First, the player must experience the represented violence as unreal. Second, the player's pleasure must attach to the make-believe challenge — the skill, the tension, the competition — rather than to the represented suffering. Third, the game itself must not function in the real world as an assertion or as an instrument; it must not be in the business of producing attitudes about actual war.

I left the argument uneasy, because that defense was built around games that behave like toys, and we now have a recognizable kind of war game that behaves more like an argument about its own fun. These games deliberately give the player the conventional pleasures of a shooter — competence, tension, the satisfying kill — precisely in order to turn that enjoyment back on the player as an accusation. They want the player to enjoy the violence and then to feel the wrongness of having enjoyed it; the fun is not an obstacle to the game's seriousness but the mechanism through which that seriousness is achieved. Some of these games are built around real, named conflicts, while others use fictional conflicts to the same end; the design strategy is the same either way.

Does the play-frame defense survive this development? If the defense is right, then the fun in these games should be just as morally sealed off as the fun in a toy shooter, which would defuse the very accusation the games are engineered to level. If the games can make the player's fun morally evaluable, then the original defense must have rested on something narrower than the claim that play is sealed off from moral assessment, and it is no longer obvious what that narrower premise is. I am interested in what happens to the argument itself, not in a cultural verdict about particular games. Say which premise the serious war game puts under pressure, whether a repaired version of the defense can be formulated that still protects the toy shooter while allowing the serious game its moral point, or whether these games force us to give up the play-frame defense altogether.
