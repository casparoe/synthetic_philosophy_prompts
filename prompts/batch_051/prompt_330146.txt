I've been rereading Gödel, Escher, Bach for the third time now, mostly the bits I skimmed as a teenager, and got into an arguement with a friend about the Ant Fugue, especially the back-and-forth with Aunt Hillary. My rough sense is that Hofstadter is trying to get you to take seriously the idea that a colony could be a self at a higher level of description — the whole pattern of ant activity as the unit, not any individual ant — and that he's deliberately blurring the line between "it behaves as if it had a self" and "it has a self" to make a point about brains.

My friend is drafting a blog post on it and sent me his summary: "Hofstadter's claim in the Ant Fugue is that an ant colony literally has a mind in the same sense a human does. Individual ants are too dumb to think, but the colony's signal traffic plays exactly the role that neural firing plays, and the colony as a whole is the self. Once you grant that a brain made of neurons can be conscious, denying the same thing to a sufficiently organized ant colony is just substrate chauvinism. The queen isn't the brain, and colony turnover is no more of a problem for the colony's mind than normal cell turnover is for yours. So there's no in-principle barrier to a colony having beliefs, a first-person perspective, perhaps even qualia."

I'm pretty sympathetic to substrate-independence and emergence, so this doesn't sound obviously wrong to me, but it does feel like he's skipped a step or two between "high-level pattern" and "literal mind with qualia," and I don't trust myself to say exactly where. Could you give me an accuracy audit: what's right, what's overstated or not what Hofstadter is doing there, where he's collapsing levels of description, and whether the qualia conclusion actually follows from the parts of his summary that are correct? Please give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
