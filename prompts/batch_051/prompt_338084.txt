Spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the School of Names and the old white horse dialogue, the one where Gongsun Long defends the bizarre-sounding line that a white horse is not a horse. I always filed it away as a word game, but my friend rebuilt the reasoning in a way I couldnt poke a hole in, and now I'm weirdly stuck. He laid it out like this.

(1) The word 'horse' picks out a shape, the horse-form, while 'white' picks out a color. (2) A white horse is the shape-plus-color compound, not the shape alone. (3) So what 'white horse' refers to is not what 'horse' refers to: the first includes whiteness and the second doesn't. (4) Therefore a white horse is not a horse. (5) There's nothing special about white here — by the same move, a black horse is not a horse, a young horse is not a horse, a horse grazing in a field is not a horse. (6) But every actual horse is some color, some age, and somewhere, so step (5) amounts to saying no actual horse is a horse; only a bare horse-form nowhere in particular would qualify. He says that last part is the point: the School of Names was drawing a line between a kind and its determinate instances, and we bulldoze over it when we treat 'is' as mere membership in a set.

I'd most like to keep the ordinary thought that a white horse is a horse, and that kind terms like 'horse' still apply to the colored, aged, located animals we actually meet. But every time I try to point at the step where I think the slide happens, he tells me I'm either assuming the very thing he's trying to show or importing tools from modern logic that the argument was built without.

You're an expert in philosophy of language who also knows the School of Names well. Think carefully, and before you name the guilty step, maybe untangle the different things 'is' and 'horse' could be doing in the argument. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost the rest of his case? If the whole chain is valid on some reading, tell me which ordinary belief I have to give up instead, and whether that's a price worth paying.
