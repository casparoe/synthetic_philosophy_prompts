I'm a philosopher of music and I've been kicking around a view about sad music that I'd like pressure-tested. The idea is this. A piece of music is sad, I want to say, not because it resembles sad speech or posture, and not because the listener ends up actually feeling sad, but because it initiates a covert motor programme and then blocks its completion. A slow, descending, quieting phrase recruits the same subvocal and postural preparation as the onset of weeping or a sigh; because the music never actually cries — no gesture is completed — that readiness is left hanging, and the unresolved bodily state gets projected back onto the sounds as sadness. I call it the Aborted-Gesture Account of musical expression. It seems to explain why sad music standardly has a low, falling, soft, slowing contour, why brisk ascending music can't manage sadness, and why the feeling is less like real grief than a pressure in the chest and throat. I'm aware it sounds James–Lange-adjacent, and I'm sure its bound to be incomplete. Still, I'd like an honest assessment: reconstruct the account in its strongest form, tell me whether it collapses into an existing view, give me the three strongest objections, and say whether any of them is fatal or just calls for narrowing the claim. And could you write the answer in British English, please.
