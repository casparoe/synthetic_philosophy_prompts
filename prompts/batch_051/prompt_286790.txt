I am a journalist who has spent several years covering virtual economies, social VR platforms, and disputes over in-game property. I am now writing a long reported essay on whether virtual objects and virtual harms are real in any sense that matters, and before I build the piece around my own working views, I want an audit of those views. Here, as precisely as I can state them, are the commitments I find myself reaching for:

1. Virtual objects are real in the same way money, national borders, and marriages are real: they are not material things but statuses and entitlements that exist because enough people treat them as existing and act accordingly. A virtual sword is real in much the way a share of stock is real.
2. This means the claim that a virtual item is merely pixels and code is a bad argument against its ownership, value, or wrongful destruction; applied consistently, that kind of reductivism would also dissolve company shares, trademarks, and citizenship.
3. Still, I cannot go as far as the strong realist position. I do not think there is a sword sitting in some server in anything like the way this desk sits in my office. What exists is the underlying data, the rule-governed practice, the expectations it supports, and the entitlements people have built on those expectations; the objects themselves are, as objects, not there.
4. If someone uses exploits to destroy a stranger's rare in-game item, the wrong done is not adequately captured by saying the victim lost the enjoyment of a service or suffered a breach of contract. There is a distinct kind of loss, closer to the destruction of something that was theirs.
5. Virtual harassment is a real wrong even when the target can log off at any time and the harasser never makes physical contact. The fact that the target could leave the space is not a serious mitigation of the wrong.
6. On remedies, my instinct is deflationary: bans, suspensions, account resets, and in-kind restitution within the platform are the right tools, and I resist extending real-world criminal law into virtual spaces. I worry that policing games with offline criminal statutes would overcriminalize conduct and extend the carceral state into spaces of play.

Before I commit this framework to print, I would like you to work through it as an auditor would. Tell me which pairs of these commitments are in tension with one another and which only appear to be; for each genuine tension, say how severe it is and what the least costly revision would be; and then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set cohere. Where a claim is settled among people who work on these questions and where it is genuinely contested, please separate those out, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
