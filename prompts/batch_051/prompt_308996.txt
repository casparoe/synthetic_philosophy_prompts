In On Lying (De Mendacio), Augustine defends the view that whether someone lies is settled not by whether the sentence uttered is true or false, but by what the speaker has in mind. He writes:

"For not every one who says a false thing lies, if he believes or opines that to be true which he says. [...] Whence it comes to pass, that he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie."

He also sets jokes aside at the start of the work, on the ground that their tone and manner carry a plain sign that no deceit is intended. I want to know whether this mental-state picture can be worked up into a precise definition of "lie" that handles more than his own examples — and whether the line he draws around jokes can be extended to other conventionalized falsehoods.

Give me a working definition of "lie" that starts from the criterion in the passage, and run it through each of the following cases. For each case, say whether it is a lie on your definition, whether Augustine's criterion would call it a lie, and whether your verdict and his come apart. I am confident that (2) is not a lie and that (1) and (6) are; about the rest I do not have a settled view.

(1) A speaker tells an audience, "The city is north of the river," meaning to be believed. He believes the city is south, so he takes the sentence he utters to be false; the sentence is in fact true.
(2) A speaker tells an audience, "The city is north of the river," meaning to be believed. She sincerely and reasonably believes the sentence to be true; it is in fact false.
(3) A host says to a guest, "I am so glad you could come," although she spent the day dreading the visit. She says it because hospitality requires some such phrase, and both parties know the words are a conventional form rather than a report of her state of mind.
(4) An actor on stage, in character, says, "I am the King of Denmark," before an audience that knows he is playing a part.
(5) A poker player holding a weak hand makes a large bet — the conventional move by which a player represents strength to the table.
(6) A person answers a pursuer who asks, "Is your friend in the house?" with "No," when the friend is in fact in the house, in order to save the friend from being killed.
(7) A person says, "He was in the shop an hour ago," which is true, while knowing the hearer will take it to mean that he is not here now, when the speaker knows he is here now — deception by a truth.
(8) A novelist writes, in the third-person narration of a novel, that a character took a train from Paris to Berlin on a particular day, although both the character and the train were invented.

Propose the definition, test it against all eight cases, and then say where your definition surprised you. In particular, tell me whether a single clean line can be held between (1) and (6) on one side and (3)–(5) and (8) on the other, where (7) falls, and whether you would need to revise Augustine's criterion or abandon it in order to keep the definition from doing something you find wrong.
