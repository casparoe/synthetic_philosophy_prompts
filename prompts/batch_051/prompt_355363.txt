Lately I've been hooked on these puzzle sheets my geometry prof leaves in the common room. Each one has twelve small panels: six on the left, six on the right, and each panel is just a handful of black lines and shapes on a white background. The task is to figure out the rule that puts the six left panels in one group and the six right panels in the other. My roommate and I did one together and ended up with totally different rules. His was about whether the total number of endpoints in a panel is even or odd. Mine was about whether the shapes hang together as one connected blob or come apart into separate pieces. We checked: both rules match every single panel. He says his is better because it's exact and countable, and I say mine is better because you can see it right away, no counting required. Neither of us will budge.

Now I'm supposed to make a page of my own for the math club next week, and I keep worrying the whole activity is rigged. For any twelve little drawings, someone could probably invent some absurd rule that fits—like "has a prime number of straight segments, or exactly four dots and no closed shapes," or something even more contorted. So when a person solves one of these, are they really finding the intended rule, or just happening to picked a rule that fits the data? And if I design a page, what makes my rule the right answer rather than whatever weird alternative someone digs up? Part of me thinks there's no fact of the matter beyond what the puzzle-maker intended, but then it also feels like some rules are obviously sillier than others, so it can't be totally arbitrary. Give me a definite verdict on whether these puzzles have a real right answer, and on how to choose between rival rules that both fit.
