I have been trying to make sense of a striking asymmetry in the way liberal societies handle punishment by the state and punishment by the platform, the employer, and the crowd. In the criminal law we are careful, at least in principle, to distinguish punishment from mere harm: there must be a defined offence, a finding of culpability, a roughly proportionate penalty, and some procedure through which the accused can answer the charge. Yet a cluster of everyday social sanctions seems to share punishment's core features while lacking all of these safeguards. A social media platform removes a user for harassment, or for a single old post unearthed years after it was written; a university withdraws a speaking invitation under student pressure; an employer dismisses someone after a campaign against the firm; a payment processor declines to serve a legal but widely condemned organisation. In each case a private actor intentionally makes someone materially worse off in response to perceived wrongdoing, often with consequences that outstrip anything a court would impose for the underlying conduct, which is itself often lawful.

What I would like is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of punishment, responsibility, or social sanction that these practices presuppose. Do they presuppose a consequentialist or incapacitation-based view, on which the point is to prevent future harm rather than to give wrongdoers their due, so that culpability and proportionality matter only as rough indicators of dangerousness? Do they presuppose that these acts are not punishment at all, but merely a refusal to associate, a safety decision, or an exercise of private property, so that the justificatory burdens of the criminal law simply do not attach to private power? Or do they presuppose an expressive view, on which the community affirms its norms by withdrawing recognition and the point is condemnation rather than the infliction of pain? Please separate what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with, mark the places where several rival theories would license the same act, and identify the points at which these commitments come into conflict, for example when the same person denies that deplatforming is punishment while also insisting that the target deserved it. Finally, say what would have to change in how platforms, employers, and the wider public treat such sanctions if we took the implicit view seriously and replaced it with the due-process and proportionality constraints normally demanded of punishment, or if we instead refused to extend punitive logic to private actors at all. Please write your answer in British English.
