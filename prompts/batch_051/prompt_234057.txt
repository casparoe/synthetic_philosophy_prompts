A state supreme court justice has spent several terms working out how far the absurdity doctrine extends — when a court may decline to apply what a statute literally says. Her recent rulings are these. (1) A local animal-welfare ordinance made it an offense to sell a puppy or kitten younger than eight weeks; a drafting error left the published text reading "eighty weeks," while the title, cross-references, and agency guidance all said eight. She read the ordinance as saying eight weeks. (2) A traffic statute makes it an infraction to fail to bring a vehicle to a complete stop at a red signal. A driver rolled through a red light at three in the morning on an empty rural road with unobstructed visibility. She upheld the citation, noting that the text was clear and that this application, even if a bit needless, was not in tension with the statute's evident safety purpose. (3) A statute made it a felony to delay the delivery of the mails. A deputy sheriff briefly stopped a postal van on a rural road to arrest a murder suspect who was riding as a passenger. She held that the statute did not reach the arrest, because applying the plain text would turn a routine and incidental feature of lawful official conduct into a felony and would defeat the statute's protective purpose. (4) A tax statute says a homestead deferral application counts as filed only when received by the assessor, and must be received by March 1. A homeowner mailed the form on February 25, but a snowstorm delayed the mail, it arrived on March 4, and the deferral was denied for that year. She upheld the denial: the outcome was harsh, but the deadline was coherent and its operation did not become absurd.

Her next case involves a security-deposit law. A landlord who fails to return a tenant's deposit, or the properly itemized balance, within thirty days after the tenant surrenders the premises is liable for twice the amount wrongfully withheld plus attorney fees. The landlord mailed a full refund, correctly addressed, on the third day. The check was lost in the postal system and never arrived, and she learned of the loss only when the tenant sued on day forty-five. The tenant argues the statute is literal; the landlord argues that penalizing her when she did everything within her power and the loss was not her doing defeats the statute's evident purpose and produces an absurd result.

Predict her ruling. Which interpretive rule — strict textualism, narrow absurdity, broad purposivism, or something else — best explains her record; which of the earlier cases is the closest analog; and what further case would most cleanly settle the remaining ambiguity if her precedents underdetermine the answer?
