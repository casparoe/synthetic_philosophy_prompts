In Book IV of the Politics, Aristotle records the common view that assigning magistracies by lot is a mark of democracy, while filling them by election is a mark of oligarchy, and then complicates that distinction by considering how different methods of appointment suit different constitutions. Suppose we take this as a diagnostic test for contemporary democratic reforms. Rank the following five uses of random selection, from most to least democratic in the evaluative sense you take Aristotle to be tracking: a full allotted legislative chamber with power over all bills; an allotted chamber that can only delay or veto legislation from an elected chamber; a citizen assembly that sets the agenda for binding referenda; randomly selected auditors with power to sanction elected officials; and a purely advisory mini-public with no formal powers. Then give each proposal a score from 0 to 1 for how strongly it should be preferred in a transition from a purely representative system. In justifying each rank and score, say which feature is doing the democratic work — equal opportunity to hold office, resistance to faction, descriptive representativeness, insulation from electoral incentives, or something else — and state whether your ranking requires amending, abandoning, or simply refining Aristotle’s lot–election polarity.
