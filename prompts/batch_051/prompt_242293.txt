I live in a student housing co-op with a written house agreement that everyone signs at move-in. The relevant clause reads: "No member may enter another member's room without permission, or monitor, record, or listen in on another member's conversations or other private communications, by any means." The clause was drafted years ago after a pair of housemates got caught going through someone's room, and everyone has always understood it as "don't snoop in rooms and don't bug the common areas."

This term our housemate Mina replaced the old router with a mesh system that, as a side effect, keeps a detailed log of every device that connects: what time it connects, how long it stays, which node it's near, and rough signal strength. She installed it to fix dead zones. But after a dispute about the utility bill, Mina used the logs to show that our housemate Sam has someone staying over most weekends (an unknown device appears around 1am, disconnects at 8am, and is strongest near Sam's node) and she said so in front of everyone. Sam is furious and says the clause was violated. Mina says she never entered Sam's room, never listend to or recorded any conversation, and only looked at data generated by her own router. The log doesn't even contain the contents of anything, just identifiers like "c8:3a:..." and timestamps, so is that even a "private communication" under the clause?

What I actually want to know is whether the clause reaches this, which honestly none of us expected. Can you work it through the four ways we've been discussing in my philosophy of law course: the plain text, the purpose of the clause, what the people who wrote it would say if we asked them, and what a reasonable housemate reading it at sign-up would take it to mean? And if those come apart, which should govern here? The other thing bugging me: if you read the clause broadly enough to catch Mina, can you avoid also banning everyone from just noticing that Sam's bike is outside or that the shower runs at 2am? Because if you can't, I'm not sure what the clause was ever really protecting.
