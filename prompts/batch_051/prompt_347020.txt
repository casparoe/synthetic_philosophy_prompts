I am a PhD candidate in philosophy of language, and I am preparing a graduate interview series on the norms of assertion. Next week's guest is a philosopher who defends the knowledge norm: the view that a speaker is properly entitled to assert that p only if she knows that p. I do not want a general-audience restatement of the view; I want eight to ten prepared questions that force her to defend it at exactly the points where the specialist literature has pushed hardest, with a follow-up after each primary question keyed to the reply she is most likely to give.

Please organize the questions around these pressure points. (1) The lottery case: a speaker who asserts, before the drawing, that her fair lottery ticket lost is ordinarily judged to have asserted improperly, even though her belief is overwhelmingly likely to be true; press her on why the knowledge norm explains this while a truth norm or a norm requiring reasonable belief appears to license the assertion. (2) Selfless assertion: a speaker can reasonably and sincerely assert a proposition she does not know when asserting it is the only way to secure an important good, and many have the intuition that the assertion is not improper; ask whether the knowledge norm gets this wrong, and when she distinguishes primary propriety from secondary or excusable propriety, press her on whether that distinction is independently motivated or built to match the intuitions. (3) Action-guidance: ask how the norm can govern assertion in practice, given that speakers often cannot tell whether they know — especially if the KK principle fails — and a norm that cannot be applied in deliberation looks idle. (4) Explanatory competition: ask what the knowledge norm predicts or explains that could not be matched by a truth norm or by a norm requiring assertion on epistemically probable grounds; in particular, have her explain the impropriety of Moore-paradoxical assertions such as it is raining, but I do not know that it is raining, which can be true while violating the knowledge norm. (5) The status of the norm: ask whether it is best understood as constitutive of assertion — what makes an utterance an assertion at all — or merely regulative, and press on how a constitutive reading avoids circularity and whether a merely regulative reading can still say what assertion is. (6) Graded propriety: ask how a single binary norm accommodates the evidently graded character of our verdicts about defective assertions, and whether invoking secondary or scalar factors would collapse the view into a norm of degree of justification or probability.

Write the questions so that each stands on its own for an audience that has not followed the recent literature. Begin your response directly with the first interview question; do not include any preamble, signposting, or commentary.
