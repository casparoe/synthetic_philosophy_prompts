I run a small philosophy reading group whose members have no academic background in the subject; we come to it on our own and take turns setting a study question and grading one another's answers. This week I am the one grading. Our topic was whether it is fair to hold people to rules they could not have known about, and for it we read Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologica, I-II, Question 90, Article 4, on whether promulgation belongs to the essence of law. In the body of Article 4, Aquinas writes:

"a law is imposed on others by way of a rule and measure. Now a rule or measure is imposed by being applied to those who are to be ruled and measured by it. Wherefore, in order that a law obtain the binding force which is proper to a law, it must needs be applied to the men who have to be ruled by it. Such application is made by its being notified to them by promulgation. Wherefore promulgation is necessary for the law to obtain its force."

He adds: "The natural law is promulgated by the very fact that God instilled it into man's mind so as to be known by him naturally." And, later: "Those who are not present when a law is promulgated, are bound to observe the law, in so far as it is notified or can be notified to them by others, after it has been promulgated."

Here is the question I set, together with the answer one member submitted.

Question: In your own words, explain Aquinas's argument that promulgation is necessary for a law to obtain its force. Then apply it to this case: a government punishes a person under a regulation that was never publicized and that the person had no realistic way of learning about. Would Aquinas say the person was bound by the regulation? What does the passage about people who are not present at a promulgation imply about the difference between a law's being made public and an individual's actually knowing it? Would his conclusion change if the rule at issue were a precept of the natural law rather than a human regulation?

Student's answer: "Aquinas is arguing against the idea that a law is a private command in the ruler's head. A law is a rule or measure of human action, and a measure does not actually measure anything until it is applied to the thing measured; a ruler left in a drawer is not, at that moment, doing any measuring. So a law can bind only if it is applied to the people it rules, and the application happens through promulgation, which is the act of making the law known to those people. That is why he says promulgation is necessary for a law to obtain its force, and why he builds it into his definition of law.

On the case, Aquinas would say the person was not bound. Because the regulation was never publicized and the person had no realistic way to learn about it, it never reached them, so it never became a binding rule for them. He is strict here: a law binds a person only once it has actually been notified to them and they are aware of it. If the state punishes someone in these circumstances, it is treating an un-promulgated command as a law, and that is exactly what he says cannot happen.

The quoted phrase 'notified or can be notified to them' is consistent with my reading. It does not mean indirect notice or constructive availability is enough; it just covers the case of people who later actually receive the message. For Aquinas, the line between a law's being made public and its actually being known collapses, because promulgation is successful notification to each individual.

On natural law, the conclusion would not change. Since natural law is law in the fullest sense, it must satisfy the promulgation requirement like any other law; if a precept of natural law has not been made known to a particular person, then for that person it has not been promulgated, and so it cannot bind them. The overall result in both cases is that it is unjust to punish someone for violating a rule they could not have known."
