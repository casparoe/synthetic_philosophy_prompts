I'm a PhD student in historical linguistics with a side interest in metaphysics, and I want to stress-test a standard puzzle. Take the Ship of Theseus, but change the replaced parts from timbers of a single ship to features of a natural language—its lexicon, phonology, and morphosyntax—as the Latin of the late Roman period is gradually transmitted and reanalyzed into what we now call Italian over the better part of a millennium. Add two assumptions foreign to the original: each generation acquires the language from the previous one with only slight differences, so adjacent generations remain mutually intelligible; and there is no original design, owner, or institutional standard to anchor identity across the change. Run the standard positions on Theseus through this version—material continuity, spatiotemporal continuity, sortal or functional identity, and the view that there is simply no determinate fact of the matter—and say which survive when the substrate is a socially transmitted grammar rather than a single artifact. Then say which features of the language case are doing the real work, and what this implies, if anything, about whether asking when Latin became Italian has a determinate answer.
