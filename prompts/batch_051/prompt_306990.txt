You are a professional philosopher specialising in personhood and moral status. I have been tracking the settled verdicts of a colleague in her seminar on African and Western theories of personhood, and I want to know what her unstated principle predicts for a case we have not yet discussed. Her verdicts so far: a healthy adult human is a person; a human newborn is a person; an adult chimpanzee that passes the mirror self-recognition test is not a person; a patient in a permanent vegetative state who is attentively cared for by her family is a person; a limited-liability corporation is not a person; and a person with end-stage dementia who no longer recognises her relatives is a person. The new case is a woman who died two years ago and is still directly addressed, remembered, and ritually honoured by her descendants as an ancestor. Work through the candidate rules that could jointly fit all six verdicts, including at least a species-membership rule and a relational, Ubuntu-style rule on which personhood is constituted or sustained by a being's place in webs of human relationship rather than by biological properties alone. State carefully what each live rule predicts about the deceased woman. Then give your best prediction of the colleague's verdict, explain why you prefer it, and name one further case, not among those listed, that would most cleanly settle which rule she is really running.
