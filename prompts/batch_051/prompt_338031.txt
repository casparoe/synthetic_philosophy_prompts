I teach a course on probability and judgment, and I propose the following criteria for when a single-case probability claim — a statement of the form "the probability that this particular event happens is p" — is well founded. It must (1) be backed by a relative frequency in a reference class that contains the individual case; (2) use the narrowest reference class for which there are enough reliable data to estimate that frequency; (3) not omit any known feature of the individual case that is causally relevant to the outcome and that would move the estimate if it were included; and (4) be stable across the alternative reference classes that are salient for the decision the probability is meant to inform.

Now consider a judge who assigns a 0.35 probability that a particular defendant will fail to appear for trial, on the basis of a risk instrument built on a reference class defined by current charge, age band, and number of prior failures to appear. The judge also knows from an interview that the defendant has held a full-time job for several years and is the sole caretaker of two children; neither factor is in the instrument, and local data are too sparse to build a reliable class that includes them. Does the judge's probability claim qualify as well founded by my criteria? Go criterion by criterion, and then say whether the case shows that one of the criteria is too strict, unnecessary, or in tension with another.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: one item for each of the four criteria and one final item on whether the criteria need revising.
