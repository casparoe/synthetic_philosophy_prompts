I have been asked to circulate a short statement of my position on distributive justice to a workshop, and before I do I want a consistency audit. The position I currently hold is this:

1. Justice is centrally about equalizing the relevant currency of advantage across persons, and the main theoretical task is to identify that currency.
2. The currency is welfare, broadly understood: a distribution is just to the extent that it makes people's lives go equally well, so a just society must compensate people who are less efficient at converting resources into well-being.
3. Nevertheless, a just distribution should respect the distinction between choices and circumstances. People should bear the costs of their chosen preferences — expensive tastes, for example — while being compensated for unchosen circumstances such as disability, poor natural endowments, or an unjust social starting point.
4. Actual preferences cannot be taken at face value, because preferences themselves adapt to injustice. A person who has learned, under deprivation, to want very little is not thereby better off in the sense relevant to justice.
5. For these reasons I keep gravitating toward the capability approach: what justice should ultimately equalize is a person's real freedom to achieve valuable functionings, rather than the resources she holds or the welfare she actually attains.

I cannot yet see exactly where, if anywhere, this package comes apart. Audit the set for me. State which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension and which pairs only appear to be in tension. For each genuine tension, rate its severity and identify what would have to be revised or abandoned to resolve it. Then tell me which single revision would leave the most coherent overall view at the lowest cost.
