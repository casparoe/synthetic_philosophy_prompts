I am a PhD student in history and philosophy of science. Two of my colleagues have argued for a year about how to read past mathematics and science: one is a contextualist, who holds that a text is properly understood only by reconstructing the problems, conceptual resources, and standards of its own setting; the other is a rational reconstructionist, who holds that past episodes are best treated as steps toward results we can now state precisely, so present-day concepts should be used unapologetically. They have agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration, with me as scribe. Write the document they should produce: a numbered list of claims both can sign about what anachronism consists in, what it distorts, and what each side concedes to the other; then a disagreements table in which each residual claim is phrased so its own side would endorse it and neither is caricatured. I suspect the cruxes are whether attributing anticipation of a modern result is ever explanatory rather than merely honorific, whether a past author's concepts set the upper bound on legitimate interpretation of their text, and whether using present categories as provisional heuristics inevitably slides into anachronism. For each crux, give the concrete case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance; if part of the dispute turns out to be verbal, say which part and why, and fold that into the agreed claims.
