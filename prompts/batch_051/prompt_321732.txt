I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and for years I've been reporting on the debate over whether private cities, charter cities, and seasteading allow people to truly opt out of states. The debate strikes me as stuck in a way ordinary empirical arguments are not. Exit advocates argue that states are service providers with unusually bad terms of service; that special economic zones are natural experiments showing that jurisdiction-level competition changes governance; and that a private city or seastead should be judged against the actually existing states it would compete with, not against an imaginary standard of full sovereignty. Critics reply that every charter city and seastead is ultimately a delegation of power by a territorial state, which remains the guarantor of the entire arrangement and can revoke the delegation whenever it chooses; that what is marketed as exit is really regulatory arbitrage that lets mobile capital shop among rules while ordinary citizens remain fixed in place; and that no proposed scheme has ever escaped the reach of some sovereign enforcement.

What puzzles me most is that both sides cite the same handful of cases — Shenzhen, Dubai's free zones, colonial Hong Kong, flags of convenience in shipping — and each side treats them as obvious confirmation of its own view. After covering this standoff for years, I've formed a working hypothesis that I'd like tested rather than confirmed. My hypothesis is that "opt out of the state" is doing two different jobs for the two camps: one side means exit from a particular jurisdiction's rules while remaining inside some enforceable legal order, and the other means exit from rule as such, so the argument is secretly about whether legitimate authority requires ongoing consent or only the ability to leave. But it could equally be a hidden empirical disagreement about whether jurisdiction-level competition disciplines states or merely sorts capital, or a dispute about what would count as evidence for a successful private city, or about whose baseline assumptions about sovereignty, coercion, and legal validity should carry the burden of proof.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict or a survey of positions. What kind of disagreement has this become? Break it down: which components are verbal, which are normative bedrock that reasonable people may reject, which are empirical hypotheses that could in principle be settled but in practice never are, and which are distinct questions mistaken for a single one? Then, given that diagnosis, say what would count as real progress — a distinction, an institutional experiment, an empirical test, a reformulated question — or, if the honest answer is that no one should expect the disagreement to move, say why. Please write the response in American English.
