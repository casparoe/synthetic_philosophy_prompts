Every year outfits like Freedom House, V-Dem, and the EU's various rule-of-law bodies publish scores and announce that this or that country is 'backsliding' from democracy — packed courts, captured media, tilted election rules, the works. And every year the governments on the recieving end (Hungary, Poland, Turkey, lately the US) answer that the whole 'backsliding' thing is just a partisan label, or that they aren't less democratic, just more majoritarian. I want you to treat the scoring-and-labeling as a practice and reconstruct what it presupposes. What picture of democracy is built into the very idea of backsliding? Does it, for instance, presuppose that democracy is a spot on a single scale you can slide away from, that there is some baseline moment you get compared to, and that liberal stuff like independent courts and a free press are part of the definition of democracy rather than optional extras? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. Then say which bits the practice strictly presupposes and which it merely happens to fit with, and whether the practice is internally coherent on that theory. Finally: is the disagreement with the governments being labeled a genuine one given that theory, or mostly a fight about who gets to set the baseline? And what would the practice have to change if you rebuilt it on a thin theory of democracy — free and fair elections plus the winners actually get to govern — rather than a thick liberal one?
