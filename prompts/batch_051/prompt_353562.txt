You are a professional philosopher working in metaphysics and modal logic. Start by listing the candidate criteria you take seriously—for example, criteria based on the gametes, the genome, the zygote, the causal history, or psychology—before settling on one to defend.

I want a working definition of a person's individual origin: a criterion for deciding, of any merely possible person described in a scenario, whether that person would be me. The criterion should get the clear cases right, give a definite verdict on the borderline cases, and defend any verdict that is surprising.

Here are the cases. For the sake of argument, treat each as possible.

(a) Same zygote, different circumstances. The person who would have developed from the very sperm-and-egg union that produced me, but carried in a different womb, in a different country, and born in a different year. I am confident this person would be me.

(b) Different gamete, different DNA. The person who would have developed from the same egg but a different sperm from my father, where that sperm carried different DNA—an ordinary half-sibling of mine. I am confident this person would not be me.

(c) Different gamete, same DNA. The person who would have developed from a numerically different sperm from my father, where we stipulate that this other sperm happens to carry exactly the DNA of the sperm that produced me. The resulting person would have my entire genome but would come from a different gamete. I do not know what to say here.

(d) Clone. A clone produced from one of my adult cells and carried to term. I lean toward saying this person would not be me, but I cannot yet say what explains the difference between this case and (a).

(e) Psychological duplicate. A person molecularly built to have my exact psychology—memories, dispositions, and projects—while I continue to exist unchanged. I lean toward saying this person would not be me.

(f) Twinning. The two people who would have developed if the zygote from which I grew had split into identical twins in the first days. I do not know whether one of them would be me, both, or neither.

(g) Frozen gametes. The person who would have developed if the very sperm and egg that produced me had been extracted from my parents, frozen for a decade, and only then united. I think this person would be me, but I want to test whether your criterion tracks the gametes themselves, the act of conception, or something else.

Propose your criterion, run it through all seven cases, and say which of your verdicts is least secure. Then either revise the criterion or defend the surprising verdicts. Finally, answer the underlying question directly: could I have come from a different sperm and egg, and what exactly does different have to mean—different DNA, a different gamete, a different causal history, or different parents—for the resulting person not to be me? In passing, say which version of origin essentialism, if any, your criterion commits me to.
