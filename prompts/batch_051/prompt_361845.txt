I've been trying to get clear on what Pearl's causal hierarchy actually licenses, and I'd appreciate a careful accuracy audit of a summary I read on a rationalist-adjacent blog. The post presented the framework as follows:

"Pearl distinguishes three rungs of causal reasoning. Rung one is association: anything expressible in terms of a joint probability distribution, such as correlation and conditional expectation. Rung two is intervention: what happens when you set a variable rather than merely observe it, written do(X = x). Rung three is counterfactuals: what would have happened, even in retrospect, had some variable taken a value it did not. Pearl's central claim is that the rungs are strictly ordered and one-directional: data from rung one cannot answer rung-two questions unless causal assumptions are brought in, and data from rung two cannot answer rung-three questions because counterfactuals require assumptions about unobserved mechanisms. Structural causal models implement this picture by giving each variable a structural equation and requiring the causal graph to be a directed acyclic graph, so feedback loops are ruled out by modeling choice. Within this framework, the do-calculus is a complete set of rules for deciding from the graph alone whether any rung-two query is identifiable from observational data; when identification succeeds, it lets you rewrite do-expressions purely in terms of observed probabilities. The author concludes that machine-learning models trained on passive data are stuck on rung one, and that any system capable of answering 'what would happen if we changed X?' must be running something equivalent to the do-calculus."

I find the three-rung picture intuitively plausible, and the claim that observational data alone cannot answer intervention questions without some causal assumptions seems right. But the claims that structural causal models exclude cycles by definition, that do-calculus is a complete decision procedure for all rung-two identifiability questions, and that answering intervention questions requires do-calculus feel as if they might be overstatements. Which parts would someone who actually works with structural causal models accept, which parts would they qualify or reject, and does the final conclusion about machine-learning systems survive a corrected version of the summary?
