I'm a coach who works with mid-career professionals on soft skills—reliability, patience, listening, not overpromising. I've been rereading Ben Franklin's Autobiography, and the part where he describes his plan for moral perfection keeps nagging at me. He says he realized trying to fix all his faults at once would scatter his attention, so he made a little book with thirteen virtues and worked on just one each week, letting the others slide unless they were egregious, and he ordered the list so that mastering one would make the next easier—temperance first, for example, because it would help with silence, order, and so on. That idea feels like it should translate into something my clients could actually use, but they are not Enlightenment printers: they don't have a daily ledger habit, they often have a boss and a family, and they sometimes come to me in crisis mode needing to fix five things at once. I need a reusable procedure—maybe a flowchart or a checklist—that a coach can follow to decide how to sequence a client's improvement targets when there are several, when to insist on Franklin's one-at-a-time rule, when to allow two or three targets, and how to define mastery of a soft skill in a way that isn't just vibes. It has to work in a 50-minute session model where the coach sees the client every other week, and the client fills out a one-page self-report between sessions. Give me the procedure with two or three worked examples, including one where the procedure tells the coach to do something the coach or client won't want to do, and flag the points where the procedure can't decide and a human has to.
