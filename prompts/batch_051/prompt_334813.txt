I spent most of my career in environmental science and only recently came to philosophy, and I keep returning to Moloch and multipolar traps: many parties, each making locally sensible moves, jointly produce an outcome none of them wants. I run a continuing-education seminar for retired scientists and science teachers, about twenty people, and would like a two-hour session in which the participants feel that trap rather than hear it described. Please design a runnable game or simulation, using nothing beyond slips of paper and a whiteboard, in which small teams drive a shared system toward a decline that everyone can see coming. The feature I most want preserved is that transparency does not dissolve the problem: even with full information, each team's locally sensible move is to keep competing, because the team that unilaterally restrains itself simply falls behind. It should not reduce to a two-player, one-shot prisoner's dilemma; the escalation should compound over several rounds. The final half-hour should be a debrief that maps concrete moments in the play onto the structure of a multipolar trap and a race to the bottom, says what predictably goes wrong when the activity is run and how to recover, and is frank about which aspect of the real phenomenon—perhaps the slow, impersonal, institutionally embedded quality of a Moloch-like process—a two-hour game cannot reproduce and must be supplied in discussion.
