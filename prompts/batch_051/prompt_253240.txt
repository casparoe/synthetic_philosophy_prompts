One of my friends is the kind of person who has actually read all of Aristotle's psychology, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer what she means by phantasia — usually translated imagination, though she says that translation is misleading — from the calls she makes, the way you'd infer the generator from a pile of labeled examples. So far she's said:

- watching a tomato on the counter: not phantasia, just perception.
- shutting your eyes and picturing that tomato: phantasia.
- remembering the layout of your grandmother's kitchen years later: phantasia; she says remembering is having a phantasm that stands in for the original thing, like a copy.
- dreaming you're being chased: phantasia.
- a dog that saw a rabbit vanish behind a bush and then runs over to the bush: phantasia; she says that's the clearest case, an animal being moved by an appearance of something absent.
- an earworm: phantasia, a quasi-perceptual leftover from past hearing.
- jerking your hand off a hot stove: not phantasia; perception and desire, no appearance of anything absent.

The trouble is I can fit at least three rules to those verdicts. (a) Phantasia is occurrent quasi-perceptual imagery, pictures and tunes in the head. (b) Phantasia is whatever supplies the phantasm that thinking has to operate on, so any episode of thinking involves phantasia even if the person reports no imagery. (c) Phantasia is an appearance of something not currently perceived, the kind of thing that can guide desire and locomotion, whether or not it feels like a picture.

The cases I actually want to predict: she knows someone with lifelong aphantasia, no mental images, no inner voice, who is nevertheless a perfectly good abstract reasoner. First, this person works through a proof about prime numbers and insists there was no image, no inner speech, just the sense that the step follows. Second, the same person walks to a café they visited once, without picturing the route, just knowing which way to turn.

Would my friend say phantasia is involved in each of those two? I'm less interested in the yes/no then in the rule she's implicitly running, which other rules fit her track record just as well, and which of the two cases (or a third you'd add) would best tell the rules apart. If the track record underdetermines it, say what she'd be committing to with each option.
