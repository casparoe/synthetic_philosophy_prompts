I retired a few years ago from a career in molecular pharmacology, and I've only recently started reading philosophy and decision theory seriously. During a long argument with a friend who works on reinforcement-learning safety, I got pointed toward Goodhart's law and the way it shows up in specification gaming: a reward function or metric is a proxy, agents find the extremal behaviors that satisfy the proxy, and the proxy stops tracking what you actualy wanted. I couldn't shake the feeling that I'd spent forty years watching the same phenomenon in biological systems. Chronic receptor stimulation leads to downregulation or desensitization, so the same drug dose that once gave a response no longer does; exposure to an antibiotic selects for resistance, so the concentration that killed the bug no longer kills it; a fishery managed by total tonnage selects for smaller, faster-maturing fish, so tonnage can look stable while the stock and ecosystem degrade. In each case, the measurable target (drug effect, kill rate, tonnes hauled) is not the thing you ultimately care about (cellular function, curing the infection, a healthy fish stock), and the system adapts so the two come apart.

My question is whether this is a deep structural identity or just loose patter-matching around the word "adaptation." If it is genuine, I'd like to know the mapping: what corresponds to the metric, the optimizing agent, and the loss of correlation in each case. And if it's genuine, can results transfer from medicine to institutional design or AI? For instance, clinicians cope with tolerance through drug holidays, cycling, combinations, or by measuring an underlying biomarker rather than symptoms. Do those strategies translate into something about how to design metrics that don't rot—metric holidays, rotating metrics, combining metrics, measuring nearer to the true objective? What would have to be true on both sides for such transfers to be legitimate rather than seductive? Finally, tell me something specific the analogy would predict that is probably false, because I want to see where the structure breaks. And be explicit about what would change your mind: which point would make you conclude that the parallel is superficial after all, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
