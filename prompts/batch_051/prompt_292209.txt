I'm a high school student, and I've ended up with one foot in my school's philosophy club and the other in a self-taught systems-programming project, and I keep noticing what looks like the same structure in two places. I want a committed ruling on whether the resemblance is real.

The first place is transcendental arguments. As I understand the strategy, a transcendental argument answers a skeptical claim not by giving evidence against that claim but by showing that the very performance of making that claim already depends on the thing the claim denies. For example, an argument whose conclusion is that there is no unified thinking subject still has to be something: a single claim, set out and evaluated over time by the person making it. The act of arguing contains the presupposition that the conclusion says is unavailable. So the skeptic is caught in a performative bind—not just wrong, but unable to make the denial without already relying on what it denies. I know different philosophers run this strategy in different directions and that there is serious dispute about how strong the result can be; I mean the general skeleton.

The second place is compiler bootstrapping. If I am writing a compiler for a language L and I write that compiler in L itself, then I cannot build or test the compiler unless I already have a working compiler for L. If I propose to replace my current compiler with a better one, I have to trust the current one enough to build the replacement with it. The attempt to get outside L in order to check or improve L keeps using L. The first compiler cannot be compiled by itself; you have to assume some existing implementation, however crude.

The mapping I can't get out of my head is this: the skeptic's claim corresponds to the proposed new compiler; the act of advancing the claim corresponds to the bootstrap process; "the denial presupposes the thing it denies" corresponds to "building a compiler for L presupposes an existing implementation of L"; and the transcendental conclusion that X is a condition of the practice corresponds to the idea that a trusted implementation is a condition of building or checking any implementation.

Is this a real identity of structure—two instances of one kind of bootstrapping argument, one epistemic and one computational—or is it a loose analogy that breaks as soon as it is pressed? My worry is that in computing the regress can bottom out outside the system, in machine code, hand-assembly, or a compiler written in another language, so the dependency is historical and practical rather than a condition of possibility. But maybe transcendental arguments have their own version of "outside," or maybe bottoming out in machine code is closer to an unargued presupposition than I think. Don't lay out the options and hand them back to me: take a definite position. If the parallel is deep, say exactly what transfers between the two cases; if it is superficial, say where it breaks and name at least one specific thing the parallel would predict about transcendental arguments or about compiler bootstrapping that turns out false.
