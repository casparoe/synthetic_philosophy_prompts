I'm on the grants committee of a small institute that funds weird early-stage projects—18-month funding, ~350 applications a year, 12 slots. Last year we did the respectable thing: every application got two reads, a 1–7 score, then a long debate about the borderline pile until we produced a full ranking. It took weeks, and by the final meeting I don't think any of us could have re-scored the same applications and gotten the same ordering. A faction on the committee wants to switch to an explicit satisficing rule: read a random calibration batch, set a clear bar for "fundable," then read in randomized order and accept the first 12 that clear the bar (overbooking a little for people who decline). The other faction says this is just lazy and that only exhaustive ranking respects genuine differences between applications. I've read enough rationalist-adjacent stuff about bounded rationality to doubt that "just try harder and read everything more carefully" is obviously right, but I also don't trust the 37% secretary-problem rule because our situation defintely doesn't satisfy its assumptions.

Please sort these six procedures into tiers by how much you'd trust them to maximize expected quality of the funded cohort (not merely avoid disasters), assuming each gets the same total reviewer-hours we spent last year: (a) full exhaustive ranking; (b) the satisficing rule above; (c) the 37% secretary-problem rule (skim a 37% sample, then take the first application better than anything in that sample, falling back to the last one if none beats the sample); (d) random lottery among applications above a bare minimum; (e) bracket-style pairwise voting; (f) a hybrid—satisficing to make a shortlist of maybe 40, then full ranking only within the shortlist. Give each a tier and a sentence or two for why it lands there, and if you think one of these is dominated by a nearby variant, say so.
