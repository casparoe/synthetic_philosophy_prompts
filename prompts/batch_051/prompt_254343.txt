I'm putting together a feature on why we treat small evasions in public life as less bad than outright lies, and I keep getting stuck on the definitional end of it. The standard line people reach for is that a lie is a false statement made with an intent to deceive. But that falls apart on a bunch of real cases, and I want a working definition of *lying* that sorts them properly—or at least a definition of the lying/misleading boundary that earns its keep.

Here are the cases I care about. A senator asked whether her office contacted the regulator about a merger says "No" when it did: textbook lie. The same senator says "I have not spoken to the CEO about this matter," which is literally true because they texted and worked through staff: textbook misleading-without-lying. A spokesperson says "The report contains no evidence of fraud" when the report did find fraud but the lab destroyed the relevant samples, so the sentence is true only in a strained sense. A witness says "I did not see her leave the building" believing it is false, when in fact she left through the parking garage. Someone asked "Do you like my painting?" answers "It really fills the room," meaning it is huge and ugly. A leader denies an obvious massacre on live TV in front of an audience that knows he's lying. And a car salesman, asked "Has this car been in an accident?", says "No, it's a single-owner trade-in" when the car was totaled in a flood.

Please give me the definition upfront, then run it through all seven cases and tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. The thing I most want to know is whether the moral line people draw between lying and merely misleading is actually tracking your definition, or whether it's a seperate judgment that happens to ride on top of it. It's fine if your answer ends with "ordinary speakers are split on this one, and here's why."
