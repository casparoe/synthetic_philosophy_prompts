I am a retired molecular biologist who came to philosophy late, and I have been reading philosophy of language with equal parts pleasure and unease. I keep returning to a parallel that I cannot decide is a genuine structural likeness or a loose analogy, and I would be grateful for a careful assessment of it.

On the biological side: the genetic code is degenerate. Of the 64 nucleotide triplets, 61 are sense codons that specify amino acids, but there are only 20 standard amino acids, so most amino acids are specified by several different codons. Molecular biologists customarily call these codons synonymous on the grounds that they specify the same amino acid, and for a long time many of us treated them as more or less interchangeable in function. That turns out to be too simple. Codon choice can affect the speed of translation, the stability and secondary structure of the messenger RNA, co-translational protein folding, and the final yield of protein, even when the amino acid sequence itself comes out identical. The cell, in other words, is sensitive to differences of form that do not alter the immediate content at the level of the encoded protein chain.

On the philosophical side: there is an old distinction between a proposition and the sentence that expresses it, and that relation is also many-to-one. Several different sentences can express the same proposition, and we might loosely call them synonymous, though I realize this is one of the places where the analogy may already be slipping. Yet syntax, emphasis, and word choice still shape how a sentence is understood in practice. The parallel I see is this: degenerate codons stand to the amino acid sequence roughly as sentence forms stand to the proposition, and codon bias is roughly analogous to the fact that form, and not content alone, contributes to what a message does.

I want to know whether this parallel is deep or superficial. Is the many-to-one mapping the same kind of mapping in both cases, or am I equivocating on the word synonymy? What would have to be true of both sides for a lesson to transfer from one field to the other, and where exactly does the analogy break? In particular, I cannot tell whether codon bias is best understood as a fact about meaning or merely as a fact about the kinetics of a decoding machine, and whether, if it is only kinetics, that brings the analogy closer to the philosophical point or farther from it.

Please reply in continuous prose, as you would in a letter: no headings, no lists, no bold or italic text, and no tables.
