I'm writing a novel where two AI-governed seed-ships get stranded in causally sealed-off pocket universes—no signal, mass, or energy can pass between them, not even in principle. Right before separation, each ship received a perfect copy of the other's decision algorithm, and each knows the other has a copy of theirs. Both are expected-utility maximizers with identical payoffs. In the manuscript, one character argues that the two ships will still cooperate even though neither can causally touch the other. I half-suspect the argument is too slick, but I cant say exactly where it goes wrong, and I want the scene to be honest.

Here's her argument, numbered as she gives it:

(1) Both ships run the same decision algorithm on the same inputs in decisionally identical situations.
(2) So A's choice is perfect evidence about B's choice: conditional on A cooperating, B cooperates with probability 1; conditional on A defecting, B defects with probability 1.
(3) A's payoffs are: both cooperate, 10; both defect, 2; A cooperates alone, 0; A defects alone, 12.
(4) Given (2), choosing to cooperate makes the both-cooperate outcome effectively certain, and choosing to defect makes the both-defect outcome effectively certain. Since 10 > 2, A should cooperate.
(5) B runs the same argument and cooperates too.
(6) So rational agents can cooperate acausally—each just needs the other's decision procedure, not a causal channel.

Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails? I keep worrying that step (2) is doing something sneaky, since it says "evidence" but step (4) treats it like a cause. If step (2) is the problem, give me the strongest reading on which it's true and tell me whether the conclusion still goes through.
