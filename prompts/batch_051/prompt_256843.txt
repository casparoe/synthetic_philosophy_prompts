I'm trying to sort out which situations I count as genuine multipolar traps — "Moloch" cases, where each party's individually sensible move adds up to an outcome none of them wants — and which I don't. Here are my gut verdicts, stated as plainly as I can.

Doping in professional cycling: each rider who dopes gains an edge; if everyone dopes, the ranking is roughly what it would have been, every rider bears the health costs, and no one can stop unilaterally without losing. Verdict: trap.

Corporate tax competition: each country cuts its corporate rate to attract investment; when all cut, investment does not relocate much but revenue collapses. Verdict: trap.

Grade inflation at selective colleges: each instructor grades a little more generously to protect their students and their own teaching evaluations; when all do, grades carry less information. Verdict: trap.

Price competition between grocery chains: each chain cuts prices to win customers; when all cut, margins shrink. Verdict: not a trap — customers are genuinely better off, and I do not think the chains were entitled to the old margins.

Harder training in professional sport: each athlete trains harder to win; when all train harder, the ranking is unchanged and everyone has less rest. Verdict: not a trap, though I cannot say yet why this differs from doping.

Rising conversation volume in a crowded restaurant: each table talks louder to be heard; when all do, no one can hear any better and the room is miserable. Verdict: trap, if a minor one.

Routine antibiotic use in factory farming: each producer uses antibiotics to keep animals alive under crowded conditions; collectively they breed resistance that threatens human and animal health. Verdict: trap.

Competing on customer service: each firm staffs up to answer calls faster; when all do, customers come to expect faster service and firms' costs rise. Verdict: not a trap — faster service is a real improvement.

I also have a tentative principle: a multipolar trap requires (1) each actor's move to be a best response to what the others are doing, (2) the resulting equilibrium to be worse for all the actors than the cooperative alternative, and (3) no actor to be able to exit unilaterally without cost. But I can already see that this principle makes harder training and customer-service competition count as traps, which I do not want, and it does not distinguish tax competition from price competition, which I feel are different. So either the principle needs extra clauses, or some of my verdicts have to go.

What principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures the pattern here? State it precisely, run it through the cases, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and say what it predicts about cases I have not settled: countries adopting cosmetic green-regulation "quality labels" to attract ESG-conscious investors; the gradual escalation of headline sensationalism among online news outlets; and firms releasing increasingly capable open-weight AI models to stay relevant while collectively making it harder to slow dangerous capabilities.
