In my philosophy course I've been studying deontological constraints, and I find the central claim compelling: there are things I may not do no matter what the consequences, so I may not murder one innocent person even to prevent five other people from being murdered. The appeal of this view is that it refuses to treat individuals as counters in a calculation and captures the sense that my own agency is governed by prohibitions rather than by a standing duty to minimize bad states of the world. But I also have an intuition I can't shake: if murder is wrong, I should want as little murder as possible, and a prohibition that leaves me standing by while the number of murders grows seems to prize the rule over the very people the rule is meant to protect. I've seen this tension called the paradox of deontology. I am not looking for a verdict on which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be debunked, what it would take for the constraint to be revised or abandoned, and whether there is a reading of "wrong" — perhaps one sense applies to my act and another to the state of affairs — on which the prohibition and the horror at unnecessary death are compatible after all.
