I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and I need advice on an editorial decision I have to make in the next two weeks. A young staff writer I mentored has admitted to fabricating quotations in three of her essays and to inventing an interview in a feature that won a small award; she says she did it because she couldn't get access in time and convinced herself the invented lines captured what the people would have said. She has offered to resign quietly. I can take the quiet path: run discreet corrections and private apologies, with her name never attached to the fabrications and no public stain on her record. Or I can take the public path several of my colleagues are demanding: publish a full account, name her, detail each fabrication, and make the correction a permanent public record in the field. One camp says only public exposure makes the writer and the institution truly take responsibility; the other says public naming is mostly humiliation, and that real responsibility is what a person recognizes and repairs in private. I'm not looking for an employment-law or public-relations answer. I want the best philosophical argument for which of these two pictures of accountability is more defensible, and which choice is better for her, for the readers, and for the magazine. Please answer in American English.
