I'm a retired hydrogeologist and Ive only recently started reading philosophy seriously, mostly Grice and the back-and-forth around him. The thing I keep chewing on is the difference between what the words themselves say and what the speaker manages to convey by saying them in a particular setting — the implicature stuff. It's made me reread a clause from my old professional society's code of ethics. It said: "No member shall knowingly issue a statement to the public that is false or misleading." Here's a situation I doubt the people who wrote that line ever pictured. A colleague of mine was hired by a town to test private wells near an old tannery. At the town hall she reports: "The mean arsenic concentration across the wells we sampled was 9.8 micrograms per liter." The state action level is 10. She doesnt mention that the wells ranged from below detection up to one well at 310 micrograms per liter, because the contract only required her to report the mean. A roomful of people reasonably conclude the water is fine. Her sentence was true. I want you to work through exactly five ways of reading the clause on that case: (1) the plain words, (2) the purpose of the rule, (3) what the drafters would have said if asked about exactly this case, (4) what a reasonable member of the public heard her say, and (5) the Gricean thought that she can be answerable for a false implicature even when her literal utterance is true. Give me exactly five numbered points, one per approach, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion.
