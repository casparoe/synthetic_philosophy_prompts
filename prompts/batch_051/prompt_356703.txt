I'm giving a seminar paper next month to a mixed audience of diplomatic historians and philosophers working on collective responsibility. My thesis is that the outbreak of the First World War is best understood as a case of differentiated state responsibility: Imperial Germany's civilian and military leadership bears a distinct and disproportionate share of causal and moral responsibility for the escalation of the July Crisis into general war in 1914, but that responsibility attaches to an identifiable network of decision-makers and to the political culture that sustained them—not to the German people or the German nation as a whole. My audience is not hostile, but it mostly accepts the post-Fischer picture of the war as a systemic failure in which all the great powers share blame, and it is suspicious of assigning degrees of national blame at all. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way a diplomatic historian or a philosopher of collective responsibility would put them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I should hope nobody asks, and end by telling me which objection, if it survived my reply, would most force me to soften my thesis.
