The sphex wasp does this famous thing where it drags a paralyzed cricket to its burrow, leaves it at the entrance, goes inside to check, and if you move the cricket a couple inches it comes back out, re-positions it, and goes inside to check again — basically forever. The standard moral in the free-will debate was that the wasp's behavior isn't flexible in the relevant sense: it can't step outside it's own routine and notice the loop, and that's what separates a sphexish mechanism from an agent with something worth calling freedom. But now we have LLMs. At inference they're a fixed mechanical rule — pick the next token, no weight updates, no stepping back to notice that the routine is failing — and yet their behavior looks wildly flexible and context-sensitive, far beyond the wasp. Does the sphexishness argument survive this? Which premise is actually under pressure: the idea that flexibility is the mark of freedom, or the idea that a fixed underlying rule can't produce real flexibility? And is there a repaired version of the sphexish/antisphexish distinction that still does the work the original argument was meant to do?
