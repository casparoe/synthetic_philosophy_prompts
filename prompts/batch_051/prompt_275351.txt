Consider a proposed constitutional reform of the void-for-vagueness doctrine in criminal law. A jurisdiction adopts the following "citizen-comprehension gate" for all new criminal statutes. Before any new criminal statute may take effect, the enacting legislature must submit its final text to an independent Office of Statutory Clarity. The Office drafts forty short scenarios, each describing concrete conduct in ordinary language and each designed to probe the boundaries of the statute; the scenarios may not quote the statute or use its specialized terms. A panel of twenty-eight registered voters, sampled to match the adult population on age, sex, education, and primary language, then reads the statute once and, without conferring, answers for each scenario whether the described conduct is clearly punishable under the statute, clearly not punishable under the statute, or whether an ordinary person could not confidently determine the answer. If fewer than twenty-two of the twenty-eight panelists place any single scenario in the same one of the two "clear" categories, the statute is void in its entirety. The scenarios, the panel's answers, and the aggregate results are published in a public register. A statute that fails may be re-enacted only with new text. For a statute that passes, a later prosecution is barred if it concerns conduct materially similar to a scenario the panel found unclear; if the conduct is materially similar to a scenario the panel found clear, the panel's classification is treated as conclusive on the question of fair notice. Existing criminal statutes have five years to pass the same procedure or they lapse.

Analyze how this mechanism would actually behave rather than whether it is a good idea in the abstract. Address at least the following: (a) how it would reshape legislative drafting of criminal statutes, and which kinds of statutory language it would select for and against; (b) who would acquire leverage over what gets criminalized, and how that leverage would be exercised or gamed by legislators, the Office, interest groups, prosecutors, and defendants; (c) the second-order effects on the size and texture of criminal codes, on plea bargaining, on judicial interpretation, and on genuinely novel conduct that resembles nothing in the register; and (d) the deepest failure modes of the mechanism, and which if any could be fixed without abandoning the comprehension test. Where possible, work through concrete examples such as a harassment statute, a fraud statute, and a reckless-driving statute.
