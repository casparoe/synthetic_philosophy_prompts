I teach moral philosophy, and for roughly a decade I have watched a particular methodological argument about simplicity in ethical theory fail to move. One camp treats theoretical simplicity as a substantive virtue in ethics: a theory with few principles, few posited moral properties, and strong explanatory unity can legitimately overturn our intuitive judgments about particular cases. On this view, a counterintuitive implication is a cost to be weighed, not a decisive refutation. The other camp treats fit with considered moral judgments, including judgments about particular cases, as the primary constraint on a moral theory. Its members regard simplicity as at most a tiebreaker between theories that already fit those judgments equally well. They see the first camp as defending elegant falsehoods; the first camp sees them as retailing a gerrymandered list of intuitions under the name of a theory.

The argument reaches the same impasse almost every time. The simplicity-first side accuses the intuitions-first side of making ad hoc adjustments so that no possible case can count against their view, and of having no explanation for why the intuitions hang together. The intuitions-first side accuses the simplicity-first side of protecting a doctrine by discounting the very data an ethical theory is supposed to account for. New examples, distinctions, and formal results do not move anyone, and I now suspect the two sides are not merely weighting the same theoretical virtues differently.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is really about the aim of ethical theory. On one conception, a moral theory is in the business of explaining and predicting our considered judgments, roughly as a scientific theory explains observations; on the other, it is in the business of identifying the underlying normative structure that produces those judgments, where the judgments themselves may be fallible appearances or even distortions of that structure. If that is right, the two camps are answering different questions under one heading, which would explain why the debate stays stalled. But I can see alternative diagnoses: it might be a substantive disagreement about whether intuitive fit is evidence at all, a difference about how much theoretical elegance may cost in terms of moral error, or a difference in what each side treats as the burden of proof.

I want a diagnosis, with reasons, rather than a verdict for either camp. What kind of disagreement is this really: verbal, substantive, methodological, or two different research questions sharing a name? Support the diagnosis by reference to what each side actually treats as data and as a constraint. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that could reframe the debate, an argument that could in principle move one of the camps, a formal or empirical result, or a reason to conclude that the debate cannot move and should be dissolved or replaced by another question. If my hypothesis is wrong, say why. Please answer in American English.
