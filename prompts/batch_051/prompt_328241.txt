I'm a novelist, and I'm working on a book whose main character runs a small sanctuary for farmed animals. She's a freegan in a pretty particular way: she won't pay for animal products, ever, but she will eat eggs from the rescued hens and use their wool, because those would otherwise just get tossed or composted. Her line is that what makes an animal product wrong is that consuming it creates demand for more exploitation, and a true byproduct — an egg that'll be laid no matter what, wool that has to be shorn for the animal's health anyway — doesn't do that. So supermarket eggs are out, but eggs from a spent battery hen are fine; buying a wool sweater is out, but spinning wool from a rescued sheep is fine. She actually thinks it's better to use the stuff than to let it rot.

Here's what I want: I've been arguing with people about this, and they keep saying it feels slippery and a bit to convenient, but I can't tell whether the view is actually wrong or whether eating eggs just trips a taboo that overrides the logic. So play it straight. What would genuinely count against this position? I want the strongest version of the demand-side objection — how confident can we be that personal consumption of rescued-animal byproducts doesn't leak back into markets, norms, or the economics of the sanctuary itself — and the strongest version of the social-meaning or complicity objection: does eating a rescued hen's eggs express something that undercuts what the character is trying to do? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely akward, and give me an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and, if so, whether that's a problem for a view like this.
