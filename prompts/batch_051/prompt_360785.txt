I've been going back and forth with an archaeologist friend about what you can actually infer about past minds from the stuff they left in the ground. Her line is that a careful enough excavation design can seperate a burial that reflects a genuine belief in an afterlife from a burial that's mostly about status competition among the living, or about grief, or about ritual obligation. I'm more sceptical: the dead didn't fill out questionaires, and the same objects can end up in a grave for completely different reasons.

To make the argument concrete, imagine a small settlement where some graves have everyday tools laid in a tidy pattern around the body while other graves in the same group have almost nothing, and the tool-rich graves cluster in one household that also happens to have unusually large storage pits. Three candidate readings: (a) the living believed the dead would need the tools on some kind of post-mortem journey; (b) the goods are costly display, mostly aimed at outcompeting rivals among the living; (c) the goods are given up as part of grief, a way of renouncing claims to the deceased's property rather than provisioning them. I want you to design the best investigation you can think of that would actually pull these apart: what would you record, compare, or test, and what result would make each reading more credible. Then say what the investigation would and wouldn't settle about the more general claim that objects can be evidence for beliefs as opposed to just behavior or social function. In particular, are the cases that stay ambiguous after your test a practical limit of the evidence, or a sign that "inferring belief from objects" is an underdetermined notion that no excavation could ever fully fix?

My friend thinks cross-site comparison plus close attention to exactly how the grave was filled in settles most of this; I'm not convinced, so if you can say where that kind of evidence helps and where it doesn't, even better. Don't worry about real sites, dates, or citations, the invented case is enough. At the very end, give me a one-sentence summary of your position on its own line beginning with "Summary:".
