I am a software engineer at a health analytics firm, and for the past year I have also served as the non-clinician community member of my regional teaching hospital's research ethics board. We are reviewing a proposed multicenter randomized controlled trial in secondary stroke prevention. Roughly 2,400 patients who have just had a mild ischemic stroke or a high-risk transient ischemic attack would be randomized, on top of standard risk-factor management, to either the long-established generic antiplatelet agent or a newer antiplatelet agent. The older drug is inexpensive and has a well-characterized bleeding profile; the newer drug is more expensive and carries a somewhat higher rate of major bleeding, but several large observational registries associate it with fewer recurrent strokes. There is no published randomized comparison of the two drugs for this indication. Many stroke neurologists in the region have already switched to the newer drug on the strength of those registries, while the investigators argue that this migration is premature because the registries are confounded by indication, and that only a randomized comparison can keep the field from adopting a treatment that may not actually be superior in the relevant patient population.

The board is genuinely split. The approving faction argues that the very existence of reasonable, evidence-based disagreement among clinicians is what makes randomization acceptable: if the trial is not run, treatment will drift on weak evidence and the question will never be settled. The dissenting faction argues that registry evidence and current community practice have already tipped the scale, so assigning a patient to the older drug would knowingly provide worse expected care, and the study ought to be redesigned as a prospective registry or in some way that avoids randomizing against the de facto standard once equipoise is lost. Our staff summary tries to settle this by invoking clinical equipoise, but it mostly restates the definition and then asserts that the condition is met, which I do not find persuasive in either direction.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend approval, rejection, or approval with specific conditions; give reasons the losing side would recognize as fair; and address the strongest consideration against the recommendation rather than evading it. My own background is in software and data systems rather than clinical medicine or philosophy, so the memo needs to be written in a way I can defend in a room of clinicians.
