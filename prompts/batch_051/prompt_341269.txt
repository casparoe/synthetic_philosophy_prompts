In my Philosophy of Artificial Intelligence course, we have been discussing the proposal that AI safety could be pursued through debate: two artificial agents argue opposite sides of a difficult factual question in front of a human judge who knows less than either agent. The proposal gains its appeal from the thought that checking an argument is easier than producing one, so a truthful agent should be able to expose the weaknesses in a dishonest agent's case even when the judge could not have found the answer alone. Our lecturer then introduced what she called the obfuscated-argument problem. As I understand it, the worry is this: on some questions, the honest side's strongest case turns on a proof, simulation, or statistical connection too long or too subtle for the judge to verify within the debate, while the dishonest side can offer a shorter, intuitively compelling but unsound story. The judge cannot distinguish the uncheckable true argument from the plausible false one, and the honest agent cannot make the truth more transparent than it actually is.

I would like you to act as an expert in the epistemology of machine reasoning and help me see exactly why this is a conceptual limit on debate-based approaches rather than a merely practical difficulty that more time, better training, or a smarter judge would remove. Please think carefully, and start by brainstorming the conditions that would have to hold for the debate protocol to track truth at all; then explain which of those conditions obfuscated arguments specifically break. It may help to contrast a question whose answer admits a short, checkable certificate with a question whose answer does not. Please write your answer in British English.
