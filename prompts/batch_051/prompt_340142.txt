I'm putting together a two-hour final for an upper-level philosophy of science class, and I want one essay question on inference to the best explanation. I don't want something a student can answer by just parroting the textbook account—it should force them to think about what "best" is doing when the explanatory virtues pull in different directions. Here's the case I'd like the question built aorund.

A radio survey finds a source that pulses every 1.337 seconds, with timing stable to one part in a billion. Three explanations are in play. E1 says it's a single rotating neutron star with a simple dipolar magnetic field: it posits one object of a kind we already have independent evidence for, has no free parameters, and makes precise predictions about pulse shape and stability. E2 says it's a binary pair of ordinary stars whose eclipses produce the pulses; it uses only very familiar objects, but it needs several independently improbable coincidences (an exactly edge-on orbit, near-perfect circularity, and a period just chosen to match the data). E3 says it's a brand-new type of "pulse source" with no specified mechanism and one adjustable parameter for each observed feature, so it fits by construction. Treat all three as physically possible for the purposes of the question—the point isn't astronomy, it's how IBE weighs competing virtues when fit is a tie.

Could you write me the question, a model answer around 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20? The marking scheme should have five must-hit points, say how many marks each is worth, and say how students typically go wrong at each point. The question should make students do three things: (i) rank E1–E3 the way IBE would rank them and say which explanatory virtue does the real work in each pairwise comparison; (ii) say whether a low prior probability for E1, based on how rare such a precisely aligned neutron star is thought to be, would automatically demote E1 under IBE—or whether prior probability has to enter the inference seperately; and (iii) explain the distinction between an explanation being loveliest (scoring highest on explanatory virtues) and likeliest (having the highest posterior probability given the evidence), and then defend a view about whether IBE is best understood as an acceptance rule or as a tool for ranking candidates. Don't ask for any citations, reading lists, or outside sources—the case itself should be enough to work with.
