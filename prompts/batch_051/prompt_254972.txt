Over the last term I have been keeping a list of my ethics professor’s verdicts about lying, and the question has recently stopped being abstract for me: my mother has dementia, and I have found myself lying to her to keep her calm. What I want now is not her case, though. I want to know what the professor would say about a new case, and what principle he is actually running.

Here are the cases he has ruled on.

1. A teacher knowingly teaches false doctrine to converts, with no material harm and no danger that anyone will discover the falsehood. The professor called this the gravest kind of lying.

2. A person spreads a false rumor to destroy a rival’s reputation, gaining nothing for himself or anyone else. The professor ranked this second.

3. A person gives false testimony that gets an innocent bystander fined, while protecting a friend from a small penalty. He called this grave, but less grave than case 2.

4. A person tells false stories for no reason except the pleasure of having successfully deceived someone; no one is harmed and no one benefits. He said this was worse than many lies that achieve some worldly good.

5. A person tells a host that a mediocre meal was excellent, purely to please the conversation; no harm. He called it a real lie, but less grave than case 4.

6. A person lies to a thief about where the family savings are hidden, preventing a burglary; no one is harmed. He called this less grave than case 5.

7. A person lies to hide an innocent fugitive from executioners, saving a life; no one is harmed. He called this less grave than case 6.

8. A person lies to prevent someone from being sexually assaulted, and no one is harmed. He called this the least grave of all.

Now the new case. A person lies to save a friend from execution, as in case 7, but the lie misdirects the pursuers and causes a third party a minor financial loss, about a day’s wages. What would the professor say? Would he treat this as closer to case 3, because a third party is harmed, or closer to case 7, because the harm is small and the good achieved is great? Which earlier case is the closest analog, and which principle would each answer be buying? I would also like to know what further case would best settle whether he is running a categorical hierarchy of kinds of lie or a weighing of the harms and goods involved. I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the rule, the rival rules that fit his verdicts, and the case that would tell them apart.
