I've been teaching a graduate seminar on Bayesian epistemology this term, and I keep finding myself persuaded by the logical induction approach to logical uncertainty. It seems to solve a problem I used to wave away: a perfectly rational Bayesian is logically omniscient, which is absurd, and logical induction tells you how an actual bounded agent should distribute credence over statements it hasn't yet proved or refuted. It gives you a clean rule for updating on proofs and disproofs as they arrive, it assigns sensible prices to things like P ≠ NP or the consistency of ZFC, and it's supposed to keep you from being exploitable by a bookie in the limit. For someone who wants a decision procedure rather than a shrug, that's very attractive.

But I also can't shake the conviction that mathematical truths are not like the weather. There isn't a further fact about whether P ≠ NP that I'm uncertain about the way I'm uncertain about tomorrow's rain; it's just a matter of what follows from the axioms. Seeing a proof doesn't feel like getting new evidence, it feels like coming to see something that was always there. If I assign 0.37 to a proposition that is in fact a theorem, my credence looks less like a degree of belief about mathematics and more like a measure of my own computational situation, or maybe just an approximation. I don't want to be told the logical induction people have already handled this, because I half-think they've changed the subject. What I want is an honest walk through the options: should I debunk the intuition and accept that mathematical uncertainty is real uncertainty, should I amend the theory into something more modest — probabilities about computation, not about the maths itself — or is there a reading where both survive? What would decide it, and what specifically do I give up under each option?
