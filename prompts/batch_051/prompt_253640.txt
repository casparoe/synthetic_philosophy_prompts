For a discussion group I belong to, I've been asked to write out my position on how far the criminal law may go in enforcing morality, and I would like a consistency check before I present it. Here is what I have come to:

1. Private, consensual conduct between adults should be none of the criminal law's business, no matter how degrading or unnatural other people find it.
2. A society may use the criminal law to protect the shared practices and institutions that make social life possible — for example, the practices surrounding the treatment of the dead — even when no assignable individual is harmed by the conduct being punished.
3. The harm principle, taken literally, is too narrow to account for everything the criminal law may legitimately do.
4. The mere fact that a majority finds some conduct disgusting is never, by itself, a sufficient ground for criminalizing it.
5. If a practice spreads widely enough, it can erode the shared attitudes that social life depends on, and a society is entitled to take that long-term effect into account when deciding what to criminalize.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. For each genuine tension, say how severe it is and what the cheapest fix would be, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
