For my EPQ I have been reading about structure-learning algorithms that fit a directed acyclic graph to purely observational data, and about why the edges in such a graph are supposed to tell us something causal rather than merely associational. The standard line I keep meeting is that, under the right assumptions, the minimal faithful Bayes net tracks the causal structure, and that an arrow X→Y should be read as a causal claim when three conditions hold: (1) the graph satisfies the Markov condition, meaning each variable is independent of its non-descendants conditional on its parents; (2) the distribution is faithful to the graph, meaning it has no conditional independencies beyond those entailed by the graph's d-separation relations; and (3) the graph is minimal, meaning no edge can be deleted without creating an independence the data do not contain. I am not sure how much work each condition is actually doing.

The case that bothers me is this. I run a greedy search on survey data recording only two variables, daily screen time (A) and sleep quality (B), and the two are very strongly dependent. The algorithm must output a DAG, so it prints A→B; the scoring rule prefers the one-edge model, and the orientation is effectively a tie-break. As far as I can tell, A→B and B→A satisfy all three of my criteria equally, because either orientation entails exactly the same conditional independence relations (none), and both are minimal and faithful to the observed dependence. Does the arrow A→B therefore qualify as tracking a causal relation under my criteria? Please check the three criteria one by one, say where the real failure lies, whether in the criteria, in the interpretation of the arrowhead, or in the claim that minimal faithful Bayes nets track causation, and then tell me whether and how I should revise the criteria. I would like the response as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
