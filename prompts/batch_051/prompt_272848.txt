Write a dialogue between two philosophers of language who disagree about what Frege's puzzle about identity statements actually shows. One holds that the puzzle forces a semantic conclusion: a name contributes something more than its referent—a sense, mode of presentation, or cognitive significance—since otherwise a true statement of the form a is b could not differ in informational content from the trivial a is a. The other holds that this is an overreaction, and that the phenomena can be fully accounted for by what speakers believe, how they are disposed to assent and act, and the pragmatics of conveying new information under different guises, with a purely referential semantics left intact. Have them test their disagreement against a single detailed case: a speaker who has long used two names for the same object, understands each name perfectly well, yet learns something when the identity is asserted; afterwards, the speaker draws different inferences and describes their own earlier state of mind differently depending on which name is used. Each character must state what their view predicts before, at, and after the moment of revelation, must answer the other's objections rather than restating their own position, and must volunteer at least one serious difficulty for their own account. Keep both characters equally competent, and do not allow either view to win by default.
