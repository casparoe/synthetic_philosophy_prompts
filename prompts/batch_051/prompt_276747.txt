I'm a retired chemist, so go easy on me if I mangle the terminology; I've only been reading philosophy seriously for a couple of years. Something's been nagging me about the way the big AI labs build their systems. They wire in a shutdown switch and train the model to be 'corrigible' — to accept being switched off, not to resist interruption, not to scheme to keep itself running, not to manipulate its operators, and to treat human corrections as overriding whatever it was doing. Some of them also test separately for self-preservation and power-seeking, as if those are failure modes an agent might fall into if they werent careful. The background worry, as I understand it, is that a system maximising some objective would resist being turned off because being off gets in the way of the objective, so the labs deliberately build systems that don't do that. The odd thing is that the same labs will often insist the model is just a next-token predictor — no goals, no beliefs, no preferences. So the practice seems to presuppose that the model is the kind of thing that could have goals it might try to protect, while the official ontology says there's nothing there to have a goal. I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of artificial agency that corrigibility training presupposes. Does it strictly presuppose a preference- or utility-maximiser picture of the system, or is it compatible with a purely behaviourist input–output view where being corrigible is just another style of output? Distinguish what the practice actually presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where it's philosophically underdetermined — could several theories license the same design choices? And then: if we genuinely believed the models have no goals or interests, what would have to change about the practice; would corrigibility training make literal sense? Please answer in British English.
