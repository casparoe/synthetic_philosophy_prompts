I have been turning over a parallel between two things that are not usually discussed together, and I cannot tell whether it is deep or superficial. On one side is Hofstadter and Sander's account of concepts as built out of analogies: a category has no fixed essence or stored definition, but is held together by a history of mappings from old cases to new ones, so that categorizing something is the act of seeing it as sufficiently like cases already assimilated. On the other side is the way a trained machine-learning classifier generalizes: it stores no rule, only a decision boundary induced by a cloud of labeled examples, and it treats a new input by computing, in effect, how similar it is to the examples it has seen, within whatever feature space the training setup fixes. Both accounts make generalization a comparison against prior cases rather than the application of a stored criterion. I want to know whether this is a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion. If the parallel is real, where exactly does it hold and where does it come apart? What would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer? For instance, if human concepts really are analogy-built in the way a classifier is similarity-built, should human categorization show classifier-like pathologies—adversarial examples, overfitting to recent or repeated instances, dependence on the order in which cases were encountered—and is there reason to think it does or does not? And if the parallel is mostly superficial, what is the structural difference doing the work: that human analogy-making can revise the feature space itself, that it uses structured mappings of roles and causes rather than geometric proximity, or something else? I would like an honest verdict on whether the parallel earns its keep rather than a defense of either side.
