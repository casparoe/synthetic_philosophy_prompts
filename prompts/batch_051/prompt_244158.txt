I am putting together an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of governance, and I would like you to design a runnable classroom activity on futarchy. The session is for sixteen students and lasts two hours, with fifteen minutes of debrief carried over to the following week. The aim is for students to feel, from the inside, both the attraction of the idea and the points at which it begins to strain. Futarchy, as I understand it, is the proposal that a polity should not vote directly for policies, but should first agree on a metric of welfare and then let prediction markets estimate which policy would best promote that metric; the policy with the highest conditional market estimate is adopted. I would like the activity to make the students run a miniature version of this. Please give them two or three concrete classroom or campus policies to choose between, such as changes to assessment rules, attendance requirements, or the use of a shared resource; fix a single welfare metric for the exercise; and have the decision made through conditional prediction markets, in which students trade contracts that pay out only if a given policy is adopted and a specified outcome is later observed. You should specify the contracts, the payout rule, the market mechanism (a simple market maker or a double auction is fine), how prices are translated into a policy choice, and what students are told before they begin. By the end, they should see that the market machinery can genuinely aggregate dispersed information, but that the deepest difficulty is not in the market at all: it lies in the choice, interpretation, and enforcement of the welfare metric.

The debrief should be where the conceptual weight lands, and I want it scripted so that it draws out, in this order: first, why separating the question of what we want from the question of what will deliver it can be a genuine epistemic advance; second, what is hidden in the act of fixing the metric, and what happens once participants begin to disagree about the metric or try to game it; third, the difference between trading because one expects an outcome and trading because one wants it, and whether any rule could tell those apart; and fourth, how small payouts, overconfidence, and the absence of real money might distort the pricing of low-probability but severe outcomes. Include a timeline for the full session, say explicitly what the facilitator should and should not say before the debrief, and identify at least two predictable failures and how to recover from them.

Be honest about what the exercise cannot reproduce about futarchy as a real proposal for government: the stakes are toy stakes, the welfare metric is handed to the students rather than contested through politics, and the epistemic situation of a seminar is nothing like that of a large, heterogeneous polity. The answer should be written in British English.
