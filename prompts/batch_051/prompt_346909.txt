I've been trying to work out what I actually think about the inside view and the outside view, so I wrote down my gut verdits on a bunch of cases and now I want to know whether there's a principle behind them. These are the cases.

The move. I'm estimating how long packing my apartment will take. When I walk through the rooms in my head, it feels like a four-hour job, but every previous move and everyone I know runs far over the original estimate, and the typical time for a one-bedroom move in my city is more like a full day. I'm confident the right answer is to go with the typical time and treat my mental walkthrough as evidence about my bias, not evidence about the move.

The chapter. I'm behind on a dissertation chapter. The department average for a chapter is nine weeks, and I'm not special. But I've broken the chapter into eleven sections, each of which looks like a two-to-three-day job when I actually list the sub-tasks, and the parts don't overlap. I don't think this is just optimism: I think the breakdown should be allowed to move me off the average, maybe to five weeks. I'd defend that to another person.

The restaurant. A friend has a detailed business plan for a new restaurant. She's thought about the menu, the lease, the licensing, the hiring, the lot. Restaurants in this city fail at something like 60 percent in the first three years, maybe worse, I don't know the exact number but I know it's grim. Her plan doesn't move me much: I'd still put her odds of surviving three years well under half, and I think the detail is mostly narrative decoration. I can't articulate why that isn't just the same move I made in the chapter case except pointed at my friend.

The colleague. A colleague has a genuinely good track record of calling which projects will fail, across about a decade and maybe a dozen calls, and she's usually right. She gives me a long technical argument that our new funding bid, which is widely liked, is going to collapse in the third year. I think I should weight her inside view far more than the base rate for bids of this sort. But the thing doing the work seems to be her past calls, not the argument itself.

The courthouse. I'm on a jury, hypothetically; I've never been on one. The prosecution has a very coherent story about the defendant, and the defense's story is messier. I also hear that people from the defendant's neighborhood are convicted of this crime far more often, which I'd treat as a relevant base rate in my own planning but seems wrong to use here. I feel strongly that case-specific evidence should drown out demographic base rates in a courtroom. I can't say why that's different from the restaurant base rate.

My tentative rule is something like: the inside view is only allowed to update me when it can be decomposed into sub-claims each of which has its own track record, or when the person offering it has a relevant personal track record. Narratives and coherent stories should be treated as noise. But that rule seems to make the courthouse story into noise too, and I don't want that. And it doesn't say why my own chapter breakdown counts when my own track record on self-estimates is terrible.

So: what's the best version of a principle that captures the verdicts I'm confident about? Which verdicts does it force me to revise? And what does it say about these further cases: a first-time founder with a polished pitch and no track record; a neurologist with thirty years of cases who has a hunch that contradicts a large randomized trial; a friend who has never once been on time to meet me and insists today will be different because she left early; and the case I keep going back and forth on, where I've spent ten hours on a coding project I estimated at six and I still feel close to done?
