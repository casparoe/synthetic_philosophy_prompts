My epistemology professor has spent the last two terms giving verdicts on cases about when a person is justified in flat-out believing a proposition, drawn mainly from the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. Her track record, which she treats as settled, is as follows.

In a fair lottery with 10,000 tickets and exactly one winner, she says a ticket holder is justified in believing of any particular ticket that it will lose, and justified in believing that exactly one ticket will win, but not justified in believing that every ticket will lose.

In a fair lottery with 20 tickets and exactly one winner, she says a ticket holder is justified in believing of a given ticket that it will lose.

In a fair lottery with five tickets and exactly one winner, she says a ticket holder is not justified in believing of a given ticket that it will lose.

In a fair lottery with ten tickets and exactly one winner, she says a ticket holder is justified in believing of a given ticket that it will lose.

In a case about a scholarly book with 400 substantive claims, where the author's evidence makes each claim independently 99 percent likely to be true, she says the author is justified in believing any individual claim, justified in believing the preface sentence that at least one of the 400 claims is false, and not justified in believing the conjunction that all 400 claims are true.

She has deliberately left open the point the two families of case share: whether a person who holds all the individually justified beliefs at once — each ticket's losing together with the claim that exactly one ticket wins, or every claim in the book together with the preface sentence — is rationally acceptable as a total state of belief, or is rationally required to drop at least one member of the inconsistent set.

Next week's case is this. A man holds a ticket in a fair lottery with 12 tickets and exactly one winner. Knowing only the setup, he believes of each of the 12 tickets that it will lose, and he also believes that exactly one ticket will win. He does not believe that all 12 tickets will lose; he in fact judges that proposition false. The question put to the professor is the one she has left open: is the man's total state of belief rationally acceptable?

Predict the professor's verdict. Identify the rule or rules that would make sense of her track record — for example, a high-probability threshold for individual belief combined with atomistic evaluation of the total set, versus the same threshold combined with a consistency requirement on the total set, or some other rule you can articulate — and say what each candidate rule predicts about the new case. Then give your best guess as to which rule she is implicitly running, with your reasons for preferring it, and say which further case you would ask her to decide to settle the question between your top two candidate rules. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
