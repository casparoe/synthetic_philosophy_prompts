Put on your philosopher-of-mind hat, the kind who knows panpsychism and the combination problem well enough to argue the details rather than just gesture at them. Before you write anything, actually think it through: brainstorm the strongest version of each side, and the spots where the other side reliably says that's not what I mean. I'd rather you slow down and get the wording right than produce something polished that one side would refuse to sign.

Two of my colleagues, M and C, have been arguing for a year about whether the subjects at the bottom of reality are many and small or one and huge, and I've finally talked them into an adversarial collaboration. M is a constitutive panpsychist: she thinks the world's little bits have their own dim experiential lives, and that a human mind is what you get when lots of those tiny subjects somehow make up one unified bigger subject. She owns the combination problem as the hardest thing for her view, and she thinks it's everyone's problem. C is a cosmopsychist: he thinks building the big mind out of little minds is backwards. On his view there is exactly one fundamental subject, the cosmos, and our finite minds are partial aspects or fragments of it. His version of the hard step is the decomposition problem, how one seamless cosmic subject gives rise to all these apparently separate, private, bounded points of view.

Draft the joint statement they should have produced. I want a stretch of claims both can sign about why they are panpsychists at all — something like, experience does not look like the sort of thing you could assemble out of wholly non-experiential ingredients, so a view that puts experience in the base of reality at least doesn't pretend the explanatory gap away. But dont let those agreed claims go soft or vacuous, and redo any wording one side would hear as a trap. Then give the exact residual disagreement, phrased so each side would accept the wording of it's own view, and explicitly flag any part of the year-long argument you think was just verbal, where one of them would say fine, that's all I meant.

Then do the cruxes: the sub-questions that, if settled, would move at least one of them. I'll suggest two you should work with, though sharpen them in your own words: first, whether the apparent unity of one human mind is better understood as a composite that got fused upward or as an aspect of one prior whole that got limited or sectioned off; second, what introspection and altered states of consciousness actually show about how many subjects there are in a head. Add a third crux if you can make it pull its weight. For each crux, give a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides would agree bears on it — split-brain cases on the rough facts everyone knows, or reports of ego dissolution under psychedelics treated only as evidence about what experience can feel like — and write down in advance what each side predicts or explains about that case.

Format rule: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Write it as continuous prose, like a long memo or email two busy philosophers could actually circulate and sign; write the disagreements out in sentences too. And no citations or named works — the content should carry itself. I know a drafter always nudges a side, so at the very end, step outside the document for a short paragraph and tell me how confident you are in the exact formulations, and which single claim you're least sure you've worded in a way both sides would actually sign rather than merely tolerate.
