I'm a retired control-systems engineer who's recently fallen into philosophy of science, and I keep hitting the claim that what separates fields that actually progress from fields that mostly reshuffle opinion is verifiability — feedback, basically: the world tells you quickly when you're wrong. In my old shop we'd have said a field is just a closed-loop servo. The world is the plant, the claims a field currently accepts are the output, predictions are what gets sensed, and the mismatch between prediction and measurement is the error signal that trims the next round of claims. So a mature field tracks the truth the way a well-tuned servo tracks it's reference, while fields with slow or mushy feedback (macroeconomics, parts of education research) are running open-loop and just drift.

Play the role of a philosopher of science who also knows what a transfer function is and who's not afraid of technical talk. Start by brainstorming the correspondence table — plant, reference, sensor, error signal, actuator, noise, loop gain, time constant — before you start criticizing. Then tell me where the mapping breaks (an engineer would immediately complain that a servo has its reference supplied from outside, and truth isn't handed to us like that), sort the disanalogies into ones that matter for the intended conclusion versus harmless ones, and say whether the conclusion — that feedback quality, and not mainly subject-matter complexity or the social structure of the field, explains why some disciplines advance faster than others — survives once they're all on the table. If the servo was doing more work than it can actually bear, tell me what a better analogy would be.
