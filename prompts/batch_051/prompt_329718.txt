Two people in my reading group fell into an argument about whether it ever makes sense to be, say, 60% confident that an open conjecture in number theory is true, and they asked me to referee. Sanjay said it does: the conjecture's truth is already fixed, but so is tomorrow's weather, and no one thinks that stops us from having partial beliefs about empirical claims; with a conjecture we simply conditionalize on different evidence, like partial results, failed searches for counterexamples, and structural analogies. Miriam said the cases are disanalogous. In the empirical case, there are two ways the world could be compatibly with all our evidence; in the logical case, the false alternative is ruled out by axioms we already accept, so a credence below 1 is really a measure of one's own computational limitations, not a judgment about how the world is. Sanjay answered that all degrees of belief are measures of the agent's evidential situation rather than objective chances, and that building in the requirement that epistemic possibilities respect every logical consequence would just assume logical omniscience. Miriam replied that this makes updating incoherent: when the proof arrives, the agent does not learn anything new about the world but merely corrects an error that was always there, whereas Bayesian updating is supposed to redistribute probability across alternatives that were genuinely open. Sanjay said that begs the question, because before the proof the alternatives were genuinely open by the agent's lights — the agent just learns which of them the axioms exclude.
