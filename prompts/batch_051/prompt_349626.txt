I am a novelist working on a book in which a retired physics teacher becomes absorbed in understanding how a bright twelve-year-old boy thinks about motion and force. The boy’s classroom teacher calls his ideas “misconceptions,” but I do not want to write the child as merely holding wrong answers; I want to know whether his errors are best rendered as an organized, theory-like way of seeing the world or as something looser. To decide how the teacher character would describe him, I have been using five criteria for what makes an idea count as a theory rather than a bare mistake: it is systematic, applying across many cases rather than appearing as a one-off error; it is productive, generating predictions and explanations for unfamiliar cases; it is coherent, its commitments hanging together as one model; it posits a mechanism, offering an underlying account of why things happen rather than only regularities; and it is robust, surviving counterevidence by being elaborated rather than abandoned.

The case I want to test is this. A student holds that a moving object carries a finite supply of force that friction and air resistance gradually use up, so continued motion always requires a continuing push. He says a heavier object falls faster because it carries more of this force from the start, and he uses the same idea to explain why a car’s engine must keep working while the car cruises on the highway, why a thrown ball arcs and drops, and why a hover puck glides to a stop even on a very smooth floor. Asked what would happen if the floor were perfectly smooth, he says the puck would glide forever, because nothing would be left to eat the force. Asked what would happen in empty space, he says it would keep going longer than on any floor, but then adds that it would still slow down because the force would run out on its own. When the tension between these two answers is pointed out, he does not see it and insists that a perfectly smooth floor is impossible, so the first question does not really arise. Does this qualify as a theory by my five criteria? Please work criterion by criterion, say explicitly where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and then tell me whether the case suggests my criteria need changing, or whether there is a kind of cognitive structure that is neither a full theory nor a bare mistake. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning with no preamble and no restating of the question.
