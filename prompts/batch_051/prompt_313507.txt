As a novelist whose work depends on attributing beliefs and desires to characters, I have found that my own instincts about such talk are inconsistent, and I want to know whether a single principle can hold them together. Here are my verdicts. (1) When a character I am drafting acts unexpectedly, I do not conclude that their beliefs or desires changed between scenes; I conclude that my model of the character was defective and revise it. For me, attributing beliefs and desires is positing an explanatory model, not reporting an inner fact. (2) Yet when I read about split-brain patients whose speaking hemisphere confabulates a reason for an action the other hemisphere initiated, I say without hesitation that the stated reason was never really believed; it was an after-the-fact story. That verdict presupposes a real distinction between genuinely believing something and merely offering a plausible rationale. (3) In severe addiction or compulsion, I find that the sincere statement that one wants to stop carries so little predictive weight that I am tempted to say the desire is causally idle, a shadow cast by the machinery. I recognize this as the eliminative materialist's impulse. (4) But I can predict and make sense of a great deal of human behavior using only beliefs and desires, with no neuroscientific detail, and I cannot bring myself to call this vocabulary simply false; it earns its keep daily and on every page. (5) And when a person says they want one thing while their conduct reliably aims at another, I want to say they really want the latter and only think they want the former, which again presupposes a fact of the matter about what a person really wants. My tentative principle is that belief-and-desire talk is warranted exactly insofar as it earns its predictive keep and should be retired wherever another vocabulary predicts better. But I cannot see how that principle can be squared with (2) and (5), and I suspect it would have something uncomfortable to say about (4). Please test the principle against the five cases, repair or replace it, and tell me what it commits me to in the case of a person in the early stages of a delusion whose plainly false belief predicts their behavior extremely well. Which of my five verdicts, if any, must I give up?
