In the chapter "Of Colonies" in Wealth of Nations, Adam Smith floats a fix for the American crisis that is way less famous than the invisible hand stuff, but more interesting for mechanism nerds. I dont have the text in front of me so I wont quote it, but as I remember it he sketches something like: the British parliament gets extended into an imperial parliament, and each colony or province gets seats in proportion to what it pays into the general revenue of the empire. Basically, your representation is a function of your fiscal contribution. He says it's politically unlikely, but he seems to think it would be better than either pure subordination or total separation.

I want to think about this as a mechanism, not as intellectual history. Suppose a modern analogue: a federation where the central parliament's seat shares are recalculated every decade from each province's net tax contribution to the center. How does this actually play out?

The obvious first-order effect is that provinces can buy seats by raising their own taxes, which transfers real wealth to the center and simultaneously shifts political power toward whoever was already able to pay. The parliament ends up representing tax dollars rather than persons, so it systematically overweights rich high-tax provinces and underweights poor ones that pay little. Maybe that's a feature if you want low contributors to have little say, but it's not neutral.

What I'm less sure about are the second-order games. A bloc of provinces could coordinate a temporary surge in contributions to win a majority for one session, then pass a law locking in their seat share and lower their taxes afterwards. The center has an incentive to classify revenue in self-serving ways: decide that some payments count as "imperial" when they come from allies and "local" when they come from rivals. If seats are proportional to contribution, there's also a weird feedback where the parliament decides tax rates, and every vote it takes changes the future composition of itself. That's like a legislature that redraws its own apportionment every time it raises revenue.

What does Smith's scheme, taken literally, actually select for? Would it produce a competent fiscal union, or a rent-seeking machine where provinces compete to be taxed in exchange for power? Would the failure modes be fixable with lagged apportionment, or flipping the formula so representation tracks population times some audited ability-to-pay measure instead of raw contribution? Or is the deeper problem that you can't make a stable legislature out of a formula that makes representation endogenous to the legislature's own decisions?
