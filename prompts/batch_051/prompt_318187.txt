I help run a residential college, and every spring the senior fellows host a wine-and-conversation evening for the incoming cohort. The stated aim is for older students to observe how the new students behave under the loosening effect of alcohol—not to catch them out, but to notice who becomes boastful, prickly, maudlin, or reckless, and who stays composed. Last week a close friend and I had a long argument about whether this tradition is defensible. He thinks it is slightly insane to use intoxication as an instrument of character assessment: alcohol, he says, manufactures a temporary self rather than revealing a true one, and putting young students in that state is more likely to normalize bad habits than to teach self-command. I replied that the tradition is, perhaps unknowingly, Platonic, and quoted this from Plato's Laws, Book 1, in R.G. Bury's translation: "And for the purpose, first, of providing a cheap and comparatively harmless test of these conditions, and, secondly, of affording practice in them, what more suitable pleasure can we mention than wine," My friend countered that my reading is exactly the kind of high-minded rationalization that lets an institution dress up a drinking custom as pedagogy.

I want a committed judgment, not a survey of possible positions. Should I defend or abandon the tradition? In your answer, stay close enough to the passage and its surrounding argument to vindicate or convict my use of Plato, even if your final verdict turns on the concrete risks of the situation rather than on the text alone.
