I cover physics and the philosophy of science. A journalism fellowship has asked me to run a two-hour workshop for about fifteen working science writers who report on physics but have little formal training in it. I want the core of the session to be a game or hands-on simulation rather than a talk. The idea I want the participants to feel before I name it is the effective-field-theory claim often glossed as the autonomy of scales: a good description of a system at a given length or energy scale does not require resolving much smaller scales, because small-scale physics enters mainly through a few parameters that can be measured at the larger scale, with any further dependence suppressed by the ratio of scales.

Design an activity in which some participants know detailed fine-grained rules for a toy system, while others see only coarse-grained outputs and must make repeated quantitative predictions. The rules should be arranged so that the coarse-grained observers can match the insiders' predictions after fitting a few parameters, even though they never learn the underlying rules. Please include a round in which the hidden fine-grained rules are changed in a way that leaves most coarse-grained predictions nearly unchanged but produces a small detectable correction, so the players themselves discover both the decoupling and its limits. Give me concrete rules and materials, a scoring scheme that works without real money, the exact information each group may see, and a debrief that maps moments in the game onto decoupling, the distinction between parameters whose influence persists at long distances and parameters whose influence shrinks as one coarse-grains further (relevant versus irrelevant), and the flow of effective descriptions under repeated coarse-graining. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, how to handle it, and which part of the real physics idea no classroom activity can reproduce and I will have to supply myself in the discussion.
