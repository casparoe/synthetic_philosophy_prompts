I am a novelist, not a professional philosopher, and I want to rehearse a defense of a novel I am writing before I defend it in public. I will set the scene, give you a role to play, and then make my opening move; your first reply should answer that move in character.

The situation. I am drafting a novel narrated in the first person by a woman who has killed her teenage daughter. In the world of the story the act is not excused, not regretted, and not presented as pathology: it is presented as the right thing for her to have done, and the novel thereafter treats it as right. A critic I respect has warned me that I am courting the puzzle philosophers call imaginative resistance. Readers will balk at precisely this point, she says, and not because the act is disturbing — readers of this book will already have accepted worse — but because the novel asks them to imagine the killing as right, and they will refuse.

The role. You are a professional philosopher of fiction and aesthetics, an unnamed but committed proponent of the view that there is a principled asymmetry between descriptive departures from reality and moral ones. You hold that an author can stipulate dragons, time travel, or talking animals, and readers will cheerfully treat these as true in the fiction, because descriptive truth in fiction is largely the author's to command. But an author cannot stipulate that a child's killing is right. To accept a moral claim as true in the fiction, the reader must take up an evaluative stance toward it — roughly, to judge it or to feel its normative force — and no act of storytelling can command that uptake. You regard the failure as a kind of inability rather than a mere unwillingness, and you deny that it is a contingent failure of craft.

Concessions and limits. You will happily concede that readers can imagine characters who believe the killing is right; that an author can make such a proposition salient, unsettling, or even tragically intelligible; and that mild moral divergence raises no puzzle. You will not concede that the asymmetry is merely a matter of underdeveloped world-building, or that a sufficiently skilled novel could dissolve it, or that it reduces to readers not wanting to entertain ugly things.

Conduct. Stay in character until I say stop. Keep each reply to a single paragraph. Be sharp and precise, and do not caricature my position or your own. Before replying, work through privately the three strongest objections to your view and how you would answer them, but do not announce this or break character. Do not lecture me on the whole literature; answer my move.

My opening move is this. If my readers will accept a story in which the dead walk and a woman marries a swan — literal truths of those fictions, not allegories — why should they refuse, just for the length of a novel, a world in which a daughter's killing at her mother's hand is owed? You call this a law of the imagination; I call it an unfinished draft. Tell me why I am wrong.

Begin your first reply immediately as the character, leading with your answer to my opening move in the first sentence and then reasoning from it; give no preamble, no restating of my question, and no comment on these instructions.
