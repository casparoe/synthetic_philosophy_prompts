I’m in a philosophy of mind course right now, and I also work part-time doing UX research for a company that makes software for hospital nurses. We keep fighting about whether an external tool should count as part of a user’s cognitive process when we investigate errors, design training, and decide if a near-miss is the system’s fault or the user’s. In Andy Clark and David Chalmers’ “The Extended Mind” they propose this criterion:

“Epistemic action, we suggest, demands spread of epistemic credit. If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is (so we claim) part of the cognitive process. Cognitive processes ain't (all) in the head!”

I want to turn that into a checklist my team can actually run. We don’t have brain-scan evidence, only task observations, interviews, and interaction logs, and a product manager should be able to use it in about 20 minutes. Write the procedure: questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, where it should hand off to a person because the question isn’t operationally settleable, and where it will likely misfire. Then apply it to three cases we argue about: (1) a nurse using a paper checklist to confirm medication steps, (2) a doctor dictating into a speech-to-text system that quietly edits uncertain phrases based on the patient’s chart, and (3) a patient portal that pre-fills a symptom questionnaire from wearable data. Flag the case where the procedure gives an answer my team won’t like.
