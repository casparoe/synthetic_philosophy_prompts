Lewis's scorekeeping model, especially as it developed into the common-ground account of presupposition, explains accommodation roughly as follows: when a speaker utters a sentence that presupposes some proposition p, and p is not already in the conversational score, the hearer can quietly add p, provided p is uncontroversial, so that the utterance counts as acceptable. The rule is usually treated as a cooperative mechanism of conversation: the score is updated so that the current conversational move can be a correct play. Now suppose one participant is a large language model trained purely on next-token prediction over a corpus of human text. It produces fluent accommodation-like behavior—honoring presuppositions, carrying forward discourse referents, and adjusting to the level of precision the human seems to expect—without possessing any common ground to update or any cooperative aim beyond predicting text. Does the accommodation model survive this development, or does the LLM case show that accommodation was never fundamentally about common ground and cooperation? Identify which premise of the standard account this development bears on, say whether the account can be repaired for the LLM case, and, if it cannot be repaired, say what the remaining phenomenon should be called.
