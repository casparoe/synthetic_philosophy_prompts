We're doing Scanlon's contractualism in my moral philosophy class, and I've gotten tangled in the aggregation stuff. I built an argument that seems to show the individualist restriction commits you to a totally nuts rule about distributive choices, and I can't tell which step to give up. Here it is.

(1) A principle can be reasonably rejected only by some particular person, on the basis of their own generic reason for objecting; there is no separate group-level reason that is just the sum of many individuals' complaints.

(2) If two individuals have conflicting generic reasons, the person with the stronger reason can reasonably reject a principle that gives the weaker reason priority, while the person with the weaker reason cannot reasonably reject a principle that gives the stronger reason priority.

(3) Therefore, when one person's complaint is even slightly stronger than each of many other people's complaints individually, none of the many is in a position to reasonably reject a principle that favors the one.

(4) If no one can reasonably reject that principle, then contractualism permits acting on it, and plausibly requires it, since we are supposed to act only on principles no one could reasonably reject.

(5) Now take a real-ish allocation case: a fixed hospital budget can either fund a treatment that prevents a harm of severity 100 for one patient, Ana, or a treatment that prevents a harm of severity 99 for each of ten thousand different patients. The harms are the same kind, just one unit less severe for the many.

(6) Ana's individual complaint is the strongest single complaint, but only by one unit. By (1)-(4), none of the ten thousand may reasonably reject a principle that spends the budget on Ana.

(7) And nothing changes if we make it a million patients with severity 99, or if Ana's severity is 100.0001 — the size of the group never gets a say unless the individual complaints are exactly tied.

(8) So Scanlon's restriction ends up being a lexical priority rule: first satisfy the single strongest individual complaint, and only when those are exactly equal may you consult the numbers.

(9) But that is a wild way to think about triage, public health, or anything distributive, and it seems to prove too much. So either I've overstated the restriction, or the move from (2) to (3) is fishy, or Scanlon can allow numbers in somewhere without becoming a utilitarian.

Which step fails, and what does giving it up cost? I'd prefer to keep the individualist restriction and reject step (6) or (8), but I'm not sure there's a principled way to do it.
