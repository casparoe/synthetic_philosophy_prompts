I'm in an early modern reading group and we've spent the last three weeks on Hume on causation, and its ended in the same standoff every time. Both camps agree on what the texts say; they just assign them completely different weights.

The first camp reads him as a through-and-through regularity theorist: causation in the objects is just constant conjunction plus contiguity and temporal priority, and the necessity we attribute to the world is really only a felt determination of the mind that we then spread onto the things. They lean on all the places where he says we have no impression of any power or necessary connexion, that all we ever observe is one event following another, and that the idea of necessary connexion must therefore come from the inward habit. They also read the two definitions of cause in the Enquiry—the one in terms of constant conjunction, the one in terms of the mind being carried from cause to effect—as Hume telling us the complete content of the concept.

The second camp reads him as a skeptical realist: he never denies that there are real necessary connections or secret powers in nature; he denies only that we can know them or form a positive idea of them, because of the copy principle. On their reading, all the apparent denials are epistemic, and the passages about nature keeping it's ultimate springs secret or hidden powers doing the real work are meant literally. For them the two definitions are just definitions of the idea we can manage, not of the thing itself; the thing itself gets referred to only by a relative idea—whatever it is in objects that grounds the regularity—without our being able to conceive it.

My own hunch is that this isn't really a historical disagreement anymore. It's a disagreement about method—whether a philosopher's view is everything their principles leave standing or only what they actually assert—with maybe a hidden metaphilosophical dispute about whether talk of a something-you-cannot-conceive is coherent enough to be called realism. But I could be wrong; it might equally be a genuine textual question, or two camps answering different questions under one name.

So: diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is. What would have to be true for it to be substantive rather than at least partly verbal? And on your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a distinction, a formal point about relative ideas, a different question—or is this the kind of thing where we should not expect anyone to move?
