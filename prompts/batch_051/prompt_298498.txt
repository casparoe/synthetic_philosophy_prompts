I am a novelist, and once a month I run a small evening seminar for other writers in my city — twelve people, ninety minutes, usually built around a single idea I want to use in fiction. I am currently working on a novel in which a public-health agency must choose between imposing a tiny burden on every patient in a district — one more form, one more day of waiting, an appointment shortened by three minutes — and sparing a few named patients a genuinely terrible outcome. What interests me for the book is not the policy answer but the psychology of the trade: how respectable, even kind, people can slide from clear thought into claiming that enough tiny discomforts must outweigh one concentrated harm, and then feel vertigo when they notice where that has taken them.

I would like you to design a ninety-minute activity for this group that makes the participants feel the pull of exactly that slide before anyone names it. It should be a game or a structured sequence of real choices with small, actual stakes to them — tedium, discomfort, lost points, mild social embarrassment, or the like; nothing injurious and no real money — rather than a discussion led from the front. If possible, the participants should build the escalating sequence of comparisons themselves, record each choice, and only afterwards look back over the whole trail and see what principle they were applying. Give me the rules, the materials, a timeline, a currency or scoring system that works without cash, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the underlying ideas: the aggregation of tiny harms, the difference between experienced harm and counted harm, the point at which a threshold starts to look arbitrary, and the temptation to treat the group as one entity whose total costs can simply be summed. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, the difference between a fifteen-second classroom annoyance and a lifetime of suffering in fiction.
