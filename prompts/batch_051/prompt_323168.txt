I have come to accept a two-part version of the inefficacy objection for individual participation in large-scale collective harms, and I want a map of what could count against it before committing any further.

The causal part says: for ordinary acts of participation in diffuse harms — buying industrially produced meat or fish, taking a discretionary long-haul flight, making a needless car trip — no single act makes the difference to whether any further unit of harm is produced. Either the relevant harm is produced at discrete thresholds that one act will not trigger, or the act's contribution is too small relative to the whole for any discrete unit of harm to be attributable to it. The normative part says: because the expected effect of the individual act on the production of harm is negligible, that effect cannot by itself ground a duty to refrain, even though the practice as a whole is seriously wrong and even though there may be separate expressive, collective, or institutional reasons for acting differently. Nothing in the view denies that the aggregate practice is wrong or that coordinated abstention would be effective; the target is only the inference from aggregate wrongness to an individual duty grounded in the act's own consequences. I hold this for meat and climate, but not for voting: there, a minute probability of decisiveness is paired with stakes large enough that the expected value of the act can remain significant.

Tell me what would count against this position, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. For each candidate defeater, say what would have to be true for it to succeed, whether that is a live possibility or merely a logical one, and whether a committed defender of the inefficacy objection should concede that it counts or dismiss it as changing the subject. Distinguish attacks on the causal part from attacks on the move from causal inefficacy to the absence of a duty. Assess at least the following: (1) threshold effects in real supply chains, together with the expected-value objection that a small probability of causing concentrated harm can outweigh a modest cost of abstention; (2) the claim that individual acts function as signals, coordination points, or norm-setting moves whose social effects are genuine consequences of the act itself; (3) universalizability, rule-consequentialist, or participation-based arguments that deny the step from negligible individual effect to no individual duty; (4) the charge that the voting exception is special pleading, either because the same expected-value structure reappears in some consumer cases or because the two classes cannot be given a principled distinction; and (5) deliberative arguments that treating one's own contribution as inefficacious licenses self-serving rationalization and thereby corrupts practical reason. Close with an honest assessment: is the position as stated insulated by its own qualifiers — no single act, cannot by itself ground a duty — so that no conceivable observation could move it? If so, is that insulation a defect, or is it what a thesis about the structure of collective action should look like? I would personally find evidence of threshold or signaling effects in actual supply chains most decisive, so characterize the kind of evidence that would settle the causal question.
