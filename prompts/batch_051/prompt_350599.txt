I'm on my university's animal research ethics board, and we're stuck on a honeybee protocol — I need a short written opinion to send around before we meet Thrusday. The lab studies whether stress changes decision-making in bees: they shake some bees for a minute, then test how the bees classify ambiguous odours, and the lab's own pilot data shows the shaken bees go 'pessimistic' — they treat ambiguous smells as 'no reward' more often than controls, the same bias you see in anxious mammals. The protocol also lesions part of the antennal lobe, without anaesthesia, to see whether the shift depends on it. Under our rules invertebrates are exempt from vertebrate-style pain review, so the researchers expected a rubber stamp. Half the board thinks the pilot data is exactly the kind of marker that should make us extend protections — maybe anaesthesia for the lesion, maybe a much stronger justification for doing it at all. The other half says a cognitive bias is still just behaviour, not evidence of felt pain, and that if we block or condition this on a hunch we're letting anthropomorphism set animal-research policy. Please draft the memo I'd circulate: recommend approve, approve with conditions (spell them out), or reject, with a bottom line the board can actually act on. Spend real time on the strongest argument on the losing side, not just the legal default that invertebrates are exempt. And if you think the way I've framed this already sneaks in an answer about where consciousness starts, or asks the board to settle an empirical question it can't settle, say that plainly and tell me what we should be deciding instead.
