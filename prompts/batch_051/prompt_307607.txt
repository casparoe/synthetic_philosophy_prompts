I've got a colleague in the department—call her Dana—who has been my accidental data set on a question I teach but never quite settled: when a theory clashes with a case intuition, which one gives? I've kept a running list of her verdicts over the years, and next month a grad student is going to present something that tests exactly the part of her pattern I've never seen pressured. I want you to help me reverse-engineer the rule she's running and then predict her.

Her record, as best as I can reconstruct it:

1. The transitivity cases. Every time a speaker's view of betterness or choiceworthiness turned out cyclic—A beats B, B beats C, C beats A—Dana shut it down before the cases got a hearing. Her line was always that transitivity is a precondition on calling the thing a ranking at all, not a datum you trade against case intuitions.

2. The loud counterexample. A grad student proposed a maximally simple monist principle about permissibility (I'll spare you the exact one; think the cleanest one-factor view you know). Dana rejected it because of a single case that everyone in the room kept agreeing about across a dozen variants. She said a principle that simple has to handle a case that loud, and its simplicity earned it nothing once it missed.

3. The ad hoc patch. Someone then offered an amended principle that handled the loud case by adding a clause. It looked gerrymandered. Dana was fine with it. She said case intuitions get to shape the principle; simplicity is the last thing to spend.

4. The one-off counterexample. In another seminar, a visitor brought a clever but fragile counterexample to a long-standing simple principle—the intuitions flipped depending on presentation order and only some people had them at all. Dana voted to keep the principle and treat that case as noise. She wasn't saying cases don't matter; she was saying that particular case doesn't.

5. The tiebreak. When two transitive theories each fit all the robust cases and differed only by simplicity, Dana took the simpler one, and said, paraphrasing, that simplicity is for exactly that situation, after the real work is done.

Here's the new case i need to predict. A grad student is set to defend a rule for ranking policy options—something like pick the option not beaten by its neighbor, a satisficing-looking generalization—that is dramatically simpler than any transitive rule fitting the same evidence. The rule gets every real, vivid case right. The thing is, it is not formally transitive: you can get a cycle, but only from a specially constructed trio of options for which nobody has any stable intuitions one way or the other.

My hunch is that her record is consistent with at least two rules. One: transitivity is a hard filter, full stop, and below it loud case intuitions beat principles, fragile case intuitions get ignored, and simplicity is only a tiebreak. Two: transitivity is very heavily weighted in reflective equilibrium but not a filter, and no one has ever offered her enough simplicity-plus-case-fit to outweigh it—though in principle a huge gain could.

What does each rule predict about her response to the new case, which rule do you think she's actually running, and what's your best guess about what she'll say? If that case doesn't settle it, give me one further case that would cleanly tell the two rules apart.
