I'm a student in an upper-division seminar on welfare and social choice, and for my final project I have to design and run one 75-minute session for the class of 16 students. The topic is interpersonal utility comparisons. I want an activity, not a lecture, in which my classmates run into the problem themselves: they compare how well off different people are, discover that they are reaching for some standard, and then see that the standard is doing more work than the evidence. The term "interpersonal utility comparison" should not be used until the debrief.

The setting is a seminar room with movable chairs and a whiteboard. I can print cards and handouts. There is no real money and no graded stakes. The full session is 75 minutes.

The mechanism I have in mind, which you should refine or replace if something works better: small groups each receive five or six case cards describing people who have suffered different kinds of losses, such as chronic pain, loss of mobility, bereavement, loss of a career, and social isolation. Each group must first rank the people from worst off to best off, then distribute a fixed budget of "relief units" among them. Afterward I hand out a second sheet for each person: a self-reported rating of the loss and a behavioral measure such as willingness to pay or effort to undo it. For at least one person these point in opposite directions — someone reports catastrophic suffering but has adapted and would pay little to reverse it, while someone else reports mild distress but would sacrifice a great deal. The groups must say whether they revise their ranking and allocation, and which evidence they trusted. The discovery should come from the groups' allocations and revisions disagreeing with one another, not from me explaining why they disagree.

The idea I want them to feel is that comparing one person's well-being with another's is neither impossible nor settled by the facts. Every method — taking the person's own report, watching their choices, or imagining yourself in their place — quietly commits to a standard of comparison, and the standards can pull in different directions.

Please design the full session: the exact rules, the materials, including what every case card and evidence sheet must specify, with the conflicting pair worked out enough to run as-is, a timeline, what I do at each step, and a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto the conceptual distinctions, such as levels versus units of well-being, self-report versus revealed preference versus extended preferences, and adaptive preferences. Be explicit about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself, and tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it and how to handle each failure without deflating the exercise.

If this plan rests on a confused premise, or if the task as posed cannot be done well — for example, if I have misdescribed what the comparison problem actually is, or a 75-minute group exercise cannot honestly get at it — say so plainly instead of complying politely, and tell me what I should do instead. I would rather redo the assignment than run something that teaches a distorted version of the issue.
