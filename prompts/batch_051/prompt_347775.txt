i'm pretty convinced by the continuity argument about Latin and Italian: every generation could understand the one before it, the changes pile up gradually and patchily, and any line you draw across the continuum looks arbitrary, so there's no real fact about when the thing stopped being Latin and started being Italian — 'Latin' and 'Italian' are labels we slap on after the fact, not joints in the world. and yet i also can't shake the intuition that the change actually happened. sometime around, I don't know, the first millenium, people in central italy really were not speaking latin anymore but an early kind of italian, and they knew it — they treated latin as a language you had to learn, not as the speech around them. that looks like more than a relabeling. i keep being told 'it's just a sorites problem' as if that resolves the tension, but i don't see that it does: that's a diagnosis of where the intuition comes from, not a proof that nothing else was going on. what i want is not a ruling on which side wins. i want to know whether the continuity argument actually contradicts the intuition or only a philosophical gloss on it, what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there's a reading on which both survive — say, one on which the boundary is made real by self-identification, or by a written standard feeding back into speech, rather than being out there in the idiolects themselves.
