I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and I've got a conceptual question that's been bugging me about calendar epochs and whether a "year zero" really could be anything other than a convention. The standard line I was taught is that the choice of epoch is purely arbitrary: any fixed origin works as long as everyone agrees on it, so there's no fact of the matter about whether the Common Era, some dynastic era, or a religious calendar is "correct." But I keep worrying about a specific development. Cosmology now gives us a fairly well-defined, observationally grounded candidate for a privileged point: the Big Bang, or more precisely the surface of last scattering, which is the earliest event we can actually observe and date. If you could set a calendrical year zero to that, does the conventionality argument still go through? I'm not asking which date is right; I'm asking what happens to the argument. Does the argument only worked because we had no physically salient zero point, or is it still sound because choosing an epoch inevitably involves further non-empirical choices (whether to count from the bang itself or the last scattering, what unit to count in, whether the scale has an explicit zero, how to anchor a civil calendar to a physical timescale)? Also, is there a useful distinction between a scientific timescale and a civil calendar here, and does that distinction do the philosophical work, or is it a distraction? I'd like a precise answer about which premise of the conventionality argument is under pressure and whether it can be repaired into something weaker but still true. Oh, and please write the answer in British English.
