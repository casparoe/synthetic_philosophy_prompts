The essay's about the old Gould and Lewontin spandrels paper—you know, the critique of adaptationism where they argue that a lot of features of organisms are byproducts of architectural constraints and developmental rules, not direct products of selection. My thesis is a bit deflationary: 'spandrel' and 'adaptation' aren't competing properties of a trait, they're answers to different questions. A trait counts as an adaptation only relative to a question about why a variant spread through a population against some alternatives. A trait counts as a spandrel only relative to a question about why some structure exists there at all, or why it has the particular shape it does given the constraints on how it gets built. So the same feature can be both, depending on which question you're actually asking.

For instance, take the protofeathers of theropods. relative to the question "why did feather-like structures spread through those populations?", maybe the insulation story is the adaptation answer. But relative to the question "why do modern birds end up with flight feathers?", the flight function is an exaptation—the feature wasn't selected for flight originally. Neither answer contradicts the other; they're answering different questions with different contrast classes. If that's right, then the spandrels critique isn't really a discovery about which traits are adaptations and which aren't. Its a reminder that adaptationism is just one question—about the spread of variants—dressed up as the only biologically interesting question there is.
