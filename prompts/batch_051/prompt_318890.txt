I have been developing what I call the answerability norm of assertion, and I would like an honest assessment of it before I take it any further.

The view is this. The norm of assertion is neither knowledge, nor truth, nor belief, but answerability. A speaker is entitled to assert that p just in case, if challenged with the question how one knows, they can give a truthful, non-evasive account of how they arrived at the claim, which I will call their epistemic route. The account need not be a justification strong enough for knowledge; it need only be an honest narration of the path by which the belief was actually formed. A speaker can therefore properly assert something false if their route was a reasonable one and they report it faithfully, as when a mechanic applies a diagnostic rule that usually works but misfires in this case. A speaker can also improperly assert something true if they cannot say how they came by it, as when a student accurately says the exam is on Friday but cannot recall whether they read it in the syllabus or heard it from a classmate.

The main argument I would offer is that challenging an assertion is naturally a way of asking the speaker for an account of themselves, and what the challenge demands is not a proof but a report of one's epistemic route. The question how one knows is, I think, the canonical challenge, and it is answered by saying that one saw it with one's own eyes, or was told by a trusted mechanic, no less readily than by producing a demonstration. But I worry that this relocates the problem: a false assertion built on an honest but flawed route still looks, from the listener's point of view, like a defective assertion, and my view seems forced to call it proper. I also do not know whether the route narrated must be the route that actually produced the belief, which no one but the speaker could ever check, or merely a route the speaker can now sincerely present, which would sever the connection to their actual epistemic position.

I know roughly about the knowledge norm, the truth norm, the belief norm, and the idea that assertion involves taking responsibility or being accountable. Is what I have called answerability just one of those under another name, a version of one of them, or something genuinely different? Where does it break? Give me the hardest counterexample you can construct, and say whether it kills the view or only forces a revision. Please write your answer in British English.
