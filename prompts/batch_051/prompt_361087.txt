Here is an argument from a reading group on animal signalling. Each premise seemed plausible when I accepted it, but the conclusion seems clearly too strong, and I cannot locate the false step. (1) A signal carries information about a state of affairs only if the probability that it is sent differs between that state and the alternatives. (2) If a signal is cheap—the cost of producing it is the same whether the signalled state obtains or not—then the act of producing it cannot by itself discriminate between states. (3) Therefore a cheap signal cannot carry information. (4) The handicap principle is often introduced as the way out: an honest signal is possible when the signal is a handicap, because the cost of the display is easier for high-quality signallers to bear than for low-quality signallers, and this differential cost is precisely what produces the correlation required in (1). (5) Now consider ordinary spoken testimony, such as telling a visitor the time of the next train. Producing the sentence is cheap, and saying it when it is false costs no more than saying it when it is true. (6) Yet the visitor normally acquires the information from the utterance. (7) So either ordinary testimony is not cheap in the sense that matters, or the handicap principle overstates its claim—but if it overstates its claim, it is unclear what keeps everyday talk honest. The conclusion seems to make a mystery out of something perfectly ordinary, so I assume something has gone wrong. I would rather preserve a strong reading of the handicap principle than treat everyday speech as secretly costly, but I suspect the argument is forcing that choice. Which step or steps should be rejected? Explain why the rejected step looked secure, and say what rejecting it costs the surrounding theory of honest signalling.
