I'm a retired chemist, and after forty years of letting probabilities earn their keep as frequencies and error bars, I've recently fallen into the philosophy of action, mostly because a friend of mine runs a small decision-theory reading group and rope me into it. She spent a career in operations research before retiring, and she has very settled views about whether a person can sensibly assign probabilities to their own upcoming choices. I've been keeping a list of her verdicts, since they strike me as more orderly than she admits.

Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it:

- Decided in advance to settle whether to go for a run by flipping a coin; asked right before the flip, she says yes, the probability is one half.
- Asked on Monday night whether you'll eat cereal for breakfast tomorrow, when you've eaten cereal every workday morning for six years; she says yes, about 0.95, because at this point it isn't really a decision.
- Already accepted an invitation, bought the train ticket, and promised to go; she says assigning near one to "will you be in Berlin on the 14th" is unproblematic.
- Standing at an unfamiliar fork in a trail, genuinely torn between the left and right paths and still deciding; she says no, you can't assign a probability to taking the left fork.

Here is the new case that has me stuck. Marta eats at the same lunch counter by her office. She's ordered the lentil soup on 24 of her last 30 visits. Today she's genuinely undecided between the soup and a new salad; she keeps going back and forth. Before she orders, should she say the probability she'll have the soup is 0.8?

I can see at least two rules that fit her four verdicts. One says deliberation crowds out prediction: you can assign probabilities to your own choices only when you aren't currently making them, whether because a coin is deciding, the behavior is habitual, or the matter is already settled. The other is more evidential: you can assign a probability whenever there is a solid base rate or causal constraint to go on, and you can't when there isn't. Those two rules agree on all four cases above but disagree about Marta. Which rule do you think she's actually running, what would she say about Marta's 0.8, and is there a further case that would cleanly separate the two readings? I'm less interested in the theoretical vocabulary than in whether her track record really forces a verdict here.
