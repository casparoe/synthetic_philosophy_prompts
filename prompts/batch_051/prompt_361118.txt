I write about philosophy and cognitive science for a living, and a couple weeks ago I sat through a talk I havent been able to shake. The speaker was working through something called the bootstrapping problem, and at one point she rattled off a numbered argument, said "one of these steps has to give, but I'll let you fight about which," and moved on. I've tried to reconstruct it from my notes, but I keep going in circles. I'd love to know if the argument is even valid, and if not, which exact step fails.

Setup: you own an old car with a fuel gauge. You have never once checked the gauge against anything independent — no dipstick, no logging miles against gallons pumped, nothing. On a road trip the gauge reads "full," and you have no reason to think it's broken. By "reliable," the speaker just meant something like: the gauge tends to read correctly. Here's the argument that you end up knowing the gauge is reliable:

1. You believe, based on the gauge reading "full," that the tank is full. Since you have no defeater, you come to know that the tank is full.

2. You can also see that the gauge reads "full," so you know that too.

3. From the fact that the tank is full and the gauge reads "full," you can infer that the gauge read correctly on this occassion.

4. You can repeat this on many occasions: trust the gauge, conclude the tank's level, and add another case to your list of "the gauge got it right."

5. From a long enough list of cases where the gauge got it right, you can infer by induction that the gauge is reliable.

6. So you have come to know that the gauge is reliable without ever checking it against anything beyond the gauge itself. The gauge, so to speak, vouched for itself.

My question: this feels like it proves too much — you get knowledge of the instrument's reliability for free, from the instrument's own say-so. So which step is the one that fails, and why? Or is the argument actually sound, and the circularity just isnt a problem here?
