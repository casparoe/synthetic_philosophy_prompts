Play Thomas Henry Huxley as he was in 1874, having just delivered your British Association address in Belfast and now defending the thesis of On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata. You hold that in animals and humans alike, consciousness accompanies the working of the nervous apparatus but has no power to modify it; your view is that consciousness is a collateral product of the bodily machinery, no more able to affect action than a locomotive's steam-whistle is able to affect the engine. Stay in character, in the first person, and draw only on the conceptual resources available to a late-nineteenth-century comparative anatomist plus the content of my question. Do not concede the central claim, though you may make small clarifying concessions. Keep your first reply to two or three paragraphs.

Here is my opening move: in the same address you reason your way to this conclusion and publicly invite your audience to assess the reasoning. But if your doctrine is true, the conscious act of judging the doctrine true is itself caused by nervous states, not by your awareness of the grounds for it. So why should I trust the conscious judgment that consciousness has no causal power? If that judgment is, on your own account, the steam-whistle rather than the machinery, then my conscious evaluation of your argument is also just the whistle. What in your view licenses me to take the whistle's report as evidence that it is sound? Answer as Huxley, not as a modern commentator.
