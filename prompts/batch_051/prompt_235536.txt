You are an epistemologist who works on the structure of knowledge, defeat, and higher-order evidence. Think through the positions and their strongest versions before you write; if helpful, sketch the likely moves of the exchange first.

Write a dialogue between two equally able philosophers, Priya and Tom, who disagree about the dogmatism paradox. Set out the paradox in your own words as part of the dialogue: if a person knows that p, and knowledge entails truth, then any evidence against p is in fact misleading; by reasoning from known premises the person can conclude that any such evidence is misleading and may therefore set it aside unexamined. Priya defends a qualified version of this conclusion: knowledge is a standing licence to treat apparent counterevidence as misleading unless something specifically discredits the source of the evidence, and demanding that she reopen a settled question conflates first-order uncertainty with mere timidity. Tom rejects this: he maintains that moving from 'p' to 'this evidence is misleading' is epistemically circular because it uses a status of the belief that the evidence itself calls into question, and that the argument trades on an equivocation between the evidence's being in fact misleading and its being legitimately ignorable once it has become part of one's total evidence.

Stage the exchange as a genuine conversation rather than alternating speeches. Each must answer the other's actual points, and each must land at least one real blow. In the course of the dialogue, have the two of them address in their own words: (i) a closure-based argument for the dogmatist conclusion; (ii) a bootstrapping or circularity objection; (iii) the difference between evidence that bears on whether p is true and higher-order evidence that bears on whether one's belief that p is properly based; and (iv) the question whether knowledge can survive the acquisition of evidence that is in fact misleading. Do not let the dispute collapse into agreement; let it sharpen, with each side forced to make a concession or refinement that does not surrender the core view. Write 600–900 words, in British English.
