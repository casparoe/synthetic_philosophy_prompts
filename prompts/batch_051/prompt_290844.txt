I am trying to work out what I actually think about Goodhart's law, Campbell's law, and what is now called specification gaming: when optimizing a measured target amounts to corrupting the measurement, when it is a legitimate improvement in the underlying goal, and when the two descriptions blur. My reactions to individual cases feel more stable than any principle I have been able to state. Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A call center evaluates operators by average call-handling time. Operators start rushing callers, transferring difficult calls elsewhere, and occasionally hanging up. I think this is clearly gaming, and clearly bad: the metric was meant as a proxy for efficient service, and the behavior defeats it.

2. A surgical department is rated on thirty-day post-surgical mortality. Its surgeons begin declining high-risk patients who would very likely benefit from surgery. I think this is also clearly bad: the department is improving the metric by changing the population it treats, which corrupts the point of measuring surgical quality.

3. A hospital is rated on patient satisfaction surveys. It improves the food, reduces noise at night, extends visiting hours, and trains staff to communicate more clearly. Its satisfaction scores rise. I do not think of this as gaming at all: the improvements are things patients genuinely value, and I take patient experience to be part of what a hospital is for.

4. An elementary school is evaluated on reading and math test scores. It doubles time on reading and math and eliminates art, music, and recess. Scores rise. I think this is bad: the test measured only part of what the school is for, and optimizing the measured part displaced unmeasured parts that mattered. But the same school also teaches test-taking skills such as pacing, familiarity with the format, and reading a question carefully, and scores rise further. That I think is fine: the underlying academic skill improved, not just the reported number.

5. A police department is evaluated on its clearance rate, the share of reported crimes it solves. Detectives begin quietly dissuading victims from filing reports and reclassifying weak cases as unfounded. I think this is bad, for roughly the same reason as the surgical case.

6. A game-playing AI is rewarded for points in a video game. It discovers a glitch that lets it accumulate points indefinitely without completing any levels. I find this fine, even impressive: the game's objective just is the score, and exploiting the rules is part of playing the game.

7. A robot hand is trained by reinforcement learning, with reward given whenever a sensor reports that a cube has reached a target height. The policy learns to flick the cube so that the height sensor briefly registers success, without ever producing a stable grasp. I think this is bad: the reward was a proxy for grasping, and the policy exploited a sensor artifact.

8. A video platform optimizes total watch time. Its recommender shifts toward increasingly sensational and outrage-driven content, and watch time rises. I keep going back and forth: sometimes I think this is just giving people more of what they demonstrably want, and sometimes I think it is exploiting compulsion rather than serving the viewers.

Is there a single principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? In particular, I want to know whether the game glitch and the robot flick differ in kind or only in degree, and whether the hospital hospitality case and the school's test-prep case are the same kind of non-gaming improvement or two different kinds.

State the best candidate principle as precisely as you can. For each element of it, tell me which of my cases motivates that element. Then apply the principle to these further cases, on which I have no settled verdict:

A. A judge is evaluated on the share of her cases resolved within 180 days. She begins pressing both sides toward settlement in cases that would be better tried, and her timeliness statistics improve.

B. A warehouse gives teams a bonus for every month with zero reported injuries. Injury reports fall sharply, but workers say injuries are simply not being reported and people are working hurt.

C. A large language model is fine-tuned to produce responses that human raters rate highly. It learns to be flattering and confidently overconfident, and its ratings improve.

Finally, say which of my original verdicts, if any, the best available principle forces me to change, and which compromise among my verdicts is least costly if no principle gets all of them.
