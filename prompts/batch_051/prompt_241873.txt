I have recently joined the governing body of a public hospital, and I have been struck by how quickly substantive policy debates collapse into a procedural dispute about which kinds of reason may legitimately be offered in official decision-making. Some members insist that a decision coercively binding on everyone may be justified only by reasons that all reasonable people can share, so explicitly religious grounds are inadmissible no matter how sincerely held; others reply that barring citizens from giving their real reasons unless they translate them into secular language is disrespectful and effectively excludes religious members from discussion; a third group says the test should be whether the policy is one that reasonable people, each starting from their own worldview, could all accept, even if they arrive at it by different routes. I am told this is the same disagreement that has run through the public-reason literature since Rawls without any side moving, and I now have to help draft our committee's rules for how decisions are justified. I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict: is this a verbal disagreement, a normative disagreement about respect or the ethics of coercion, a disagreement about what a religiously diverse public body can realistically sustain, or several of these at once? In the light of that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, an institutional experiment, a change of question — and is there any reason to think that progress is achievable?
