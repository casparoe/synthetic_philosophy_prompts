I run a small curation team at a model-organism database. Each week we work through a queue of roughly forty genes and decide, for each, whether to record a function annotation in our controlled vocabulary and what kind of annotation to record. Each decision gets about twenty minutes and a couple of primary papers. These annotations are downloaded by thousands of researchers, who use them for gene-set enrichment, network inference, and candidate-gene prioritization, and they are periodically audited against community standards, so an inconsistent or poorly justified annotation can propagate through the literature for years. Anything a curator is genuinely unsure about is escalated to me or a senior curator for sign-off, and I want the procedure to keep those escalations rare but well-defined enough to be defensible. The problem that keeps recurring is philosophical. Some of the experimental literature implicitly treats a gene's function as what natural selection shaped it to do over evolutionary history (the etiological account): on that standard, recently acquired activities, promiscuous side activities, and pseudogene transcripts are hard to annotate even when they measurably do something. Other literature treats function as what a gene product actually contributes to the organism's current physiology (the causal-role account): on that standard, those same things count as functional. The disagreement stopped being abstract last month, when my newest curator, an evolutionary biologist, refused to annotate a transcribed pseudogene that clearly had a detectable effect in a reporter assay, and a senior curator overruled her on the grounds that a detectable effect is all our users need.

Please turn this distinction into a single reusable decision procedure my curators can follow, written so a new curator with no philosophy background can use it. I want an ordered checklist of tests drawing on the evidence we actually see in typical papers — loss-of-function and null phenotypes, evolutionary conservation and substitution signatures, expression data, in vitro activity, and interaction or localization data — that says which early steps can accept or reject an annotation quickly, where the etiological and causal-role standards would diverge on the same evidence, and how to adjudicate that divergence rather than leaving it to curator temperament. It should mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and it should say explicitly which notion of function our primary annotation is recording, or whether we should record both under different evidence codes. Then apply the procedure to three worked cases: (1) a transcribed pseudogene that slightly stabilizes a neighboring transcript; (2) a deeply conserved metabolic enzyme with a reproducible in vitro side activity that is almost certainly irrelevant in vivo; and (3) a de novo gene with no detectable homologs but a strong loss-of-function phenotype. Make at least one of these come out the way a working curator would resist — annotating something we usually would not, or refusing something we usually would. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, and which philosophical disagreement it cannot settle no matter how carefully it is followed.
