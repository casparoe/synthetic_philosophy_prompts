Since retiring from a career in the physical sciences, I have been reading my way into the philosophy of economics, and I have found myself in a recurring argument with a member of our discussion group, a former portfolio manager. He insists that a market is best understood as a mind: prices are its beliefs, trading is how it revises them, and the whole apparatus aggregates dispersed private information better than any single expert could. From this he draws a strong conclusion about deference. If an asset price looks irrational to an outsider, it is probably the outsider who is confused, much as a layman should hesitate before contradicting a specialist; and any attempt to interfere with price formation is an assault on knowledge itself. He uses this reasoning to dismiss talk of bubbles and to suggest that even puzzling market movements are wiser than they appear.

The analogy is attractive to me because, in my former life, I worked with instrument networks that pooled noisy measurements into a unified estimate, and the market does look superficially like such a system. But I have since come across the no-trade theorem, which seems to pull the analogy apart. If traders are rational Bayesians with a common prior, and it is common knowledge that everyone trades only because they expect to gain, then private information alone should produce no trade at all: a willingness to buy or sell would itself reveal what the other party knows, and a rational counterparty would revise rather than transact. Real markets, of course, show enormous trading volume, most of which seems hard to attribute entirely to shifting circumstances and differing risk preferences. So the very activity that is supposed to make the market a good mind appears to depend on something other than the rational aggregation of information.

I would like you to audit the market-as-mind analogy for me, as a philosopher would. What is supposed to correspond to what — beliefs, evidence, updating, judgment, perhaps memory — and where does each correspondence break once the no-trade theorem is kept in view? Which features of a mind does the analogy quietly import that a market lacks, such as a single set of beliefs, a coherent utility function, or an intention to get things right? And does his conclusion survive: should prices command the deference owed to a knowledgeable person, or does the no-trade result show that the market's very activity undermines the claim that it knows what it is doing? If the analogy fails, tell me what a better analogy would be, if there is one. Please answer in American English.
