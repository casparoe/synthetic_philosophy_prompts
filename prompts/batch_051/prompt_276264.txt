I'm putting together a short research proposal for a six-month grant at the intersection of AI alignment and formal epistemology, and I need help turning a half-baked hunch into something fundable. The hunch concerns the Löbian obstacle to self-trust. As I understand it, the obstacle runs like this. If you model an agent's reasoning as a formal system strong enough to talk about its own proofs, then a rule like 'if I prove P, then P' cannot be adopted as a general principle of its own reasoning. Löb's theorem says each instance of that schema is derivable only if P itself is already derivable, so the self-trust step adds nothing; and if the system somehow proved the general soundness of all its own proofs, it would thereby prove its own consistency, which a consistent system can't do. So a proof-based agent either already has the conclusion in hand when it trusts its own proof, or it goes inconsistent. I'm probably garbling the formal details a bit, since I'm coming at this from the alignmnet side and not from logic.

My half-formed idea is that this gets framed as a fact about what a consistent machine can prove, but the version that actually matters for bounded agents like us (or a model checking its own chain-of-thought) is about evidence and calibration, not provability. Maybe self-trust is not a theorem an agent asserts but a policy: a prior over its own reliability, plus rules for when to update on its own outputs, when not to double-count evidence it generated itself, and when an error should make it stop trusting its current reasoning. I want the proposal to state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, explain why the existing proof-theoretic results leave the epistemic or decision-theoretic version open, lay out what I'd actually do month by month for six months, and say what would count as the project failing. It would also help to say whether 'the obstacle dissolves' is even the right success condition, since the theorem might just be a hard ceiling that no amount of redescription moves.
