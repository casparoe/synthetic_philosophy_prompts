I'm a PhD student in the history of medieval political thought, and I recently hit a confident paragraph on Aquinas and tyrannicide in a widely used survey of the history of political ideas. The gloss runs roughly like this: "Aquinas supplies the earliest fully worked-out Christian defense of tyrannicide. In the sedition discussion he argues that a tyrant rules for his private advantage rather than the common good, so that rebellion against a tyrant is not the sin of sedition that rebellion against a just king would be. From this he concludes that subjects are morally authorized to remove a tyrant, and that a private citizen who strikes down a manifest tyrant acts not as a murderer but as a minister of justice, once it is clear that no gentler remedy will succeed." The author then draws the general lesson that medieval political thought already contains a robust right of resistance.

This matches my broad memory of the Summa, but two things make me hesitate. I recall that in the De regno Aquinas says the remedy against a tyrant should proceed by public authority rather than by the private presumption of individuals, and that the right to act belongs to a superior authority or to the whole community; I also suspect the survey is quietly crediting Aquinas with the more enthusiastic position one finds in John of Salisbury. I don't want to misjudge the book or overcorrect it, so please audit the summary for me. Separate what is accurate from what is a subtle overstatement or conflation, say how a defender of Aquinas's actual position would correct it, and tell me whether the chapter's downstream claim about a right of resistance survives the correction. Please also say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict about the summary, and where your reading of Aquinas is most likely to fail.
