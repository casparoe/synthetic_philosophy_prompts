I've been reading a pop-econ book and the author glosses Milton Friedman's F-twist argument (the "unrealistic assumptions" essay) like this. His summary, roughly: Friedman showed that the realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant; all that counts is predictive accuracy, so any objection to a model that goes after its assumptions is automatically beside the point. That's why economics is fine assuming fully rational profit-maximizing agents even though nobody actually is — the predictions work, which is what proves the assumptions are harmless. Adding realistic detail, the author says, would always make the theory worse, since it clutters the model without improving forecasts. This smelled a bit off to me. I'd always read Friedman as making a narrower point about not judging assumptions in isolation, not that assumptions literally never matter; and I don't think predictive success "proves" assumptions are harmless as much as it just fails to refute them. But the author is a professional and I'm not an economist. Which parts are Friedman's actual view, which are overstatements or conflations, and does the last claim about realistic detail follow from any of it? Give me a definite verdict on each part rather than a survey of possible readings.
