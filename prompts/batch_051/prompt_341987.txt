I recently joined a metaethics reading group because a debate at my job kept invoking the is-ought gap, and I realized I had only a slogan-level handle on it. Last week two of the group's most careful members, Rhea and Marcus, got into a long exchange about it, and I came away genuinely unable to decide who had argued better. I will reconstruct their main moves as faithfully as I can.

Rhea opened with what she called the one piece of metaethics she would defend without qualification: "You cannot validly derive an ought from an is. A set of purely descriptive premises never entails a normative conclusion, and anyone who tries commits a version of the naturalistic fallacy—though I don't mean the fallacy in its definitional sense, just the derivational gap." Marcus pushed back: "As a claim about entailment, that is false. From the premise that the deadline is Friday, I can validly infer that either the deadline is Friday or you ought to ask for an extension. The premise is descriptive, the conclusion contains an ought, and the move is just disjunction introduction." Rhea replied: "That is a trick, not a counterexample. The ought is inert, pasted onto a true descriptive disjunct. The principle has to be understood as ruling out substantive normative conclusions." Marcus: "But 'substantive' is not a logical notion. Once you add it, you are no longer stating a fact about validity; you are making a substantive claim about which inferences count as relevant or illuminating in moral reasoning, and the status of that claim is exactly what the gap was supposed to settle." Rhea then shifted ground: "Consider thick concepts. 'Cruel' describes a recognizable kind of treatment and simultaneously condemns it. If I establish that a practice is cruel, the conclusion that it ought not to be done seems to follow immediately. That does not refute the gap; it shows the evaluative content was already built into the apparently descriptive premise, and that is exactly where the gap locates itself." Marcus answered: "That move concedes the game. If much of our ordinary moral vocabulary—cruel, generous, negligent, fair—is already evaluative, then the gap only holds for a purified, merely descriptive language nobody actually uses in moral argument. And the step from 'this is cruel' to 'this is wrong' is either analytic, because the evaluation is part of the concept, or it is an ordinary argument with a suppressed premise. Neither is a fallacy." Rhea came back: "The suppressed premise is the point. It would have to be something like 'cruelty is wrong,' and that is a normative premise. So the derivation is not from is to ought after all, and the gap has done real work by forcing it into the open." Marcus: "But I openly grant that premise; I am only pointing out that it travels with the word. So in practice the alleged gap is already bridged by the language itself, and the remaining question is which thick concepts to accept, not whether any ought can be pulled out of an is."

I could not sort out which criticisms stuck. Please approach this as a professional metaethicist and adjudicate the exchange move by move: which claims are right, which are overstated or mistaken, and what distinctions the two of them are failing to draw. I would especially like explicit rulings on three questions: (a) does Marcus's disjunction example refute the gap as a logical thesis, and what repair, if any, does Rhea's appeal to "substantive" provide? (b) does the thick-concept exchange leave the gap with any critical force, or does Rhea's response there concede too much? and (c) is Rhea right that the suppressed-premise point vindicates the gap? Before you rule, spell out the different senses of "derive" and "ought" you think are in play, so your verdicts track the distinct issues rather than running them together. Then tell me how confident you are in the overall verdict and in each of the three sub-verdicts, and single out the point you are least sure about.
