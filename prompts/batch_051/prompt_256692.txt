An argument from a seminar keeps troubling me. It seems to show that intuitionism cannot even state its own rejection of classical logic. Here it is:

(1) To deny that every proposition obeys the law of excluded middle is to assert that some proposition is a counterexample to it.
(2) Intuitionists deny that every proposition obeys the law of excluded middle; in their terms, the schema P ∨ ¬P is not valid for arbitrary P.
(3) So intuitionists are committed to some proposition Q for which ¬(Q ∨ ¬Q) holds.
(4) But intuitionistic logic proves, for any Q, ¬¬(Q ∨ ¬Q): assume Q ∨ ¬Q fails. From Q we get Q ∨ ¬Q, contradiction; hence ¬Q. But ¬Q also gives Q ∨ ¬Q, again a contradiction. So the failure of Q ∨ ¬Q is itself contradictory.
(5) Thus intuitionism demands a counterexample whose existence its own logic rules out.
(6) So the intuitionist can reject excluded middle only by reasoning classically about the rejection — exactly the inference they reject.

Give me a committed diagnosis: which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost the intuitionist? Defend one definite answer rather than surveying every possible reply.
