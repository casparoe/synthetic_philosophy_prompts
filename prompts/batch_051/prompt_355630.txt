I keep flip-flopping on the outside view, and I want someone to walk me through what a negotiated settlement would even look like. The theory side: when you're judging how long an empire has left, the rational move is to put it in a reference class of similar polities and take the base rate, not to get absorbed in the particular story of its army, economy, succession rules, and rivals. I've seen this work on deadlines and projects, and it's the only anti-overconfidence device that feels like it actually has teeth. But the intuition side keeps rebelling, and it gets worse when I run the rule backwards over history. Take almost any overachieving empire, and at every point past its first century or two the outside view would have said: old, already in the tail, at meaningful risk of breakdown. Rome after the second century: the eastern half had over a thousand years left. The Ottomans were half-dead from 1700 on, then lasted into the 1920s. Britain spent its whole post-Victorian decline being about to collapse, and it never did; it just slowly handed things back. In each case the inside details discriminated better in hindsight than the base rate did, and I can't unsee that. So either my reference classes are hopelessly off every single time (which would be convenient), or the outside view is not built for one-off, path-dependent things like empires. The obvious out is to say I'm miscoding the classes, but that worries me, because the whole point of the method was to stop me from re-coding my way to whatever prediction I already liked. What would actualy justify each move here — dropping the inside-view intuition as hindsight or survivorship bias, restricting the outside view to a class of problems, or a compatiblist reading — and is there a concrete backtest, runnable without hindsight, that could tell which one is doing the real work?
