I'm a software engineer, and at work I do a lot of probabilistic modelling — A/B tests, model evals, that sort of thing. I keep ending up in the same argument with a colleague about what we can actually infer from the same numbers, so I've written down my current beliefs about uncertainty and disagreement to check whether they hang together. Here they are:

1. Probabilities are subjective degrees of belief, not properties of the world. A model parameter doesn't have a true probability sitting inside it.
2. Rationality doesn't require any particular prior. There's a whole class of reasonable priors, and someone isn't irrational just for starting from a different one than mine.
3. I half-remember a theorem — something like, if two people share a common prior and have common knowledge of each other's posteriors, they can't agree to disagree. That sounds right.
4. More generally, if two ideal reasoners have exactly the same total evidence, neither has private information, and both update by Bayes' rule without error, they can't rationally end up with different probabilities. If they do, at least one of them has screwed up somewhere.
5. Calibration matters: someone who says '70%' on loads of predictions and is right 30% of the time is doing something wrong, even if their probabilities are all internally coherent.
6. In practice I think 'agree to disagree' can be perfectly rational. Me and a colleague can see the same data and both be reasonable in landing on different conclusions, because of different starting points and background assumptions.

I can't tell weather these all add up. I've a vague feeling that some of these are claims about ideal reasoners and some are claims about messy real people, and that might dissolve whatever tension there is — but I'm not convinced that's the whole story.

Could you go through them and tell me (a) which pairs are actually in tension and how severe each tension is (fatal / serious / manageable), (b) which pairs only look like they're in tension and why they dissolve, (c) what the cheapest fix to the set of six is and what it costs, and (d) which single revision would clean up the most.

For the format, use these sections in this order: 'Real tensions', 'Only apparent tensions', 'Cheapest fix', 'The one revision that does most work', and finish with a 'Bottom line' of two or three sentences. A short paragraph per section is plenty.
