I keep encountering a claim, in arguments about voting, eating meat, and personal carbon footprints, that one person's contribution to a collectively caused harm is morally negligible. Sometimes the claim is that the harm is overdetermined — the election result, the number of animals raised, or the total emissions would be the same regardless of what I do. Sometimes it is that the probability of my being the pivotal actor is so small that the expected harm of my action rounds to zero, and expected harms that small should not guide action.

I want to know how much of that is an empirical claim and how much is a conceptual one, and how one might actually bring evidence to bear on the empirical part. Take the strongest version of the objection for whichever concrete case you think makes it hardest to answer — buying one factory-farmed chicken, skipping a vote in a national election, or taking one transatlantic flight. Design the best test you can of the claim that the individual act makes no difference to the relevant outcome. What exactly would you measure? What pattern of results would count for the claim and what would count against it, and which results would be decisive rather than merely awkward? Be concrete about the empirical levers — supply-chain elasticities, discontinuities in production, pivotal-voter probabilities, social contagion, policy feedback — and say what a precise estimate of each would and would not settle.

Then be honest about the residue. Which parts of the objection are left untouched by any such measurement: whether a very small expected difference counts the same as a realized difference, whether there is something morally important about participation, complicity, or the kind of character one forms that is not captured in expected outcomes, and whether it is ever justified to round a tiny probability down to zero rather than multiply it out. Would a result that found a real marginal effect show that individual acts matter, or only that the empirical framing of the objection was wrong? And if a committed defender of the objection could accept the evidence and still keep the conclusion, is that a sign that the view is well-grounded or that it is protected from falsification?
