I’ve been arguing with colleagues about whether the replication crisis is best understood as a problem of incentives or of inferential machinery, and the discussion keeps returning to the following hypothetical. Suppose a coalition of the leading journals in psychology, medicine, and economics announced that, beginning next year, no study may make a confirmatory claim unless its analysis was preregistered and Bayesian. Concretely: a confirmatory study must specify in advance the full model, the prior, the parameter or parameters of interest, and a smallest effect size of interest; a result may be claimed as supported only if the posterior probability that the effect exceeds that smallest size is at least 0.95. Frequentist p-values may be reported only as descriptive summaries and may not ground a confirmatory claim. Any analysis absent from the preregistration must be labeled exploratory and cannot support a confirmatory conclusion. Nothing else about funding, hiring, promotion, or journal prestige changes. Predict, with your chain of reasoning made visible, how the published literature and researchers’ day-to-day behavior change over the following ten to fifteen years.

In your answer, trace mechanisms rather than merely listing outcomes. Consider at least: whether p-hacking is eliminated, transformed, or merely relocated—for example, through prior-hacking, model-hacking, or retroactive redefinition of the smallest effect size of interest; how publication bias and the file-drawer problem change; whether the proportion of published results that independently replicate goes up, and roughly how much; how the rule affects exploratory work, theory-building, and the kinds of questions researchers choose to pursue; and what new forms of gaming or status competition emerge. Where you are confident, say why; where your prediction depends on details such as how priors are audited, how strictly deviations are punished, or whether the rule is applied uniformly across fields, say what would have to hold for the outcome to look very different.
