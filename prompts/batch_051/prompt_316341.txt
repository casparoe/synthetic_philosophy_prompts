I am a retired research chemist who has only recently started reading philosophy of science, and I have hit something I cannot settle on my own. A long-form article in a popular science magazine summarized Richard Dawid's idea of non-empirical theory confirmation roughly as follows. Because string theory and multiverse cosmology are unlikely to receive empirical tests in the foreseeable future, Dawid holds that physicists should stop treating experimental data as a necessary condition on theory choice and should instead accept a theory as true once it is mathematically elegant and no serious rival has been found; the mere absence of rivals is itself evidence for the theory and can, in Bayesian terms, be just as strong as an experimental confirmation. On this telling, string theory is already rationally vindicated, and further testing is a luxury rather than a necessity.

Skeptical that the article was being fair, I tracked down Dawid's own paper, "The Significance of Non-Empirical Confirmation in Fundamental Physics" (2017), and read the following passage:

"Non-empirical confirmation denotes confirmation by evidence that is not of the kind that can be predicted by the theory in question, i.e. that does not lie within the theory's intended domain. Let me, at this point, just give a first rough idea of the difference between empirical and non-empirical confirmation. If string theory ended up making specific quantitative predictions, data in agreement with those predictions would lie within the theory's intended domain and therefore amount to empirical confirmation. In the absence of empirical confirmation, exponents of the theory may rely on different kinds of reasoning. For example, they may argue that the theory is supported by the striking difficulties to come up with promising alternatives. Those difficulties clearly cannot be predicted by string theory itself. The observation that those difficulties exist is a contingent observation about the research process of which the development of string theory is just one part. Therefore, this observation does not constitute evidence within string theory's intended domain."

I am inclined to think the article has turned a modest methodological point into a license to ignore evidence, but I do not trust my instincts here. Please go through the article's summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a careful defender of Dawid could accept, which are simplifications or conflations, and which are outright misrepresentations; then say whether the article's concluding claim about string theory being vindicated follows from anything in the passage. However the analysis goes, end with a one-sentence bottom line beginning "Verdict:" stating whether the summary is broadly fair, partly fair, or unfair.
