As I've got it, accomodation goes roughly like this: when a speaker says something that presupposes p, and p isn't already in the common ground, p gets quietly added to the conversational score — provided nobody objects and, crucially, p isn't itself the point of the utterance. Here's the case. In a staff–student committee, the chair opens the last item by saying that before they break they should sort out the timetable for the new second-term essay. But no second-term essay has been agreed; whether to introduce one is the very question on the agenda, and the chair, who plainly wants one, has used the phrase the new second-term essay as if it is old news. The students are split, but none of them speak, because the chair is their dissertation supervisor and they don't want to seem hostile. Does the proposition that there will be a second-term essay get accommodated here? Go through the criteria one by one — the definite description triggers the presupposition, and it wasn't in the common ground, but the awkward bit is the proviso that the presupposed content mustn't be the point of the utterance, which may be exactly what the chair is trying to sneak in. Then say whether this comes out as genuine accommodation, as a disguised assertion, or as a case that shows the criteria need an extra clause about presupposing contested material.
