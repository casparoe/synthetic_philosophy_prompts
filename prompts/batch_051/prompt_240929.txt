For a graduate reading group on scientific change whose members include working scientists (a string theorist, an observational astronomer, a geophysicist, and a synthetic chemist) as well as philosophers of science, write a decision tree that takes a reader from the starting admission that Lakatos's basic picture is right — scientific theories belong to research programmes with a hard core, a protective belt, and positive and negative heuristics — to a named position on the following question: what should a working scientist do about a degenerating research programme, and at what point may or should it be abandoned? The deliverable should be the instrument itself: eight to ten yes/no or two-option forced-choice branch nodes in sequence, explicit routing rules from each answer to the next node or to a leaf, and a one-paragraph characterization of each leaf.

Design every node so that it asks for a judgement about a concrete case, not for allegiance to a label. No node may be of the form "do you accept this named view?" The following cases must do the diagnostic work, and each node should appeal to at least one of them: string theory and the landscape; the WIMP dark matter research programme versus modified gravity; Wegener's continental drift and its decades-long reception; the phlogiston programme versus Lavoisier's oxygen theory; and the Ptolemaic epicycles versus Copernicus. A node may, for instance, ask whether a fact already known during a theory's construction but not used in constructing it should count as strong evidence for the theory, whether a community's gradual defection from a programme is itself evidence that the programme is degenerating, or whether a protective adjustment that saves the phenomena but suggests no new tests should count against a programme.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of historical figures. Identify the main binary choices that actually separate positions on this question, at minimum these five: (a) whether progress consists in novel empirical predictions or in something broader, such as explanatory unification, overall problem-solving effectiveness, or accuracy in saving established phenomena; (b) whether rational rejection of a degenerating programme requires a superseding rival programme that has already absorbed its previous successes; (c) whether appraisal is prospective, a code a scientist could apply in the present, or essentially retrospective, a community's later reconstruction; (d) whether the progressive/degenerating distinction is genuinely binding or a post hoc rhetorical ranking; and (e) whether the hard core may be revised in a crisis without abandoning the programme. Show the combinations of answers these choices allow, and locate each of the following positions as a leaf in that space: orthodox Lakatosian sophisticated falsificationism, the rational-shelving view; a Popper-style falsificationism that treats degeneration as grounds for prompt rejection; a Kuhn-style view on which abandonment is a matter of community conversion that rationality can only reconstruct in hindsight; a Laudan-style problem-solving revision that replaces novel predictions with a programme's overall problem-solving record; a Feyerabend-style deflationary view that denies that the progressive/degenerating distinction does binding methodological work; and an instrumentalist view that retains a degenerating programme exactly as long as it still saves the established phenomena. Aim for six or seven leaves, and ensure every leaf is reachable under at least one real combination of answers.

For every allowed combination of branch answers with no leaf, diagnose it briefly: say whether nobody defends it because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored. Flag any pair or cluster of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate with the chosen nodes — for example, if the Kuhn-style and Feyerabend-style leaves both license retaining an unrefuted but largely abandoned programme, say so and identify the additional question that would separate them, even if you exclude it for reasons of space. Do not paper over such indistinguishability.

Keep the whole instrument free of footnotes, citations, and suggestions for further reading; define any technical term inline, including heuristic, novel prediction, ad hocness, problem-solving effectiveness, and hard core, the first time it appears. The finished tree should be readable in twenty minutes by a working scientist with no prior exposure to Lakatos.
