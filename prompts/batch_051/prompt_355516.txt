Consider the following procedural proposal for a legislature. Every bill reported from committee must carry a public rationale: a section that does not appeal to revelation, sacred texts, ecclesiastical authority, miracles, or any claim whose force depends on the truth of a particular religion, and that offers considerations a citizen could in principle share regardless of their religion or lack of one. To avoid endless definitional argument, the rule is enforced by a panel of forty lay assessors drawn by lot from the electoral roll. The assessors see only the public-rationale section — not the bill's title, sponsors, or floor record — and answer one question: does this rationale offer a reason for the law that someone who does not share the author's ultimate commitments could recognize, in good faith, as a reason? If fewer than three-quarters answer yes, the rationale is tagged non-public; the bill then waits through a sixty-day cooling-off period and must pass a second recorded majority vote. Bills whose rationales clear the threshold go to a consent calendar. In floor debate members may say whatever they like, including openly theological argument, but only the public rationale enters the official justification record and may later be cited by courts.

I am not asking whether this rule is just, whether public reason is the right ideal, or whether it should be adopted. Treat it as a mechanism and analyze how it would actually behave: how it changes which justifications get offered and where authentic religious argument goes; which legislators, parties, and religious traditions are best or worst placed to comply; how bill drafters, interest groups, parliamentary factions, and assessors would game it; what it does to the sincerity and usefulness of the official record, to judicial reasoning, and to how citizens see their own reasons represented; and where it fails first. Then say whether its main failure modes can be patched with small amendments without gutting the scheme, or whether they are built into its structure. Begin with your single most important finding in one sentence, then give the analysis. Keep the entire response to no more than 800 words.
