I am an undergraduate taking a seminar on Schopenhauer and Nietzsche, and I’ve been reading Schopenhauer’s *On the Basis of Morality* in the A. B. Bullock translation on Project Gutenberg. The text opens with an epigraph drawn from Schopenhauer’s *On the Will in Nature*:

“To preach Morality is easy, to found it difficult.—”

(SCHOPENHAUER: Ueber den Willen in der Natur; p. 128)

I recently listened to a student-run podcast on Schopenhauer and Nietzsche, and one panellist summarised their disagreement as follows:

‘Schopenhauer’s great innovation was to found morality on compassion rather than on reason or divine command. For him an action has moral worth only when it is unselfish and intended to relieve another’s suffering; justice and loving-kindness are simply two sides of compassion. Nietzsche rejected this because he thought pity makes people weak, so he held that morality should be based on self-overcoming rather than compassion.’

I am not sure how much of this to trust. It sounds broadly right, but given the epigraph’s contrast between preaching a morality and founding one, I suspect the summary may be smoothing over the difference between offering a metaphysical ground for compassion and merely praising compassion as a feeling. Could you go through the panellist’s account sentence by sentence: say which parts a careful reader of Schopenhauer would accept, which parts conflate distinct claims, and which parts misrepresent Nietzsche? In particular, does Nietzsche simply reject compassion outright, or does he diagnose something more specific about its moral role? And does Schopenhauer’s epigraph help expose where the summary goes wrong, or is that only rhetoric on my part?
