I am a novelist drafting a scene in which a mathematics tutor and her exhausted pupil re-enact, in compressed form, the old Lewis Carroll dialogue in which Achilles tries to force a conclusion on a reluctant Tortoise. In the original, as I understand it, the Tortoise accepts two premises of a Euclidean argument, and she will even accept the further claim that if those premises are true then the conclusion is true. What she will not do is take the step to the conclusion on the strength of that further claim, because she treats the license for the step as one more premise, which then needs a further license, and so on without end. The usual moral is that a rule of inference cannot be made to fire by adding more premises. My scene gives the pupil a bounded ledger instead of an unlimited notebook: she can hold exactly three claims in view at once, and any attempt to bring a fourth claim before her causes her to lose whichever claim has been in view longest. She therefore accepts the two original premises and then the conditional, at which point her three slots are full; when she accepts the next higher-order conditional, the first premise drops out; if she then brings that first premise back before her, the second premise drops out in turn. I want the scene to be psychologically real, but I also want to know whether my variation preserves the logic of the original or changes it. Walk through the structure. Does the original regress still arise under the three-slot constraint—does it terminate, cycle, or simply become a different kind of failure? Is the original regress best read as vicious or as merely never-ending, and which of those readings survives the bounded-memory version? Most of all, is my pupil's failure still the Tortoise's failure, or have I replaced a logical point with a psychological one?
