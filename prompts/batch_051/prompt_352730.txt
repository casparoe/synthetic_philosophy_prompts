Something's been bugging me and I want it run through properly. Nobody I know thinks a human with a bigger brain or more neurons deserves more moral consideration then a human with a smaller brain. If you suggested a hospital weigh one patient's suffering more heavily because his cortex was ten percent larger, people would treat it like you'd brought back phrenology. But the minute the conversation turns to animals, brain size and neuron counts become everyones go-to evidence for whether a species can suffer: more neurons, so more likely to be conscious, so more deserving of protection. Same property, waved away as irrelevant within a species and treated as close to decisive across species. I want to know whether anything actually justifies that asymmetry, or whether we're just biased toward animals that look smart to us. Give me the strongest principle you can find that says braininess matters between species but not between humans, test it against the hard comparisons (a human with profound microcephaly, a pig with a large brain but dull behavior, a crow with a tiny brain and striking behavior, whichever you think is the real stress test), and then give an honest verdict: keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up.
