I am a novelist researching a historical novel centered on the Armenian genocide. I have adopted a fixed research policy: I will read historians who disagree with one another about interpretations, causes, and the handling of sources, but I will not read — not even to understand a denier I mean to put on the page — writers whose project is to establish that the genocide did not occur or has been grossly exaggerated. I have kept this rule through two novels. Recently an editor objected that a novelist who writes a denier has to know a denier's strongest moves firsthand, not filtered through a scholar's rebuttal, and that my rule boils down to 'only reading the side I already trust.' I think this is wrong. There is a difference between revising a settled account and manufacturing the appearance of an open question, and denialist prose is designed to exploit a careful reader's good faith rather than correct the record. But I cannot deny two things: revisionist scholarship is sometimes initially smeared as denialist, while denialists are practiced at presenting themselves as revisionists — and I have sometimes leaned on my rule to avoid reading difficult material. Is this policy defensible for a novelist in my position? What test would let me place a particular book or author on one side of the line rather than the other, and where would such a test still fail? Is there a version of the rule that lets me do the research a careful novelist should do without lending a hearing to a case that does not deserve one?
