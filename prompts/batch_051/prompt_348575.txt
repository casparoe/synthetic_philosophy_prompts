I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of science course, currently on a unit about conventions in measurement, and I keep noticing a pattern in debates over calendar reform that I don't have a word for. When someone proposes renumbering the years—adding a year zero so that arithmetic over dated intervals works cleanly, or shifting the epoch to a religiously neutral or astronomically significant origin—the proposal is almost always rejected on practical grounds: software, legal records, and historical scholarship are all keyed to the existing count. Yet when the reformer points out that the existing epoch is just as arbitrary, that arbitrariness is treated as harmless, because it is only a convention. The challenger is expected to prove the new convention superior, while the incumbent's arbitrariness never counts against it. I can see that coordination and path dependence lie nearby, but I am after the more specific configuration in which a convention acknowledged to be arbitrary acquires, purely by already being in place, a default authority that any equally arbitrary alternative must earn. Does this have an established name? How exactly does it differ from path dependence, status quo bias, and ordinary conservatism about inherited practices? If there is no name, what would you call it, and where are its edges—does the same thing happen with units of measurement, or only where switching costs are high and coordination is dense?
