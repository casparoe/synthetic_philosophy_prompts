I came to philosophy late, after a career in the natural sciences, and I keep noticing how seldom arguments about large historical change state their causal assumptions in a form that could in principle be checked. I want you to do that here, in the form of a reasoned counterfactual prediction.

Let me begin with the causal claim to be tested, in a deliberately simplified form. A demanding doctrine of predestination leaves believers uncertain about their standing with God; that uncertainty generates a need for evidence; the religious culture directs that need into disciplined worldly labour carried out with ascetic restraint; and this habit, once detached from its theological origin, supplies the psychological energy behind methodical commercial activity. Max Weber's name is attached to a richer version of this chain, and for present purposes that is all you need to know about it.

Now the counterfactual. Imagine that the Reformation in north-western Europe proceeds exactly as it did until roughly 1560, except that the Reformed churches of the commercial towns in the Low Countries, the Rhineland, and the Swiss cities introduce one doctrinal change. They continue to teach strict double predestination: God has decreed from eternity who is saved and who is damned, and no human act can alter either decree. But they now teach, emphatically and successfully, that no earthly circumstance whatever constitutes evidence of one's standing with God. Neither prosperity nor poverty, neither moral improvement nor backsliding, neither church membership, the sacraments, nor any inward feeling of assurance may be read as a sign; to attempt such a reading is presumption. Pastors no longer direct their congregations to examine their lives for marks of election, and the consistory treats the question of one's standing as one that no conduct can answer. The psychological pressure of an uncertain election is therefore kept fully in force, while every sanctioned outlet for reading one's life as evidence is closed off. Everything else remains as it historically was: the suspicion of luxury, the doctrine of the calling that directs religious energy into secular occupations, and the social position of merchants.

Predict the economic culture of these towns over the following three or four generations. Does one still obtain the methodical, ascetic, capital-accumulating conduct described in the chain above, or does the anxiety discharge into something else—fatalism, revivalism, outward conformity, or slow loss of belief—or does the doctrine quietly lose its grip on conduct? I am less interested in your yes or no than in the reasoning.

Argue for your prediction by decomposing the chain I sketched into its component links: theological premise to psychological state, psychological state to practical norm, practical norm to economic conduct, and economic conduct to institutional change. For each link, state how confident you are in it, what would break it, and how much of the Weberian account depends on that link specifically rather than on the others. Then step back and address the meta-question your own answer commits you to. Is it intelligible to say that a religious idea was a cause of economic transformation in this scenario, and how would your prediction differ if you held either that ideas only rationalise prior material interests, or that ideas are causally effective only when they specify shared social practices rather than private mental states? Finish by identifying the single weakest link in your chain, and name one fact about the actual Reformation that, had it been otherwise, would most change your conclusion.
