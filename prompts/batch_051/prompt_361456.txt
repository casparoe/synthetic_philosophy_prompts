I'm a PhD student in education policy, and I'm trying to get the mechanics of a scheme a visiting fellow sketched a few years ago, not a moral verdict on it. The idea: scrap zoned school funding and give each child a weighted voucher worth 1.0 times the state's average per-pupil spend, with weights 1.4 for low-income, 1.3 for English learners, and 2.0 for a centrally assessed special-education code. Any registered non-profit school — including private, religious, converted public schools, and homeschooling co-ops — must accept the voucher as full payment for any admitted pupil, no top-ups. For-profit schools can take the voucher but may charge a means-tested top-up capped at 10% of it. Over-subscribed schools fill places by lottery; the only entry is a short registration form, so no tests, interviews, or auditions, and siblings and staff children get a preference capped at 25% of places. Schools taking vouchers can't expel a pupil for two academic years except after a criminal conviction. Homeschool co-ops can draw 70% of the voucher as reimbursement for approved tutors, materials, and exam fees, against reciepts. What would this actually do? I want the incentives on parents, schools (including new entrants), co-ops, and the state; the ways each of them would game it; what happens to special education, rural areas, and the district schools left behind; and which failure modes could be fixed by tweaking the mechanism rather than scrapping it. Please write the whole answer in British English.
