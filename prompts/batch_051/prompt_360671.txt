You are a philosopher of logic who has taught the Achilles-and-the-Tortoise problem many times, and you are also an experienced explainer of difficult ideas to people who have never studied formal logic. Please think carefully before you answer, and begin by brainstorming a few possible ways to structure the session before settling on one.

I am the president of my high school's philosophy club, and next Friday I am leading a forty-five-minute session for about fifteen students on a problem from a short Lewis Carroll dialogue: the Tortoise accepts a conditional statement and its antecedent, yet refuses to draw the conclusion, and each time Achilles hands him the principle as a new premise the Tortoise accepts the new premise but still does not draw the conclusion, so the exchange regresses forever. None of the students have studied formal logic; they know what an if-then sentence is from ordinary English, and one or two have seen syllogisms in a literature class.

I do not need you to explain the problem to me. I need a plan for how I should explain it. Please give me:

1. The order in which the ideas must be introduced, with an explanation of what depends on what. I suspect the distinction between a premise and a rule of inference has to be secured before the force of the dialogue appears, but I want you to make the dependency explicit and tell me if something else must come first.

2. The single concrete example that should carry the whole session. State the example and justify it: what it must have and what it must not have. Tell me why not to build the session on a syllogism like all men are mortal, Socrates is a man, therefore Socrates is mortal, since that would be the tempting choice.

3. The misconceptions the students will bring in or form, and exactly where in the sequence each one should be headed off. I especially expect that they will think the rule modus ponens is just another premise that Achilles can add and then everything is fine, and that the Tortoise is simply being stubborn. For each misconception, give me a short line or question I can use to defuse it at the right moment without sounding condescending.

4. One check I can run at the end of the session to see whether the idea has actually landed — something that would distinguish a student who understands why the regress keeps going from one who merely found the Tortoise irritating.

5. What I should explicitly decline to get into at this level, and how to say so, so the discussion does not drift into formal metalogic or the literature on the justification of deduction.

Make sure the structure of the session itself models the distinction between premises and rules of inference rather than merely stating it.
