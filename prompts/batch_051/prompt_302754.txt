I just spent an hour going back and forth with a friend about whether average-case benchmarks in alignment are basically worthless for one-shot catastrophic risk. He laid out his argument in steps and I could't find the slip, but the conclusion feels like it got stronger than the premises warrant. The argument was:

(1) An average-case guarantee for an alignment method is just a claim about how the method performs on average across some distribution of environments.

(2) A method meant to prevent an existential catastrophe is deployed effectively once: you get one environment, one draw, no retries.

(3) In a one-shot setting, what actually happens is fixed entirely by the single environment you draw, not by what would have happened in the others.

(4) So the average across the other environments is decision-irrelevant in a one-shot setting; the average is not the thing you actually get.

(5) A worst-case guarantee, by contrast, says something about every environment in the class, so it still bears on the single draw no matter how that draw turns out.

(6) Therefore, in catastrophic-risk alignment, average-case guarantees have no evidential or decision-theoretic force, and only a worst-case guarantee can justify deploying a system.

Step 4 feels too fast to me, but I can't make the objection precise, and my friend says either I point to a step or I should accept the conclusion. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step or transition fails, why, and what would the corrected version of that step say? Also, could the final conclusion still be right for a diffrent reason than the one this argument gives?
