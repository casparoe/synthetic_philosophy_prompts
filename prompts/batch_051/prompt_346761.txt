I help run a film-and-philosophy discussion group, and a recent conversation has made me want to build our next session around Pixar's Ratatouille and the epistemology of aesthetic testimony. A friend admitted that she had spent a year confidently recommending a celebrated film she had never actually seen, relying entirely on a critic she trusted; I had to concede that her recommendations had been more reliable than my own first-hand judgments about films I had watched. The question I want to explore is whether aesthetic judgment can legitimately be acquired from others' testimony, or whether it has to be earned first-hand. My tentative view is that Ratatouille is genuinely divided on this. On one side it seems to insist that authority belongs to the person who actually tastes: the critic must sit down and eat, the dish is judged on the palate rather than on the name attached to it. On the other side, the whole plot runs on borrowed reputation, hidden authorship, and the public's willingness to trust reviews, brands, and a famous motto. I would like a reading that does justice to both pressures rather than forcing the film to settle the debate.
