Our audit committee must decide whether a supplier is in breach of Article 6 of our group's Responsible Sourcing Code. Article 6 reads:

"Suppliers shall not use forced or bonded labour, and shall not obtain labour by means of threats, deception, or the confiscation of identity documents. All work must be freely chosen, and every worker shall be free to leave upon giving reasonable notice. Wages and benefits paid for a standard working week shall be sufficient to meet the basic needs of workers and their dependants."

The supplier operates a garment factory in a district where a large mine closed two years ago, leaving many workers with few options. It pays the equivalent of 20 per cent above the local going wage for comparable work, but that wage is 25 per cent below the cost of a basic basket of food, housing, healthcare, and schooling. It uses no threats or deception, does not retain identity documents, observes a 48-hour standard week, and overtime is genuinely voluntary; workers are free to leave and often say the job is the best available to them. Managers informed our auditor that they chose the site because the mine closure had created a pool of workers who would accept wages below those in the capital, and that the factory could pay double and still meet its profit target.

The Code was drafted after an earlier scandal involving locked dormitories and confiscated passports, and the discussions around Article 6 focused on that kind of coercion; the wage clause was added late and was never tested against a case of wholly voluntary work at wages below basic needs.

Does this supplier breach Article 6? Give the answer under four approaches: the plain text; the purpose of the clause; what the drafters would have said if this case had been put to them; and how a reasonable reader of a public sourcing code would understand it. Then say which approach should govern, and whether it should matter that the jobs are the best available in the district or that the employer could easily pay more. In particular, address whether an arrangement can be both genuinely beneficial to the workers, given their alternatives, and exploitative.
