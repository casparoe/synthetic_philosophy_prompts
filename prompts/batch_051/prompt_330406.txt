Since retiring from the natural sciences, I have been attending a reading group on the ethics of war. Last week two members fell into a prolonged disagreement about whether a conscript in an aggressor state who fights while scrupulously obeying the rules of engagement is morally on a par with a soldier defending against that aggression. The first argued that the distinction between the justice of a war and the justice of the conduct within it must be kept strict: ordinary soldiers rarely have reliable information about whether their state’s cause is just, and if we hold each soldier liable for the justice of the war itself, the laws of war become unworkable. The second rejected that. She said a person who knowingly takes part in a wrongful war is not excused merely by fighting cleanly, and offered a comparison: an executioner who follows correct procedures while carrying out a sentence on someone he knows to be innocent is not morally on a par with an executioner who executes the genuinely guilty, even if both follow the same protocol. The first replied that this analogy ignores the coercive and epistemically degraded position of a conscripted soldier, and that the primary wrong lies with the state rather than with the ordinary participant. The second answered that these considerations reduce culpability by degrees but do not establish moral equality. The first then asked why, if the two combatants are not moral equals, an adversary should still treat the unjust combatant as possessing the same combatant rights. The second replied that we should recognize those rights for the same reason we recognize procedural rights of a defendant we believe to be guilty—not because the person is innocent, but because of what is owed to persons even when they are in the wrong. The first said this seemed to abandon moral equality in favor of a weaker principle about the rights of the guilty, which was exactly his point. I came away uncertain where the argument went wrong, if it did. I would welcome a step-by-step adjudication: who is right about which specific point, where each side overstates its case, and what a defensible position should say about how the justice of a war bears on the moral standing of individual combatants.
