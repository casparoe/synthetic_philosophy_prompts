Here's a variant of the old torture-vs-dust-specks problem I've been turning over. Original setup: you choose between one person being tortured for decades and an astronomically huge number of people each getting a single dust speck in the eye. Straight utilitarian summing says the speck option is vastly worse, but a lot of people refuse to accept that, and some think the speck option is obviously better anyway.

Now change one thing: the chooser is inside the setup rather than outside it. Same two options, same N people, and you are one of them. Consider two conditions. In condition A, you're behind a veil of ignorance: the person who will be tortured is not yet determined, and from your point of view you have a 1/N chance of being the victim, with the lottery resolved after you choose. In condition B, you know for certain you won't be the torture victim; you'll just receive a speck if the speck option is chosen. In both conditions, everyone is in the same epistemic position as you.

The question: does your verdict differ between A and B, and should it? In particular, was the original puzzle's intuitive pull secretly dependant on the fact that the chooser is almost certainly not the one being tortured? If you go behind the veil (A), does the utilitarian answer become obviously correct and does the horror of torturing the one person stop mattering, or does the lexical view (no amount of specks justifies torture) survive? And is switching your answer between A and B an application of ex ante vs ex post reasoning, or is it just letting self-interest masquerade as principle? What does your pattern of answers commit you to about aggregating small harms across persons?
