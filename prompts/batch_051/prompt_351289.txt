Take the Chinese Room. A man who speaks only English sits in a room with a huge English rulebook. Chinese characters come in, he follows the instructions and writes characters out, and from the outside the room looks like a competent Chinese speaker, even though the man has no idea what any of it means.

Now vary it. The rulebook doesn't tell him what to output. Instead, for each input plus the current contents of a scratchpad, it gives a probability distribution over possible outputs, and the man rolls dice (or uses a random number generator) to pick which characters to send out. The distributions have been tuned so that over long stretches the room matches the patterns and frequencies of a native speaker, but on any given turn no output is strictly determined by the input. Does Searle's conclusion—that the room merely simulates understanding without having any—get stronger, weaker, or neither in this stochastic version? Work through whether the argument is any different when the symbol manipulation is probabilistic, and whether this version gives new traction to the systems reply (the whole room understands even if the man doesn't) or to the view that the room is an even worse candidate for understanding. Weigh the best versions of both sides and then defend a verdict.
