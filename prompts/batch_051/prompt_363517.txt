I am an undergraduate in an advanced seminar on the politics of artificial intelligence, and in two weeks I am responsible for leading the session on AI development races. We have been reading Fearon's rationalist account of war — the argument that, because war is costly, states should generally be able to find a settlement both sides would prefer to fighting, so the real puzzle is why they end up at war anyway — and we have discussed how the three mechanisms he identifies, private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and issue indivisibilities, have been applied to races between AI labs and militaries. I want the class to come away with a felt understanding of why individually reasonable choices can jointly produce an outcome everyone would have preferred to avoid, and why the main obstacle to coordination is often credibility rather than ignorance. To that end I would like to run the session as a game rather than a lecture, so that the other students feel the mechanisms operating on their own decisions before they are named.

The class has fourteen students and I have seventy-five minutes. Please design a runnable activity, with explicit rules and payoffs, in which two teams act as AI labs or states deciding how much of a private research result to reveal, how many resources to put into a risky capability push, and whether to accept some negotiated division of the eventual benefit. The game should make a cooperative outcome jointly preferable in some clear sense, but also make that outcome fragile for reasons the participants cannot simply talk their way past. Use small real stakes, such as a modest prize split or participation points, so the choices are actually felt. The rules should be simple enough to introduce in five minutes and to play for about half an hour, leaving the rest of the session for the debrief.

During play I do not want the names of the mechanisms given away. For the debrief, please give me a detailed sequence that first elicits from the participants why they chose what they did, then maps particular moments in the game onto the three mechanisms, and then asks how each mechanism might show up in real AI races: private benchmarks and safety evaluations as a source of private information, deployment commitments that a lab cannot make credible after a breakthrough as a commitment problem, and the possible winner-takes-all character of transformative AI as a version of indivisibility. Please phrase this as questions for me to ask rather than as a mini-lecture, and indicate the points at which I will have to supply an idea the game cannot generate on its own.

Finally, please tell me what will probably go wrong when the activity is actually run — for example, teams may cooperate simply because the members know and like one another, one team may misunderstand the rules, or the debrief may flatten into discussion of personalities — and how I should handle each problem. Also be frank about which feature of Fearon's account, or of real AI races, a classroom game cannot reproduce. I suspect participants will know much more about each other than real labs do, and will bring prior reputations into the room; tell me where I should simply acknowledge such gaps and fill them as facilitator.
