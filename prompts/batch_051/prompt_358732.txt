I fell into Hume's story about the origin of government the other week, and one paragraph feels like it's doing an awful lot of work. It's from David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature, Book 3, Part 2, Section 7 ("Of the origin of government"):

"Now as every thing, that is contiguous to us, either in space or time, strikes upon us with such an idea, it has a proportional effect on the will and passions, and commonly operates with more force than any object, that lies in a more distant and obscure light. Tho' we may be fully convinc'd, that the latter object excels the former, we are not able to regulate our actions by this judgment; but yield to the sollicitations of our passions, which always plead in favour of whatever is near and contiguous.

This is the reason why men so often act in contradiction to their known interest; and in particular why they prefer any trivial advantage, that is present, to the maintenance of order in society, which so much depends on the observance of justice. The consequences of every breach of equity seem to lie very remote, and are not able to counter-ballance any immediate advantage, that may be reap'd from it."

The way I reconstruct the argument, he's saying something like this:

(1) The near, vivid, and immediate moves us far more than the remote, even when the remote thing is much more valuable.
(2) Maintaining the rules of justice — or, more generally, any shared good that asks each person to forgo a present advantage for the sake of the group — is a remote benefit, whereas each little breach of it offers a small but immediate benefit.
(3) So, left to ourselves, each of us will take the small immediate advantage over the remote shared good whenever they conflict.
(4) Therefore, large-scale cooperation on shared goods can only be stable if some contrivance makes the common interest immediate for somebody, by giving them an immediate stake in enforcing the rules.
(5) But there are stable, large-scale cases of uncoerced cooperation on shared goods: Wikipedia, the Linux kernel, volunteer disaster mapping, that kind of thing. Not small face-to-face groups, and no magistrate forcing anyone to contribute.

So the argument seems to prove that those projects are impossible, which theyre plainly not. Where does it actually break? I keep going back and forth about whether the psychology in (1) is too strong, whether I've stretched his point about justice into a point about every shared good, or whether the cases in (5) just change what counts as immediate. Which premise would you reject, and what does the rest of Hume's account lose if you do? I'd hate to give up (1) entirely, since it's what makes his story about why we need government do its work, but I can't see how to keep it without losing (5).
