I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, but right now I'm a bit stuck on a feature I'm writing about a clean-air zone in a British city, because the story keeps turning into a philosophy puzzle. The main person, who I'll call Darren, drives a 2014 diesel van for an evening delivery job. The zone was introduced five years ago, before Darren moved to the area from abroad. It's marked on the main roads and there's a website and there was a leaflet campaign, but Darren left school young, reads very little English, and doesn't own a computer, and the back streets he uses to avoid traffic aren't signed at every entrance. For something like nine months he drove in and out most nights without realising he owed a daily charge. Nothing arrived in the post until a single enforcment notice came, by which point the charges and penalties added up to over six thousand pounds. The tribunal accepted that he genuinly didn't know the zone existed, but said that didn't matter because the offence doesn't require knowledge. The council's line is that the rules have to apply to everyone equally and the information was reasonably available. Darren's line, in his broken English, is that he isn't asking for a reward: he just can't see why a rule he had no realistic way of finding out about should count as something he chose to break.

I've got the news story, but I want the feature to get at the principle rather than just he-said-council-said. Is the right question whether Darren could have found out, or whether the council could have reached him, or something else? Is there a coherent way to draw a line between rules that bind you whether or not you know them and rules that only bind you once you've actually been told? And if a philosopher had to redraw that line from scratch, what facts about Darren's situation would they want that the tribunal record probably won't tell me?

I keep going round in circles between three answers: that not knowing can never excuse someone, because everyone would claim it; that not knowing should excuse whenever someone genuinely couldn't have found out; and that the state should have to actually tell a person about a rule before it can fine them. Are any of those actually defensible once you spell out the commitments, or are they all just slogans? What would the council be signing up to if it adopted each one, and which would you tell me to build the feature around?
