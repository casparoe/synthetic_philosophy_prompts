Bertrand's paradox asks for the probability that a chord of a circle, chosen "at random," is longer than the side of an equilateral triangle inscribed in the circle, and the three classical sampling procedures give different answers: choosing two endpoints uniformly on the circumference gives 1/3; choosing a point uniformly along a radius and drawing the chord perpendicular to the radius there gives 1/2; and choosing a point uniformly in the disk as the chord's midpoint gives 1/4.

Now vary the threshold. Let the circle have radius R, and ask instead for the probability that a random chord is longer than the side of an inscribed square, that is, longer than R√2. Run each of the three classical sampling procedures with this new threshold and compute the resulting probabilities. Do all three procedures still disagree, or do some of them now coincide? And what does this variation show about how much of the original paradox comes from the equilateral-triangle threshold, as opposed to the underdetermination of "random chord" in general?
