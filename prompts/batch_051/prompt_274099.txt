I'm running a small reading group on what a grammatical rule actually is, and we've spent the last two sessions on the difference between rules and lists: regular past tenses on one side, suppletive forms on the other. I want to close next week's session with a short poem rather than a summary, something the group can take home, but it has to be accurate enough that a morphologist wouldn't wince.

Write a sonnet about suppletion — where a paradigm is built from forms with unrelated roots (go/went, be/is/was, good/better) — and about what that means for the idea of a grammatical rule. The octave should build the tidy picture first: the past tense as a rule that generates walk/walked and talk/talked, and the nineteenth-century Neogrammarian idea that sound change, once the conditions are properly stated, admits no exceptions. The volta should be the reader's arrival at go/went, a form the rule never generated, which had to be memorised whole. The closing couplet should say what that implies: knowing a language isnt just running the rule, its also storing an arbitrary list. Make the poem's own grammar do some work as well — most past-tense verbs in it should be regular, and the first irregular past tense should be the go/went that turns it.

And please write the whole thing in British English (spellings and all), since thats how our group materials are written.
