My uncle died a year ago and left me forty acres in the upper Midwest, about half of it an unlogged stand of hemlock and yellow birch. I've spent the last three months trying to figure out weather to put it under a conservation easement, sell the timber rights, or do a careful selective cut, and somewhere in there I realized my philosophical commitments about the value of nature might not actually hang together. I'd like an audit before I use them to justify a decision. Here's what I think I hold:

(a) At bottom, all value comes from sentient interests; things matter because they matter to some creature that can be made better or worse off. So nature is valuable instrumentally: carbon, clean water, flood control, timber, places people love and use.

(b) But I also have the last-person intuition and can't shake it: if I were somehow the last sentient creature alive and about to die, it would still be wrong to clear-cut the last remaining old-growth forest in the universe, even though no one would ever benefit from it or care.

(c) I think relational value is real and not reducible to just a bundle of services: the worth of this particular woods to me and the town partly consists in our ongoing relationship with it — my uncle's journals, the trails three generations of kids walked, the way the place has shaped the town's sense of itself. The relationship itself is a bearer of value, not just a conduit for benefits.

(d) I think naturalness matters even when it makes no difference to anyone's experience or interests: a self-seeded, never-cut forest has more value than an ecologically near-identical planted or regrown one, even if no ordinary visitor and no downstream beneficiary could tell them apart.

(e) But for public purposes, I think the only legitimate arguments are instrumental, ecosystem-service, public-reason-ish ones: timber values, flood protection, carbon, budget numbers. I think bringing intrinsic-value or relational-value language into a town meeting is counterproductive and maybe even dishonest, and I find the ecosystem-service case is comfortably on my side for preserving most of the land anyway.

(f) And yet I hold that non-intervention is itself a kind of respect: if the best available ecology said we should actively manage the grove — cull an overabundant deer herd, girdle trees to mimic disturbance, maybe plant oaks against a shifting climate — to maximize biodiversity or carbon uptake, I'd think that made the place worse or less wild, and I'd resist it even if the instrumental ledger came out ahead.

I'm not asking you to decide what to do with the land — I'm asking whether this set of commitments is jointly tenable. Which pairs are actually in tension and which only look that way? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing everything as equally bad, and for each real tension say what would have to give and what the fix would cost. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
