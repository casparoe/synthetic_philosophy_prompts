I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I am drafting a character who slowly convinces himself that ordinary moral considerations are swamped by tiny probabilities of astronomically large outcomes. I have been reading Nick Bostrom’s “Infinite Ethics” to understand the grammar of this kind of reasoning. Bostrom writes:

Ethical theories that hold that value is aggregative imply that a canonically infinite world contains an infinite quantity of positive value and an infinite quantity of negative value. This gives rise to a peculiar predicament. We can do only a finite amount of good or bad. Yet in cardinal arithmetic, adding or subtracting a finite quantity does not change an infinite quantity. Every possible act of ours therefore has the same net effect on the total amount of good and bad in a canonically infinite world: none whatsoever.

I recognize that this passage is about infinite value, but it seems to me to contain the same underlying engine as expected-value fanaticism: once the arithmetic of aggregation is allowed to dominate, everything finite and ordinary drops out of the frame. What I want is not an explanation of Bostrom’s argument, but a systematic translation of the debate over expected-value fanaticism into the working vocabulary I actually use when I construct a novel: plot, character, motivation, narrative stakes, point of view, pacing, dramatic irony, and the felt difference between tragedy and farce. Show me where that translation is clean, where it has to distort the philosophical claims to make them fit, and what has no narrative counterpart at all. Then tell me whether the residue points to something the standard formulations are missing, or to something my novelist’s framework cannot see.
