You are a philosopher of cognitive science who has worked on the interpretation of behavioral tests of mind. I want a committed, argumentative answer, not a survey. Think carefully about the operationalization before you commit, and do not leave your verdict open.

Alan Turing proposed replacing the question whether machines can think with an imitation game, in which a human interrogator converses with two hidden participants, one human and one machine, and tries to identify the machine. I want to know whether a strengthened version of this behavioral test can settle a conceptual claim. The claim is this: if an artificial system is conversationally indistinguishable from a normal adult human under an open-ended adversarial protocol, then it is correct to say that the system thinks, or has a mind, in the sense that licenses mentalistic attitudes such as belief, intention, understanding, and perhaps conscious experience.

Design the strongest feasible test of that claim. Be concrete about: the interaction channel and time limits, the number and selection of judges, the number and selection of human foils, the instructions given to both humans and system, the adversarial probes to be used, the blinding, the statistical decision rule, and what would count as a positive result. Include at least one experimental condition aimed at catching mere surface competence: for example, test whether the system can notice and repair a false implicature in its own prior utterance when an interlocutor complains, can refuse a subtly incoherent request rather than confabulate a response, or can distinguish between a metaphor it should extend and one it should reject.

Then answer three questions. First, what would a positive result establish about the system's behavioral competence? Second, what would it establish about whether the system's internal states have the right structure or phenomenal character to count as thinking, understanding, or experiencing? Third, what residual part of the claim would remain unsettled even by the best behavioral test, and is that residual part a discovery about the limits of behavioral evidence or a sign that the original claim was never fully operationalizable?

I want you to take a definite position. Either the test would settle the conceptual claim in a philosophically interesting way, or it would settle only a weaker surrogate claim, or the conceptual claim is not empirically settleable and should be revised. State your position in the opening paragraph, then defend it through the design of the test and the analysis of what its outcome would show.

Hard limit: 1,000 words.
