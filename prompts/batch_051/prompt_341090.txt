I'm a novelist, and the book I'm drafting centers on a machine that's supposed to learn concepts from very few examples, the way people do with Bongard problems. I want to make sure the underlying logic is sound before I hang a plot on it. A Bongard problem is basically two panels of little diagrams, six each; all the left-hand ones fit some rule and all the right-hand ones violate it, and the solver has to say what the rule is.

Here's the argument I keep turning over:

(1) For any finite set of examples, infinitely many rules sort them the same way.
(2) A Bongard problem gives you only a finite set of examples, plus the hint that there's supposed to be one rule.
(3) So the examples by themselves never single out one rule as the correct answer.
(4) A solver therefore has to bring in something extra: a preference for some rules over others, like simplicity or naturalness.
(5) But that preference is a fact about the solver's constitution, not a fact delivered by the examples.
(6) So there's no fact about the boxes that makes one rule the correct one; "correct" just means "the rule the puzzle-maker had in mind."

I can't tell if (6) follows, or weather something snags earlier — maybe in how (2) reads "the hint that there's supposed to be one rule," or in the jump from "the examples don't settle it" to "nothing settles it." Go step by step: is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and under what reading of it; if it is valid, tell me which premise I should be least comfortable about.

Also say how confident you are in all that, and single out the point you're least sure of.
