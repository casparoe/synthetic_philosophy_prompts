Two friends of mine spent most of a dinner arguing about a paper one of them is drafting, and I came away unsure who was right at each step. The first friend, a systems neuroscience postdoc, is writing up an experiment in which subjects could press a button to shorten an unpleasant noise. She wants to report the result in purely neural terms: activity in a network including the ventromedial prefrontal cortex predicted the button presses, and nothing is gained by saying that subjects pressed because they wanted the noise to end. The second friend, who works on philosophy of cognitive science, objected that she cannot have it both ways. The experiment's setup assumes that subjects understand the instructions, form an intention, and act on it; if those mental states never cause any behavior, the study's rationale collapses. She replied that her causal model is complete at the neural level, so citing the desire as an additional cause would be double-counting. He said that inference only follows if you accept a causal exclusion principle—the idea that a complete lower-level cause crowds out a higher-level one—and that principle is exactly what is in dispute. She said she is not globally ruling out mental causes: she is open to the view that wanting-to-stop just is a neural pattern, in which case mental talk is a lower-resolution description of the same cause. He answered that on an identity view she could state both descriptions in the Results section without double-counting, so leaving the mental description out is a rhetorical choice rather than a scientific requirement. She said it is still the right choice, because coarse folk-psychological states will not generalize across subjects as well as a neural mechanism. He replied that predictive utility is not causal exclusion: thermostats, economies, and game strategies are all explained by higher-level states that are realized differently underneath. I would like a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange: who is right about which specific point, where each argument overreaches, and what, if anything, both are missing.
