I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level moral philosophy course, and I am grading a take-home midterm composed of short-answer questions. The exam included this question:

Exam question: Define moral generalism, moral particularism, and holism about practical reasons. Then reconstruct the argument, associated with Dancy, that holism supports particularism. Finally, evaluate whether a generalist is committed to rejecting holism and whether a particularist is committed to accepting it. Illustrate the evaluative part with the contrast between invariant principles such as Rossian prima facie duties and a context-sensitive moral feature of your choosing.

One student submitted the following answer:

Student answer: Generalism is the view that competent moral thought requires a body of general principles: the features picked out by those principles — that an act would be a lie, that it would relieve pain, and so on — make the same kind of contribution wherever they occur. Particularism is the denial of this: it holds that moral judgment can be fully sensitive to the particular case without deriving its verdict from any set of general principles. Holism is the view that a feature that counts in favour of an act in one context may count against an act, or be morally inert, in another.

Dancy's central argument for particularism runs from holism. If reasons are holistic, then no feature carries a stable valence across contexts. A genuine general principle, however, ascribes a stable valence to the feature it mentions. Such principles will therefore either yield incorrect verdicts in exceptional cases, or have to be loaded with so many contextual qualifications that they no longer guide judgment in the way generalism requires. Holism therefore supports particularism.

Given that argument, a generalist must be an atomist: to defend genuine principles, she has to say that the features they mention count the same way wherever they appear. By the same reasoning, a particularist must accept holism, because if features did have stable valences, we could simply state true invariant principles and moral thought would collapse back into their application.

Ross is useful here. His prima facie duties — fidelity, gratitude, and the like — look generalist, and he tries to respect context sensitivity by saying that a prima facie duty can be outweighed. But being outweighed is not the same as switching valence. Once we notice that even pleasure can switch valence — pleasure at another person's suffering does not count in favour of an act and arguably counts against it — a Rossian list of invariant prima facie duties cannot capture the real reasons. So Ross faces a dilemma: either he embraces holism and moves toward particularism, or he keeps principles that are false. The debate therefore really comes down to whether moral features have stable valences.
