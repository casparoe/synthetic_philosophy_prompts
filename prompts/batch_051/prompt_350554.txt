You are a moral philosopher who works on the structure of everyday virtues, and you have been commissioned to write a short illustrative entry for an edited volume on frugality. The editors have proposed an under-discussed distinction. On one reading, frugality is a deontological constraint: a person acts wrongly, or at least falls short of virtue, when she uses more resources than her own purposes require, whatever the wider consequences. On the other reading, frugality is a value to be promoted: what ultimately matters is that resources are conserved overall, and an individual's consumption is only one causal lever among many, so an occasional or even deliberate extravagance could in principle serve frugality better than scrupulous personal thrift.

The editors have asked contributors to produce a worked example in which the two readings prescribe opposite verdicts about the very same behaviour. The example should be mundane rather than fanciful — an ordinary purchase, a household decision, or a practice within a small community will do — and it should make explicit the structural feature that produces the divergence, such as a case in which one person's visible thrift causes others to feel licensed to waste, or in which one person's conspicuous extravagance tightens a social norm of conservation.

Before settling on your example, brainstorm three or four candidate cases and briefly note why each succeeds or fails at separating the two readings cleanly. Then present the strongest case in full, spell out exactly what the constraint reading and the promoting reading would each prescribe and why, and close with a general lesson about what changes when a value is treated as a personal constraint rather than as a goal to be promoted.
