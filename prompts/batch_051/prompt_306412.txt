I'm a novelist. My main character is a theoretical physicist in her late thirties who is quietly convinced her field is winding down, and I want to make sure the attitude I give her isn't a strawman. For background I asked a science-writer friend to explain the "end of physics" idea, and he sent me this paragraph:

"The long arc of physics is a story of unification: Newton showed that the heavens and earth obey the same laws, Maxwell unified electricity, magnetism, and light, and the electroweak theory fused the weak force and electromagnetism. Today the standard model plus general relativity accounts for basically every confirmed result, with dark matter and dark energy as ugly loose ends, and the main remaining gap is a quantum theory of gravity — string theory is the leading contender. Once a final theory is in hand, physics will in principle predict everything physical that can happen, because chemistry is applied quantum mechanics, biology is applied chemistry, and the rest is just stamp collecting. We'll know we've reached the end because the theory will have no adjustable constants left and no experiment will surprise us again. The only argument against this is that physicists have wrongly announced the end before, but that's just saying the horse can't win because the horse didn't win last time; the mathematics this time has actually converged."

I've read it a few times and it sounds plausible but maybe a bit too neat — like the kind of thing a character could say without being challenged, and I don't want that. Could you help me work out what's actually defensible? Please think as a philosopher of physics who also has a working physicist's feel for the field. Before you write the clean answer, brainstorm the main moves in the final-theory debate as you'd explain them to a smart non-scientist, and list the places where the paragraph might be quietly sliding between different claims. Then lead with the bottom line: how much of that paragraph could my character say without showing she hasn't kept up. Then go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a working physicist would accept, what she'd wince at, and what's just wrong. After that, tell me whether his conclusion — that once a final theory exists, physics is over and everything else is stamp collecting — survives the corrections, and whether that last horse-racing sentence is a fair reply to the historical fact that physicists have predicted the end before and been wrong. I also want the answer to touch on the deeper "would we even know it was final" question, since that's what the novel keeps circling. Hard limit of 1,000 words. I'd rather have density then polish.
