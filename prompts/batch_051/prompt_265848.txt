I need a second opinion on a scheme I saw floated at the very end of a systematics paper (the author was clearly fed up with taxonomic inflation, though the proposal might have been half a joke). The rule goes like this. Every time a taxonomist publishes the description of a new species, they recieve a block of "species credit" that sits in escrow for, say, fifteen years. If another taxonomist publishes a formal synonymization during that window — that is, a peer-reviewed argument that the new species is not genuinely distinct and should be sunk into an existing one — and the synonymization is later accepted in the literature, the challenger gets a chunk of the escrowed credit and the original describer forfeits the rest. If the species survives the fifteen years untouched, the original describer collects the full credit, multiplied by the number of later treatments that cite it as valid. The stated goal is to make junk species expensive instead of cheap, and to reward descriptions that actually last.

I get that taxonomy is not a prediction market, and I expect it gets gamed immediately; still, I'd like a careful walkthrough. What does it do to who describes species, which groups get described, and which groups never get revisited? Does it reward real judgment or just reward working on organisms nobody cares to challenge? How do journal editors and reviewers adapt, and where does collusion show up first? The part I keep getting stuck on is that whether a species "survives" depends heavily on which species concept the reviser uses — a reproductively isolated population might be a good species under a diagnostic or phylogenetic concept but get sunk under a biological species concept, or the other way around. So does the scheme quietly make one species concept the de facto currency? Could that be fixed, say by requiring the challenger to sink the species under the concept the original describer used, or does that just create new workarounds and splintering?
