I am a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, writing a paper on whether the badness of a pain is intrinsic to the pain state itself or is conferred by the subject's aversive response. I want a thought experiment that forces the two positions apart. Please design a two-phase case. In both phases the same subject has the same burn at the same site, and the nociceptive sensory profile is held fixed: the subject can accurately report the injury's location, intensity, and sensory quality, and identifies the experience as pain. Phase one removes every affective and motivational component—no felt unpleasantness, no dislike, no avoidance, no desire for relief—modeled on pain asymbolia (the clinical pattern in which a patient reports a stimulus as painful but says it does not hurt), though sharper and cleaner than any actual case. Phase two restores exactly those affective and motivational components while the sensory profile remains qualitatively identical. The dissociation should result from an intervention within recognized neurology or pharmacology; no science-fictional mechanisms. Now evaluate the two phases under two theories. Theory A: the badness is intrinsic to the pain state—any experience with the full nociceptive sensory profile of a burn is itself bad for the subject, and indifference to it does not cancel that badness. Theory B: the badness is response-dependent—a noxious sensory state is bad for the subject only because, and insofar as, she dislikes it, is bothered by it, or is motivated to end it. For each phase, say what each theory predicts about whether the subject is in a state that is bad for her, and derive the prediction from the theory's commitments rather than stipulating it. Conclude by identifying the structural feature of the case that does the discriminative work, what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed, and how a defender of whichever theory fares worse would attack the case.
