I am a high school policy debater. Several times each tournament, during the ten or fifteen minutes of preparation time before a round, I have to decide whether a piece of evidence is strong enough to read in front of a judge. Increasingly, the decisive issue is that the model behind a card rests on assumptions that do not describe the real world: consumers are perfectly rational, markets are frictionless, the labor market is perfectly competitive, the climate responds linearly, and so on. Both sides attack evidence by saying the assumptions are unrealistic, but that cannot be the end of the matter, because every model worth using in economics or climate science has unrealistic simplifying assumptions.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure I can actually run during prep, grounded in what the philosophy of science has to say about idealization and unrealistic assumptions, especially the debate around what is usually called Friedman's F-twist: the claim that the realism of a model's assumptions does not matter as long as the model predicts well. The procedure should steer between two errors. One is throwing out a useful model merely because its assumptions are false. The other is admitting a model whose predictions come out right only for the wrong reasons. In translating the theory into a checklist, be explicit about which distinctions must survive and which can be dropped under my time constraint.

The deliverable should be a set of questions to ask in order, with a standard for what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure stops with "do not read this evidence," and the points where it cannot settle the question and a person has to exercise judgment. It should help me distinguish idealizations that can be relaxed without changing the result from assumptions whose falsehood is doing the causal work, and it should say how a model's track record of prediction, if any, changes the assessment.

Then apply the procedure to three pieces of evidence of a kind that actually show up in rounds: a widely cited study concluding that a fifteen-dollar minimum wage will not reduce employment, built on a model that assumes perfectly competitive labor markets; an emissions projection used to support a carbon tax, drawn from an integrated assessment model with a damage function the paper itself describes as highly uncertain; and a study arguing that rent control reduces rental housing supply, based on a simple supply-and-demand model with no informal rental market and no adjustments for housing quality. At least one application should produce an answer I will not like, and at least one should show that a model with obviously unrealistic assumptions can still be legitimately usable.

Finally, say what the procedure cannot do: the model weaknesses it is blind to, and the situations where following it would still let bad evidence through.

Keep these constraints in mind: I have about twelve minutes; I know basic economics and statistics but not econometrics; my partner must sign off on anything we read; and the judge may know very little social science. The procedure must be usable without searching for new sources mid-prep, and it should rest on features of a model that are visible from the abstract, the assumptions section, and the robustness checks the paper actually reports.
