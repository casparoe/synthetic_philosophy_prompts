Since I retired I've been reading game theory and I keep chewing on the dollar auction. For people who havent met it: you auction a dollar bill; the highest bidder pays their bid and gets the dollar, but the second-highest bidder also pays their bid and gets nothing. Bids are supposed to climb past a dollar, and the usual moral is that this shows the sunk-cost fallacy—people throw good money after bad because they're already in deep.

But it seems to me that the standard interpretation is too quick. Once you are the second-highest bidder, stopping is not a clean exit: you already owe your last bid and get nothing. The only way to avoid that certain loss is to bid one more increment, and your past bids are now part of the forward-looking cost of every path open to you. So escalation looks less like a fallacy and more like a rational war of nerves, where each raise is a signal about whether you'll flinch.

I'd like a proposal for how to test the claim that what looks like escalation of commitment in the dollar auction is mostly rational brinkmanship under uncertainty about the other bidder's limit, rather than the sunk-cost fallacy. Design the best experiment or comparison you can. Say exactly what result would push you toward each reading, and say what the experiment could never settle even in principle—for instance, whether the bidders are better described as maximizing money or maximizing the value of not losing a contest, since you can always redraw the utility function after the fact. Please also tell me how confident you are in what you propose and single out the point you're least sure of.
