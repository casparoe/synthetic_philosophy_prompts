I'm a novelist working on a book where the protagonist — everyone around her says she knows what she's doing and does it anyway — and Ive realized I have no sharp account of what weakness of will actually is, as distinct from changing your mind, being impulsive, or just being mentally unwell. Can you help pin it down?

Act as a professional philosopher who works on weakness of will (akrasia) and also knows behavioral economics. Start by brainstorming two or three candidate definitions and say what each would do with the cases below. Then pick the one you think is best, run it through all eight, flag any verdict that feels off, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising call.

Cases:
1. A man has genuinely resolved to quit smoking. At a party, cigarette already in his hand, he judges that he should not smoke it — and smokes it anyway.
2. A woman drives a getaway car because a robber is holding a knife to her side.
3. A student sits down to write a paper; from 9pm to 2am he scrolls on his phone, thinking every ten minutes or so that he should stop, but doesnt.
4. A woman has bought a plane ticket to leave her partner. At the airport she starts to doubt the decision and eventually reaches a settled new judgment that staying is best. Her friends call it weakness; she calls it a change of mind.
5. In January, a man prefers getting $130 in June to getting $100 in May. By the time May arrives, he flips and prefers the $100 now to the $130 in June. Nothing changed except which date is near.
6. Same temporal pattern as (5), but the person is a recovering gambler taking an immediate small payout he would have preferred, from a distance, not to take.
7. A person with OCD knows the front door is locked but goes back three times to check it, hating herself each time.
8. A soldier believes he should hold his position, flees when the shooting starts, and later says he didn't decide anything — his legs just carried him.

My tentative verdicts: 1 and 3 are clearly weak-willed, 2 clearly isn't, and I'm torn on 4, 5, and 7. Cases 5 and 6 are why I'm asking. If hyperbolic discounting is just how human evaluation works — near rewards weighted extra steeply — then the man in May genuinely judges the smaller immediate reward to be better, so maybe there's no akrasia in 5 at all. But from the outside it looks exactly like caving.

I want the definition itself, not a literature summary.
