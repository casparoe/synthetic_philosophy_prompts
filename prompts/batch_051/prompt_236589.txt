An interdisciplinary workshop on the foundations of rational choice has convened an adversarial collaboration between two decision theorists who have spent a year talking past each other. The first defends expected utility theory (EU) as the correct normative account of preference under risk: on this view, rational preferences over risky options satisfy transitivity, continuity, and the independence axiom, and are represented by maximization of expected utility, with all attitudes toward risk expressed by the curvature of the utility function. The second defends risk-weighted expected utility theory (REU): on this view, a rational agent maximizes a risk-weighted sum in which each outcome is weighted by the increment the agent's risk function r assigns to the cumulative probability of receiving that outcome or better; when r(p) = p for every p, REU collapses to EU. The REU defender denies that independence is rationally required, holding that it forbids a coherent global attitude toward risk of the kind displayed in the Allais common-consequence pattern (the pattern of preferring a sure $1 million to a 10% chance of $5 million, an 89% chance of $1 million, and a 1% chance of nothing, while also preferring a 10% chance of $5 million to an 11% chance of $1 million, with nothing otherwise). Both sides accept transitivity and first-order stochastic dominance, and both confine the dispute to choice under risk with known probabilities, not ambiguity, uncertainty, or gains and losses relative to a reference point.

They have agreed to produce a single signed joint statement. Draft it, using three sections.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of claims both can sign, worded so that neither side feels a conclusion has been smuggled in. These should be substantive, not ceremonial: they should pin down the shared formal ground (including the r(p) = p reduction), the shared empirical ground (the robust Allais pattern in standard framings), and the shared normative floor (which dominance and consistency constraints survive on both theories). If a claim would be accepted by one side only as a rhetorical concession, revise it.

2. Disagreements. A table with three columns: Issue (stated neutrally), EU position, and REU position. Rows should include at least: the normative status of the independence axiom; whether the Allais pattern is rationally permissible or a bias to be corrected; whether risk attitude is exhausted by utility curvature; and whether the nonlinear risk function r is an explanatory construct or an extra parameter that merely re-describes curvature. Each entry must be worded the way that camp would word it, not the way its opponent would.

3. Cruxes and tests. Identify the sub-questions whose settlement would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete case, experiment, or argument both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. I expect three cruxes to survive the drafting: (i) whether Allais-type preferences disappear or persist under transparent, regrouped framings that make the common consequence salient; (ii) whether an REU agent can be exploited in a sequential-choice money pump, or whether any such pump assumes a form of independence or consequentialism at the plan level; and (iii) whether, once utility is fixed independently, a nonlinear r makes different and more accurate predictions than a suitably recalibrated concave utility function. Do not let the joint statement presuppose an answer to any crux.

Where the drafting reveals that an apparent disagreement was verbal—for example, a dispute over what to call "risk aversion" rather than over any condition on preferences—record it separately in a brief note, not as a substantive disagreement.
