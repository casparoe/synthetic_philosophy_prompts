I've been trying to pin down what I actually think about loyalty and fairness, mostly because I keep catching myself applying one standard at work and another at home. I'm a software engineer and I don't have any formal philosophy background, so I'll state this as plainly as I can. I tried turning my views into something like unit tests: most of them pass on their own, but the integration test of how I actually behave fails, and I cant find the bug. I also don't want to bias you by saying which of these I'd most like to keep or drop, partly because I genuinely don't know. Here's the list, roughly in order of how strongly I hold each one:

1. A parent should save their own child from a burning building before a stranger's child, and a parent who didn't wouldn't be making a harmless personal choice—they'd be doing something wrong.
2. A hiring manager shouldn't give a job to a friend just because they're friends. That's bias, and it's unfair to the other applicants.
3. What makes (2) wrong is that, viewed from outside the friendship, there's no relevant difference between the friend and the other candidates, and a rule has to work from outside.
4. Loyalty to big groups—my-country-right-or-wrong patriotism, unconditional rooting for the company you work for, ethnic solidarity—is mostly a bug, not a virtue. I think of partiality as a bit like caching: it's efficient when you're dealing with people you actually know, but scaled up to millions it just reproduces stale, tribal judgements.
5. The only special obligations I really understand are ones you take on voluntarily, like promises, contracts, or debts of gratitude. I don't see how simply being born into a group or a family gives you reasons.
6. When I'm spending my own money, I think it's fine, maybe even a bit praiseworthy, to support local businesses over slightly cheaper or better options elsewhere, just because they're local.
7. Moral judgements should be universal in form: if an act is wrong when a stranger does it, it's wrong when my brother does it.
8. And yet if a manager has discretion, giving a struggling direct report extra chances because they have a good working relationship is just good management, not unfairness.

Could you audit this set for me? I'd like to know which pairs (or larger clusters) are in genuine tension and how severe each tension is, what the cheapest fix would be even if its just weakening one belief, and which pairs only look like they pull against each other but dissolve on closer inspection. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what I'd be giving up by making it. Please write the answer in British English.
