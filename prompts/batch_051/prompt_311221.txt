I'm working on a feature for a science and ideas outlet about whether we can legitimately take other people's word for moral matters the way we take a doctor's word for a diagnosis. The debate has a name for this, moral testimony, but people mean different things by it, so I've been trying to pin down a working definition for my own notes. The four criteria I've landed on are: (1) moral content — the belief you end up with is a first-order moral claim, not merely an empirical or descriptive fact; (2) say-so basis — the fact that the other person asserted it is your main reason for believing it, rather than the underlying reasons they could give you; (3) asymmetry — you take the other person to be better placed than you to answer the moral question itself, not just better informed about the surrounding non-moral facts; and (4) no independent grasp — you hold the belief without the kind of first-person understanding you'd have if you'd reached it through your own reasoning rather than the other person's.

The case I keep chewing on is from my own newsroom. A colleague who has been on the receiving end of a certain kind of remark told me that a phrase we use in copy to describe a group is demeaning. I'd always read it as nuetral shorthand. She explained, briefly, that it's the kind of phrase people use to put someone like her down, and that hearing it in the newsroom makes her feel like she doesn't belong. I came away beleiving the phrase is demeaning and that we should stop using it. What I can't sort out is whether I just took moral testimony. On one hand, I didn't outsource the whole judgment to her: I already had a standing principle that we shouldn't use demeaning language, and she supplied the missing piece about how the phrase lands. On the other hand, "demeaning" isn't a neutral description, and I'm not sure I could have reached the judgment without her say-so.

Walk me through the four criteria one by one and tell me whether this case satisfies each of them. Where a criterion doesn't cleanly apply, say whether that's a problem with the case or with the criterion. Then give an overall verdict: does this count as moral testimony on my working definition, or do the criteria underdetermine it? Also tell me which single criterion the case puts the most pressure on, and whether I should revise it and how.

Format: return a JSON object with this schema. "overall_verdict": a string, one of "qualifies", "does_not_qualify", or "criteria_underdetermine". "criterion_verdicts": an array of four objects, each with "criterion" (the criterion name as a string), "verdict" (one of "satisfied", "not_satisfied", or "unclear"), "reasoning" (one or two sentences applying the criterion to the newsroom case), and "criterion_needs_revision" (a boolean, true if the case reveals a problem with that criterion). "most_strained_criterion": a string naming the criterion the case puts the most pressure on. "proposed_revision": a string, one sentence saying how to revise that criterion, or "none". "what_the_case_shows": a string, one or two sentences on whether the case should count as moral testimony and why. "confidence": a number from 0 to 1 for how confident you are in the overall verdict.
