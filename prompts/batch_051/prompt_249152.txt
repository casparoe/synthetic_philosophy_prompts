At a party last weekend I got pulled into an argument between two people I know, and I can’t stop turning it over. One of them said that intellectually he knows other people are conscious, but he sometimes catches himself feeling as though he is the main character of reality and everyone else is an NPC. He said the feeling isn’t just a quirk: the only mind he can be absolutely sure of is his own, and everything that looks like evidence for everyone else’s inner life — people wincing, laughing, describing their dreams, hesitating before they speak — is exactly what a sophisticated automaton could do without feeling anything. His claim was that no observation could ever settle whether anyone else has a private point of view, because any possible evidence is third-person, behavioral evidence, while consciousness is a first-person thing. So the belief that other people are minds is a leap of faith that goes beyond anything evidence can support.

His friend pushed back hard. She said this is all built on a double standard. She said she doesn’t have direct access to anyone else’s consciousness either, including the first man’s. If he gets to count himself as conscious on the strength of his own private access, then by that standard everyone else can count themselves as conscious too, and none of them is special. The only difference is that each person’s access is private to them, which is a property shared by every candidate mind. The first man answered that she had just proven his point: from where he sits, the so-called privacy of every other person’s access is itself an inference from behavior, because he is the only one whose access he actually has. She came back that his position wasn’t really about evidence at all, but about an unfalsifiable suspicion — a hypothesis designed to survive any possible observation — and that he could equally wonder whether his own self from yesterday was conscious, since he only has memories, not direct access. He said that was different, because identity across time is required for the very act of wondering in the first place. At that point they turned to me, and I honestly didn’t know what to say.

Please go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about which specific point, where each argument overreaches, and what both of them are missing. Then give an overall verdict on who was more right. Finally, tell me how confident you are in your adjudication, and single out the point you are least sure of.
