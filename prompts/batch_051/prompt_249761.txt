A policy person cornered me after a department colloquium and pitched this, and I want to think it through with someone. The proposal: scrap periodic eligibility reviews for unemployment and disability benefits and replace them with a standing audit lottery. Every month, 2% of claimants are randomly selected for a full audit — bank statements, cohabitation checks, undeclared income, the works. If the audit finds you were overpaid, you repay it out of future benefit payments plus a 30% penalty, and this applies even when the overpayment was the department's own mistake rather than anything you did. If the audit comes back clean, you get a small bonus, about a week's benefit. Separately, anyone can file a confidential tip that someone is defrauding the system. A tip doesn't trigger an audit by itself; it puts the named claimant at the front of the queue for the next lottery draw, and if the tip leads to a penalty, the tipster gets 10% of it. If the tip is found to be baseless and the tipster is identifiable, the tipster pays a fine.

Treat it as a mechanism, not a moral position. What does this do to who claims, who tips, and how the auditors behave? Where does it get gamed first — by claimants, by tipsters, by the agency itself? And I keep flip-flopping on whether the penalty-for-official-error clause is the load-bearing part or its a disaster: does the clean-audit bonus actually protect honest claimants, or just make them paranoid? Walk me through it, and tell me which failure modes could be fixed without gutting the scheme.
