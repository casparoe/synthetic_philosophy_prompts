My sister has drug-resistant epilepsy, and her neurologist recently raised the possibility of a corpus callosotomy, the surgical disconnection of the two cerebral hemispheres. Trying to understand what such a procedure would mean for her has forced me to think through the split-brain findings, and I have arrived at a view I want examined before I let it influence how I think about her case. My question is conceptual, not medical.

I have been calling the view the Criterion of Answerable Integration. The proposal is that two mental events belong to one conscious subject at a time just in case they are jointly available to a single act of practical self-government at that time. By practical self-government I mean a process in which the system can weigh the two events against each other, register a conflict or coherence between them, and on that basis form or revise one decision, avowal, or correction of error. If no single process could, even under favorable conditions, bring both events to bear on the same decision or revision, then they are not states of a single subject. By "subject" I mean the practical subject: the entity that owns its states by being able to answer for them.

I want to be clear that I am not proposing an introspective test. Part of the view is that how things seem from the inside is the wrong place to look, and that phenomenal unity, to the extent it is real, is a shadow cast by answerable integration. The criterion also lets us avoid the forced choice between saying split-brain patients are two subjects and saying they are merely one subject whose parts are out of touch. The patient is one subject to the degree that answerable integration across the hemispheres is possible, and otherwise not. When material presented only to the right hemisphere drives a correct left-hand choice that the speaking left hemisphere cannot explain, the left hemisphere often rationalizes afterward rather than genuinely weighing the right hemisphere's content; on the criterion, there is no single subject spanning that pair of contents at that time, because no single act of decision or revision could be based on both. But when cross-cueing makes a right-hemisphere content jointly available to a single process, unity is restored. Subject count is therefore graded and context-dependent, not a simple count.

I suspect this may be a repackaging of something in the access-consciousness or agency literature, or else it may simply be circular. I want an honest assessment: (1) Is the criterion coherent, or does the notion of "a single act of practical self-government" already presuppose the very subject it is meant to define? (2) What are the three strongest objections, and are any fatal rather than merely uncomfortable? (3) Where does it stop being distinctive, and does it mislead anywhere? (4) Does it get the split-brain cases wrong in a way that should matter for how I think about what a callosotomy would mean for my sister?
