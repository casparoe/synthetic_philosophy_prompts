Here's an argument I got backed into at a workshop on Bayesian confirmation theory, and I can't shake it even though the conclusion is obviously mad. It started from the problem of old evidence, but sharpened until it seems to show something stronger.

(1) For evidence E to confirm a hypothesis H for an agent, learning E has to raise the agent's probability of H: P(H|E) > P(H).

(2) If an agent already knows E, then P(E)=1 for that agent.

(3) Whenever P(E)=1, P(H|E)=P(H) exactly. So evidence you already know can never confirm anything for you.

(4) Now take a theory built precisely to explain a long-known anomoly: general relativity and the precession of Mercury's perihelion is the stock example.

(5) When scientists evaluated general relativity, the perihelion data was old news; any serious scientist had P(perhelion)=1.

(6) By (3), the perihelion anomaly could not confirm general relativity.

(7) But that's perverse: explaining the anomaly was one of the main reasons physicists took the theory seriously, and the same structure — a theory proposed to explain data already in hand — describes a huge amount of successful science.

(8) So either (1) is false, or the move from (2)-(3) to (6) is dodgy, or a vast amount of what scientists call confirmation is no such thing.

Which premise or step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep (1), since abandoning it feels like abandoning Bayesianism altogether. Give me exactly five candidate diagnoses as a numbered list, ranked from most to least plausible, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion, in British English.
