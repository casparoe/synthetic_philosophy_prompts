I am a novelist with a working vocabulary drawn from craft: plot as a sequence of irrevocable choices, characters whose existence is itself a consequence of those choices, point of view, and the familiar distinction between a story's being worse and a particular character's being treated badly. Restate the nonidentity problem in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can — the cases in which a decision brings one set of future people into existence rather than another, the claim that such a decision cannot wrong any of those people if their lives are worth living, and the turn to impersonal principles to explain why the choice may nonetheless be wrong. Then give me the residue report: which parts translate cleanly into narrative terms, which parts you had to fudge or distort, and which parts have no counterpart at all in how I think about fiction. Finally, tell me whether the residue reflects a limitation of my narrative vocabulary or a sign that the distinction between person-affecting and impersonal wrongness is less stable than philosophers assume. I want to know whether my instinct — that a novelist already lives inside the nonidentity problem — is insight or confusion.
