my roommate and i have been going back and forth on an argument that seems to prove way too much, and neither of us can find the load-bearing step.
(1) other things equal, if a child would have a life worth living, having that child is a good thing.
(2) what's true for a family is true for a city: adding people whose lives are worth living can't by itself make things worse, even if the new people live a bit less well than the current residents, as long as nobody already there is made worse off.
(3) "no worse than" chains: if each step isn't worse than the one before it, then the first isn't worse than the last.
(4) so start with a small city of very happy people; add one person whose life is barely worth living, then another, then another, the average falling a little each time. by (2) each step is fine, and by (3) the whole chain is fine.
(5) the endpoint is an enormous city of people all living lives only just barely worth living, and the argument says this is no worse than the small, genuinely happy city you started with.
(6) that's absurd — the small happy city is just better.
so one of (1)-(4) has to go, but each one sounded undeniable on its own. where does this go wrong, and what do i have to give up? i'd most like to keep (1), and i half-suspect the slide is in (3), but i cant say exactly why. i also notice i only call the endpoint absurd when i'm thinking about averages; the moment i think about total happiness, the huge city sounds better, which makes me distrust both. does the whole thing still work if you swap "city" for "species," so the conclusion is that a vast species of barely-worth-living lives is no worse than a small happy one?
