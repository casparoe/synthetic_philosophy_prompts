I've been puzzling over an asymmetry in how we judge our own and other people's emotions, and I want to know whether it can actually be defended.

We think it is perfectly normal — often a sign of sensitivity — to be genuinely frightened during a horror film or genuinely sad when a character in a novel dies, even though we know all along that the events never happened and the characters are not real. No one expects the feeling to disappear once we remember it is fiction. But in the rest of life we treat emotions as irrational when the belief that anchored them turns out to be false: if you are angry at a friend for a lie he never told, or afraid of a burglar you believe isn't there, we expect the anger or fear to dissolve, and if it does not, we think something has gone wrong. So we seem to give emotions a pass exactly when the missing belief is known from the start — but only when fiction is doing the work.

A third case makes the puzzle sharper. Imagine someone sitting alone, vividly and deliberately imagining that someone he loves has just died, until he is genuinely, painfully grief-stricken. Most people would call that morbid or self-indulgent, not sensitive. Yet the novel-reader and the bare imaginer seem to do the same kind of thing: each conjures up an event he believes did not occur, and each ends up with real tears rather than pretend ones. If we want to say the novel-reader's grief is fitting and the daydreamer's isn't, we need something in fiction — the finished artifact, the author's intention, the shared audience, a make-believe frame — that mere imagining lacks. I cannot yet see why any of those should make the difference between a rational emotion and an irrational one.

So my question is: is there anything that justifies treating these cases differently, or are we just inconsistent? I would rather keep our respect for emotions about fiction than dismiss them as make-believe, but I want to know what that costs. Please say how confident you are in your answer and single out the point you are least sure of.
