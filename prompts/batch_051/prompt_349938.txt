Hey, I'm a phd student in philosophy of social science, working on causal explanation in history, and I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher of history with a special interest in causation. Take your time, think carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming the main candidate causes and how you'd classify them before you commit.

Take the 2008 global financial crisis as the episode. I already know the standard list of causes — housing bubble, subprime lending, securitization and CDOs, credit rating agencies, bank leverage, shadow banking, global savings glut, deregulation — so don't just rehearse that list. What I want is a carefuly analysis of the crisis through the lens of overdetermination and multi-causal explanation.

Specifically:
- Which candidate causes, if any, were individually sufficient for some major feature of the crisis (the bust, the panic, the depth of the recession)? Which were merely necessary? Which function as INUS conditions (insufficient but necessary parts of unnecessary but sufficient conditions)?
- If you subtract one factor — say, subprime lending — but hold the rest fixed, do we still get a crisis of roughly the same shape, a smaller one, or none? Run a few of these counterfactuals and be explicit about what each one assumes.
- Does the episode show that big historical events are often overdetermined, or does it show that 'overdetermination' is usually too strong and what historians actually need is something like joint causation and causal fields?
- Where does the overdetermination lens break down or misdescribe what happened? Timing, panic dynamics, and the policy response all strike me as places where it might go silent.

Be honest about where the theory has nothing to say instead of forcing it to speak. I'd rather get a disciplined mapping of causes to their causal roles than a smooth story.
