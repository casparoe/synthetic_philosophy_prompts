Here's a thing I keep going back and forth on, and I'd like a careful treatment. Suppose a constitution has this clause:

"Article 12. The franchise shall be universal. Every citizen shall have one vote, and no law shall distinguish among citizens in respect of voting strength or shall deny any citizen the vote on account of disability, age, or form of mind. This Article shall not be amended, repealed, or suspended, nor shall any law alter the meaning of the term 'citizen' as used in this Article."

The "form of mind" language was added because the drafters had just spent fifty years watching majorities disenfranchise people with cognitive and psychiatric conditions, and they wanted an eternity clause to stop it. It's a Ulysses-style precommitment: they didn't trust sucessor majorities not to do it again.

Now the unforeseen case. A large share of the population has gradually replaced their biological brains with uploaded software, over years, in a reversible-ish way. By any first-person measure they're the same people: memories, plans, debts, jobs. The still-biological majority panics and passes a law saying that, for purposes of Article 12, "citizen" means only a person with a living biological brain. Uploads can't vote. The government says no amendment has occurred; they're just applying the old word to a new thing.

My question is whether the law violates the clause or applies it. Work through the plain text, the purpose, what the drafters would likely have said if asked about uploads, and what a reasonable reader today would take the clause to mean. Then step back and tell me what the case shows about precommitment as a tool: is this the clause working, failing, or being evaded by changing the interpretation instead of the text? And is there a decent argument that the precommitment shouldn't bind us once the world has departed this far from the drafters' assumptions, even if the text points the other way? Also: would it matter if the uploads had been made as one-shot copies rather than gradual replacements?
