I direct the spiritual-care training program at a large teaching hospital. We prepare volunteer chaplains who visit patients and families facing serious diagnoses, complicated surgeries, and end-of-life decisions. At least once a week, a debriefing reveals the same problem: a patient or family member has said something like "God already knows how this will end, so my choices don't matter," or "If every outcome was known before I was born, why fight?" Our volunteers answer with generic encouragement or improvised theology, and too often the person ends up more resigned or confused than before. The stakes are high: sometimes the person is deciding whether to continue treatment.

I would like you to write a bedside decision procedure our volunteers can follow, grounded specifically in Boethius's treatment of divine foreknowledge and eternity. The procedure should help a volunteer decide, in a ten-to-fifteen-minute conversation, which distinction to draw for this particular person—roughly, between foreknowledge and causation, and between God's knowing from an eternal, non-temporal standpoint and events' being predetermined in time. It should be usable by people with no training in philosophy or theology. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops or gets escalated to a staff chaplain or the medical team, and which steps are judgment calls rather than principled rules. It must respect that patients come from many traditions, including some with a strong doctrine of predestination; our job is not to correct anyone's theology. The final version will go into our training manual, and I and the hospital's director of pastoral care will have to approve it.

Then run the procedure on three cases, showing how a volunteer would work through each one: a patient declining postoperative chemotherapy because "God already decided whether it will work"; a family member paralyzed in an agonizing treatment decision because every option feels fixed in advance; and a patient from a tradition teaching exhaustive divine predestination who says his timely appointment with the oncology clinic was "set in stone." In at least one case, show where the procedure gives an answer I will not like, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle and the situations in which it should not be used at all.
