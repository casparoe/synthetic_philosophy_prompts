For the past year, two longtime posters on a rationalist-adjacent forum I read have been arguing about whether hypocrisy undercuts a person's standing to blame. The dispute recurs whenever someone criticizes an institution for wrongdoing and a third party points out that the critic is implicated in the same or similar conduct: the polluting company's sustainability officer who attacks another firm's emissions; the investor who condemns fossil-fuel financing while still holding the assets; the AI-safety advocate who uses frontier models. The first poster defends what she calls the standing view. On that view, a criticism can be true and its reasoning can be sound, and yet the critic can lack the entitlement to issue it; persistent, unreckoned-with hypocrisy is a way of losing that entitlement. The second poster defends the content view. On that view, a criticism stands or falls with its claims and arguments; facts about the critic's conduct bear at most on the audience's guess about the critic's motives or reliability, and a retort about the critic's hypocrisy is, unless the critic's behavior is introduced as evidence about the norm itself, a tu quoque.

The two have agreed to write an adversarial collaboration instead of another year of comment threads. Draft the document they should produce. It need not make them agree; it should make each of them able to sign every sentence of the joint portion and recognize her own view, rather than a caricature, in the residual disagreement.

The document should include:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. Begin with claims about what a charge of hypocrisy does and does not show. Then formulate their shared commitments about the difference between a criticism's truth, its argumentative force, and the propriety of the critic's making it. Avoid vacuous agreements; every claim should say something that at least some reasonable participant in this debate would have been tempted to deny.

2. A disagreements table. For each remaining dispute, state the sub-question in neutral terms, then give the formulation the standing theorist would accept and the incompatible formulation the content theorist would accept. The wording on each side should be precise enough that the other side recognizes the disagreement as substantive rather than strawmanned.

3. A crux list. A crux is a sub-question such that getting a well-specified answer would force at least one side to revise its view. For each crux, say which side would have to change what if the crux resolved against her.

4. For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or condition both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded before any appeal to the case. I suspect one crux concerns whether standing to blame is anything over and above the audience's trust in the critic; another concerns whether blame is the sort of speech act that requires the blamer to be committed to the relevant norm in a way the hypocrite is not; a third concerns whether a hypocrite's blame has some wrong-making feature that would not be present in an otherwise identical, non-hypocritical act of blame.

Finally, say which parts of the dispute, if any, are verbal. For example, if standing and credibility have been used interchangeably, that is a distinction to record, not a victory condition; but if making that distinction collapses one of the apparent disagreements, say so explicitly.

The document should argue from cases and principles; do not include a bibliography or ask the reader to consult sources.
