American defamation law allows liability for false statements of fact but generally not for statements of pure opinion, and that boundary is doing strange work when the statement is a rating or forecast. A securities analyst who writes that a company is a bad credit risk and likely to default is almost certainly protected; an analyst who writes that the company missed three debt-service payments can be sued if the statement is false. Yet the forecast is usually assembled from checkable, falsifiable figures, and once the outcome arrives a wrong forecast can seem just as factual as a wrong account of last quarter's payments—and often more damaging to the target. What, if anything, justifies treating ratings and forecasts differently from factual claims? Develop the strongest candidate justification (future-tense indeterminacy, the reasonable reader's expectations, disclosed methodology and hedging, the social value of speculative commentary, or something else), test it against a rating that was based on demonstrably falsified inputs, and say whether the distinction should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned.
