I'm trying to collect the main live options for responding to Fitch's paradox, and I want to see if I've missed one. By the knowability principle I mean roughly: for any true proposition p, it's possible to know p. The punchline is that from this plus some tame-looking logical assumptions you can prove that every truth is actually known, which is bad for verificationists and other anti-realists. So far my list has: (1) reject the principle and treat Fitch's proof as a refutation of moderate antirealism; (2) restrict the principle to 'Cartesian' propositions — ones that can be known without the act of knowing destroying their truth, so the trick proposition 'p and nobody knows p' is excluded; (3) go intuitionist and block the last classical step, so you only get that you can't exhibit an unknown truth, not that every truth is known; (4) refomulate knowability with an actuality operator, so what's knowable is that p is actually true, not that p is true simpliciter; (5) reject the substitution of 'p and nobody knows p' for p in the principle as an illicit move into a modal/epistemic context. I want another response to Fitch's paradox that's not on this list, and not just a rewording, special case, or combination of any of those. Say what core move it makes and whether you think it works or just relocates the problem. Also keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested response as the consensus.
