I have been trying to understand Nozick's experience machine as an intuition pump rather than as a single decisive argument. In the standard version, you are asked to imagine a machine that stimulates your brain so that you have whichever experiences you choose—writing a great novel, forming deep friendships, climbing a mountain—while your body floats in a tank. You would not know you were plugged in; the simulated world would simply seem to be your life. Most people refuse to plug in, and that refusal is held to carry considerable weight: it is supposed to show that we value actual contact with reality over and above how our lives feel from the inside.

Now change one feature and hold everything else fixed. The machine is not a simulator but a real, physically instantiated habitat: a large sealed enclosure with real soil, real weather, real books, real institutions, and other embodied people who entered voluntarily. The amnesia and the apparent ordinariness of the life inside are preserved. Upon entering, you lose all memory of the outside and of your choice, so that, once inside, the world simply seems to be your life. But the events are not illusions fed into a brain; your friendships, failures, discoveries, and achievements are genuine events, caused in the ordinary way by real people and real processes. The only restriction is absolute causal isolation: nothing inside the enclosure ever affects anything outside it, and nobody can leave or send any signal out. Compared with ordinary life, the only thing missing is causal contact with the world outside.

I would like you to run the standard Nozickian analysis on this altered case. Does the revised version still pump the refusal-to-plug-in intuition, or does the intuition collapse once the experiences are made real rather than illusory? If it collapses, does that suggest the original thought experiment was drawing its persuasive force specifically from the simulated, deceptive character of the machine rather than from any general preference for reality—and would that weaken the conclusion the original is typically used to support? If it does not collapse, what exactly is doing the work? I want both the first-order verdict and an account of what the variation reveals about how the thought experiment persuades.
