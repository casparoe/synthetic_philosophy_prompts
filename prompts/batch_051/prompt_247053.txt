Consider the standard gradual-replacement argument for the conclusion that a whole-brain emulation could be the same mind as the biological original. In its familiar form, the argument asks us to imagine that each of your neurons is replaced, one at a time, by a component that exactly reproduces the replaced neuron's causal role: its input–output behavior and its contribution to the dynamics of the surrounding network. Because the functional organization of the brain is preserved at every stage, the argument continues, consciousness cannot coherently be supposed to fade away across the replacements. A subject whose consciousness faded would at some point be unable to notice or report the fade, and the result would be an intolerable dissociation between the system's actual states and the states the system says it has. The conclusion drawn is that what matters for preserving the same mind is preserved functional organization, not the biological substrate.

Now suppose the actually feasible route to whole-brain emulation does not proceed by component-by-component replacement with exact functional isomorphs. Instead, it proceeds by scanning a brain at some resolution, reconstructing its connectome, and then instantiating a simulation in which synapses are modeled statistically, ion channels and other subcellular mechanisms are lumped into aggregate parameters, and the dynamics reproduce the original's behavior and coarse-grained activity patterns without replicating every fine-grained causal detail. Let us grant that the resulting emulation is behaviorally indistinguishable from the original and matches its neural activity at the level of population codes. It is nonetheless a fresh construction from a static snapshot, not the endpoint of a continuity-preserving sequence of replacements.

Does the gradual-replacement argument survive this change? In particular: which premise of the original argument is doing the work, and is that premise available in the scan-and-instantiate case? If the argument does not survive in its original form, state the strongest repaired version you can, one that says something weaker but still important about when an emulation preserves the same mind, and say exactly what the repair gives up. If you think it does survive, show how the original argument's premises apply to an approximate, statistically specified emulation built from a snapshot rather than through a continuity-preserving sequence.
