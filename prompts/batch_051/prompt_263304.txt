I work as a security engineer on the team that runs our company's public bug bounty program. The program's terms include the following clause, which I am expected to apply when triaging communications from external researchers: "A report is extortionate—and may be referred to law enforcement—if the reporter threatens to disclose a vulnerability, or conditions nondisclosure, delayed disclosure, or remediation assistance on payment or other consideration." We have always applied it to the cases its authors plainly had in mind: a researcher who writes "pay us or we publish," or one who withholds a proof of concept until a fee is agreed. This week we received something that fits neither pattern. A researcher writes that she has discovered a way to enumerate our users' email addresses through a public endpoint, that she intends to publish a technical writeup in ten days, and that she is "available for a consulting engagement beginning this week; please see the attached rate card and schedule before the publication date." The attachment is a standard day-rate card for advisory services, and she never states that payment would delay or suppress the writeup. Two of my colleagues read the message as a carefully laundered threat; two read it as two independently legitimate items—a disclosure timeline plus a professional solicitation—that the program ought to permit.

I keep getting stuck on the point underneath. Publishing the finding is exactly the kind of disclosure the program is supposed to encourage, and offering paid consulting is unobjectionable, so the clause seems to make the combination extortionate without saying at what point a combination becomes extortion. Please work through what the quoted clause requires of us here under (a) a literal reading of its wording, (b) a purposive reading, (c) what its drafters would likely have said if this exact message had been put to them, and (d) what a reasonable recipient of the message would understand her to mean; then tell me whether there is a coherent line that places this message on one side, and, if so, where that line runs.
