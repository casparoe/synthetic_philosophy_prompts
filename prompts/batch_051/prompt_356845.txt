I got into an argument with a friend over whether we're living through a "collapse" or not, so I picked up a popular history book about fallen civilizations — Bronze Age Greece, the Classic Maya, Rapa Nui, the lot. The authors throughline is roughly this: the old story treated societies as machines that run on resources and fall over when they overshoot, but a younger generation of archaeologists mostly rejects that picture. On their view "collapse" is the wrong category, because complex societies rarely end — they reorganize, and ordinary people persist. The Classic Maya didn't vanish; the lowland cities thinned out and political power moved around, but there was no civilizational death. Easter Island is the big test case: the book says the old ecocide story, where islanders cut down every last tree to move statues and then turned on each other, has basically been disproven. Radiocarbon dates and rat-gnawed seeds supposedly show the islanders adapted, farmed in stone mulch, and only crashed after European contact and introduced disease. Even the Bronze Age collapse, on this telling, was more a reshuffling of trade networks and elites than an actual collapse. And the book's bigger moral is that environmental explanations for collapse aren't just outdated — they're pernicious, because they blame the victims and let empire off the hook.

I'm inclined to like this story — I'd rather not believe the Easter Islanders destroyed themselves — but the book is so breezy about "debunking" that I can't tell how much is actual consensus and how much is the author swinging the pendulum too far. Is this a fair summary of how specialists think about collapse now? I'd love to know which parts are solid, which are common simplifications, where someone in the field would say it distorts things, and whether the moral lesson survives if some of the specifics are less settled than the book implies.
