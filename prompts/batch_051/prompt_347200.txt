Here's a claim I keep hearing, often stated as if it's obvious: costly signals are honest because they're costly — a peacock's tail, a gazelle stotting at a lion, whatever. But I've never seen anyone clearly seperate that from a rival story: the signal is an index, meaning it's honest because a low-quality animal physically can't produce the big tail or the vigorous display, so honesty comes from constraint and the cost is just along for the ride. And then there's the cheap-talk option: if interests mostly align, or partners interact repeatedly, a zero-cost signal can stay honest anyway. So what I want is not another summary of the handicap principle but a test. How would you test whether the cost is doing the honesty work, as opposed to constraint or common interest? Give me the cleanest concrete design you can — a field experiment, a lab setup, a model, a natural comparison — and say exactly what result would favor the handicap reading, what would favor the index reading, and what any clean result would still leave unsettled. Also tell me whether the usual examples (peacock tail, stotting, antler size, begging calls) are actually good tests of this or mostly just illustrations.
