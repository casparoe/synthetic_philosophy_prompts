Write a dialogue between two linguists who disagree about what an alphabetic writing system should encode. One character defends a phonemic ideal: an alphabet is fundamentally a way of transcribing speech, and an orthography is better to the extent that its grapheme-to-phoneme correspondences are regular, transparent, and predictable. The other character defends a morphophonemic ideal: an orthography can be a theory of a language's morphological and lexical structure, and it is legitimate for spelling to keep a morpheme visually constant across related words even when its pronunciation changes, as with the shared root in sign and signature or electric and electricity.

The two should be evenly matched and should genuinely respond to each other's actual points rather than talking past one another. In the course of the dialogue, have them address at least these questions: what it means to call an orthography phonemic, given that no practical writing system marks allophonic detail; whether an orthography should be optimized for the learner, the fluent reader, the writer, or the foreign learner; whether spellings of the kind just mentioned are arbitrary historical residue or the product of deeper rules; and whether the acquisition costs of a deep orthography are repaid later in skilled reading. Let them use concrete linguistic examples and arrive at no tidy resolution. Write the dialogue itself, not an essay about it. The answer should be written in American English.
