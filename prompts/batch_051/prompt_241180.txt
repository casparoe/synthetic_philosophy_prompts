I keep coming back to the passage in Darwin's The Descent of Man, in the chapter on the moral sense, where he proposes that any animal with well-marked social instincts, once its intellectual powers got anywhere near ours, would inevitably acquire a conscience. He's not just saying we share empathy with other animals. He's saying the whole moral sense — guilt, obligation, the voice that says "you must" — can be built up out of social instincts plus intelligence, habit, and concern for the approbation of others. I find this genuinely compelling: it unifies human morality with animal sociality, it explains why conscience is so bound up with shame and reputation, and it gives one developmental story instead of having to posit a separate moral faculty or a divine spark.

But then I hit the intuition. When I think about something like torturing a child for fun, the judgement that this is wrong doesn't feel like an elaborated social instinct. It feels categorical — like I'm detecting a demand that would bind me even if my tribe approved of it, even if I'd been raised to enjoy it. Conscience doesn't just urge; it judges. And I can't shake the sense that if I fully accept Darwin's story, I'm explaining the feeling of authority rather than the authority itself.

So here's my question. I don't want to be told that one side obviously wins. I want the strongest case that the intuition is a leftover from a pre-evolutionary picture of morality and should be debunked, and the strongest case that the intuition is tracking something the genealogy can't touch and the theory needs amending. Or, if there's a happy reading, what exactly would have to be true for both to survive. Which should yield?
