Here's a parallel I can't make up my mind about, and I'd like someone to tell me if it's deep or just pattern-matching. Choosing a calendar epoch — the AD/BC divide, the Unix epoch's 1970, the Holocene calendar's attempt to shift the zero back near the start of the current geological epoch — seems to me a lot like choosing the zero point of a utility function. In both cases the choice is arbitrary in one sense but not entirely free in another: some choices make everyday arithmetic and comparisons easer, and people keep slipping into treating the zero as though it carried real information. Measurement theory has a neat way of saying what survives such arbitrary choices: utility functions are only defined up to positive affine transformations, so the zero and the unit are convention, but ratios of intervals are meaningful. If that machinery transfered to calendars, it would predict that questions like 'which year zero is actually the right one?' are pseudo-questions, and only intervals and differences between dates should be treated as meaningful. But I'm not sure that prediction holds, because a calendar's zero has to interact with recurring cycles — weeks, leap years, months, liturgical or fiscal cycles — and those interactions might make some epochs objectively more convenient, not merely conventional. Is there a real structural parallel here that breaks in an instructive place, or am I just getting distracted by the shared word 'zero'? Give me your bottom line in the first sentence — the actual verdict — and then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating the question. And write it in British English.
