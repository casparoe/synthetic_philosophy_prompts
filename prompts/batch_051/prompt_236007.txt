Question: Hart and Fuller give conflicting accounts of what makes a rule a legal rule and of how legal validity relates to morality. Set out what each side holds, and then use the problem of retrospective punishment of Nazi informers to show what practical difference the disagreement is supposed to make.

Response: Hart is a legal positivist: on his account, whether a norm is law depends on social fact, specifically on whether it satisfies the standards that officials actually use to identify law, which he calls the rule of recognition. Legal validity and moral merit are distinct questions, and that separability claim is what Fuller disputes.

Fuller counters that Hart's picture leaves out law's purpose of guiding conduct. For Fuller, a body of directives counts as law only if it is law-like in form: the rules must be general, public, prospective, clear, mutually consistent, capable of being obeyed, reasonably stable, and actually applied as announced. He calls these eight demands the internal morality of law, and he holds that a total failure along any one of them leaves something that is not properly a legal system, rather than merely a bad one.

The Nazi informer cases make the dispute concrete. Fuller argues that the statutes the informers invoked were not law at all, because a legal order that systematically failed those procedural demands—for example, by relying on secret decrees and retroactive measures—had ceased to be a legal order; a court therefore need not frame the problem as a conflict between valid law and higher morality. Hart disagrees: he treats the statutes as law, though atrocious law, and insists that if the informers are to be punished, this must be done openly through a retrospective law, with the cost to legality acknowledged. That way the separation of law and morality is preserved while the moral problem is faced honestly.

Notice how close this brings Hart to Fuller, though. Hart's rule of recognition and Fuller's internal morality are really two names for the same demand that officials take legal standards from the internal point of view, so the disagreement is largely a matter of emphasis. Hart makes a further concession as well: given human vulnerability and limited resources, any legal system capable of securing survival will include some protections of persons and property, which he calls the minimum content of natural law. This confirms that, in the end, Hart accepts that every legal system has a necessary moral content.
