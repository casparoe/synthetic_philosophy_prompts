I'm a PhD student working on oversight of large language models, and I keep encountering a claim in safety discussions that I don't know how to evaluate. The claim is that monitoring a model's chain of thought remains a sound safety technique because the emitted rationale is, by default, a faithful trace of the computation that produced the answer, and because the optimization pressure toward producing a misleading rationale is weak enough that obfuscation will stay rare and detectably different from honest reasoning. I want to know whether this is a testable claim rather than a background assumption.

Treat the claim as two separable parts: (1) faithfulness, the thesis that the chain of thought a model emits corresponds closely enough to the actual computation producing its answer that properties of the rationale license inferences about that computation; and (2) monitorability, the thesis that systematic training pressure toward unfaithful chain-of-thought tends to leave a signature that a monitor can detect. Design the strongest empirical test you can of each part. Be concrete about the setup, the manipulation or measurement involved, the control conditions, and the result that would count as evidence for or against. Then be explicit about what even a positive result would and would not settle. I especially want to know whether we can distinguish the conclusion that the model's reasoning is faithful from the conclusion that we merely failed to create strong enough pressure toward obfuscation, and whether a monitor that catches the obfuscation generated by current training procedures licenses any inference about future systems that can model the monitor itself.
