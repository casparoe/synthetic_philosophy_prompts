ok so i've been chewing on something about biopics and historical novels and i want a real stress-test before i say it out loud. i call it the fidelity ledger. the usual way people talk about whether a historical film is 'inaccurate' is scene by scene: wrong date, invented line, compressed timeline. i think that's the wrong unit. what we owe the real dead, if we owe them anything, is not per-scene accuracy but net truthfulness across the whole work. treat fabrication as a loan taken out against the record. every invented scene or line is a withdrawal, and the work can borrow as much as it likes as long as it deposits something back somewhere else: a later scene built on something the person verifiably did or said that the invention had displaced, a thread that restores the complication the fiction smoothed over. by the final frame the account should be at or near zero.

but some withdrawals are unsecured. you can't borrow against the moral sign of a persons signature act, because nothing later can restore the take-home impression. take Amadeus: it turns Salieri into the envious mediocrity who destroys Mozart. that isn't a loan, it's theft, because the whole valence of Salieri's actual life gets inverted, and no later scene about him being a decent, respected composer nets it out.

what i want from you: first, is this already a thing (it feels too obviously right to be new) and if so what's it called? second, does the ledger do any actual work or is it just 'dramatic license' with an accounting metaphor stapled on? third, break it: give me a case where my bookkeeping says fine but it clearly isn't, and a case where it says not fine but a decent person would defend the film. and the thing i can't shake: we'd never allow this for the living — no newspaper gets to run a false paragraph about you as long as it prints a correction in the same issue. so maybe i've just built a machine for lying to the dead. be charitable but be mean where it matters.
