Ive ended up, mostly from working with these systems rather than from reading philosophy, at a pretty strong geometry-of-meaning view: for a large language model, the meaning of a word is its embedding — its position in a high-dimensional space. Semantic similarity just is cosine similarity, analogy relations are vector offsets, and if some semantic fact about a word can't be recovered from the geometry, then it isn't really part of the meaning. Tell me what would count against this. I want the evidence, formal results (linear-algebra stuff included), and arguments that should actually move me, sorted into what's fatal and what's just awkward. Also say what the view predicts that a non-geometric account of meaning wouldn't, so I can see what it actually risks. And I want an honest answer on whether this view has been padded with enough caveats — it depends on the layer, the right embedding, the right distance metric — that nothing could falsify it any more, and whether that should bother me.
