I volunteer for a crisis listening service, and in training we were taught to aim at empathic concern while refusing empathic distress: the first, we were told, is attunement to another person's pain, and the second is a disorganizing secondhand suffering that helps no one. That distinction has always felt to me like a quiet concession to Nietzsche, who thought pity was not the crown of the moral life but a pathology, one that adds to the total sum of suffering and weakens both the one who feels it and the one who receives it. A friend who works on the ethics of suffering in the natural world recently pressed this into a tidy argument, and I cannot find the exit.

Here it is. (1) On a broadly Schopenhauerian picture, what makes a response compassionate is that I am moved by another's suffering: I participate in it, feel it as suffering myself, and do not merely register that something bad has occurred. (2) If I feel another person's suffering, then the total felt suffering in the world has gone up: the original pain is still there, and my own distress has been added to it. (3) The aim of morality, at least on the suffering-reduction view I half-accept, is to make the total amount of felt suffering smaller, not larger. (4) So the Schopenhauerian form of compassion is partly self-defeating: the very act of sharing someone's pain manufactures more of the thing it exists to end, and it counts as a liability unless it is later cashed out in effective help that removes more pain than it caused. (5) The liability generalizes. Because I can suffer with only the suffering I attend to, I have standing reason to look away: mute the news, do not ask how the treatment actually went, avoid the vivid first-person accounts that would make distant pain real to me. The less I perceive, the less secondhand suffering I add. (6) But a moral psychology that tells me systematically to avoid the sight of pain is monstrous. Since I cannot accept the conclusion, one of the premises must go.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1), the idea that genuine compassion is feeling with rather than merely feeling about, and (6), the refusal to make the good person a strategic ignoramus; those are the two ends holding the wire taut. I cannot tell whether the right move is to abandon the suffering-reduction picture, to re-describe what happens when one person shares another's pain, or to deny that my own compassionate distress is the kind of thing morality should put on the ledger. Name the failure precisely, explain why the step looked like innocent bookkeeping, and price the alternatives. Please keep your answer under 800 words.
