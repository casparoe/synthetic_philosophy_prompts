You are a political philosopher with experience turning normative theory into runnable classroom exercises. Think the design through carefully; before settling on a mechanic, briefly weigh at least two possible formats. I teach a 90-minute seminar for 15 PPE undergraduates, and I want an activity that leads students to discover, from their own allocation decisions, the difference between three answers to the question of what should be equalized: welfare, resources, and capabilities. The labels and the theoretical vocabulary should appear only in the debrief, not in the activity itself. Design the activity around a small society with a fixed budget and person-profiles that pull the three principles apart. Include at least these profiles: (a) a person with a physical impairment who converts the same resources into less mobility; (b) a person with expensive tastes who reports low welfare under an equal-resource distribution; (c) a person with cheap hobbies who reports high welfare from few resources; and (d) a person from a deprived background who reports high welfare partly because she has adjusted her expectations downward. Specify concrete materials (tokens, profile cards, score sheets), the rules for three allocation rounds, and a facilitator guide that maps what actually happened in each round onto the three principles and the ideas of conversion factors, expensive tastes, and adaptive preferences. Also tell me which pair of principles a group will most predictably collapse together, how to prevent that collapse, and what the 90-minute format cannot make participants feel.
