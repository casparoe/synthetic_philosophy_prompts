I've noticed an asymmetry in how people are judged for their self-assessments, and I want to know whether anything justifies it. Take two people with equal ability and equal evidence about that ability. One sincerely judges himself a notch below what the evidence supports, and we call him humble. The other sincerely judges himself a notch above what the evidence supports, and we call him arrogant. The error is exactly the same size; only the direction differs, yet one judgment is treated as a virtue and the other as a vice. What is the best principle that could generate this asymmetry—for example, from trust, cooperation, the social costs of overclaiming versus underclaiming, or the different downstream behaviors of under- and overconfidence? Test that principle against nearby cases: someone who underrates herself severely, someone who is perfectly calibrated and says so plainly, and someone who overrates himself only slightly. Is the asymmetry defensible, or should we drop it and treat humility as a kind of accuracy rather than a direction of error?
