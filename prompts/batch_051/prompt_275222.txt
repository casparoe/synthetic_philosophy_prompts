I'm a backend engineer by training, and I've been building a small language-drilling app in my spare time that targets English past-tense and plural formation. The team keeps circling around the same design argument: should we implement a rule engine that applies -ed (or -s) and stores exceptions in a list, or an example-driven model where every inflected form is retrieved or generated by analogy to stored forms? Every attempt to settle it turns into a disagreement about something more basic, namely what a grammatical rule is — whether it is a real mental mechanism, a summary of observed regularity, or something else. I have no formal philosophy or linguistics background, so I want a document I can circulate that sorts people by their answers to concrete cases rather than by which label they like.

Please write a "which view of grammatical rules do you actually hold?" sorting quiz for the team and, later, for curious users of the app. It should be a diagnostic instrument: each question presents a concrete case — go/went (suppletion), foot/feet and mouse/mice (historically regular sound change that has since become opaque), a novel word in the style of the wug test, a child's goed or foots, and at least one invented miniature case, such as a language whose past tense is formed uniformly by a suffix except for exactly three verbs that show a vowel change — and asks respondents what they would say about it, with answer options that never announce which position they point to. The quiz should sort people into four positions: (1) a dual-route, words-and-rules view on which a productive symbolic rule handles regulars and a separate mental lexicon handles irregulars; (2) a stronger rule realism on which exceptions are themselves entries within the rule system rather than a separate memory system; (3) a pure exemplar or analogy view on which there are no rules at all, only stored examples and similarity, with regular-looking behavior emerging statistically; and (4) an instrumentalism on which a rule is a useful summary of observed regularity and nothing is claimed about mental machinery. Please also flag a fifth position — that grammatical rules are social conventions or norms — and either place it in the tree or explain why it answers a different question than the other four. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: first articulate the diagnostic dimensions (for instance, whether rules exist as mental objects, whether regular and irregular production are one mechanism or two, and how many cases a generalization needs before it counts as rule-like), then locate the positions in the resulting space, and note combinations of answers that are coherent-looking but unoccupied or unattractive.

Structure the response in exactly these sections, in this order: "The questions" (roughly 900 to 1,100 words; eight to ten questions, each a short concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so that no option gives away the position it belongs to); "Scoring key" (roughly 300 to 400 words; how patterns of answers map onto the five positions, including how to handle genuinely mixed response patterns); "The positions and their awkward commitments" (roughly 500 words; one paragraph per view, including what each view says about suppletion and about historically sound-change-derived forms like foot/feet); "What this quiz cannot separate" (roughly 200 words; the pairs or triples of positions the instrument conflates, and why). Use plain language where possible, but the terms suppletion, sound change, and productivity may be used once they are defined.
