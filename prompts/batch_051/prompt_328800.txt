I keep going back and forth on what "absolute rotation" is supposed to mean, because Newton seems to think theres a real distinction between true motion and merely relative motion. In Definition V of the Principia he uses a sling to explain centripetal force: 

A stone, whirled about in a sling, endeavours to recede from the hand that turns it; and by that endeavour, distends the sling, and that with so much the greater force, as it is revolv'd with the greater velocity, and as soon as ever it is let go, flies away.

Then later in the Scholium he has the bucket and the pair of globes. I want a working definition of absolute rotation (or true circular motion, if you prefer) that gets the following cases right. If you can't get all of them, say which one forces a revision and whether the revision is ad hoc.

Cases:

1. A stone being whirled in a sling at constant speed.
2. A bucket of water hanging from a rope, after the bucket has been spinning for a while and the water has climbed up the sides, with water at rest relative to the bucket.
3. A person sitting on a merry-go-round that is turning steadily, eyes closed, feeling pulled outward.
4. A Foucault pendulum swinging at the North Pole: the plane of its swing stays fixed while the stars wheel around it.
5. Two globes connected by a cord, alone in an otherwise empty void, revolving around their common center of gravity so that the cord stays taut.
6. The entire visible universe rotating once a day around a stationary Earth, in the sense of the fixed stars all going around the pole.
7. A stationary bucket of water on the ground, with a flat surface.

The definition should not just be "relative to the lab frame" or "relative to the fixed stars" unless you can defend that against cases 2 and 5. And after you run it through the cases, tell me honestly whether the term "absolute rotation" is doing real work or is just a name for a family of effects.
