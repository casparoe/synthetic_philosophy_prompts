I've been arguing with my cousin, who volunteers at a small historical society, about a dispute in her town. The official planning file from the 1960s makes a neighborhood clearance for a new road look clean: posted notices, compensation offers, a recorded vote. The people who grew up there remember it very differently—intimidation, no real consultation, promises that were never kept. Now the society wants to put up a marker, and the question is which account the marker should treat as the baseline. My cousin keeps falling back on the standard line: when memory and the record conflict, the record should win, because the record was written down at the time and hasn't been rearranged by later needs, where as memory gets edited every time it's retold.

But I can't shake the thought that this argument was built for paper records that were hard to alter quietly. Most of what will count as "the record" for today's controversies is born-digital—posts, emails, databases—written by interested parties trying to shape how the future will remember, often editable with no obvious trace, surfaced by platforms and search engines, and as soaked in present-day passions as anyone's memory. If the record is now just another kind of contested memory, does the standard argument for defering to it survive? I'm less interested in what the marker should actually say than in what happens to the argument: is it dead, does it need repair, or does it only apply to a narrower class of records—and which premise (fixedness, contemporaneity, disinterest?) is doing the real work? If it collapses, what rule should someone in my cousin's position use instead?
