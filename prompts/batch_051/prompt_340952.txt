I recently retired from a career in the natural sciences and have started helping grade short essays for a philosophy discussion group. Would you grade the following answer for me?

Question: Both Johan Huizinga and Josef Pieper are often read as defending an activity — play for Huizinga, leisure for Pieper — as something pursued for its own sake rather than as a means to something else. Compare how each thinker arrives at this position, what each regards as the main modern threat to it, and whether their notions of an end in itself amount to the same thing.

Student answer: The two thinkers begin from a similar diagnosis of modern life. Huizinga describes play as a free activity set apart from ordinary life, governed by its own rules, and undertaken for no material purpose. Because it unfolds inside its own bounded world, it is cut off from the serious business of survival and is therefore, for Huizinga, essentially non-serious; he thinks modern industrial society degrades play by professionalizing and commercializing it. Pieper makes a parallel point about leisure: modern culture treats leisure as intervals of non-work whose real purpose is to restore people for more work, whereas genuine leisure is a receptive stillness in which one is open to reality for its own sake rather than for what can be extracted from it. On this reading, Pieper's leisure is best understood as a species of Huizinga's play, specifically the contemplative play of the mind at rest. The two authors accordingly converge on a shared thesis: an activity is valuable when it is chosen for its own sake, and both locate the main threat to that value in a society that insists on attaching an external goal to everything.
