I run a small rationality meetup (about twelve people, mostly engineers and a few philosophy-curious programmers, 90 minutes) and I've been trying to kill the "I talk, they nod" format. This week I want to do Sen's liberal paradox. The idea, as I understand it: you'd like a group's collective choices to obey three things at once — if everyone prefers X to Y, the group should too (Pareto); each person should be sovereign over at least one pair of options that's their own business (minimal liberalism); and the group's overall ranking should be a coherent ranking rather than a loop. The paradox is that these three pretty mild looking commitments can force a cycle, even with only a couple people and a couple options. The usual toy example is two people and one copy of a book, but I don't want to re-run it because everyone can feel the setup is rigged.

So here's what I need: design an activity for that group, roughly 75 minutes, where participants construct the cycle themselves and only notice where they've ended up at the end. I'd like it to use real small stakes from the actual evening — snack choice, who controls the music, thermostat, whether someone has to read their cringey old blog post aloud — so the preferences are genuinely theirs and not hypothetical. The mechanics should give each person a "personal domain" where there call is final, plus a global preference-aggregation step where Pareto intuitions do the work, and a third step (implicit, ideally) that surfaces the loop. The name "liberal paradox" should not be said until afterwards, and there should be no framing lecture upfront. The takeaway I'm after: people should leave able to explain to a friend why the paradox isn't a bug in any one condition but something structural about having all three at once; if they just remember "Sen proved something weird about liberty," the session failed.

Then write the debrief, minute-by-minute-ish. Map the moments in the game onto the three parts of the paradox — Pareto, individual decisiveness, and the transitivity/acyclicity assumption that nobody notices at first. After the reveal, go through where each condition can be resisted and what it costs to resist it there: dropping Pareto means the group knowingly passes up a unanimous improvement, restricting the personal sphere means you need a stable line between "my call" and "our call" (and that line tends to slide under pressure), and restricting the domain of permissible preferences starts to look like deciding for people what they're allowed to want. Be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: a hand-picked three-option local cycle is not the theorem, so the facilitator has to carry the step to generality without lecturing. Also predict what goes wrong when it runs — people will try to bargain out of the loop, or dispute someone else's stated preference, or propose a fourth option that breaks the setup, or decide the whole thing is a trick — and say how to handle those reactions without deflating the point.
