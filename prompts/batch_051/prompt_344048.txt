I'm an undergraduate teaching assistant attached to a first-year module called "Who Owns Information?", and next week I have to lead a fifty-minute tutorial on what happens to ownership when an owner disappears. I'm not asking for an explanation of the material for myself—I've done the background reading—but for a plan of how I should teach it. The students come in with little more than the intuition that if nobody is maintaining a piece of software, hosting it, or enforcing a copyright on it, then it is effectively up for grabs.

The case I had in mind to carry the tutorial is a volunteer-run server keeping alive a small online game from about twenty years ago: the original developer went bankrupt, and the rights now appear to sit with a holding company that has no reachable representative. I want to know what I have to put in place first. My own guess is that I cannot teach the difference between abandonment and orphaning until I first separate the existence of a right from the practical possibility of enforcing it, but I'm not sure whether that separation should come before or after I introduce what property is for. I also don't know whether this case is the right vehicle, or whether I should use something blunter like an unoccupied house or a long out-of-print book.

So: give me a committed lesson plan. Fix the order in which the pieces must be introduced and explain what depends on what. Pick one carrying example and say what it must have and what it must lack. List the misconceptions the students will likely bring or form—I'm expecting "non-enforcement equals abandonment," "preservation trumps paper rights," and "an orphan work is a work whose author died"—and tell me at which point in the sequence each should be headed off. Give me one closing question or poll I can use to tell whether the distinction has actually landed. And say explicitly what I should refuse to explain at this level and why. Don't hedge or lay out alternatives: make the calls and defend them.
