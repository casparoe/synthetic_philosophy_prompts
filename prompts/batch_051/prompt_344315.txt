I have been trying to settle an old disagreement among my friends about charter cities and seasteading, and I have come to think the dispute is really about what counts as exit. Here are the criteria I use for saying that a person has opted out of a state, as opposed to merely moving into a subordinate jurisdiction that the same state tolerates. First, the rules governing the person's ordinary activities—contract, property, labor, and civil wrongs—are made and enforced by institutions that are not agencies of the state in question. Second, the state in question does not, in the normal course, impose its taxes, permits, police power, or compulsory courts on the person. Third, the arrangement is not revocable by that state through its ordinary legislative process; at most it can be unwound for breach of treaty-like terms. Fourth, the person's standing does not secretly rest on services the state is still supplying—defense, title, currency, or the final guarantee that makes the private institutions reliable.

Now apply these criteria to the following case. The Helios Charter District sits on a 99-year lease from a coastal host state under a charter statute. The district writes its own civil and commercial code, operates its own courts of first instance and appeal with no merits review outside the district, funds itself through a residence fee that replaces all host-state taxes, and maintains its own police force. The host state retains criminal justice, border control, and national defense; a constitutional court of the host state may invalidate district legislation that conflicts with the national constitution; and the charter, despite its 99-year term, was enacted as ordinary legislation and can in principle be amended or repealed by a later parliament. Alphonse has renounced his host-state citizenship, owns nothing outside the district, has signed the district's residence contract, and has had no contact with host-state agencies except a yearly immigration stamp at the district boundary.

Using my criteria, has Alphonse exited the host state? Take the criteria in order and say where the answer is genuinely indeterminate. I am particularly interested in whether the host state's retained criminal, defense, and constitutional powers are the real obstacle to exit, and whether a demand for full legal finality overstates what exit should require—perhaps making it true by definition that no one can ever leave a state while a system of states remains.
