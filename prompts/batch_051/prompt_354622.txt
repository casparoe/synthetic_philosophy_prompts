I work at a small startup that runs a site where tenants rate landlords, and we also publish a "rent outlook" for each building — a model's guess about whether rent goes up or down over the next twelve months. A landlord has threatened to sue over a one-star rating, and the default view around the office is that we're safe because a rating is "just an opinion" and a forecast is "just a prediction." I started trying to actually understand the opinion/fact distiction in defamation law, and now I think that's too quick. I wrote up a short essay for the team. I'd like comments on it, but not a reading list or case citations — I want the reasoning, not homework. Please comment specifically on: (a) which parts of the argument actually hold up; (b) where I'm mischaracterizing how the law's fact/opinion line works; (c) what the strongest objection to the main claim is, and whether the essay already answers it; and (d) whether the type-system analogy at the end earns its keep or is just a cute engineering metaphor.

Here's the essay:

When a landlord calls a three-star rating defamatory, the reflex is to say it's just an opinion and opinions can't be defamatory. I think that reflex bundles two different things. A single star rating is an evaluation, sure — it expresses a stance. But when we average hundreds of ratings and publish the number as data, the site is now making a claim about how the landlord actually is. The number can be wrong in a way that has nothing to do with taste: the reviews could be fake, or the sample could be garbage. That wrongness is about the world, not about the rater's stance, so it starts to look like the kind of statement that can be proven true or false — which is roughly where I understand the line to be.

Forecasts are weirder. A prediction about the future feels even more opinion-like, because the future hasn't happened yet. But our rent outlook also makes a pile of verifiable claims about the present: what data went in, what the model weighted, that it wasn't trained on stale listings. A forecast can be false in the way a shoddy calculation is false, even though the future is open. So I want to say both ratings and forecasts can carry a factual payload, and what matters is not whether the thing is a number or a sentence, but whether a reasonable reader would take it to assert something checkable.

Where I end up is a kind of type system for statements. Reports assert a stable, checkable state of affairs ("the heat's been broken since March"). Evaluations express a stance that evidence alone won't settle ("this building is overpriced"). Predictions are about the future but carry embedded factual claims about the present. The defamation risk, I think, is mostly type confusion: a prediction packages itself as a report, a single person's opinion packages itself as an average rating, a report gets labeled "just one person's view." And the engineering habit of treating structured data as truth and free text as opinion is exactly the wrong map.
