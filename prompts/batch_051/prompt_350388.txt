I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about pain asymbolia, and I came away defending a position I would like you to stress-test. My view is that the badness of pain is intrinsic to its felt character: to be in pain just is, at least in part, to be in a state that feels bad, and the unpleasantness is not a separable attitude, representation, or evaluative gloss that could come apart from the sensation while leaving the pain itself intact. My friend kept pressing the asymbolia cases — patients who say they still feel the pain and can rate its intensity and location, but who report that it does not bother them and who do not withdraw from it — as evidence that the badness and the painfulness can dissociate. I found myself wanting to say that these patients are not really feeling the same pain minus its badness, but I could not make that stick.

Tell me what would count against my view, and give me a map of its exposure rather than a polemic. In particular: (1) How strong is the asymbolia evidence, and what exactly would the best version of the case against me establish — that pain sensation and pain affect are two components, that the badness is a representation of bodily damage, or merely that the felt badness can be selectively abolished in pathology? (2) What other phenomena would count against me: morphine patients who say the pain is still there but no longer unpleasant, hypnotic analgesia, dissociations between pain intensity and pain unpleasantness, or the apparent neuroanatomical separation of sensory and affective pain pathways? For each, I want to know whether it is decisive or merely awkward, and what a defender of my view can honestly say in response. (3) What would a defender of my position themselves concede counts against it? In particular, is there a version of the view that has been qualified into unfalsifiability by defining real pain as pain-with-badness and dismissing asymbolic pain as something else, and is that a problem for a view of this kind or the correct way to mark a genuine distinction? (4) Which alternative theories of pain affect are actually supported by the dissociation data — evaluativism (unpleasantness as a representation of bodily damage as bad), imperativism (unpleasantness as an imperative to protect or withdraw), or attitudinal views (unpleasantness as a felt attitude of dislike toward the sensation) — and do they preserve anything worth saving from my view, or do they abandon the claim that pain is intrinsically bad?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which of them should actually move me. Be willing to conclude that the view is well insulated if that is the honest answer — but if it is, tell me whether that insulation is a sign of strength or of having stopped making a substantive claim.
