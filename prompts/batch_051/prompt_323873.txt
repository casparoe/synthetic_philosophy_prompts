So we read the Epicurean argument that death can't be bad for the person who dies. The reasoning is that you and death never overlap: while your alive, death isn't there, and once death is there, you're gone, so there's no time when it could actually hurt you. At first that sounded airtight to me. But then I realized the argument assumes being alive and being dead is like a light switch, a single moment where you flip from one to the other. In real medicine it doesn't work like that: there's brain death where your body keeps going on a ventilator, there's people who get revived after being clinically dead for a few minutes, and dying is usually a process that takes days. So the argument seems to lean on a clean line between alive and dead that might not even exist. I want to know whether the Epicurean argument survives that, or if the blurriness breaks it, and which exact premise gets messed up. Is it the premise that once your dead you don't exist, or the premise that there's no time when death and you coexist, or something else? And is there a repaired version that still proves something weaker, or do we have to give up the whole argument?
