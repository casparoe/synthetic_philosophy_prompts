Im a novelist, not an economist, so forgive me if I've garbled this. I'm writing a novel about a gambler who keeps taking bets that are 'good' mathematically and keeps getting ruined anyway, which is how I fell into the ergodicity economics rabbit hole. The argument I keep finding is weirdly frozen. The expected-utility side says a rational agent maximizes expected value (or expected utility) and that's the end of it. The ergodicity side says expected value is only the right object across an ensemble of parallel copies, and a single agent living through time should maximize the time-average growth rate instead — otherwise you get strategies where the ensemble average climbs forever while almost every individual path goes broke, like staking a fixed chunk of your bankroll over and over on a favorable coin toss. The arithmetic part (Jensen's inequality, arithmetic vs geometric mean, non-ergodicity) doesn't seem to be what's contested. The expected-utility people keep saying the time-average rule is just log utility wearing a different hat, and the ergodicity people keep saying no, it's a claim about dynamics and growth, not a taste for log wealth — and then both sides repeat themselves for another year. My working hunch is that they're answering different questions, one about a distribution across people and one about a path through time, but I dont trust an outsiders diagnosis. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a substantive normative dispute about which average a single indivisible life should care about, a verbal dispute where 'rational' means two different things, a disagreement about what economics is for (describing choices or advising them), or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move it — a theorem, a sharper question, a different standard of evidence — or is there a reason to think the stalemate should be permanent?
