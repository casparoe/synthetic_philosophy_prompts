I'm putting together material for a seminar on the philosophy of technology, and the session I'm working on concerns a family of views one might call teleological accounts of AI succession: the thought that human dominance is not a terminal state but a stage in a directed process whose next stage is machine intelligence. I've been rereading Samuel Butler's "Darwin among the Machines" (1863), usually credited as the first serious statement of this idea, and I keep returning to a passage that locates the succession in the order of "kingdoms" rather than in problem-solving ability:

We have used the words "mechanical life," "the mechanical kingdom," "the mechanical world" and so forth, and we have done so advisedly, for as the vegetable kingdom was slowly developed from the mineral, and as in like manner the animal supervened upon the vegetable, so now in these last few ages an entirely new kingdom has sprung up, of which we as yet have only seen what will one day be considered the antediluvian prototypes of the race.

I've already compiled the standard teleological narratives that keep coming up, and I want you not to give me any of them again. They are: (1) the organic-succession view itself, on which machines are the next kingdom in a chain of increasingly complex forms; (2) the self-improvement cascade view, on which once a system can improve its own design, a runaway process of recursive self-improvement makes supersession follow from the internal dynamics of intelligence rather than from any larger historical pattern; (3) the evolutionary-handover view, on which biological species are transient vehicles and humans will simply be superseded by our own artificial descendants once they outcompete us; and (4) the cosmological-acceleration view, on which AI is the latest phase in a universe tending toward greater complexity, integration, and processing of information, with biological humanity as an early slow phase.

Act as an expert in the philosophy of technology and the history of ideas. Give me one additional teleological account of machine/AI succession that is not on this list and is not a rewording, special case, or combination of the four I already have. Before settling on your answer, briefly brainstorm two or three candidate ideas and say why you reject them or choose one; then present the new account precisely, explain what it takes to be the source or mechanism of the directedness, and finally say whether Butler's quoted passage already contains a seed of this account, is neutral toward it, or would have pushed against it.
