Last autumn my country spent five weeks in a genuine constitutional interregnum: the written constitution contained no procedure for the situation our institutions were in, and four different bodies each claimed, with plausible legal arguments, to be the lawful authority. I came out of that crisis less interested in who was right than in the structure of the problem, and I now teach an upper-level seminar called Authority After the Rules, with fourteen politics and philosophy majors who have already had an introductory course in political thought. In discussion they can say the correct things in the abstract — that validity cannot be grounded in the rules themselves, that a legal system ultimately rests on acceptance, that de facto power is not the same as legitimate authority — but I can tell these remain phrases rather than convictions. I want to run a simulation before we do the reading, so that the distinctions register later as names for something they have already experienced.

Please design a seated role-play for a 70-minute session with those fourteen students. The scenario is a small fictional polity whose written constitution is silent about the exact situation it faces: for example, two bodies each claim the constitutional power to appoint an interim executive, a third body is the only one empowered to review such appointments but cannot lawfully be constituted, and the armed forces announce they will obey whoever can demonstrate that they are legitimate. The facilitator must not arbitrate; any resolution has to emerge from the students' own choices about whom to treat as authoritative. The rules and briefing sheets should be arranged so that no single actor has the legal power, the numbers, and the acceptance needed to impose a solution unilaterally. The force of the idea should surface in what happens rather than in any lecture afterwards: participants should run into the fact that the text cannot settle the dispute, that someone must eventually act without prior authorization, and that authority in the gap consists in being treated as binding by enough of the relevant others. The debrief should then map specific moments in the simulation onto concepts the class will meet later: the difference between legal validity and de facto obedience, the thought that the ultimate test of valid law is a shared social practice rather than another written rule, and the thought that a crisis reveals where sovereignty actually sits.

I would like the full design: a one-page constitution for the fictional polity with the gap deliberately built into it, exact roles with one-paragraph briefing sheets, the physical layout, an order of moves with a timeline, and the trigger that tells the facilitator when to stop. Then provide a debrief script with questions in the order they should be asked. Also include an honest note on what a classroom role-play cannot reproduce (I suspect it has to do with real stakes, fear, and time pressure), and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — for example, students asking the facilitator to settle the rules, or the group drifting into ordinary policy bargaining instead of a struggle over authority — and how to correct each.
