I'm one of the maintainers of a medium-sized open-source project (a database driver that a bunch of other projects depend on), and we keep going in circles about breaking changes. One of the newer maintainers, who reads a lot of economics blogs, proposed a rule he calls safe Pareto improvements. Under his rule, any change to our public API has to be a Pareto improvement in the following sense: at least one downstream project has to be made better off, and no downstream project is allowed to be left worse off. Concretely, the change proposal goes out with a compatability impact report. Any downstream maintainer whose code would break can file a public harm claim stating how many hours it would take them to migrate. If a claim is filed, the change can still ship, but only if the proposer puts that many hours of funding (at a fixed hourly rate we all agree on) into an escrow account. The harmed project can draw from that escrow only by submitting actual migration commits, and after twelve months whatever they haven't drawn goes back to the proposer. A maintainer who files three claims that end up more than half unused is barred from filing harm claims for a year. If no one files a claim, the change just needs a simple majority of maintainers.

I honestly can't tell if this is a clever way to make breaking changes pay for themselves, or a machine for gridlock, manufactured outrage, and free money for whoever complains loudest. I'm not asking whether it's morally a good idea; I want to know how it would actually behave. Who would it incentivize to do what: downstream users, proposers, the other maintainers voting, and anyone who could file fake or padded harm claims? Where does it get gamed first, and what does it do to our appetite for ever cleaning up a messy API? Are the failure modes patchable, or does fixing them just push the gaming somewhere else?
