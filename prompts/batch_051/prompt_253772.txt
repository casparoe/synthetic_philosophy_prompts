Suppose a collapsed regime's internal-reporting statute contains this clause: "No action may be maintained against any person in respect of a communication made to an officer of the State under this Act." The drafters intended the clause to protect citizens who supplied information to the security apparatus, and everyone understood that the apparatus would use the information as it saw fit. The apparatus is now abolished, and a neutral successor court must decide whether the clause bars an action by a woman whose neighbour reported her under the Act. The report was formally accurate but motivated by private spite, and it led to her being imprisoned for a year without any hearing. Treat the case as one the drafters never foresaw: a fair court reviewing their work after the institution that gave the clause its point has disappeared. Work through what the plain words, the original purpose, what the drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader in the current state would take the clause to mean each yield. Then press the legal-positivism/natural-law fork: must the court first decide whether this clause is law at all, or can it reach a defensible result through interpretation and the choice of remedy alone? Say exactly where the two camps part, and what each camp thinks the other is dodging.
