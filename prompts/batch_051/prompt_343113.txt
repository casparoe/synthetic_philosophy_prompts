I'm a grad student in philosophy of language and I keep getting pulled into the slurs argument at conferences, and I'm struck by how stuck its gotten. One camp treats a slur as semantically different from its neutral counterpart: the derogatory evaluation is part of the word's literal or conventionally implicated content, which is why even quoted or embedded uses ("if he were a ___, would you date him?") still sting, and why a speaker who says "I used the word but meant nothing by it" sounds like they're making an excuse rather than reporting a fact. The other camp says the word's meaning is basically exhausted by its neutral counterpart, and the harm lives in pragmatics—taboo, tone, social meaning, the very act of choosing it. On that view you'd expect offensiveness to be more context-sensitive and more cancellable than the semantic camp allows, and they point to reclamation, in-group use, and metalinguistic discussion as evidence.

My working theory is that the stalemate is mostly not about missing facts. I suspect the two sides are silently sorting the data differently: each treats the other's best cases as peripheral, and they want their theories to do different jobs—one wants lexical entries and truth conditions, the other wants an account of how linguistic actions build or express contempt. But I can't tell whether that diagnosis is right, or whether there's a genuine empirical or formal disagreement hiding underneath (about acquisition, processing, or how sharply semantics can be separated from pragmatics at all). So: what kind of disagreement is this really? And what, if anything, could actually end it—an experiment, a formal result, a sharper distinction, or a change of question?
