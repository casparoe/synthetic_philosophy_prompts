I am a novelist working on a book set around a bitterly fought statewide ballot initiative, and I want to get the political theory right. One of my characters, a state senator trained in constitutional law, defends representative government against the initiative process with an argument he draws from James Madison's Federalist No. 63, published March 1, 1788, under the name Publius. Here is the passage he keeps returning to:

"As the cool and deliberate sense of the community ought, in all governments, and actually will, in all free governments, ultimately prevail over the views of its rulers; so there are particular moments in public affairs when the people, stimulated by some irregular passion, or some illicit advantage, or misled by the artful misrepresentations of interested men, may call for measures which they themselves will afterwards be the most ready to lament and condemn. In these critical moments, how salutary will be the interference of some temperate and respectable body of citizens, in order to check the misguided career, and to suspend the blow meditated by the people against themselves, until reason, justice, and truth can regain their authority over the public mind?"

I want to know whether my character's argument from this passage actually holds up, or whether I should have a second character dismantle it. Here is the argument as I imagine him making it, with each step spelled out:

(1) In any free polity there is a real distinction between the public's transient will, what it favors under excitement, fear, or manipulation, and its durable, cool, deliberate will, what it favors once the disturbance has passed.
(2) Direct-democratic devices such as the initiative and the referendum give the public's transient will the force of law immediately, or nearly so; the proposal becomes binding without any distinct institutional moment at which a cooler body can halt or delay it.
(3) A representative legislature, especially one with a longer-tenured second chamber, supplies precisely that institutional moment: it can suspend the blow meditated by the people against themselves until the transient passion subsides into reason, justice, and truth.
(4) A defensible theory of popular government should privilege the durable will of the public over its transient will, since the durable will is what the people themselves would ratify once they were thinking clearly.
(5) Therefore, wherever the public can be inflamed, direct-democratic mechanisms are inferior to representative chambers, and the burden of proof lies on those who would introduce or use them.

Please act as a specialist in democratic theory and constitutional design. Begin by brainstorming the strongest reading of each step and the places a smart opponent would attack, then settle on a considered verdict. Is the argument valid? If it is, say so and defend the conclusion against the strongest objections. If it is not, identify exactly which step or steps fail, explain what kind of failure each is—a false premise, an equivocation, a suppressed empirical claim, an illicit leap—and give the strongest repaired version of the argument that does go through, if any does. I am less interested in the familiar referendum-good-versus-referendum-bad debate than in whether this specific argument is sound. Finally, tell me which argument or consideration would be most likely to change your mind about your verdict, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
