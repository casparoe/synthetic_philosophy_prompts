I keep hearing the cluelessness objection to consequentialism: to judge whether an action is right you have to trace its consequences as far as they ripple out, and for any ordinary action the ripples run further than we can possibly know, so the comparison consequentialism demands can never actually be made.

Now suppose that problem gets fixed, on paper. A well-resourced group builds what they call a 'consequence atlas': for any action in a given context, it outputs a probability distribution over the total well-being produced by that action over a 500-year horizon, with model uncertainty included. It's not a predictor of what actually happens; it's a calibrated distribution, and the group has a decent track record of calibration over shorter horizons. Assume it does what it says on the box.

Does the cluelessness objection survive this? I'm not asking whether we'll ever build the atlas or whether the numbers would be trustworthy in practice; assume they are, and tell me what happens to the argument. The two obvious answers are that the objection was only ever about lacking such a distribution, in which case it collapses, or that it was never really about that, in which case it just relocates to some other premise — the 500-year cutoff, the definition of total well-being across future people, the fact that a very wide distribution still leaves you unable to choose, or something else. I want the premise-level version: which step of the old argument fails or survives, whether there's a repaired argument that still bites, and what a consequentialist is rationally allowed to believe after the repair. Please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
