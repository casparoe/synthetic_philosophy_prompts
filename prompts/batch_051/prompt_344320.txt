I want you to stress-test a position I have come to hold about the torture-versus-dust-specks problem. The dilemma, as I will use it, asks whether it is worse for one person to undergo severe, prolonged suffering or for an astronomically large but finite number of people each to undergo a barely noticeable, momentary irritation. I now hold that the dust-specks option is the worse one, and for a more specific reason than ordinary additive utilitarianism. My view is that the disvalue of harm is a single quantity measured on a ratio scale with a true zero: a dust speck has strictly positive disvalue, torture has a large but finite disvalue, and for any two positive quantities on such a scale some finite multiple of the smaller exceeds the larger. On this view there is no non-arbitrary cut-off below which harms stop counting, and no lexical threshold that places severe harms beyond comparison with minor ones. The stubborn intuition that torture is worse is real, but I take it to be a predictable artefact of scope insensitivity and of our inability to represent very large numbers, rather than evidence about the structure of disvalue.

I would like you to tell me what would count against this position, treating it as a substantive, falsifiable claim rather than a terminological preference. In other words, what would have to be true about the structure of disvalue, about our elicited judgements, or about decision theory for my view to be false? Which arguments, empirical findings, formal results, or variants of the dilemma should trouble me most, and which are merely awkward? What does my view predict that its rivals do not—for instance about how elicited judgements should change as the numbers are made concrete, about the mirror-image case for benefits, or about decomposing a single severe harm into many brief moments—and would the failure of those predictions really matter? What would a defender of this Archimedean, additive view have to concede counts against it, as opposed to what critics merely assert? Are there commitments of the view that could turn out unacceptable, for example concerning sub-threshold harms, the individuation of harms, the continuity of welfare, or the results of debiasing? Is there a less arbitrary version of the rival lexical-threshold view, grounded perhaps in psychophysics or in the structure of phenomenal experience, that I am wrong to dismiss? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which responses remain live, and finish with an honest assessment of whether my position currently risks anything, or whether it has been insulated by the way I have formulated it—and, if it has, whether that insulation is itself a problem.

I would be most moved by decision-theoretic or measurement-theoretic results and by debiased elicited choices, not by further intuition-pumping about the original case. Please write in British English.
