I'm a novelist, drafting a scene in which a cautious character and a reformer argue over a long-standing local rule whose original rationale has lapsed. I want the cautious character to defend the rule with something like Chesterton's fence: the reformer's inability to state the rule's purpose is not enough to justify removing it, because old arrangements often encode knowledge their inheritors have forgotten. For the novel I am changing one structural feature of the standard thought experiment. The reformer proposes a reversible trial suspension rather than permanent abolition: the rule is stood down for a fixed period, the effects are monitored against agreed indicators, and the old rule is restored automatically unless the suspension is renewed. Mistaken removal is therefore cheaply corrected, which the original case seems to assume it is not. I would like the philosophical analysis rather than scene prose. Does Chesterton-style reasoning survive once reversibility is added? In particular, which of the considerations behind the original case — the epistemic claim that a reformer's ignorance is weak evidence of uselessness, and the precautionary claim about asymmetric, irreversible costs — are weakened or eliminated by reversibility, and which remain in force? Under what conditions, if any, would the cautious character still be entitled to block even a reversible trial?
