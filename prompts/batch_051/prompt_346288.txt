I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas beat, and I've been chipping away at a feature about how much of the machinery we treat as universal in language is actually load-bearing and how much is just the way familiar languages happen to do things. After a bunch of conversations with semanticists I've landed on a working view I'd like you to stress-test for me. The view: no usable human language could lack indexicals outright. By indexicals I mean the whole deictic apparatus — first- and second-person pronouns, demonstratives like "this" and "that," tense, and the way "here" and "now" anchor to a context of utterance. The claim is not that every language needs free-standing pronouns; person can be marked on verbs, and a sign language can point. The claim is that a language with zero context-anchoring resources couldnt be lived in by speakers like us. If you replaced "I" with each speaker's name, "now" with clock times, and "here" with spatial coordinates, something indispensible about situated talk goes missing. The thought that sold me is the shopping-cart case: a person can know in a purely descriptive way that the shopper with the torn sugar sack is making a mess and still fail to register, I am making a mess. That missing step feels like exactly the work indexicals do.

But I've been reporting long enough to know that a "feels like" is exactly what I should mistrust. So write me the honest map of what would count against this view. I want to know: which actual or attested patterns — pro-drop languages, person-agreement on verbs, languages that get by without third-person pronouns and lean on demonstratives instead — come close enough to indexical-free to embarrass the claim? Which constructed languages or paraphrase schemes, of the sort philosophers reached for when they tried to scrub context-sensitivity out of scientific talk, would have to work for the view to collapse? What would a formal semantics have to show — for instance, that the descriptive paraphrase is truth-conditionally adequate and that the leftover "I am making a mess" effect is a fact about cognition rather than about what the language can say — before a defender of my view would have to concede? And which of these would a committed defender concede as genuinely counting against them, rather than wave off as resting on a sloppy definition of "indexical" or on confusing sentence meaning with what a speaker does with a sentence. One more thing: I'd personally find it most persuasive against my own view if someone showed a full, incrementally-expressible language-game in which everybody got by with names plus stable descriptions, and the only thing missing was a certain first-person feel. Tell me if that thought is as compelling as I think it is.

Use these sections, in this order. "Sharpening the view" (about 150 words): restate the claim precisely and flag the scoping choices that determine what counts as a counterexample. "Empirical threats" (about 250 words): the real linguistic patterns that come closest to indexical-free and how much damage each one does. "The paraphrase project" (about 300 words): the strongest attempt to build an indexical-free language — eternalizing sentences, self-naming conventions, whatever you take to be the best version — and what it would have to deliver. "Where the essential-indexical argument could break" (about 250 words): the points at which a critic should push on the shopping-cart line, and which replies a defender can give up without losing the view. "Ranking the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward" (about 250 words): a table with each defeater, what it would show, and how much it would actually hurt. "Bottom line" (about 150 words): how exposed the view is, and whether its defenders have insulated it to the point where nothing could count against it.
