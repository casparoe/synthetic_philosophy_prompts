Let's use the Varsity Blues admissions scandal as a case study. You know the rough facts: in 2019 it came out that a bunch of wealthy parents had paid a fixer to bribe coaches and fake standardized-test scores so their kids would get into selective colleges. I want to analyze this through the distinction between merit and desert. Roughly, merit is what the rules of an existing competition say you've earned, while desert is a pre-institutional moral claim about what someone deserves. The usual critique of meritocracy is that it blurs the two, so the system can tell its winners they earned their place by "having merit" even when that merit was bought, inherited, or gamed through perfectly legal paths. The scandal looks like an easy confirmation of that critique, but I suspect it's more awkward than it looks.

So do this in both directions. First, run the theory over the case: set out who the players are, what the merit/desert distinction says each of them did wrong, and what it explains about why the scandal produced the particular outrage it did. Then turn around and stress-test the distinction: compare the illegal side door (bribery and fake scores) with the legal side doors (legacy admissions, donor preferences, recruited-athlete slots, expensive test prep and admissions consulting). If the distinction can't draw a principled line between the illegal and legal side doors, what exactly is it tracking? And what should we conclude about whether "meritocracy" is a legitimating ideology rather than a real distribution by desert? Finally, tell me whether the episode is best read as a confirmation of the critique, an anomaly for it, or a case the framework never anticipated—and which part of the framework, if any, survives.
