I’ve been slowly coming around to the Sidgwickian line in *The Methods of Economics*—sorry, *The Methods of Ethics*—where he argues that mere priority or posteriority in time isn’t itself a reasonable ground for caring less about a good. He’s not saying we shouldn’t account for uncertainty, opportunity costs, or the possibility that later goods might not arrive; he’s saying the bare fact of laterness isn’t doing any independent normative work. I’ve started applying this to how I think about climate policy, retirement savings, and my own procrastination, and I now lean toward the view that pure time preference—discounting well-being only because it’s temporally distant—is a form of temporal myopia, not a principled stance. My position, as precisely as I can put it: once you’ve corrected for risk, opportunity cost, and changes in how connected a future self or future person is to my present self, there is no rational or moral justification left for a positive pure rate of time preference.

What would count against this view? I’m less interested in the standard replies than in a serious map of where the position is exposed. Sort the possible defeaters from genuinely fatal to merely awkward, and include empirical findings (e.g. about hyperbolic discounting, intertemporal cooperation, evolution, or what actually helps agents improve their long-run decisions), formal arguments, and philosophical cases. Which of these would a committed Sidgwickian have to concede as counting against them rather than dismiss as changing the subject? And has the view been insulated into unfalsifiability—by me or by its defenders—in a way that makes it immune to any evidence? If so, is that a problem or a feature for a view of this kind?
