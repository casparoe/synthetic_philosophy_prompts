I'm a graduate student in philosophy of biology, and there is a circularity worry I keep coming back to. The claim that natural selection favors the fitter is meant to explain why some traits spread and others disappear, but when fitness is cashed out as actual survival and reproductive output, the explanation seems to shrink toward the empty: the fitter organisms just are the ones that in fact survive and leave more descendants. The standard reply I have encountered is that fitness should instead be understood as a probabilistic propensity—a disposition to survive and reproduce in a specified environment—which can come apart from actual outcomes, since a genuinely fit organism can have bad luck. That reply has never fully satisfied me, because it does not say how the propensity is supposed to be measured except through the very outcomes it is meant to explain.

I want to think through a concrete proposal for dealing with this at the level of editorial practice. Suppose a major evolutionary-biology journal adopts the following policy. A paper may assert a fitness difference or a claim of selection only if it includes, before data collection, a pre-registered operational definition of fitness that does not use realized survival or reproductive output as its measure. Authors must specify in advance: the trait or traits hypothesized to be under selection; the relevant environment or environments; which components of fitness are being measured (viability, mating success, fecundity, gamete output, or some combination); the time horizon over which fitness is assessed; and at least one pattern in the data that would count as evidence against the adaptive claim. Reviewers are instructed to treat as a serious flaw any post hoc change of environment, trait, fitness component, or time horizon, and to reject papers that define fitness in a way guaranteed to correlate with the trait under study.

Analyze this as a mechanism, not as an abstract philosophy-of-science position. What would it actually incentivize among graduate students, lab heads, and field biologists? Where would it be gamed first, and which forms of gaming would be scientifically harmless, which would be damaging, and which would simply relocate the original circularity rather than break it? Would the policy, on balance, make published claims about selection more trustworthy, or would it mainly select for tractable organisms, short time horizons, and tidy questions? Where do you expect the policy to fail first, and is there a modification that retains the pressure toward independent measurement without those distortions?
