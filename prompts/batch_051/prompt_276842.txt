I'm taking a class on the ethics of consent, and I've been stuck on something from my university's sexual misconduct policy. The relevant part says: "Consent must be clear, voluntary, and ongoing. A person cannot give consent if they are asleep, unconscious, or incapacitated by alcohol or drugs." That is the whole definition; it doesn't mention deception anywhere.

A friend of mine started seeing a guy who told her, unprompted, that he'd had a vasectomy. She has a medical condition that makes hormonal birth control risky, so she agreed to have sex without a condom partly because she trusted him. She is now pregnant, and he has admitted he lied about the vasectomy. She says her consent does not count becuase it was built on a lie; he says she consented to the sex itself, and the lie just makes him a jerk, not someone who violated her consent. The people who drafted the code were clearly picturing a passed-out freshman or someone being physically forced, not a lie told days earlier.

Does her consent count as "voluntary" under the text? Does "clear" do any work here? If I read the clause to catch his lie, can I stop it from also catching lies about whether someone is single, how much money they make, or whether they are in love — and if I can't, does that mean the code should leave deception out of consent entirely, or that the line has to be drawn somewhere else? I care less about what the university would actually do with her complaint than about what the plain words, the purpose of the rule, what the drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable student reading the handbook would expect each imply, and which should win. Oh, and please answer in American English.
