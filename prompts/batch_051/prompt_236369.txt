Okay I've been turning over an idea I want someone to actually kick the tires on. I call it stance hysteresis. Here's the claim. When you first start interpreting some AI system in belief-desire terms — saying the chess engine "wants to control the center" or the chatbot "is trying to win the argument" — that first attribution has to earn its keep: you're making a jump from "it's next-token prediction" to "it has an intentional profile." But once you've made that jump, every later belief or desire you attribute is nearly free, because it just slots into the frame you already built. So the evidence threshold for the first intentional attribution is (maybe correctly) high, but the threshold for the tenth is basically whatever sounds coherent. I think this means the intentional stance has a built-in status quo bias: the cost of building an interpretive frame makes you stick with it after the evidence that got you in is gone. My proposal: a rational attribution policy would be to either never make the first attribution, or to make it and then treat all the later ones as fiction-for-prediction rather than as discoveries about the system. You can't coherently say the first one was earned and the later ones are too.

Tell me the three or four strongest objections to this. Is there anything actually wrong with it, or is it just Dennett's intentional stance plus a sunken-cost fallacy on the interpreter's side? Does it make a real prediction that could fail? And am I smuggling in a picture where the first attribution is a different kind of act than the later ones, when its all judgement calls the whole way down?
