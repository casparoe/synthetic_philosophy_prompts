I used to run a small lab, and these days I mostly read moral philosophy, so forgive me if I'm using terms a bit loosely. I keep turning over the difference between two readings of a single value: frugality. On the deontological reading, I have a duty to be frugal—not to let usable resources go to waste, right here, by my own hand; it's something I owe directly, not just an input into the global ledger. On the consequentialist reading, what matters is that there be less waste all told, and my own frugality is only an instrument for that.

Here's the spec. Build me a thought experiment, in an ordinary-life setting (kitchen, small household, community garden, whatever), where a consistent deontologist of the first sort ends up doing something that is, taken across the relevant period, more wasteful—more resources used and eventually more discarded—precisely because they refuse to do some local act of wasting. And an act consequentialist of the second sort, facing the same setup, does the local act of wasting (throwing out the leftovers, say) and turns out to be the one who is, on the whole, genuinely frugal. The inversion has to come from the structure of the two readings themselves, not from a bolt-on duty like a promise or a professional role. The deontologist should look principled, not silly—if the best version of their view would say your case depends on a dumb maxim, rebuild the case.

Then do the check: state the act or pair of acts, walk through what each theory says and why, name the exact feature that flips the usual association (I suspect its the aggregation, but I want it pinned down), say what happens when you remove that feature, and give the strongest objection a deontologist would raise—my guess is they'll say you've rigged it by tacitly assuming the consequentialist's accounting from the start. Tell me whether that objection lands.
