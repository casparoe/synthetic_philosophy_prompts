I am drawn to the view that scientific progress needs a notion of verisimilitude, or truthlikeness. It feels almost forced: our mature theories are rarely true in the strict sense, yet we still want to say Newtonian mechanics is closer to the truth than Aristotelian physics, and that abandoning phlogiston was progress toward the truth rather than a sideways move between two falsehoods. Without some notion of verisimilitude I do not see how the claim that science aims at truth survives. But I also have a firm intuition that falsity is binary—a false claim simply fails to match the world—and that grading false theories by their distance from the truth looks like projecting our own standards of similarity onto reality rather than discovering a fact about the theories. I know Popper's formal definition ran into Tichý–Miller-style counterexamples, but I do not want to lean on a technical result, because the deeper issue looks conceptual. Walk me through what it costs to keep the intuition and give up verisimilitude, what it costs to keep verisimilitude and debunk or quarantine the intuition, and whether there is a third reading—such as truthlikeness relative to a contrast class, a set of questions, or a theoretical language—on which both can be retained. Say what would justify each possible verdict rather than simply picking a side. Please write the response in American English.
