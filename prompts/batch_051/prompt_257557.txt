I've been going down a rabbithole on the Born-rule problem in many-worlds: why should squared amplitudes guide an observer's expectations when the wavefunction never collapses and every outcome is real. The strategies I can separate out so far are: (1) Everett's measure/frequency story—squared amplitude is the unique additive measure on branches, and in the long-run limit typical branches have frequencies matching it; (2) the Deutsch-Wallace decision-theoretic derivation, where a rational agent in quantum betting games is forced to use squared amplitudes; (3) Zurek's envariance, where swap symmetries between system and environment force equal coefficients to get equal probability, and fine-graining extends it to unequal coefficients; (4) the self-locating uncertainty approach, where before branching you're unsure which post-branching copy you'll become, and a separability principle—your credence in being a given observer on a branch shouldn't depend on what's happening on other branches—is supposed to deliver the squared weights. I'm not counting 'postulate the Born rule as a brute axiom' (that's giving up) or 'probability talk is just false in MWI' (that's changing the subject), and Gleason-type uniqueness results feel like the math behind (1) rather than a separate strategy. Give me a fifth strategy that's genuinely different from all four—not a rewording, special case, or combination—and say what it does that the others don't. If there isn't one, say why these four actually exhaust the space.
