I retired from a career in physical chemistry a few years ago and now audit a moral philosophy seminar for pleasure. Last week the group discussed prioritarianism and the leveling-down objection, and two regulars—Alan, a former statistician, and Bea, a retired civil engineer—stayed afterward to argue. The background they accepted was this: start with an unequal distribution in which the better-off are much better off, then make the better-off worse off down to the level of the worse-off while helping no one. An intrinsic egalitarian, who thinks equality is good in itself, seems committed to saying this change is an improvement in one respect, even though nobody benefits. Alan called that an absurd implication; Bea thought the objection was less decisive than it looked. I wrote their exchange down as best I could afterward:

Alan: "The leveling-down objection sinks any view that counts equality as intrinsically good. Prioritarianism is not such a view. It assigns value to each person's well-being through a curve that gives extra weight to improvements for people at lower absolute levels. In a pure leveling-down, the worse-off person stays put while the better-off person falls, so total weighted value declines. End of story: prioritarianism has no component that could call that move better in any respect."

Bea: "It avoids the conclusion only by smuggling in the premise. Ask why a benefit should count more when the recipient is worse off. Either the answer is something about the gap—which is an equality thought in disguise, and then the leveling-down implication comes back—or it is nothing more than an arbitrary mathematical curve, in which case the view hasn't been justified. You can always evade an objection by defining the offending idea out of existence, but that is not an answer."

Alan: "Neither alternative holds. The priority weight attaches to the absolute level of the recipient, not to a comparison with others. If everyone were equally badly off, a unit of benefit at that low level would carry the same high weight. There is no variable in the prioritarian value function that tracks distance between people, so the function cannot register even a sliver of improvement in pure leveling-down cases. Whatever one thinks of its ultimate rationale, the specific implication you object to is absent."

Bea: "That is what worries me. You have built a value function so that, in the one kind of case where the views clearly part company, it yields the intuitively acceptable result, while in cases where somebody actually benefits the curve can be adjusted to mimic the egalitarian's recommendations. So the disagreement looks manufactured. Unless you can say why the curve has its shape for some independent reason—not just because it dodges leveling-down—prioritarianism is not a rival; it is a reinforced definition."

Alan: "Even if I granted that the rationale needs more work, the leveling-down objection still would not reach the view. But I do have a rationale. It is worse to be badly off quite apart from how others are faring; pain, deprivation, and helplessness are morally more urgent at lower levels of well-being. If importance has that shape, the concave curve is well-grounded and equality never enters. Pure leveling-down is then straightforwardly a loss. The view has not dodged the objection; it has denied the premise that equality itself has value."

Bea: "But if your ground is that low absolute well-being matters more, then your view is no longer addressing the dispute about equality at all. It says equality itself is worthless and what we should care about is the absolute situation of the worse off. That is a substantive theory, but it is odd to call it a superior response to the leveling-down objection, because it simply declines to theorize about the value the objection was probing. And when we look at poor people in an unequal society, part of what seems bad is precisely that some have so much while others have so little. Your theory avoids the awkward implication by refusing to recognize that comparative feature—the very feature the egalitarian thinks we should recognize."

Alan: "One person's refusing to recognize a feature is another person's having shown it cannot bear evaluative weight unless we are prepared to praise making some people worse off as an improvement. I am not prepared to do that, so I deny that the comparative feature has intrinsic value. That is not abandonment of the argument; it is taking the example seriously."

I cannot tell whether Alan has answered Bea's charge or simply restated his position, and whether Bea's objection about the grounding of the curve is a different objection from the leveling-down objection or a deepening of it. Please go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about which specific point, where each argument succeeds or fails, and who, if either, has the stronger overall position. Please write your answer in American English.
