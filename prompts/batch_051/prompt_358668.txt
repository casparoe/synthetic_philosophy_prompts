I’ve been turning over a puzzle about how you should change your mind when someone tells you an if-then sentence rather than a flat fact, and my gut keeps giving me different verdicts. I want to know if there’s one rule behind all of them or if I’m just inconsistent.

Here are the cases. Case A: I’m trying to locate my lost phone, and I’m already sure it’s either somewhere in the house or somewhere in the car. A friend says, “If it’s in the house, it’s in the kitchen.” My instinct is that I should raise my confidence that it’s in the kitchen and lower my confidence that it’s in the house but not the kitchen, while leaving the car possibilities exactly as they were. Case B: Same setup, but the friend says, “I don’t know whether it’s in the house, but if it is, it’s in the kitchen.” That feels different to me, even though the conditional part is the same. Here I feel like the car possibilities should shift a little too, though I can’t say why or in which direction. Case C: I’m equally unsure which of four zones I’m in, and I get a radio message: “I don’t know whether you’re in the Red zone, but if you are, you’re in the northern part of Red.” My instinct is that I should shift confidence from southern Red to northern Red, and also rejigger the other zones in some way, though it feels forced. Case D: A friend says, “If the coin landed heads, then the light is on,” and I already know the light is on. Here I think the coin should be nudged toward heads, or maybe it shouldn’t move at all, because the light being on was already accounted for. Case E: A weather report says, “If the front comes through tonight, it will rain.” I’m already pretty sure the front won’t come through. I feel like I should lower my confidence that the front comes through and it doesn’t rain, but leave my confidence in no-front roughly alone, even though the forecast feels like it’s giving me information about no-front too.

I’ve tried to find a single principle that gets all five. The obvious rule would be: treat the if-then as ruling out the case where the if-part is true and the then-part is false, then renormalize everything else. But that doesn’t seem to capture what I feel in B or C. Is there a better principle, or are some of my verdicts just wrong? What does the best principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: you’re told, “I don’t know whether you’re in the Blue zone, but if you are, you’re in the eastern part,” and you had no opinion about Blue versus non-Blue, but you did have strong opinions about east versus west. Could you end with a final line that is just a one-sentence summary of the principle you think is best? Put that line at the very end, starting with “Best principle:”.
