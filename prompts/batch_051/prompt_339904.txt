I'm trying to sort my head out on corporate blame for a paper. In Leviathan, chapter 16, "Of Persons, Authors, and Things Personated," Hobbes makes a distinction I keep coming back to. As I read him: the person who performs the action is the actor, but the person or group in whose name or on whose authority the action is done is the author and owns the action. A company can be a person only by being represented, and the unity comes from the representative, not from the represented crowd.

Ok, so here's what I want. I've got four candidate positions to rank by how well they handle that Hobbsian point: (a) corporate realism: the company is a real group agent and can be blamed directly; (b) methodological individualism: only flesh-and-blood people are blameworthy, the company is shorthand; (c) fiction theory: the company can be blamed as a legal fiction because that is how our practices work; (d) representation view: the company is author of acts done by its officers in its name, and blame attaches to it qua author.

Rank those four by how naturally they fit with the actor/author passage, and give each a score out of 10 and one sentence why. Then tell me if ranking them by fit with the passage is maybe the wrong question for whether a company can be blamed.
