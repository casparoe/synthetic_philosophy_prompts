I am a novelist, and over the past several years I have arrived at a small theory about human values that I would like honestly assessed. I have begun calling it the anamorphosis of value.

During drafting, I write most of my characters in close third person, and from that inside position a character's values reliably appear to me as an irreducible many: a wish to be just, a wish to be admired, a wish to be left alone, each exerting its own pull, with no common currency among them and no single concern that seems to generate the rest. But when I later reread from the outside, as a reader must, or when a reader describes the character back to me, the same person seems to snap into focus as having exactly one thing they are really about. The apparent plurality dissolves, and the many values show up as expressions, masks, or local applications of a single master concern.

I no longer think this is a discovery about the characters. I think it is an artifact of where the describer is standing. The outside, retrospective, narrative position cannot help but compress a person into a single through-line, because narrative intelligibility seems to demand exactly one such line; the inside, deliberative position cannot help but scatter into many values, because it must represent live alternatives as genuinely pulling against one another. The analogy I would use is an anamorphic image: the same painted surface is a tangle of marks from one angle and a legible single figure from one fixed point, and neither view is the illusion.

My central claim is that the familiar quarrel between value monism and value pluralism is, in significant part, a quarrel between those two standpoints. Values are not in themselves either one or many; they present as many to the person deciding and as one to the person narrating, including the person narrating themselves in retrospect. I think this is true of actual persons, not only fictional ones, and that it explains why people so often say that only in looking back did they see what they had really cared about all along.

I know the rough outline of the monism/pluralism debate, and I do not pretend this is a scholarly contribution; it is a working theory gathered from craft. What I want is an honest assessment. Please reconstruct the strongest version of the view, say whether it merely restates a familiar position under a new name and what, if anything, remains distinctive in it, and then give the three or four strongest objections. In particular, I suspect the view wrongly predicts that one can never see one's own master value from the inside, and people plainly sometimes do. Tell me whether that is a fatal defect or a fixable one.
