It has become clear to me that my views about the absurdity doctrine in statutory interpretation — the idea that a court may sometimes decline to follow the clear words of an enactment because applying them to the case at hand would produce a result so absurd that the legislature cannot plausibly be taken to have intended it — are really just a bundle of reactions to particular cases, and I would like to recover the principle, if any, that stands behind the bundle. Below are my verdicts on six hypothesized cases. In each case, you may assume that the text is clear and that the literal reading is exactly the one I describe.

1. The grounds-crew case. An ordinance says: "No person shall operate any motorized vehicle within the botanical garden." The garden's own maintenance crew drives a licensed electric cart through it while moving compost and pruning tools, and the garden is cited. I think the literal reading applies, but enforcing the ordinance against the garden's own grounds crew would be absurd, and a court should hold that it does not reach them.

2. The beach-sand case. A statute says: "Whoever removes any sand, stone, or earth from any public beach commits an offense." An adult beachgoer scoops a small handful of sand into a plastic bag to take home as a souvenir. I think the text covers him, but treating that as an offense would be absurd, and a court should hold that the statute does not reach him.

3. The fireworks-van case. A statute says: "A person who knowingly transports more than ten pounds of commercial fireworks for sale commits a felony." A nineteen-year-old delivery driver for a seasonal fireworks stand transports 10.2 pounds in a sealed van, with no aggravating circumstances. I think the application is harsh and the threshold arbitrary, but it is not absurd; a court should follow the text and leave the matter to the legislature.

4. The bus-lane case. A traffic regulation says: "Between 7 a.m. and 10 a.m., no vehicle other than a bus may be driven in the bus lane." A driver pulls into the bus lane for roughly eight seconds to clear a path for an ambulance, then returns to the ordinary lane. I think a court should apply the regulation; the driver's benign motive does not make the literal reading absurd.

5. The drafting-slip case. A consumer-protection provision says: "In any action under this section, a court shall award reasonable attorneys' fees to the party that does not prevail." The title of the section and every surrounding provision make it unmistakable that the provision was meant to award fees to the prevailing consumer, and the "not" looks like a printing error. I think a court should read the "not" out and award fees to the prevailing party; applying the text literally would defeat the statute's evident design.

6. The visiting-lecturer case. A nineteenth-century state law makes it a misdemeanor for any person "to bring any foreign laborer into this state under a contract for labor or service." A university contracts with a foreign academic to deliver a four-week lecture series. I think the literal terms cover her, but applying the law to a visiting lecturer is exactly the kind of case in which a court should depart from the letter; the law is aimed at a quite different evil.

My provisional principle — offered only as a target for you to test — is that a court may depart from clear text only when the specific application is one the enacting legislature, if it had expressly considered it, would have treated as outside the statute's point, and not merely because the result seems harsh, mistaken, or bad policy. I do not trust this principle yet. It seems to fit my verdicts only if it is quietly supplemented with unstated exceptions for triviality, benign motive, or manifest drafting error. I also notice a tension I cannot resolve: the beachgoer's handful of sand and the fireworks driver's 10.2 pounds are both small departures across a line drawn in terms of the very thing the statute names, yet I excuse one and not the other. A principle that keeps both verdicts should be able to say why that is not inconsistent.

So please give me the most precise principle you can — or a small set of candidate principles, with the strongest case for each — that captures as many of these six verdicts as possible. Say exactly which verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and whether you would revise the verdict or the principle. Then apply your preferred principle to a further case I have not resolved: a city ordinance makes it an offense to "feed any wild animal in any city park"; a licensed wildlife rehabilitator retrieves an injured hawk from a park and gives it a few drops of sugar water before transporting it away, conduct the text plainly covers. What does your principle say about her, and does that prediction seem right to you?
