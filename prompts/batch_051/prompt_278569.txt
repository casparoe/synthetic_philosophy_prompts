I'm a PhD student in the anthropology of religion, interviewing secular parents who are raising their kids inside devout extended families. One of my participants, Dana, has given me a bunch of concrete decisions she's made, and I'm trying to reverse engineer the principle behind them before our next interview.

Her track record so far:

- She's fine doing Christmas Eve Mass with the grandparents when they visit. The kids stand and kneel politely with the congregation, don't take communion, and afterward she answers any questions straight.
- She said no to her mother's offer to send the oldest to the parish's week-long summer Bible camp, even though the kid wanted to go for the crafts and songs and the grandparents would have paid.
- When her six-year-old asked whether God is real, she said flatly no, and added that Grandma and Grandpa believe yes and he can decide for himself as he grows up.
- At a family funeral an aunt told the kids that Grandpa is in heaven now. Dana let it pass in the moment and corrected it with the kids in the car on the way home.
- She let the younger one be a flower girl in a church wedding, aisle and altar and all, on the grounds that a one-off family role isn't indoctrination.

New case: it's Thanksgiving at the grandparents'. Grandpa asks whether the nine-year-old can say grace before the meal, a short prayer. The kid is flattered and says he wants to do it. What should I expect Dana to say if she's being consistent with her own past calls?

I'm less interested in the bare verdict than in the structure: what rule best explains her, what other rules fit the same five decisions just as well, and exactly where they split on the grace case. Give me your best guess about her call and why you perfer that reading over the alternatives, then propose one further case that would cleanly tell the candidate rules apart.
