I'm a philosophy professor and I've got to write a one-page proposal for a faculty seminar slot, which would turn into a ~7,000-word paper over the semester. Its on the internal/external reasons debate, and I want to build it around a specific passage from Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature, Book II, Part III, Section III — not one of the usual ones.

Hume writes: "Here then reasoning takes place to discover this relation; and according as our reasoning varies, our actions receive a subsequent variation. But 'tis evident in this case, that the impulse arises not from reason, but is only directed by it. 'Tis from the prospect of pain or pleasure that the aversion or propensity arises towards any object: And these emotions extend themselves to the causes and effects of that object, as they are pointed out to us by reason and experience. It can never in the least concern us to know, that such objects are causes, and such others effects, if both the causes and effects be indifferent to us. Where the objects themselves do not affect us, their connexion can never give them any influence; and 'tis plain, that as reason is nothing but the discovery of this connexion, it cannot be by its means that the objects are able to affect us."

My half-formed idea is that everybody reads this as Williams-style internalism before the fact: reasons only get purchase through motives the agent already has. But the actual argument looks narrower — it shows reason can't do anything when the objects of reasoning are totally indifferent, and that it merely directs an impulse that already exists. What it doesn't obviously show is whether reasoning could generate a new motivating concern: e.g., I start out not caring about some activity, reasoning displays its causal links to something I do care about, and I thereby come to have a reason that wasn't in my original motivational set. If that gap is real, the passage is too weak to do the work internalists want it to do, but it may also be too strong for externalists to just dismiss. What I want from you is a draft of the proposal that does the following: states the question as a determinate interpretive-and-argumentative question, says why the usual internalist and externalist positions don't settle it, sketches the method (close reading of the passage plus a run of cases where reasoning seems to produce or fail to produce new concerns) and why that beats the obvious alternatives (just arguing over Williams' examples, or doing a broad historical survey), then says what a positive result would look like, what would count as failure, and whether this is honestly doable in one semester. Dont worry about citations; I'll handle the references myself.
