After a long fallow stretch as a composer, I've been trying to articulate what I actually do when a new piece works, and I keep returning to a picture I'll call the knob-twiddling model: creating consists in taking an existing conceptual space — a genre, a style, a set of constraints — and exploring it by turning its knobs, where a knob is any adjustable parameter of that space. On this picture the difference between a dull derivation and a striking variation is only a matter of which knobs are turned, how far, and in what combination; no fundamentally new ingredient ever needs to be added. I've been working with three criteria for a genuinely creative act: (1) novelty — the result realizes a combination of parameters not previously realized, rather than reproducing an existing instance; (2) intentionality — the maker has some control over the turning and selects among the resulting variations for reasons; and (3) depth — the variation opens up further variations rather than closing the space down. Now consider a borderline case: a system — an AI, or a person working strictly from a fixed recipe book — that does nothing but turn knobs on a pre-given genre. It can recombine the knobs arbitrarily, but it cannot add, remove, or redefine a knob, and the space itself never changes. It sometimes produces combinations no one has heard before, yet every one of them was already latent in the space. Does this count as creative under my three criteria? Go through the criteria one by one, note where they underdetermine the verdict, and say whether the case shows that the criteria — or the knob-twiddling picture itself — need revising.
