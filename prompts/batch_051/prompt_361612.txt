I sit on a clinical ethics committee at a midsized hospital; we get consults that have to be turned around in about an hour, and whatever we write in the chart can get pulled into risk management or a court fight later. In the last year I've come around to moral particularism — the Dancy line, roughly, that a feature like "this would prolong life" or "the family is in agreement" can count for action in one case and against (or for nothing) in another, so a small set of general principles is never doing the real reasoning. Fine on paper, but I need it operationalized. Write me a six-ish-step review protocol our committee can actually run: it should force us to lay out the specific facts of this case before we reach for autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence etc., say how to record a decision not to apply some familiar principle, and mark at each step whether we're checking a box or making a judgment call. Then run it on two or three thinly-described cases, at least one where the protocol pushes toward a conclusion no one on the committee wants, and end by telling me where the procedure will misfire or quietly smuggle a principle back in. Keep it usable by a nurse and a surgeon at 2 a.m.
