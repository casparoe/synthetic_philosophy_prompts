For an epistemology seminar handout, write a decision tree that sorts a reader's response to the bootstrapping problem and the problem of easy knowledge into a named position, building the tree from commitments rather than from the names. Anchor everything in one case: a subject with normal color vision in good light sees a red table, believes "that table is red" without any further checking, and then—by steps the tree should make explicit—moves to "my color vision worked reliably on this occasion" and, after accumulating such cases, to "my color vision is generally reliable." The branch nodes should be questions a reader can answer without already knowing the positions, and should include at least: whether the initial perceptual belief is prima facie justified before the subject has independent evidence that perception is reliable; whether knowledge and justification must be closed under known entailment; whether warrant transmits from the particular perceptual belief to the reliability conclusion; and whether a track record produced by the very process under assessment can count as evidence for that process's reliability. Locate at least these leaves in the resulting space, using a named view only where the combination of answers is actually defended and writing "unnamed" otherwise: the skeptical response, dogmatism, closure-denying tracking views, process reliabilism, and transmission-failure or cornerstone views. For every combination of answers the nodes generate that has no occupant, say in one sentence whether it is empty because incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored. If any two named positions collapse in your tree, flag them and say which further question would separate them. End with two concrete diagnostic questions, phrased without using the labels, that would tell which branch a reader is actually on.
