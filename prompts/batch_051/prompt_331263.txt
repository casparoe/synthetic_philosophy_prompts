I've been rereading Orwell's "Politics and the English Language" (1946) and keep getting stuck on one of the sentences in his diagnosis of bad prose. He writes:

"As soon as certain topics are raised, the concrete melts into the abstract and no one seems able to think of turns of speech that are not hackneyed: prose consists less and less of words chosen for the sake of their meaning, and more and more of phrases tacked together like the sections of a prefabricated hen-house."

Now here is the case I want to apply it to, which Orwell obviously never had in mind. A student asks a large language model to draft an essay. The output is grammatical, idiomatically fluent, and every sentence is, by construction, close to the most statistically probable way of putting that sentence. In one way that's the prefabricated hen-house made literal: no one chose the words for the sake of their meaning. In another way Orwell's target seems to have been the writer whose own thought is vague or insincere, and who reaches for a stock phrase to hide that; a language model has no thought to hide. So how should the passage be read here? Give me the verdict you get (a) from the words of the passage taken on their own, (b) from the passage's job in Orwell's larger argument about how thought corrupts language and language corrupts thought, and (c) from what Orwell probably would have said if you'd shown him this system. If those three come apart, tell me which one should govern, and wether the case shows the passage is really about words on the page or about the relation between a writer and their words.
