I've been going back and forth with my flatmate about Hume's essay on suicide — I read it years ago for an early modern philosophy module and am reconstructing the argument from memory, so the reconstruction may be off. The line I keep coming back to is meant to show that suicide isn't a sin against God. Here is the version I've been defending:

(1) If suicide is a sin against God, it must be because it goes against God's will for the course of that person's life.
(2) But God's will for the course of a human life is nothing over and above the general laws of nature; there is no special providence that singles out particular lives.
(3) Whatever happens through the general laws of nature is in accordance with God's will, not contrary to it.
(4) When someone kills themselves — swallowing poison, say — the death occurs through the same general laws as any other physical event.
(5) So a suicide is in accordance with God's will, not contrary to it.
(6) Therefore suicide is not a sin against God.

I half-suspect the trick is in (3), because the argument seems to prove too much: if everything that occurs through the general laws is in accordance with God's will, then murder and surgery are as well, and that surely can't settle whether something is a sin. But my flatmate says this isn't a bug — she claims the real Humean point is that the objector has to either condemn all human intervention in the course of nature or drop the providence objection — and that when run properly the argument goes through. She keeps hinting that I'm running together two senses of a term the old theodicists kept apart, but she won't say where. I can't tell whether I've bungled the reconstruction or whether her reading papers over a genuine equivocation.
