I'm trying to nail down my actual position on computationalism for a grad seminar and I keep getting tangled in what feels like thin commitments. You're a professional philosopher of mind who actually works on this stuff; think carefully before answering, and start by mapping the exact points of tension rather than giving me a history of the debate. Here's roughly what I think, after rereading Turing's 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence and some dynamical systems stuff:

(1) The brain is, in the relevant sense, a computer: mental processes are computational processes, not some non-computational extra.
(2) Turing's contrast in that paper between digital computers as discrete-state machines and the nervous system as continuous is basically right and has to be taken seriously.
(3) The correct computational account of cognition is continuous/dynamical rather than symbol-crunching: the relevant computational states are continuous activation patterns, not discrete symbols. This is what respects Turing's continuity point.
(4) Nonetheless, a suitably programmed digital computer could in principle pass the imitation game and instantiate a mind, because discreteness isn't an essential barrier.
(5) But I also want to say that a digital simulation of a continuous system has a different status from actually executing the relevant computation: a digital simulation of the brain's continuous dynamics would not itself be thinking, even if it were behaviorally indistinguishable from a thinker.

I suspect (4) and (5) are pulling hard against each other, and maybe (3) and (4) too. But I also have a nagging feeling that (2) and (3) are actually compatible and I'm missing something, wich makes me doubt my own sense of where the problem is. Rank the tensions by severity; tell me which of these I can hold together cheaply, which require giving something up or refining it, and what the least costly revision is. I don't want a survey of computationalism, I want an audit of this specific set of commitments.
