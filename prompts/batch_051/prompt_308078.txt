Driver licensing in most jurisdictions splits the assessment into a written test on the rules of the road and a separate behind-the-wheel test, and in many places the two components are non-compensatory: a perfect score on one does not rescue a failure on the other. The same pattern shows up across professional certification, from aviation and medicine to skilled trades, where candidates must pass both an examination of factual and procedural knowledge and an assessment of observed performance. The everyday vocabulary for this already marks a contrast — the "knowledge test" against the "skills test" — as if these were two different kinds of competence. What theory of knowledge does this practice presuppose? In particular, does the two-component testing regime strictly presuppose that knowing how to do something is not reducible to knowing facts, or is it compatible with the view that practical competence is just possession of the right facts — with the observed-performance test justified on other grounds, such as the fact that written tests sample only a small portion of the relevant facts, or that they fail to measure performance under pressure? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories of knowledge would license it. Then consider what the regime would have to change if its designers were forced to adopt, consistently, a thoroughgoing intellectualism about practical competence: which tests would survive, which would be redesigned, and how would the hard cases — the fluent theorist who cannot perform, the skilled performer who cannot articulate what they do — be treated?
