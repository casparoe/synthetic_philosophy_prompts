I am a retired experimental physicist, and since leaving the bench I have been reading philosophy of science with the mixture of sympathy and impatience you would expect. The debate I keep returning to is whether string theory can be rationally accepted, or at least legitimately pursued, in the absence of direct empirical confirmation — the position sometimes called non-empirical theory confirmation. As I understand it, the case for that position rested on three planks. First, there appeared to be no credible rival framework that unified gravity and quantum field theory while surviving theoretical consistency tests; the theory looked, in this respect, unique. Second, the history of fundamental physics seemed to show that when a framework had withstood a long process of theoretical scrutiny and resisted piecemeal modification, its central commitments tended eventually to be vindicated. Third, string theory repeatedly generated explanations for phenomena it had not been designed to explain — most famously, the microscopic counting of black hole entropy.

The development I want you to assess is the string landscape. From the late 1990s onward, work on flux compactifications indicated that string theory does not lead to a single low-energy world. It appears to permit an astronomically large number — estimates are often quoted as something like 10^500 — of metastable vacuum states, each giving different low-energy physics: different particle spectra, different values of the cosmological constant, different effective laws. What had been expected to be a rigid theory with a unique vacuum turned out, at the level of observable low-energy physics, to be an enormous space of possibilities.

Here is the problem as I see it. The first plank had drawn its force from uniqueness. But uniqueness at the level of the whole framework, together with enormous underdetermination at the level of low-energy physics, may be a much weaker epistemic position: a framework that can accommodate almost any observation by selecting an appropriate vacuum is no longer in the same business as a theory that makes a definite prediction. The obvious defense is that the original argument was always about the framework, not about any particular vacuum, and that vacuum selection is a separate problem to be handled by a measure over the landscape or by anthropic reasoning. I want you to scrutinize that defense rather than simply accept it or dismiss it.

Does the non-empirical confirmation argument survive the landscape problem? Does it survive as originally intended, does it require repair, or has it been quietly demoted from a claim about the confirmation of a theory to a claim about the confirmation of a framework compatible with a vast range of possible worlds?

Please answer in the following five sections, in this order, with the rough lengths indicated.

1. The argument restated (about 150 words). Restate the three planks in your own words precisely enough that your later analysis can refer to them.

2. What the landscape changed (about 120 words). Explain the string landscape and what it did to the prior expectation of uniqueness.

3. The premise under pressure (about 150 words). Identify which plank is most directly weakened, and say whether the other planks are affected only indirectly or remain intact.

4. Repair or demotion (about 200 words). Assess whether the argument can be repaired — for instance, by shifting the unit of analysis to the framework as a whole and adding a measure over vacua plus selection effects — and whether any such repair retains real epistemic force or merely avoids falsification.

5. Verdict and reversal conditions (about 100 words). Give your bottom line, and state what empirical or mathematical development would make you reverse it.
