I teach an upper-level philosophy of biology seminar, and the part of the species problem I usually feel sure about is the Mayrian argument that species are real units because reproductive isolation does a job: it stops gene flow, and once gene flow stops, lineages can pull apart and stay apart. The gaps between species are basically gaps in the flow of genes. But the more I read from the whole-genome sequencing side, the more it looks like the opposite is true as a matter of course: gene flow across species boundaries is constant, not exceptional—wolves and coyotes, modern humans and Neanderthals, bacteria picking up genes from fairly distant relatives—and yet the groups mostly don't collapse into a blur. So I dont know what the argument is claiming anymore. Does it require isolation to be absolute, or just strong enough relative to the selection that keeps the groups distinct? Which premise does the leakiness actually bear on?

What I want to know is whether the classic argument survives this development, needs repair, or is dead, and whether a repaired version can still say something important—say, species are real but porous, or what maintains the gaps is selection against maladaptive hybrids rather than isolation per se, or something in that neighborhood. If it's dead, tell me what the leftover claim should be. And to be clear, I'm not asking how to classify any particular population or whether we should split some taxon: I'm asking what happens to the argument.
