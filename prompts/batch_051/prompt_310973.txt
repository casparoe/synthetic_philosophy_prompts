I've gotten myself tanged in an argument about Hobbes that seems to prove too much, and I'd like a second pair of eyes. I was rereading Leviathan for my seminar on early modern social contract theory, and I keep coming back to chapter 14. There Hobbes says that in the state of nature every man has a natural right to every thing, even to one another's bodies, and that the only way out of the war of all against all is for men to lay down or transfer some of those rights. But he also insists that there is a limit to what can be transferred: a man cannot abandon the right to resist those who assault him to take away his life, because the whole point of laying down rights in the first place is self-preservation. So far so good.

But then the argument runs like this. (1) Every subject retains a natural right to defend his own life, even against the sovereign. (2) The subject himself is the judge of when his life is threatened, since in Hobbes's account there is no higher earthly judge to decide for him before the sovereign exists, and the sovereign's judgment on this point is just one party's judgment. (3) Therefore every subject may rightfully resist the sovereign whenever the subject sincerely judges that the sovereign threatens his life. (4) But if every subject may rightfully resist on his own private judgment of mortal danger, then the sovereign cannot reliably command obedience, because any subject can appeal to his own fear to justify disobedience. (5) So the social contract fails to generate the very stability it was supposed to generate; rival private judgments of mortal danger reproduce something like the state of war among subjects and sovereign. (6) Therefore Hobbes's own account of inalienable self-preservation undermines the absolute authority he wants the Leviathan to have. I'm pretty sure there's a slide somewhere, maybe between (2) and (3), or maybe in the move from resisting a particular act to resisting the sovereign's authority generally, but I can't put my finger on it. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
