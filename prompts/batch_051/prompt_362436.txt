I teach a philosophy of cognitive science module to roughly 25 undergraduates, in a 90-minute seminar. I want a participatory exercise—a game, simulation, or structured workshop—that makes the students feel the force of the frame problem before they are ever told what it is, and that leaves them seeing why it is a genuinely hard conceptual problem for building an intelligent agent, not just an engineering annoyance about missing rules.

The exercise should run for about 50 minutes, leaving the rest of the session for debrief. Students may work individually or in small groups. Feel free to include roles, cards, props, a whiteboard, a board or table to record facts, and a scoring system—but the scoring must not involve real money or any prize that matters.

Design the activity around a small, finite environment: a kitchen, a bedroom, a workbench, or something comparably bounded. Participants should play the part of a control system that must keep an explicit inventory of standing facts about the environment and carry out a sequence of actions in pursuit of a mundane goal, such as making tea, packing a bag, or moving an object from one room to another. The rules should be arranged so that participants first succeed by doing what people spontaneously do—assuming that everything not touched by an action stays exactly as it was—and are then forced to reason without that assumption and discover, concretely, how many facts they had been silently taking for granted.

Do not include any lecture up front, and do not let the words 'frame problem' appear until the debrief. The problem should happen to them through the structure of the rules, incentives, or roles, not be explained to them first.

The debrief should do the following. First, map specific moments in the exercise—the first failure, the explosion of bookkeeping, the feeling that the game has become rigged—onto the core of the frame problem. Second, carefully distinguish the narrow frame problem, which concerns representing and efficiently updating what does not change when an action occurs, from the qualification problem, which concerns specifying all the preconditions an action needs if it is to have its intended effect, and the ramification problem, which concerns tracking the knock-on changes an action does produce; participants will tend to run these together, so the debrief should show where each one surfaced. Third, be frank about what the exercise cannot reproduce: in particular, the sheer size of a realistic commonsense knowledge base, and the difference between a human's unreflective sense of what can safely be ignored and a formal default rule that says everything unmentioned persists. Fourth, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—for example, groups treating the exercise as a memory test, or one group trying to win by writing down absurdly vague facts that smuggle in the default—and how to handle each failure in the debrief.
