I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun reading the philosophy of mind and the cognitive science that tries to make its questions tractable in the laboratory. The long-running debate between the depictive account of mental imagery associated with Kosslyn and the propositional account associated with Pylyshyn has caught my attention, because much of it appears to turn on a single analogy rather than on direct inspection of the representation itself. The analogy, as it is often pressed, is that forming a mental image is like having a picture in the head — a display on an internal screen that the mind's eye can inspect. The analogy is then made to do real argumentative work: people take longer to mentally scan between two points that are farther apart in the imagined scene, and longer to rotate an imagined object through a larger angle, and from this it is concluded that the image behaves like a physical picture and therefore that the underlying representation must be depictive rather than descriptive. As a physicist I am accustomed to the fact that two very different internal mechanisms can produce indistinguishable input–output behavior, so I want to know exactly how much weight this analogy can carry.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. The five items should be: (1) an explicit mapping of the analogy — what is supposed to play the role of the picture surface, the spatial distances on it, the act of looking, and the observer; (2) the respects in which the mapping genuinely fits the scanning and rotation findings and what it licenses us to predict or explain; (3) the features of an actual picture that the analogy silently imports but a mental representation may lack, in particular the need for a separate interpretive process or viewer; (4) the disanalogies that damage the depictive conclusion rather than merely decorate it, including how the scanning and rotation results might be explained by tacit knowledge, task demands, or other non-depictive factors; and (5) a verdict on whether the depictive conclusion survives once the load-bearing parts of the analogy are isolated, and what a better analogy, if any, would be.
