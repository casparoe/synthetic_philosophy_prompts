I am preparing a handout for an undergraduate metaethics seminar on the wrong kind of reasons problem, and I would like you to produce a sorting instrument: a scoring quiz titled 'Which position on the wrong kind of reasons do you actually hold?'. The quiz should sort readers by what they are willing to say about concrete cases, not by which label they prefer, so no question should use theoretical terms such as 'right-kind reason', 'wrong-kind reason', 'fittingness', or 'state-given', and no question should simply ask the reader which view they identify with.

Please build the quiz against the following background, which you may paraphrase in the orientation: the problem arises when there is an incentive to hold an attitude—admiring someone, believing something, fearing something—for reasons that have nothing to do with the merits of the object. Representative cases include a demon who credibly threatens to harm you unless you admire him, an offer of money to believe a proposition for which you have no evidence, and a powerful person who punishes anyone who fails to fear his harmless pet. The dispute is whether such incentives are reasons for the attitude itself, reasons of some other kind, reasons only to bring the attitude about in oneself, or not reasons at all.

The following four positions must appear as leaves in the scoring key, described in ordinary terms:

1. Object-given purism: only features of the object of the attitude are reasons for the attitude itself; incentives bear only on acts of bringing about, suppressing, or managing the attitude.
2. Dual-standard view: state-given considerations are genuine reasons for the attitude, but they belong to the appraisal of whether holding it is rational, prudent, or beneficial, not to whether the attitude is correct or fitting.
3. Constitutivist view: right-kind reasons are those settled by the constitutive aim of the attitude—truth for belief, accuracy for fear, and so on—while wrong-kind reasons are real but can never satisfy the attitude's own standard.
4. Pragmatist monism: there is no deep right-kind/wrong-kind divide; all reasons for attitudes are ultimately practical, and what gets called the right kind is merely what serves the attitude's function well enough.

The quiz should have 8 to 10 questions. Each question should present a short, concrete scenario of one to three sentences, followed by a forced choice between exactly three options, each expressing a distinct plain-language judgement about the case. Do not include a 'depends' or 'it is complicated' option. Vary the attitude under discussion across belief, admiration, fear, hope, desire, and trust, and vary the incentive. At least the following cases must be used: the demon's threat to induce admiration; payment to believe an unverifiable proposition; a pill that would produce a confident belief about a partner's fidelity; and a harmless creature that a powerful person demands you fear. You may add further cases of your own, but keep them morally uncomplicated and free of graphic violence.

Build the scoring key from the answer patterns rather than from the labels. First identify the distinctions that the questions jointly track; then locate the four named positions among the logically available combinations of answers. For any consistent answer pattern that no named position occupies but which a plausible reader might actually produce, list it with a one-sentence diagnosis of why it is empty (because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). If any two of the four positions end up indistinguishable on the questions provided, flag the pair and say in one sentence which further case would separate them.

Return the instrument as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "title": the quiz title.
- "orientation": two or three sentences of plain-language instructions to the reader, making clear that no knowledge of the technical vocabulary is needed and that there are no trick cases.
- "questions": an array of 8 to 10 question objects, each with "id" (a short string such as "q1"), "scenario" (the concrete case), "question" (what the reader is asked to judge), and "options" (an array of three objects, each with "key" as "a", "b", or "c", and "text" as the plain-language judgement).
- "positions": an array containing the four positions, each with "name", "description" (one sentence), and "awkward_commitment" (one sentence on the most uncomfortable consequence the view must accept).
- "scoring_key": an object mapping each position name to an object that maps each answer key, such as "q1a", to an integer weight between -3 and 3; include only the questions that materially bear on that position.
- "unoccupied_combinations": an array of objects, each with "answer_pattern" (an array of answer keys) and "diagnosis" (one sentence explaining why the combination is empty).
- "unseparated_positions": an array of objects, each with "position_a", "position_b", and "separating_case" (one sentence describing a further case that would separate them, or "none" if they are the same view differently described).
- "usage_note": a short paragraph explaining how the reader should tally the scores, how to interpret close or tied results, and what the scoring key does and does not licence the quiz author to claim.

Write all prose in British English, using spellings such as 'behaviour', 'judgement', 'specialise', and 'colour'.
