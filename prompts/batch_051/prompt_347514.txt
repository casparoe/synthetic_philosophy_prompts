I have been reading Book III of the Nicomachean Ethics, where Aristotle analyzes courage (andreia), and I keep being pulled out of the text by modern examples. Aristotle treats courage as a mean concerned with fear and confidence, and he takes a noble death in battle as its paradigmatic object; he then distinguishes several nearby but, in his view, lesser states, including the courage of citizen-soldiers who stand their ground chiefly for honor and to avoid disgrace, courage born of spiritedness, experience, or optimism, and the endurance of danger without the appropriate motivation. We, by contrast, live in a largely peaceful society in which most people never face a battlefield, yet we freely call people courageous for what they risk in civilian life: a compliance officer who reports fraud despite threats to career and livelihood, a nurse who stays on a hospital ward during an outbreak, a protester who accepts beatings and arrest.

Suppose Aristotle were confronted with these cases and asked whether any of them exhibits courage in his strict sense. Would he answer yes, and under what conditions? Or would he place them among the near-cousins and treat the modern usage as a symptom of a society that has lost touch with the martial virtue he regarded as central? What would he identify as the closest present-day analogue of the battlefield case, if any? Work out the strongest Aristotelian line on one of these examples, and in doing so say how much it matters whether the feared harm is death rather than disgrace, unemployment, or social ruin. End with a line beginning "Verdict:" that states in a short phrase how Aristotle would classify the compliance officer's case, for example genuine courage, citizen-courage, spiritedness, mere endurance, or not courage.
