Here is a step-by-step argument about pure time preference and the practice of discounting the future. I want to know whether it is valid, and if not, which step fails.

(1) Pure time preference is a preference for a good to come earlier rather than later solely because of its temporal position, after controlling for uncertainty, changing needs, opportunity costs, and the possible absence of the future agent.

(2) A preference between two otherwise identical goods is rational only if it is based on some respect in which the goods differ that is relevant to their value for the agent.

(3) In a case of pure time preference, the earlier and later goods differ only in when they occur.

(4) The time at which a good occurs is not, by itself, relevant to its value for the agent.

(5) Therefore pure time preference is not based on any respect relevant to value for the agent.

(6) Therefore pure time preference is not rational.

Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails and what is the strongest reading of that step on which it would be true? If the argument is valid, does its conclusion rule out a positive rate of pure time preference in social discounting, or does it only apply to an idealized individual case? I ask because I want to know whether the residual discount rate in cost-benefit analysis must be zero, or whether something other than pure time preference can survive this argument.
