I retired a few years ago from a career in observational seismology, where I spent most of my time thinking about how a network of instruments can reveal the size of a disturbance that no single instrument records on its own. Since then I have been reading my way into political philosophy, and a reading group I belong to spent this past winter on republicanism and the ethics of military institutions. Out of those discussions I developed a thesis I have been calling the Distributed Cost-Registration Thesis (DCR), and I would like an honest assessment of it before I try it out on people who actually work on the subject.

The thesis is this: a state needs not only the physical capacity to fight but also an accurate, population-level representation of what fighting would cost in human terms. A fully professional army registers those costs in a small, self-selected slice of the citizenry; the median voter may have no direct or familial exposure to them. Broad conscription, by contrast, distributes training, disruption, and risk across a wide cross-section of families, so the expected human cost of any prospective war is represented throughout the electorate itself—as memory, as family narrative, as anticipated disruption. My claim is that this informational function, rather than raw manpower or deterrence, is the deepest justification for conscription in states with strong traditions of armed neutrality: Switzerland most obviously, Sweden across the twentieth century, Finland through its Cold War posture. Neutrality, on my account, is a standing promise to one's own population and to potential belligerents that the country will not enter a war cheaply, and that promise is credible only while the population carries a realistic representation of the price of breaking it. A professional force would let that representation decay within a generation, even if technical military capacity remained unchanged. The prediction, subject to the usual confounds, is that a state moving from broad conscription to a fully professional force should gradually become less inhibited about discretionary military operations abroad—not because its generals or politicians become more hawkish, but because the civilian electorate's estimate of the human cost has quietly degraded.

I realize there is an older civic-republican case for citizen-soldiers, and a familiar inequality-of-sacrifice critique of volunteer forces; I think my claim differs from both by being informational rather than virtue-based or narrowly distributive. It may, of course, be an old idea wearing new clothes, or it may simply fail. Please act as a political philosopher who works on the ethics and political economy of military institutions. Begin by steelmanning the DCR: restate it in its strongest form, make explicit the suppressed empirical premises, and separate it cleanly from the neighboring views I have mentioned. Then give me the four or five strongest objections you can think of, empirical or conceptual, and finish with a candid verdict on whether any of them is fatal rather than merely qualifying.
