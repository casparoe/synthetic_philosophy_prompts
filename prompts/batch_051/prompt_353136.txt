Suppose a big comparative-cognition journal introduces the following policy, explicitly sold as operationalizing Morgan's canon — the old comparative-psychology rule that you shouldn't explain animal behavior in terms of higher mental processes when a lower, leaner one will do. Every submission that attributes something fancy to a nonhuman animal (causal reasoning, planning, metacognition, theory of mind, whatever) has to include a section called a Morgan commitment. In it the authors must (a) describe, concretely enough that someone could actual model it, the simplest associative or learning-based process they think could generate the same data, and (b) name the one additional experiment or observation that would most cleanly break the tie between the rich account and the lean one. A rotating panel, weighted toward people who study animal learning, scores each Morgan commitment. Papers whose commitments are judged evasive or underspecified aren't rejected on that basis; they're published with a public lean-account-unaddressed tag, and the scores are posted alongside the article.

Walk me through how this would actually play out. What does it do to the claims authors allow themselves, the species and tasks they pick, the way discussions get written, the kinds of negative results that become publishable? Where does it get gamed first, and by whom — authors, the panel, editors, the journal's own brand? Does it shift the field toward lean explanations, or just produce a ritual of ass-covering with a new jargon? What does the field look like five years in? Be explicit about which parts of your answer are forced by the incentives and which are just your read on how status-driven researchers behave. And if the whole setup is ill-posed — say, if the lean/rich distinction can't be drawn sharply enough to support scoring, or if the real issue is philosophical and not something a journal procedure can settle — tell me that flat out instead of going along with the premise.
