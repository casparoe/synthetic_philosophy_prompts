I've been thinking about process-based versus outcome-based supervision for training AI systems, and I've built an argument that seems to prove process supervision is pointless — and if it went through it would also show that telling a student which line of algebra was wrong can't teach them anything more than saying "wrong answer." I'm convinced that conclusion is false, but I can't find the bad step.

Setup so we're on the same page: outcome supervision trains on the final answer only (right or wrong), while process supervision also gives a signal on each intermediate step (this line is fine, this line is where it went off the rails). Here's the argument:

(1) A step in a solution isn't correct or incorrect all on its own; the same step can be right in one derivation and ruin another, because its correctness depends on how it bears on the answer the derivation is trying to reach.
(2) So a process label for a step is ultimately a judgment about the final answer, just made at a finer grain.
(3) But the only information the final answer gives you is whether it is right — and that is exactly the outcome signal.
(4) Therefore process supervision adds no information beyond what outcome supervision already contains; it's just outcome signal spread over the intermediate steps.
(5) If a training signal adds no information, it cannot make learning succeed where it otherwise would fail; information is all a signal has to offer.
(6) So process supervision cannot improve learning over outcome supervision.
(7) But it does: models trained with step-level feedback solve hard multi-step reasoning tasks that outcome-only models fail on, even when the step labels are noisy or generated from final-answer checks.

I trust (7), and I'd be reluctant to give up (1), since it seems like a basic point about context-dependence. But something in the move from (4) and (5) to (6) feels off, and I can't name it. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to about what process supervision is actually doing?
