I teach an upper-level undergraduate course in formal epistemology and decision theory, mostly philosophy majors, about sixteen students, and we have an eighty-minute session coming up devoted to the money-pump argument for the transitivity of rational preference. I want to begin with a structured activity rather than a lecture, so that students feel the force of the vulnerability themselves and only later have it named in the reading. Please design a runnable classroom game of roughly forty to fifty minutes including debrief. The game should have two phases. First, an elicitation phase in which participants produce pairwise preferences among three or four alternatives, arranged so that a good number of them naturally end up with a cyclic pattern without realizing it; I'd like you to specify the alternatives, the exact elicitation procedure, and a framing that hides the cycle. Second, a trading phase with real but small stakes — snacks, tokens, or points converted to a modest prize — in which a "house" or "bank" offers each participant a sequence of individually attractive trades, each costing a tiny fee, such that many participants walk themselves back to their original option strictly poorer. The staging constraint is the important part: the sure-loss structure must not be visible in advance; if students spot the cycle, the activity has failed. Include distractors or a cover story so they keep choosing one trade at a time. Then provide a debrief script that maps each moment — elicitation, myopic pairwise choice, the small fee, the return to the starting point, the sure loss — onto the corresponding step of the money-pump argument, and that states clearly where each step could be resisted and what it costs to resist it: refusing a trade one just sincerely preferred, adopting a policy of never choosing option-by-option, being "sophisticated" and evaluating the whole plan in advance, or denying that vulnerability to an exploiter is the same as irrationality. Please also sketch a variant, even if only as a note, in which the full sequence of trades is announced in advance, so the contrast between myopic and sophisticated agents becomes visible. Tell me what will predictably go wrong when this is run with real undergraduates — participants noticing the cycle early, colluding, not caring about a small stake, the bank turning into an antagonist — how to handle each, and which feature of the philosophical argument the activity cannot reproduce and therefore must be supplied by the facilitator. Be frank about whether what the game shows is really that cyclic preferences are irrational, as opposed to merely exploitable.

Where you make empirical claims about how students will behave or what the exercise will teach, say how confident you are in those claims, and single out the point you are least sure about.
