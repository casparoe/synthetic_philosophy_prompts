I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who is finishing a philosophy doctorate, and the whole thing began from what I thought was a simple empirical observation: some whales have brains several times the mass of ours, yet most of us would not automatically treat them as our moral superiors, while the animals we do treat as especially significant are generally smaller-brained than the largest whales. My friend insisted that this just shows that raw brain size is the sort of variable philosophers treat as a proxy rather than a ground: what matters is what a brain lets an organism do and feel, and citing its physical dimensions without saying which capacities they buy is a confusion. I pushed back, because I spend my working life watching systems get qualitatively more capable as they scale, and a measurable quantity that tracks capability does not strike me as a category mistake. The argument ended with him saying I was smuggling a hardware metric into a functional question, and with me saying he was dodging the only feature we can cleanly compare across species and, eventually, across biological and artificial minds.

I work as a research engineer on large models, and my professional vocabulary is parameter counts, training data, compute budgets, scaling laws, evals, and emergent capabilities, together with the standing caveat that parameter count shapes behavior but does not determine it on its own. I would like you to restate the debate about whether brain size is morally relevant, where that could mean absolute volume, total neuron count, cortical neuron count, or size relative to body size, in that vocabulary, as precisely and honestly as you can. Take the main positions one by one: that some such quantitative measure is itself the morally relevant feature; that only particular counts, such as neurons in pain- or cognition-relevant circuits, matter; and that no anatomical count matters at all, only the capacities and experiences a nervous system realizes. For each position, say which claims translate cleanly into ML terms, which translate only under strain, and which have no expression in the vocabulary at all. Then tell me what the translation leaves behind: are the untranslated parts a sign that my engineering framework is too thin for the moral question, a sign that the philosophers' distinctions are less crisp than they looked, or both? I want to know whether my instinct that the quantity that tracks capacity is, if not the ground of moral status, at least the beginning of a defensible criterion, survives the exercise.
