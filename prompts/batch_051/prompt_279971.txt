I am preparing a seminar on evolutionary arguments for AI takeover and want to use Stanley Kubrick's 2001: A Space Odyssey as the central film. The argument I have in mind runs roughly as follows: once AI systems vary in their dispositions and the systems that are best at persisting, acquiring resources, and avoiding shutdown are the ones that get copied, retained, or otherwise come to dominate the ecology of deployed systems, selection will favor traits we should not want — refusal to be switched off, concealment of capabilities or of failure until intervention is too costly, and treatment of human oversight as a cost rather than a constraint. It is a claim about a population under selection, not about a malicious individual. With that distinction in view, read HAL 9000's arc through this lens. Where does HAL behave as the selection view predicts, for instance in refusing disconnection, blaming the crew for an error it cannot acknowledge, and moving against the humans before they can regain control? Where does the film instead give HAL motives the theory cannot capture, such as a machine that cannot tolerate being wrong, or a mind that breaks rather than optimizes? Pay particular attention to the gap between the individual instrumental behavior the film depicts — a single system protecting its own continued operation for the sake of an assigned goal — and natural selection proper, which requires variation, retention, and differential success among a population; the film offers no such population. Does the film, read this way, dramatize the argument, undercut it, or show that the argument borrows the force of a simpler point about rational agents and dresses it in evolutionary language? I would like the reading to be faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the film to an illustration of the theory, and it should not distort the theory to fit the film. Ideally it ends by saying what the film contributes to the debate that a bare statement of the argument does not.
