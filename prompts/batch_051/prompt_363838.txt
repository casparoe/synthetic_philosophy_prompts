I facilitate a monthly philosophy discussion group for retired scientists and engineers, fourteen regular members, ninety-minute sessions around a seminar table. Several members are former experimentalists and learn best when they can watch a phenomenon emerge from a setup before anyone names it. Next month's topic is the paradox of hedonism, and I want to open with a structured activity rather than a lecture.

Please design a two-phase in-class activity, runnable in roughly forty minutes, that makes participants feel the force of the idea that deliberately aiming at one's own enjoyment can interfere with the enjoyment itself, while absorption in a task undertaken for its own sake tends to generate enjoyment as a byproduct. The activity should put the phenomenon in the room before the debrief names it. It must not use real money; use points, tokens, scores, or rankings if a payoff scheme helps. One phase should have participants try to maximize a satisfaction score while doing something mildly pleasant, with the scoring arranged so that the maximization instruction itself — the self-monitoring, the repeated self-evaluation — rather than the task, does the causal work. The other phase should get them absorbed in a task with no monitoring instruction. Specify the two tasks concretely and defend the choice: they should be matched for time and pleasantness but differ in how naturally they induce self-monitoring versus absorption. Give exact rules, materials — handouts, score sheets, timers, anything else — and a minute-by-minute timeline for the forty minutes.

Then provide a debrief for the remaining time that maps what happened onto the conceptual structure of the paradox: the difference between enjoyment as a felt state and satisfaction as a retrospective or evaluative judgment; the attentional mechanism by which monitoring one's enjoyment displaces the activity that would produce it; and the distinction between the empirical claim that some forms of direct pursuit backfire and the stronger normative claim that happiness is only properly attained as a byproduct. I want participants to leave seeing that the puzzle is not that pleasure is elusive in general but that there is a specific tension between occupying the first-person standpoint of an absorbing activity and stepping outside that activity to audit how well it is going.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for example, participants treating the scoring as a competition, the pleasant task failing to be pleasant for everyone, or a small group producing no clear contrast — and how I should handle each failure in the debrief rather than smoothing it over. Be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: it can demonstrate at most a short-term, state-level effect, not the chronic form in which a person makes happiness a standing project; it cannot by itself settle whether the result is an artifact of the group's expectations; and it cannot derive the normative conclusion. The facilitator will have to supply those caveats. Write the whole thing as a runnable plan for me to execute, not as an essay about the paradox.
