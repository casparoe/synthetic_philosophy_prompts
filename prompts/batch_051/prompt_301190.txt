I spent much of the evening in a long and unresolved argument with a friend about whether a particular metaphysical dispute is merely verbal, and I would like to work out where the disagreement—mine and his, and the one between the two positions themselves—actually lives.

Consider two metaphysicians who agree on all the microphysical facts in some region: there are many simple particles arranged in the pattern people ordinarily call table-shaped. The first holds that, in addition to the particles, a table exists there—composition occurs, and a composite object is present. The second holds that no table exists over and above the particles; strictly speaking there are only particles arranged table-wise, and ordinary statements about tables are at best convenient ways of speaking. They make identical predictions about what anyone would observe, and they agree about the practical consequences of either form of words. What they seem to disagree about is only which sentence to assert: "a table exists" or "no table exists."

Let the proposition at issue be: the dispute between these two metaphysicians is merely verbal. I will understand "merely verbal" to mean that the parties could in principle be brought to agree that they accept all the same non-linguistic facts, and that the residual disagreement concerns only the choice of expressions, with no difference in experience, expectation, prediction, or action following from that choice. My friend ended the evening with a credence of roughly 0.2 that the dispute is merely verbal; I ended at roughly 0.6. I suspect we diverge mostly over whether one of the two ways of speaking is more natural or more fundamental, but I do not trust my own diagnosis.

Please give me your own credence in that proposition, and derive it rather than merely assert it. I ask for a full decomposition: identify the sub-questions on which your credence depends—for example, whether the two positions really have identical implications for every possible observation; whether there is a fact of the matter about which of the two ways of speaking is more natural, more fundamental, or more apt for describing the world; whether the two frameworks are exactly intertranslatable and whether that intertranslatability is symmetric; and whether the apparent disagreement conceals some further commitment, such as a view about vagueness or about whether composition can be indeterminate. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, show how they combine into the final number, and state which sub-question your judgement is most sensitive to. Then tell me which sub-question most plausibly explains the difference between a person at 0.2 and a person at 0.6. Finally, indicate what evidence, argument, or consideration would move your credence most.

The response should be written in British English.
