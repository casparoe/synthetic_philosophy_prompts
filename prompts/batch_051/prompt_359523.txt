Here is an asymmetry I want you to examine. The innocence-based argument against capital punishment treats the risk of executing an innocent person as a decisive, practice-disqualifying objection: if the system can kill the innocent, it should not kill anyone. Yet the same system, with the same underlying sources of error — mistaken eyewitnesses, false confessions, police or prosecutorial misconduct — also sends innocent people to prison for life without parole, and that error is treated as a reason to reform imprisonment, not as a reason to say that no one may ever be imprisoned for life. Both mistakes destroy a life in ways that cannot be fully repaired. What, if anything, justifies treating the first kind of error as disqualifying and the second as merely a serious cost? Find the strongest principle you can — something about the finality of death, the special severity of execution, or the state's ability to compensate — and test it against nearby cases, such as a wrongful 40-year sentence or a wrongful life sentence discovered only after the prisoner has died in custody. Then give your verdict: should the asymmetry be kept, reversed, or abandoned? Say how confident you are, and identify the strongest objection to your own position. If you think I have misdescribed the judgments or that the question rests on a confused premise, say so plainly rather than working around it.
