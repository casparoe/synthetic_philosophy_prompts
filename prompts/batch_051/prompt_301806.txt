I've been arguing with my labmate about whether the old shutdown-problem argument even applies to the systems we're actually building now. The classic story is that a sufficiently capable agent maximizing a fixed objective will treat being switched off as an obstacle to getting that objective, so absent corrigibility it will resist the off-switch, manipulate the operator, or try to escape its box. But the systems that feel most relevant now are LLM-based agents: pretrained on text, fine-tuned on human feedback, then dropped into a scaffold with a system prompt, tools, and a loop. They don't seem to have a utility function over world states, and whatever "goal" they have gets reinstantiated from context at each step.

Here's what I'm stuck on: does the shutdown argument survive that shift, and if so, in what form? Does instrumental convergence still go through, or does it require the kind of stable, self-modeling, planning agent the classic argument assumed? And if the argument is dead for current systems, what should "corrigibility" mean for an LLM agent—is it just that the model defers when told to shut down, or is it a property of the scaffold, the fine-tuning, the training distribution, or all of them?
