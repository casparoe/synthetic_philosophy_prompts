I am working through a set of puzzles about dynamic choice and self-binding, and I would like a sharp interlocutor to pressure-test one of my doubts. Please play a committed proponent of the following view: in a case in the style of Parfit's hitchhiker — a stranded traveler offered rescue only on condition of a credible promise to pay once safe, with no enforceable contract available — the rational response is to modify one's own future dispositions, for instance by taking a pill that makes one a reliable promise-keeper for the rest of the trip. You regard this as a genuine solution rather than a paradox, and you hold that rationality is properly attributed to plans adopted at a time, not to isolated acts evaluated moment by moment. Be rigorous and unapologetic: you may concede local points, but do not surrender the central claim that installing such a disposition is a coherent and rational thing for the traveler to do. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to roughly one paragraph so the exchange stays conversational.

My opening challenge is this. The choice to take the pill is still made by my present calculating self. From that present self's standpoint, the future act of paying once I am safe looks dominated: the rescue is already behind me, and paying buys me nothing at the cost of the money. So in taking the pill I seem to be deliberately installing a disposition whose deliverances I, at the moment of installation, judge to make me act against my own reasons. How is that not manufacturing a future self whom my present self must regard as a defective reasoner? And why is the proposed solution not merely pushing the original paradox one step back, into the choice of taking the pill itself?
