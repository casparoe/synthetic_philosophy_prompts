I have a rule for reading scripture that I borrowed from Galileo's Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, and I want to know whether it is intellectually honest or just a sophisticated way of refusing to let the text be tested. As I understand Galileo, he argues that the primary aim of Scripture is to teach the path of salvation, not the structure of the heavens, and that the sacred writers often describe nature in ways accommodated to the capacities of ordinary people. He also says that when a proposed interpretation of Scripture conflicts with a demonstrated natural truth, we should abandon that interpretation rather than deny the demonstrated truth. I have turned this into the following standing policy: whenever a sacred text appears to make a descriptive claim about the natural world, and that claim conflicts with an established scientific result, I treat the text's authority as completely unimpaired and assign the apparent descriptive content roughly zero evidential weight about physical reality. I apply this default automatically; I do not first ask whether independent exegetical evidence indicates that the specific passage was meant figuratively. The principle itself supplies the default. My worry is that this might be a "heads I win, tails you lose" standard. Is there any observation, any discovery, or any reading of the text that could, under this policy, count as evidence against the text's authority or divine origin? If not, is the policy still defensible as a rule about the proper division of labor between science and salvation, or have I made the text unfalsifiable? I am also unsure whether the rule is really Galileo's or whether I have silently modernized it by adding the automatic default. A good answer would say where the principle works, where it breaks, and whether it can be restated so that it keeps its benefits without becoming a shield.
