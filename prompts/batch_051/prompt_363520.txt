I am a journalist who covers science, ideas, and culture for a general-interest magazine, and I need a reusable editorial procedure for a decision I face several times a month. When I report on contested technologies and practices, I keep encountering objections framed in the language of purity, sanctity, contamination, and disgust: a gene-edited tree is described as violating the integrity of living kinds; a proposal to analyze DNA from ancestral remains is called a desecration; a water-reuse project is opposed as unclean even by parties who grant that the treated water meets every health standard; a patient group says that describing a medically useful transplant as a waste product degrades what should be treated as a gift of tissue. Each time, I must choose one of three framings before I write. I can treat the objection as a genuine ethical consideration to be weighed; I can treat it as a psychological or cultural reaction to be explained and contextualized without endorsing it; or I can treat it as group-boundary or status work dressed in moral language. My problem is that unaided intuition is unreliable: some sanctity-based claims carry real weight, and some disgust reactions are simply rationalized. The stakes are fairness to sources, accuracy for readers, and my credibility if I get the framing wrong.

I have no formal training in philosophy, and my editor, who signs off on framing, has not read the philosophical literature and is suspicious of jargon. I would like a decision procedure, in plain language, that I can run in under an hour between reporting and drafting and defend to that editor. For each step, give the question, the bar for a sufficient answer, and the instruction that follows. Mark explicitly where the procedure may terminate with a definite framing, and where it must hand the matter to me or my editor because it has reached a question of values rather than a question of facts. Preserve the distinctions that do the real work — between a defilement claim and a harm claim, between a rule applied symmetrically and one applied only to out-groups or novel practices, between an aversive reaction and a genuine obligation — and drop whatever a deadline journalist cannot reliably determine.

Then run the procedure on the following three cases. (a) A conservation group argues that blight-resistant chestnut trees produced by inserting a single wheat gene should not be released, because crossing that boundary between kinds of life is wrong regardless of the ecological benefit. (b) A coalition of descendant communities argues that a museum should not sequence DNA from ancestral remains in its collection, calling the act sacrilegious; the museum says the sequence data are needed to determine repatriation claims, and no living individual is identifiably harmed. (c) A patient-advocacy group argues that describing a fecal microbiota transplant as a transfer of waste is degrading, that it should instead be framed as tissue donation, and that the degrading framing is itself a moral harm to patients.

My own instincts are a problem here, and I want the procedure to correct them where they are wrong: I lean toward dismissing the descendant communities’ claim as a disguised custody dispute, I find the living-kinds argument in case (a) intellectually sympathetic, and I suspect the patient-advocacy claim in (c) is mostly squeamishness in dignified language. Please make sure the worked applications show at least one outcome I will not like. Conclude by saying what the procedure cannot do, and which residual decisions will remain editorial judgment rather than mechanical application.
