I teach an undergraduate seminar on idealisation in economics, and next week we are reading Aumann's agreement theorem (two Bayesians who share a prior and each know the other's posterior cannot coherently agree to disagree) and the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem (in a market of fully rational traders who share a prior, purely speculative trade should not occur). I want a handout that makes my students feel the gap between those results and the looser slogans they arrive with: that prices aggregate dispersed information, that persistent disagreement is a sign someone is irrational, and that the market's verdict is as close to the truth as we get.

Please write a deadpan parody, of about 800 words, of a compliance agreement for an invented macro hedge fund that has operationalised the two theorems as firm policy. You may call it something like 'Common Prior Capital LLP'. The document should read as a real internal policy leaked from the firm's intranet, in the register of a legal contract: recitals, definitions, and numbered clauses. Its provisions should include: staff must calibrate and register their priors in the Prior Registry; any conversation forecasting a traded instrument between two staff whose registered priors differ in the third decimal place is prohibited; any observed instance of two staff of differing posteriors socialising without reporting each other counts as a 'Bounded-Rationality Leakage' incident and triggers mandatory reconciliation; and a no-trade covenant forbids executing a trade with a counterparty whenever, under the shared firm prior, the counterparty's quoted price implies a posterior the firm does not itself hold, since by Milgrom–Stokey one of the parties would thereby be revealed as irrational. The satire should exaggerate a real, if crude, reading of the theorems—their idealising assumptions (common priors, common knowledge of rationality, costless and truthful reporting) become contractual obligations—and it should quietly land the standard objections without stating them: the assumptions almost never hold outside the model; the results concern ideal agents rather than actual traders; and a firm that suppressed every disagreement would destroy precisely the dispersed, conflicting information that, on the Hayekian picture, prices exist to transmit. Keep it deadpan throughout: no winking, no explicit moral. The piece should work for a reader who has never met the theorems, and be funnier to one who has. No footnotes, citations, or scholarly apparatus.

Structure the response under these headings, with approximate lengths: 'Recitals' (80 words); 'Definitions' (120 words); 'Conditions of legitimate disagreement' (180 words); 'Trading and the duty to abstain' (180 words); 'Disposal of non-conforming priors' (150 words); 'Severability and acknowledgment' (90 words).
