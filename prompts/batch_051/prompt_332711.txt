A friend of mine has fallen hard for "ergodicity economics" and keeps insisting that the standard expected-value calculus is just wrong for most real decisions, because a real person lives one path through time and never recieves the ensemble average. His claim, concretely, is this: when a gamble will be repeated many times, the quantity a chooser ought to keep track of is the time-average growth rate of wealth, not the ensemble mean. So, for example, he says nobody should take a coin flip that multiplies your wealth by 1.5 on heads and by 0.6 on tails, repeated daily, even though its one-round expected return is +5% — because the time-average growth rate is about −5.1% per run, so a single person who keeps betting will almost surely go broke, while the positive ensemble average is being propped up by a few lucky paths. He also thinks ordinary people somehow feel this, and that a bunch of what we call risk aversion is actually an implicit sensitivity to time averages.

I don't want to just argue in circles about this. I want to know how you would test the claim. Tell me what exactly would be measured, what result would count in favor of the time-average view, what result would cut against it, and what the test could never settle even if it came out perfectly — especially the question of whether "ought to" tracks the data at all, or whether we're just quarreling about two different normative criteria.
