You are a moral epistemologist and a philosopher of cognitive science. Begin by brainstorming two or three distinct justifications for the policy described below, then work through the strongest objections before giving your verdict.

I am a philosophy PhD student working in moral psychology, and I have adopted a rule about which intuitions I treat as evidence when I reason about objective moral questions. The rule is this. When a moral intuition I find compelling—for instance, that some kind of act is wrong, or that some class of beings belongs inside the scope of moral concern—is robustly present in WEIRD samples (Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic) but absent, weaker, or reversed in at least one large non-WEIRD sample, I remove that intuition from my evidence base entirely. I do not merely lower my confidence and retain a residual; I set the intuition aside as unusable, even when I cannot explain the cross-cultural difference and even when the intuition still feels self-evident to me. When an intuition appears stable across otherwise very different societies, I let it back in, but only as weak and defeasible evidence. I apply the rule only to moral intuitions. I do not apply it to perceptual or probabilistic intuitions, even though I accept that those too are shaped by culture in documented ways. My rough defense is that perception and probabilistic judgment can be checked against the world in ways that moral judgment perhaps cannot, and that local social technologies are more likely to shape moral judgment than to shape seeing. But I can see the suspicion that the rule is ad hoc: a device for discarding exactly the moral judgments that make me uncomfortable while keeping the ones I like, guided by a principle that, applied everywhere, would leave me trusting almost nothing.

Evaluate this as a policy for a real researcher with limited time and no cross-cultural fieldwork of her own. When is it reliable, and when will it lead me astray? What is the strongest defensible version of the rule—what exactly should be discounted, under what conditions, at what rate, and on what principle that is not simply the assumption that agreement is evidence? Is my exemption for non-moral intuitions defensible, or is that the weak point? Be concrete about what a defender of moral objectivity and a skeptic about moral objectivity should each say about the rule.
