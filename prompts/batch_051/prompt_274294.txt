I am a science journalist working on a piece about how learners manage to induce general concepts from very few examples, and I have been thinking hard about Bongard problems. For context: a Bongard problem displays two panels of six simple line drawings each; the left panel conforms to a rule, the right panel violates it, and the task is to recover the rule from those twelve examples alone. I have been calling the view I have settled on contrast compression. It holds that a concept is not a summary of what the positive examples have in common; it is the shortest available description of the boundary that separates the positive set from the contrast set. On this view, solving a Bongard problem is not a matter of abstracting a prototype from the left panel, but of searching for the cheapest separator between left and right given the descriptive vocabulary the learner already has. What is stored is the boundary, not the category. This would explain why twelve carefully chosen examples can suffice: the number of examples required is proportional to the complexity of the shortest separator, not to the size or variability of the category itself. It would also explain why the right-hand panel is not just negative noise; the contrast class is doing much of the conceptual work. I think the same principle applies to ordinary concepts. We learn chair, for instance, by finding the cheapest boundary that divides chairs from the nearest contrasting things such as stools, benches, and sofas, and the concept chair simply is that separating rule. I can already see that this sounds close to minimum description length, and perhaps to Solomonoff induction, to the decision boundaries used in support-vector machines, and to version-space learning. I need to know whether contrast compression is a real idea or just a metaphor in technical dress.

Please act as a professional philosopher of cognitive science with expertise in concepts, simplicity, and generalization. Think through the proposal carefully. Start by brainstorming three or four of the strongest, most charitable reconstructions of the idea, and name the established view each reconstruction most closely resembles. Then give me a candid assessment. Is the view internally coherent? Is it distinct from minimum description length, Solomonoff induction, version-space learning, Tversky's contrast model, or prototype theory, or has one of those positions already said everything it says? Most importantly, where does the view break? What gets wrongly counted as concept learning, what sorts of concepts resist being identified with a separator between a category and a contrast class, and what does that resistance show about the general claim?
