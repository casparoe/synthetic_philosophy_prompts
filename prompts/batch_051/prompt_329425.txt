Take Maxwell's demon as a thought experiment about the second law. The usual setup is a box of gas divided by a partition with a small trapdoor, and a demon who opens the trapdoor so that fast molecules accumulate on one side and slow molecules on the other, making one side hotter and the other colder with no obvious expenditure of work. The standard diagnosis is that the demon has to measure speeds, those measurement outcomes have to be stored somewhere, and when a finite memory clears old measurements to make room for new ones, the erasure is irreversable and that's where the second law gets its entropy back.

Now suppose the demon's speedometer is noisy. A molecule in front of the trapdoor that is genuinely faster than the box's median speed gets read as 'fast' with probability 0.8 and 'slow' with probablity 0.2. A genuinely slower-than-median molecule gets read as 'slow' with probability 0.8 and 'fast' with probability 0.2. The demon uses the same policy as before: open the trapdoor when it reads a fast molecule on the left, or a slow molecule on the right, and otherwise keep it shut. It has an ordinary finite memory, so each noisy measurement still has to be erased before the next one.

Walk me through what this does to the standard resolution. Does a steady temperature difference still build up, and how does its size compare to the perfect-measurement version? Where exactly does the compensating entropy come from now — the erasure of the noisy records, the mistaken openings that let wrong molecules through, or some combination of both? Is there a threshold error rate, short of the completely useless fifty-percent noise, where the demon stops being a puzzle and just becomes a strangely complicated space heater? And say what the noisy case tells us about where the real burden of the second law lives: in the cost of forgetting, in the quality of the information itself, or in the whole accounting taken together?
