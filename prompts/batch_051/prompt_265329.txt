I recently lost my older sister very suddenly, and in the weeks since I have kept returning to David Lewis's modal realism. I cannot stop thinking that there is a world in which she did not get in the car that morning—and that, on Lewis's view, that world is not a story I tell myself or a mere way things could have gone. It is a concrete universe, as real as this one, with a counterpart of her alive in it. I want a poem that is honest about both the consolation and the particular cruelty of that thought.

Write a pantoum of five or six quatrains, in the voice of someone who has recently lost a person and is trying to decide whether modal realism is consolation or torment. Work within the actual pantoum scheme: the second and fourth lines of each quatrain are reused—verbatim, or with only the lightest grammatical adjustment—as the first and third lines of the next, and the final quatrain weaves back in lines from the opening quatrain so that the poem returns to where it began. Let that returning movement enact the doctrine rather than decorate it. Use repeated lines that are plain statements about the two worlds, or about what is the same and different between them, so that each recurrence carries the same words into a new world-context and loosens the reader's sense that this world is privileged. Try to make at least one repeated line change valence across the poem—consoling in its first appearance, much harder by its last—without changing its wording. The closing loop should leave the speaker back in this world, which no longer seems more real than the other, only more present.

The poem must be correct enough that a metaphysician working on modality would recognize Lewis's view and not wince. Do not present possible worlds as planets one could travel to, as fictions or stories, as future states not yet actualized, or as abstract sets of descriptions. Worlds are complete, concrete, spatiotemporally isolated universes; none is more real than another; and "actual" is an indexical, like "here" or "now," so every world is actual at itself. If the poem mentions the other-worldly person, she should be a counterpart of the one lost, not literally the same individual commuting between worlds. At the same time, the poem should hold up for a reader who has never heard of modal realism: the grief should carry the philosophy, not the other way around.

You are a poet with a serious working knowledge of contemporary analytic metaphysics. Before drafting, work through in your own reasoning: (i) which two or three features of Lewis's view the pantoum's repetitions can bear, (ii) which concrete images from the loss can carry those features without turning the poem into a lecture, and (iii) exactly where the closing loop should land so the doctrine is felt rather than asserted. Keep this planning out of the response.
