I serve on the discipline committee of a small professional engineering association, and we must decide whether to permanently revoke the membership of an engineer who signed a fabricated structural-inspection report for a footbridge. The fabrication was caught by a routine audit before the bridge opened, and no one was hurt. The engineer clearly understood what he was signing, had alternatives, and acted to keep an overdue project on schedule. He has since apologized, paid for a new inspection, and undergone treatment for anxiety.

Last night I argued about the case with a close friend. My friend insists the engineer is fully blameworthy and deserves the harshest sanction available: the man knew the rule, could have complied, and chose not to. I found myself resisting. I argued that whether he could have done otherwise depended on his temperament, his risk tolerance, and his fear of conflict, none of which he chose. If his choice flowed from a character he did not choose, then he never chose the action that flowed from it. Being truly responsible for the action would require being responsible for the way he is; being responsible for the way he is would require having chosen the way he is; and that would require a still earlier self, and so on backwards. My friend replied that a test of blame nobody could ever pass cannot be the right test of whether this particular man deserves blame. He said he is not asking whether the man created himself, only whether a competent professional understood the standards and failed them.

I now cannot tell whether my objection is a genuine insight or a philosophical sleight of hand. Which of us has the better argument? If my friend is right that ordinary blame does not require self-creation, what exactly does it require, and how should our committee talk about the blame element of an expulsion? I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I want the strongest reconstruction of each side and your own judgment.

At the very end, please state your conclusion as the final line, in the form \answer{fully blameworthy}, \answer{not blameworthy}, or \answer{blameworthy only in a practical sense}.
