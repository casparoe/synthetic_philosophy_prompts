I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long-form piece about a debate I keep encountering in moral philosophy but have never seen laid out carefully: whether moral language describes how the world is or does something else entirely. One general-audience book I've been using as background puts the expressivist position and its central difficulty like this:

"Expressivism begins from the thought that moral language doesn't fit the picture on which words get their meaning by standing for bits of the world. To say 'lying is wrong' is not to report that an act has some special moral property; it is to express a negative attitude, much as shouting 'Boo!' expresses dislike. That, its defenders argue, is why moral claims grip us in a way that neutral descriptions don't. The view's standing difficulty was noticed by Frege and pressed by Geach: if 'wrong' serves to vent an attitude, what is it doing in an unasserted context, such as the antecedent of 'If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong'? In that embedded position the speaker isn't condemning lying at all. So the moral term appears to mean one thing inside the conditional and another thing standing alone, which would leave arguments that use a moral premise and a moral conclusion -- a straightforward case of modus ponens -- open to a charge of equivocation. Expressivists have spent decades trying to answer this, but the philosophical consensus has settled against them; the argument is now standardly taken to show that moral judgments are ordinary beliefs about how things are."

I don't know this literature well enough to know how much of that to trust. Could you give me an accuracy audit? Going through the summary roughly sentence by sentence, I'd like to know which parts a careful expressivist would accept, which are common simplifications, and which are subtly off or overstated. I'm especially interested in whether the final claim -- that the Frege–Geach argument shows moral judgments are ordinary beliefs -- actually follows from what precedes it, or whether the book is presenting a live debate as a settled verdict.
