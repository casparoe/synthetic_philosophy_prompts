I'm teaching an upper-level interdisciplinary seminar called Risk, Luck, and Rationality, with eighteen students split between economics and philosophy, and I have one 75-minute session in which I want to stage a game that makes them feel the difference between time-average and ensemble-average growth without my ever naming the distinction until the debrief. The backbone of the game is a multiplicative coin-flip: on heads a player's current wealth is multiplied by 1.5, on tails by 0.6, so that the expected per-flip growth factor is 1.05 while the typical long-run path grows at about 0.949 per flip. I want the students to commit, before any flips happen, to a fixed fraction of their current stake that they will wager on every flip for the whole game, and then to play out enough rounds that the divergence becomes impossible to ignore. The crucial scaffold is that the group simultaneously tracks the class-wide mean wealth after every round, so that at some point the class sees the ensemble mean climbing 5% per flip while nearly every individual trajectory is shrinking, and none of the individual players should expect their own path to resemble the class mean.

Please design the full activity end to end: the exact rules (including whether and why players may or may not revise their committed fraction mid-game), roles and materials, a payoff scheme that needs no real money, a timeline for the 75 minutes, and a scheme for recording and displaying both individual paths and the running ensemble mean so that the divergence is visible in the room rather than asserted by me. Then specify the debrief, mapping concrete moments in the game onto the distinction between maximizing expected value and maximizing expected log growth, and onto the condition under which the two come apart: repeated, multiplicative risk applied to a single pool of capital. The debrief should be sequenced so that the students first articulate the puzzle in their own words, then receive the vocabulary, then connect it back to what their own recorded path did.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance, one or two students landing an improbably good path and the room mistaking luck for strategy, or participants updating their committed fraction after seeing how the early rounds went — and how to handle each failure without lecturing. Be candid as well about what the game cannot reproduce, such as the infinite-horizon limit, the large-ensemble limit, or the distinction between a single long-lived player and a population of parallel players, and say exactly what I will need to supply in the debrief for those gaps.
