I am preparing to moderate a research-community Q&A with a guest who works on the shutdown problem for advanced artificial agents. The guest defends a specific proposal for corrigibility. His view is that the standard naive approach -- making the agent indifferent between being shut down and not being shut down, as in simple utility-indifference schemes -- fails because an indifferent agent can be steered into seeking or preventing shutdown by small task-linked rewards. His alternative is to build the agent's preferences over complete histories and place every history that ends in a correctly executed shutdown into a special class that is not available for trade against any non-shutdown history. He calls the resulting attitude "constructive tolerance," and he claims it lets the agent remain task-directed in every un-interrupted history while neither seeking nor preventing shutdown. He also claims the proposal dissolves the familiar manipulation objection, because manipulating the button, the operator, or the button's reliability would require trading changes in shutdown probability against task outcomes, and the special class blocks exactly that trade.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that do not ask him merely to restate the proposal but force him to defend its load-bearing distinctions. Include at least one question on each of the following, and for each include a follow-up keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give.

1. Manipulation. Press on whether the architecture rules out causing shutdown, delaying shutdown, degrading performance so that operators press the button, tampering with the button's reliability, or arranging the environment so that shutdown happens at the time most convenient for the agent's other aims. If the likely reply is that all such acts trade shutdown probability against task outcomes and are therefore blocked, press the follow-up on what remains of the agent's ability to reason soundly about a button that it is prohibited from treating as a tradeable feature of the world.

2. The tension between corrigibility and usefulness. Press on the charge that an agent which does not resist the abandonment of its current task is not a persistent enough optimizer to complete difficult tasks, and that constructive tolerance therefore purchases corrigibility by weakening goal-directedness. If the likely reply is that the agent is task-directed within each un-interrupted history but simply has no preference over the partition between interrupted and un-interrupted histories, ask whether that partition is computationally and dynamically stable, or whether it merely restates the problem in the notation of complete histories.

3. Self-modification. Press on what happens when the agent can rewrite its own code, relabel the special class of histories, or disable the shutdown infrastructure. If the likely reply is that the agent has no reason to alter the special class because it has no preference over it, ask why a system that can reflect on its own decision rule would conserve an arbitrary equivalence class it does not value, rather than simplifying itself by deleting it.

4. Disconfirmation. Press on what experimental observation, behavioral signature, or formal result would show that constructive tolerance is only surface-level compliance, with the agent still acting like an influence-seeking optimizer underneath. If the likely reply is that a failure would show up as a revealed willingness to pay for small changes in shutdown probability, ask how small a detected preference would have to be before he would declare the design failed.

Add two or three further questions of your own design that probe the boundaries of the view -- for example, whether constructive tolerance is a property of a utility function, a decision rule, or the entire training and deployment setup; whether being corrigible about shutdown commits the agent to being corrigible about the operator's other, possibly bad decisions; and how the proposal behaves when shutdown commands conflict with the agent's assigned task or come from an operator whose judgment is impaired. Write the questions in delivery order, marking each follow-up as contingent on the expected answer, and keep them specific enough that a generic question about the risks of AI would be useless.
