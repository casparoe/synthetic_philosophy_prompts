I'm a software engineer and I've never studied philosophy, but I've been reading a fair bit of maths for fun recently — mainly topology and a bit of abstract algebra — and I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a word for.

The clearest example is continuity. You first learn it as the epsilon–delta thing: for every epsilon there's a delta, and small changes in the input produce small changes in the output. That's obviously about closeness. Later it gets redefined as: the preimage of every open set is open. At first that looks like a sideways move — what has 'no jumps' got to do with open sets? But this version generalises to spaces with no notion of distance, and it makes a lot of proofs that were fiddly epsilon-chasing into one-liners. A similar thing happens with groups. The definition (a set with an operation that's associative, has an identity and inverses) doesn't look like an attempt to capture 'symmetries of some object', even though symmetries are the standard motivation. It looks chosen so that the theory of subgroups, quotients and homomorphisms goes through cleanly — and it also covers cases like the integers under addition that don't look like symmetries at all.

I'm trying to pin down what this move is called: choosing or revising a definition not because it matches the first intuition, but because it makes the theory work, generalises to new cases, and makes the important theorems simple. Is there an established name for that in the philosophy of mathematics? I'm not just after a term; I want to understand exactly how this differs from ordinary abstraction or generalisation, and where the boundry is. Does it make sense to say the open-set definition is the right notion of continuity, or is 'right' here just shorthand for convenient? If there's no standard name, what would you call it, and how would you cash it out? I'd be grateful if the answer could be in British English.
