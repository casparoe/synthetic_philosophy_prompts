In one of the fragments of the *Pensées* on justice and force, Pascal makes a claim I have been chewing on for some time. He observes that justice has an epistemic defect: people dispute what it demands, so any appeal to it invites argument. Force, by contrast, is immediately and uncontroversially recognizable; no one needs a seminar to identify who is being coerced. From this he draws the conclusion that, because we cannot make justice strong, we have made the strong just. I have been trying to turn this observation into a small predictive theory of institutions, which I call the asymmetric-dispute-cost view.

The central claim is this: an institution's authority over a population is proportional to the gap between the cost of disputing its rules and the cost of complying with them. When disputing a rule is much more costly than obeying it, the rule reads to those under it as legitimate, whatever its justice. When the two costs come close together, the institution becomes publicly contestable and starts to lose its taken-for-granted character. When the cost of disputing falls below the cost of complying, people exit, evade, or revolt. I think this captures something real about tax systems, property arrangements, family norms, and constitutional order. It also captures why authority often engages in what I call self-protective devolution: it raises the costs of dispute (sedition laws, etiquette, procedural complexity, indoctrination) while lowering the costs of routine compliance (convenience, automaticity, administrative simplification).

I know this can sound like Weber on legitimacy, Hobbes on covenants, Foucault on discipline, or Williams's basic legitimation demand. I also know my principle has trouble with high-cost moral challenges such as civil disobedience, heresy, or martyrdom, where people accept enormous costs to dispute rules they could easily obey; but I do not claim such cases must happen, only that the theory predicts when they do, an order is already in crisis.

My question is an honest-assessment request. Is asymmetric dispute cost a genuine mechanism, or just Pascal's point redressed in social-science language? Does it add anything beyond the commonplace that power needs legitimacy or that legitimacy is manufactured? And where does it break most clearly beyond the martyrdom cases I have already conceded — which of the examples would a critic rightly say I have misdescribed, and is there a way to repair the theory without turning it into an empty tautology?
