I have recently been placed in the position of having to adjudicate a dispute in my professional association: a colleague gave a firm verbal undertaking to serve a demanding role for the association, now wishes to withdraw, and defends his withdrawal by saying that he never had any accompanying feeling of being bound—he was, he claims, merely being agreeable—and therefore never really obligated himself. That defense has forced me to ask what, if anything, makes a promise bind in the first place. While reading David Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature, I came upon the following passage in Book III, Part II, Section V, "Of the obligation of promises":

"All morality depends upon our sentiments; and when any action, or quality of the mind, pleases us after a certain manner, we say it is virtuous; and when the neglect, or non-performance of it, displeases us after a like manner, we say that we lie under an obligation to perform it. A change of the obligation supposes a change of the sentiment; and a creation of a new obligation supposes some new sentiment to arise. But 'tis certain we can naturally no more change our own sentiments, than the motions of the heavens; nor by a single act of our will, that is, by a promise, render any action agreeable or disagreeable, moral or immoral; which, without that act, wou'd have produc'd contrary impressions, or have been endow'd with different qualities. It wou'd be absurd, therefore, to will any new obligation, that is, any new sentiment of pain or pleasure; nor is it possible, that men cou'd naturally fall into so gross an absurdity. A promise, therefore, is naturally something altogether unintelligible, nor is there any act of the mind belonging to it."

The passage seems to argue that promising cannot be a natural source of obligation: once we rule out resolving, desiring, and willing the promised action as the act of mind behind a promise, the only candidate left is willing the obligation itself; but obligations are constituted by sentiments, and the will cannot create a new sentiment any more than it can alter the motions of the heavens. Promising, on this view, becomes intelligible and obligatory only through human conventions founded on social necessity and interest.

I find the argument both persuasive and disquieting, because it would reduce my colleague's excuse and my own grounds for holding him to his word to a single question about what the relevant convention, in this association, actually requires. Yet I am not sure Hume has shown as much as he thinks. The claim that one cannot will a sentiment seems right but may miss the possibility that a promise obligates in a different way: not by creating a new sentiment, but by changing what the promisee may legitimately expect, or by exercising a normative power that gives the promisee a reason or right they would otherwise lack.

Write an essay on what ultimately explains why promises bind. The essay should interpret Hume's argument in this passage, then test it against at least two rival explanations—one centered on the expectations or reliance that promising induces, and one that treats promising as a moral power by which the promisor gives the promisee an assurance or a right. Show what each rival would have to say about the move in the passage that obligations reduce to sentiments, and where Hume's conventionalist conclusion would still survive even if that move is rejected. Reach and defend a definite verdict about the source of promissory obligation; do not end on a non-committal note.
