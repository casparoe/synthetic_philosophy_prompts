A friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about this, and neither of us could state the principle we both felt must be there. We kept returning to an asymmetry in our own judgments. Consider an engineer who discovers that her company has been falsifying safety certifications for a device used in hospitals. She raises the matter internally, is ignored, and then reports it to the regulator. The nearly unanimous reaction is that she is a whistleblower and that she did the right thing, perhaps the brave thing. Now consider a man who discovers that his brother has for years been claiming a fraudulent dependent on his tax returns. He raises it with his brother, gets nowhere, and reports him to the tax authority. Here the nearly unanimous reaction is that he is an informer, that he has betrayed his own family, and that he should have found some way to stop the fraud without going outside the family. I find that I hold both verdicts firmly, and I cannot state the difference between the two cases in a way I would be willing to defend.

The candidate distinctions that came up all seemed to fail. Size of harm does not separate them: make the tax fraud large and the safety violation small but genuinely dangerous, and the asymmetry remains, with the reporter of the large fraud still seen as more treacherous than admirable. Exhaustion of internal channels can be equalized, since the brother raised the matter first as well. The thought that the company is a large impersonal institution while the brother is a person is the most promising, but the principle that personal loyalty always outweighs institutional loyalty would license covering for a family member's serious wrongs, which most of us reject. And if someone says the company case is different because it protects innocent third parties, change the tax case: suppose the brother's fraud helps finance a side business that employs people in unsafe conditions. My reactions survive that change, and this worries me.

My friend's final proposal was that the difference lies in exit: the engineer can leave her employer, while the brother cannot shed his family. But the judgments do not seem to track exit costs. Make the engineer economically trapped in her job or the brother thoroughly estranged, and the asymmetry barely moves. So I am left asking whether anything justifies treating these two disclosures differently. Give me the strongest principle you can find that generates both verdicts, test it against the hardest nearby cases, and then say honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept or one of the verdicts should be surrendered.
