I am a retired research scientist; my working life was spent in the natural sciences, and my engagement with philosophy is recent. I have been asked to circulate to a reading group a short statement of my views about how world history can and cannot be written, and before I show it to anyone I would like an audit of whether the position hangs together. My habits of thought are those of someone trained to ask about evidence, causation, and symmetry; I have not been trained in this field, and I may be using terms loosely. Where that matters, please say so.

Here is roughly where I have landed:

(a) There is no standpoint-neutral way to narrate world history. Every narrative has a center of emphasis, a spatial and temporal scale, and a set of organizing questions, and these are chosen by the historian rather than found in the record.

(b) It does not follow that all histories are equally good. Some accounts get the record wrong and some get it right, and some accounts are more adequate to the past than others in a way that is not reducible to their usefulness for present-day communities.

(c) What counts as historically significant — a turning point, a major event, a region that matters — is assigned retrospectively rather than discovered in the archive; there is no purely factual answer to the question of what mattered.

(d) Equal coverage is not the remedy for Eurocentrism. A world history that gives every region equal space but no connective structure is a catalog rather than a history, and any connective structure will privilege some places and periods over others.

(e) It is a fact about the past, not a projection of the historian, that western Europe played a disproportionate causal role in the making of the modern world. A history that denied this would falsify the record.

(f) At the same time, standard Eurocentric narratives distort the past. They treat European developments as the engine of world history and other regions as reacting to them, and they explain Europe's rise through internal virtues — science, institutions, culture — while explaining the different trajectories of other regions through internal defects. The second is a methodological error even if the first were only a choice of emphasis.

(g) I therefore accept a symmetry principle for large-scale historical explanation: the same kinds of causes — ecology, contingency, conjuncture, the timing of contacts — should be available to explain outcomes in any region, and an explanatory style is suspect if it is applied to one region and withheld from another.

(h) For example, I think the early modern divergence in economic fortunes between western Europe and parts of Asia is better explained by contingent and conjunctural factors — resource endowments, the timing of oceanic connections, the pressures of empire — than by any deep difference in culture or institutions.

(i) The remedy cannot be a history told from everywhere at once, because there is no everywhere to stand in. The goal is not to eliminate centers but to make them explicit, plural, and revisable.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what the least costly revision would be. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set cohere, and whether any one commitment is doing more work than I realize. I would rather learn this now than in front of the group.
