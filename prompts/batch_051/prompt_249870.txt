I've been trying to get clear on what makes an act of political violence count as terrorism, and my immediate verdicts on particular cases pull in different directions. I'm not asking for a survey of statutory definitions or official usages; I want to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern in my own judgments, and what that principle says about cases I haven't considered.

Here are the cases. (1) A separatist group detonates a car bomb in a crowded commercial district and announces that the attacks will continue until the government withdraws from a disputed region; dozens of civilians are killed. I call this terrorism without hesitation. (2) The government, fighting that insurgency, shells a residential neighborhood in the separatist region and states that it will go on doing so until the local population hands over the group's fighters. I also want to call this terrorism, even though it is a state acting in wartime. (3) An armed faction assassinates a cabinet minister who personally directed a brutal counterinsurgency; its statement says only that the minister "paid for what he did" and threatens no one else. I find myself unwilling to call this terrorism—it seems like a targeted political killing. (4) A rebel group mortars a remote army base, killing soldiers, as part of a campaign to degrade the military's ability to hold territory. I do not call this terrorism; it is an ordinary attack on a military target. (5) The same group leaves a bomb in the family housing area of that base, killing spouses and children of soldiers, and says the purpose is to make troops refuse their postings. I call this terrorism. (6) Activists opposed to fossil fuels burn down an unoccupied car dealership at night after leaving a message that all dealerships selling a certain vehicle will be "treated as targets" until the manufacturer takes it off the market; no one is hurt. I resist calling this terrorism, though I cannot quite say why—it feels like coercive sabotage rather than terrorism. (7) A group takes control of a city's water treatment system and credibly threatens to contaminate the water supply unless a mining company abandons a planned project; no one has been harmed yet. I lean toward calling this terrorism.

The pair that bothers me most is (5) and (6): each threatens a defined audience with further violence to force a policy change, yet I treat one as terrorism and not the other. Is the difference really just that (5) kills people while (6) destroys only property? Does that make (7) attempted terrorism or something else, since no harm has occurred? And does (3) force me to say that a political killing counts as terrorism only when it is meant to coerce a wider audience? Please state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures my verdicts, show how each clause is motivated by specific cases, and tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed—a night-time bombing of an empty government building accompanied by a public ultimatum, and a cyberattack that opens a dam and kills people downstream as a warning to the capital. Then say honestly which of my verdicts would have to be abandoned if no acceptably simple principle delivers all of them.
