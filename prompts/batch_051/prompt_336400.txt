The Hart–Fuller debate is usually framed as a dispute over a single question — whether law and morality are necessarily or conceptually connected — but the longer I read the two sides, the less I believe that is where the disagreement actually lives. Hart grants that law ordinarily serves human needs and that judges face a genuine moral choice when a statute is wicked; Fuller grants that the word “law” can be used in a purely descriptive way for systems that fall short of his internal morality. Both reason from many of the same materials: the grudge-informer cases, the postwar German courts’ treatment of Nazi statutes, and the problem of retroactive criminal law. Yet across the famous Hart–Fuller exchange and its descendants in analytic jurisprudence, the two camps have moved barely at all. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a verbal dispute about how to use the word “law,” a substantive dispute about what judges and citizens ought to do when positive enactments are deeply unjust, a clash between two different projects — a descriptive account of legal institutions versus an account of law’s point or aspiration — or something else altogether, such as a disagreement about which cases count as data? My working hypothesis is that the impasse is really a disagreement about what a theory of law is for, rather than about the extension of the concept in hard cases. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: locate the disagreement in what each side actually says, separate what is merely verbal from what is substantive, and then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis — or explain why progress should not be expected.
