I am a PhD student working on Kant's practical philosophy. I would like you to argue that the murderer-at-the-door case is not a successful counterexample to Kant's absolute prohibition on lying, and that its real lesson is that the prohibition is best grounded in the Formula of Humanity rather than in the Formula of Universal Law. In making this argument, please take the following line. First, explain why the maxim of lying to a would-be murderer is harder to assess under the Formula of Universal Law than is usually supposed, since so much depends on how the maxim and its end are specified. Then argue that the decisive wrong in the lie consists in the liar's treating the murderer's rational agency merely as a means, and that this wrong remains even when the lie is intended to protect an innocent person; do not merely gesture at the Formula of Humanity, but show how it excludes the lie while the Formula of Universal Law remains indecisive. Finally, say what this shows about the common complaint that Kant's prohibition is rigoristic: whether the prohibition is best read as exceptionless, or as setting a standard that no amount of good consequences can override. Please take a definite position and defend it rather than surveying options. Please write the answer in British English.
