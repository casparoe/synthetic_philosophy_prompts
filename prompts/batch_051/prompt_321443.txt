Epistemic practice seems to treat the same move as a virtue or a vice depending on where it is made. Every chain of justification has to stop somewhere: Albert's Münchhausen trilemma says that any attempt at justification ends in infinite regress, circularity, or a halt at a premise that is not itself justified. Now consider the third option. When a foundationalist lets the chain stop at perceptual seemings, memory, or elementary logical rules, and says these are justified non-inferentially or need no further support, the result is treated as respectable — standard epistemology, not a failure of nerve. But when someone lets the chain stop at a revealed text, a charismatic authority, a private moral intuition, or the bare insistence that a proposition is just self-evident, we call it dogmatism and treat it as no justification at all. Structurally, though, the same move has been made: the regress is ended by declining to give further reasons. So is there a principle that really distinguishes the foundational stopping points from the dogmatic ones, or only a psychological and social story about which stopping points we happen to share? I want the strongest candidate principle you can construct, tested against the hard cases that make it uncomfortable: disagreement about what is self-evident; mathematical axioms accepted because they are obvious; perceptual illusions; culturally local common sense; a scientific community's consensus functioning as bedrock; and the testimony of the many versus the testimony of the few. Then give an honest verdict: should the asymmetry be kept, reversed, or given up — and if given up, what should replace it?
