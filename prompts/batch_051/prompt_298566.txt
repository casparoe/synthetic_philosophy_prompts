I've been trying to pin down what I actually think about metrics and when they corrupt the thing they're supposed to measure, going by my gut on cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, covers all of them. I know theres a slogan about measures becoming targets, but I want something more precise than a slogan.

When a bank tracks customer-service reps by average call length, and reps start cutting calls short or transferring the hard ones, so scores improve but customers leave angrier, I think that's clearly bad. Same with a hospital that ranks surgeons by surgical mortality rate, and the older surgeons quietly stop taking the high-risk patients who need them most. A software team that gets a bonus per bug fixed, and developers start shipping sloppy code and then 'fixing' it, or split one bug into five tickets — bad, though I have to admit it's clever. A university that ranks departments by citations per paper, so people form citation cartels and slice one result into three papers — bad. But when my fitness app gives me a badge for ten thousand steps and I pace around the kitchen at 11 p.m. to close the ring, I don't think that's bad at all; the point was to get me moving and I moved. And a language app that tracks streak days, so people do one trivial lesson a day to keep the streak alive — I think that's fine, because the habit of opening the app is itself most of what the streak was for. I also get queasy about paying teachers on test-score pass rates: if the test is well designed, drilling to it just seems like teaching, but if it's narrow it feels like a cheat, and I can't tell which verdict is really mine.

There's a line between the kitchen pacing and the hung-up calls, but I can't state it. It looks like sometimes optimizing the measure destroys the point and sometimes it serves the point, and I want a principle that draws that line. Which of my verdicts would it force me to drop? And what does it say about a case I keep flip-flopping on: an AI lab that screens job candidates by benchmark performance, so everyone in the applicant pool has trained on data that looks suspiciously like the benchmark. I want to say that's bad, but on my own fitness-app logic maybe I should say it's fine.
