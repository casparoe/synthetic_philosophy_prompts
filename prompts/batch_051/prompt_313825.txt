You're going to act as an expert on philosophy of history who has thought hard about the objectivity question. Think carefully, and start by restating the three positions in your own words before you try to map them onto anything — dont just jump to a verdict.

Take the fight over the 1619 Project as your case. Quick recap for anyone who needs it: in 2019 the New York Times Magazine published a series of essays, helmed by Nikole Hannah-Jones, arguing that the United States' real founding should be dated to 1619, when the first enslaved Africans were brought to Virginia, and that a lot of what Americans tell themselves about their history — economic growth, the shape of democracy, even the motivations for the Revolution — is better explained by slavery and its long afterlife. The project won a Pulitzer, got assigned in schools, and also drew a sharp rebuttal from a number of historians, a competing initiative, and a big public argument about whether it was history or advocacy.

The three positions. Ranke held that the historian's job is to reconstruct the past as it actually happened, from the sources, with the historian's own present kept out of the picture. Carr thought that ideal misunderstands what a fact is: facts only become historical facts when a historian selects and arranges them, so history is always written from some standpoint, in a back-and-forth between present concerns and the surviving record. Hayden White went further and said the narrative shape itself — what gets to be the beginning, the middle, the end, whether the story is a tragedy or a progress story — is a literary imposition the historian brings to the material, not something found in the material.

Now do two things, in both directions. First, listen to the episode through the theories: for each of Ranke, Carr, and White, say what that position would have to say about what the 1619 Project was doing, and about what its historian-critics were doing. Be disciplined about the mapping: who or what is the "historian," what counts as the evidence for each side, where exactly does each theory locate the disagreement. Second, test the theories through the episode: which features of this controversy does each theory explain well, which does it misdescribe or have to stretch to handle, and does the whole affair show that objectivity should be kept as a regulative ideal, abandoned as a pretense, or something more complicated. Where a theory is silent, say so instead of forcing it to speak.
