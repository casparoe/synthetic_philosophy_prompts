You are a professional philosopher specializing in formal epistemology, the foundations of probability, and the theory of statistical inference. I am a software engineer without formal philosophical training, and I build Bayesian models in industry. I have written down my working views on how priors should be chosen, and I would like a consistency audit before presenting them to a reading group of colleagues. Please think carefully, and start by brainstorming a few natural readings of each commitment and what each one strictly commits me to. Then work through them pair by pair. I want you to separate genuine tensions from merely apparent ones: some commitments that look incompatible may dissolve once the relevant notions are pinned down. Rank the real tensions by severity, say what the cheapest fix or distinction would be for each, and identify which single revision would do the most to make the whole set cohere. Do not pad the output with weak tensions; press hard on the two or three genuine stress points. Do not give me a reading list or citations; treat the commitments themselves as the object of analysis.

My commitments are as follows.

1. Rational degrees of belief obey the probability axioms and update by Bayesian conditionalization on new evidence.

2. Symmetry demands equal probability. If two possibilities in a prior are related by a symmetry of the setup rather than by any information I actually have, the prior should assign them equal probability.

3. When my prior information can be stated as a constraint, such as a known mean or a known variance, the right prior is the maximum-entropy distribution that satisfies exactly that constraint and nothing more. The reason is that, among the distributions that respect the constraint, it is the one that adds the least information. I am aware that for continuous parameters differential entropy, unlike discrete entropy, is not invariant under reparameterization; I have not worked out whether that undermines this principle.

4. For a continuous parameter about which I have no meaningful information, the right default prior is the Jeffreys prior: the prior proportional to the square root of the Fisher information. I hold this because that rule is invariant under reparameterization, while a flat prior is not, since changing coordinates changes the flat prior.

5. There is no uniquely rational prior in general. Two reasonable modelers with the same evidence can rationally adopt different priors, and at least one of them need not be making a mistake.

6. A prior is, at bottom, a modeling assumption like any other. Its justification comes from how well the resulting model predicts, calibrates, and generalizes, not from a separate principle about ignorance that has epistemic authority over and above downstream performance.

I would like the audit to be precise enough that I can use it to revise what I present to my colleagues.
