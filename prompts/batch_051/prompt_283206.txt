Okay, so I'm in a philosophy of law seminar and we read Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.'s 1899 essay "The Theory of Legal Interpretation," and one passage has been bugging me all week. He writes: "Thereupon we ask, not what this man meant, but what those words would mean in the mouth of a normal speaker of English, using them in the circumstances in which they were used, and it is to the end of answering this last question that we let in evidence as to what the circumstances were." My professor glossed this as Holmes saying that interpretation aims at something objective — the public meaning of the words — rather than at prying into the author's private head. Fine.

But then I started thinking about how judges actually read statutes, and the practice seems to do almost the opposite. A court gets a statute with a phrase that could go narrow or broad, and the opinion immediately digs through a Senate committee report or some floor statement to show what Congress was "trying to do," then reads the statute to fit that goal even when the plain words point the other way. That isn't some fringe maneuver; its just how a lot of opinions are written. So what view of statutory meaning does that practice presuppose? Does it commit the courts to something like a group intention — the idea that a legislature is the kind of thing that can mean something over and above what its words say? Can the committee-report practice be reconciled with Holmes's normal-speaker criterion on a charitable reading, or do the two flatly conflict? And if a legal system really took Holmes's view seriously all the way down, what would have to change about how opinions handle legislative history — would committee reports just become irrelevant, or would they survive as evidence of something else?
