After a reading group on ontology, two of the participants fell into an argument, and afterward both asked me to referee it; I couldn't, so I'm passing it to you. Our reading had pitted two philosophers against each other. One defended the view that, strictly speaking, there are no tables, chairs, or statues — only simples arranged tablewise, chairwise, and statuewise. The other held that composite objects are as real as their parts, and that the sentence "there are no tables" is a drastic revision of our best-confirmed understanding of the world. The argument I need settled is not about which of those two philosophers was right. It is about whether their exchange was a substantive dispute about reality at all, or a merely verbal one.

Here is the back-and-forth as it developed. Amara argued that the original dispute is merely verbal. Each ontologist, she said, can accept every observation, every scientific result, and every ordinary fact the other cites; what differs is only how they use "exists" in sentences like "there are tables." One restricts the domain to simples, the other to the ordinary composite objects of everyday life. That is like two people who ask whether there is any food in the house, one meaning "in the kitchen" and the other "in the whole house": once the restrictions are written out, the disagreement dissolves, leaving only a choice of vocabulary. Theo replied that this misses what the two philosophers actually do. They do not merely use "exists" with different implicit domains; each asserts, in the unrestricted sense, that the other's claim is false. The nihilist denies that there are tables, full stop; the universalist denies that there are only simples, full stop. They understand each other's words perfectly and still disagree about whether composition occurs — whether the simples genuinely add up to a further object. That, Theo said, is a disagreement about the structure of the world, not about language. He added that Amara's own position is unstable, because if she says there is no fact of the matter that makes one of them right, she has taken a substantive stand in the very debate she calls verbal. Amara answered that this meta-level stand is semantic rather than ontological, and pressed him: if the difference is factual, what observation or consideration could discriminate between the two descriptions? Theo said many genuine factual disputes — in fundamental physics and in mathematics — are settled not by direct observation but by which theory is overall simpler, more parsimonious, and more coherent with our other commitments, so the absence of a decisive experiment does not make a dispute verbal. Amara said that answer gives the game away: parsimony and simplicity are properties of our choice of framework, not discoveries about what is in the world, so settling the dispute by them is choosing a language rather than discovering a fact. Theo accused her of conflating the evidence for a claim with what makes it true; framework preferences may be how we choose between theories, but it does not follow that there is no objective fact of the matter about whether composition occurs. They were still circling this point when the session ended.

Please adjudicate the argument as they actually made it: who was right about which specific point, whether Amara's restricted-domain analogy is apt or misleading, whether Theo's appeal to the disputants' unrestricted usage survives her reply, whether her demand for a discriminator proves what she thinks, and where each side mischaracterized the other. Then say who is more right overall and what both of them are missing. Finally, say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration, if made, would lead you to a different verdict, and where your own reasoning about this is most likely to fail.
