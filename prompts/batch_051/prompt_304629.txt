I co-host a small podcast on the ethics of emerging military technology, and next month we are recording an episode with a guest who defends a tracking-and-tracing account of meaningful human control over autonomous weapons. On her view, a weapon system remains under meaningful human control only if two things hold. First, the system must track what the responsible humans care about: it has to respond both to the moral reasons of the people who design and deploy it and to the concrete situation in which it operates. Second, the system's outcomes must be traceable: however the machine behaves, the chain of design and operation should allow each result to be followed back to at least one identifiable human. She argues that this amounts to neither a demand for a human to approve every individual engagement in real time nor a license to field whatever system can be built; it is a design standard for keeping responsibility from evaporating. The episode has become personal for me: my sibling works in sensor support for unmanned platforms, and I have begun to suspect that the familiar assurance that somebody remains in the loop does more to reassure the public than to secure actual control. I therefore want the conversation to test whether this account really distinguishes meaningful human control from mere administrative traceability.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate this position. I would like at least one question on each of four pressure points, and for each one I want the follow-up you would ask if she gives the reply her position most naturally suggests. First, whether the tracking condition can survive a system that learns in deployment and starts selecting targets in ways its designers did not explicitly foresee, provided it stays within constraints set in advance — is a system still responding to human reasons when those reasons have been compressed into parameters and thresholds? Second, whether the tracing condition is a forward-looking condition on control at all, or only a backward-looking condition on accountability, and whether the account would count a system as controlled when its failures are always attributable after the fact but never preventable in the moment. Third, the objection that the account proves too much, since existing point-defense and close-in weapon systems seem to satisfy both conditions even though no human can intervene in real time. Fourth, the objection that it proves too little, since it would rule out systems whose particular operations cannot be traced to a specific human even when their overall use is embedded in careful human oversight. Finally, include one question on whether the account closes the responsibility gap or merely relocates it: if a system satisfies both conditions and still produces a harm for which no one along the chain seems fairly blameworthy, what has been gained? Arrange the questions so that later ones can exploit the concessions she makes in responding to earlier ones.
