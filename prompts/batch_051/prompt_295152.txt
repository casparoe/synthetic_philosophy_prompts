I teach philosophy at a university, and next term I have been asked to give a single fifty-minute guest lecture on cognitive penetration of perception to students in a teacher-training program, most of whom have never taken a philosophy course. I keep coming back to a remark William James makes in the preface to *Talks to Teachers on Psychology*: “I have found by experience that what my hearers seem least to relish is analytical technicality, and what they most care for is concrete practical application. So I have gradually weeded out the former, and left the latter unreduced; and now, that I have at last written out the lectures, they contain a minimum of what is deemed 'scientific' in psychology, and are practical and popular in the extreme.” That remark creates a genuine puzzle for me. The natural way to present cognitive penetration is to begin with a distinction between perception and cognition, then define top-down effects on perceptual experience, and then run through the standard cases. But James suggests exactly this sort of analytical technicality is what teacher-audiences least relish, and that what they need is concrete practical application. Yet if I reverse the order and start with vivid examples, I worry the students will leave with a fuzzy notion that perception is just influenced by what we think, rather than understanding the specific claim that cognitive states can alter perceptual experience itself.

Please plan the session for me. Do not write the lecture itself; give me a teaching plan that respects James’s warning without sacrificing precision. In particular: what idea must be in place before the question of cognitive penetration can even be formulated without confusion; which single case or example should carry the session and why; what misconceptions these teacher-trainees are likely to bring or form—for instance, they may treat attention as an instance of cognitive penetration, or conflate cognitive penetration with the trivial fact that people interpret ambiguous stimuli differently—and where in the sequence I should head each one off; what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and how to signal that; and one final question or short exercise that would tell me whether they have understood the distinction rather than merely remembered the phrase.
