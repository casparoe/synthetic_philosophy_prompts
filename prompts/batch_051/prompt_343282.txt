In my epistemology of testimony course, we have been working through the debate between reductionists and anti-reductionists about testimonial justification, and I want to write a short paper applying that debate to the Salem witch trials. Our anchor text includes this passage from Hume, An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, Section X ("Of Miracles"), Part I:

> To apply these principles to a particular instance; we may observe, that there is no species of reasoning more common, more useful, and even necessary to human life, than that which is derived from the testimony of men, and the reports of eye-witnesses and spectators. This species of reasoning, perhaps, one may deny to be founded on the relation of cause and effect. I shall not dispute about a word. It will be sufficient to observe, that our assurance in any argument of this kind is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony, and of the usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses.

I read this as the core of the reductionist position: trust in testimony is an inference from observed regularity between reports and facts, with no default title to credence. The contrasting position we have discussed, roughly Reid's, is that testimony is a distinct first principle: we are naturally disposed to trust testimony unless and until specific defeaters override that default.

The facts of the episode, as I understand them: in 1692, the Salem court admitted "spectral evidence"—accusers' testimony that the defendant's specter or apparition tormented them—alongside more ordinary eyewitness testimony; nineteen people were hanged and one was pressed to death before the governor disallowed spectral evidence and the trials collapsed.

Instead of a historical narrative, I want a disciplined analysis that runs in both directions. (1) On Hume's principle, what were the magistrates, jurors, and clergy rationally required to do with the spectral and the ordinary testimony, given what Salem's experience could and could not support at the time? (2) On the Reidian alternative, where exactly should the default title to belief have been defeated under the actual circumstances? (3) Which features of the episode does each theory explain or misdescribe, and where is each silent? (4) Does the episode, so analyzed, weigh in favor of either theory, or does it expose limitations in both?
