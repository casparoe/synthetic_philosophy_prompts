I am taking a course this term on the philosophy of time and relativity, and through a friend in computer science I have also been reading about how distributed systems handle time and ordering. I keep returning to a parallel that I cannot decide about, and I would like help determining whether it is deep or merely verbal. The Rietdijk–Putnam argument for the block universe starts from the relativity of simultaneity: two observers in relative motion can disagree about which spacelike-separated events are simultaneous, so there is no privileged global now, and the present cannot be a metaphysically special moment. The conclusion drawn is that the only coherent picture is a four-dimensional block in which all events exist tenselessly. In distributed systems there is an analogous starting point: processes have no shared physical clock, so there is no global wall-clock time against which to order events, and trying to impose a single total order on the basis of local clocks produces causality violations. The standard resolution, Lamport's happened-before relation, gives up total order in favor of a partial order, and events incomparable under that relation are treated as concurrent. So both fields begin from something like the absence of a privileged global now, but they draw very different conclusions: distributed systems stops at a partial order and treats concurrency as real, while the Rietdijk–Putnam argument goes further and says all events are equally real in a static block.

My question is whether this is a genuine structural parallel or just pattern-matching on the idea that there is no global time. If it is genuine, does the distributed-systems outcome suggest that the correct lesson from relativity is only the light-cone partial order, and that the step from no privileged present to eternalism is an extra metaphysical leap? Or is the block universe the proper analogue of the completed event log, with the present being something like an arbitrary cut through that log? I would like a careful mapping of what corresponds to what, an account of what would have to be true on each side for results to transfer, and at least one place where the parallel predicts something false.
