I’ve spent a lot of time in rationalist-adjacent discussions of psychiatry and the philosophy of medicine, and one objection keeps recurring: mental disorder categories are irreducibly value-laden, because the functions whose failures they supposedly track—attention, mood regulation, social cognition—are singled out precisely because we care about them, not because nature provides a value-neutral list. I want to test whether a naturalist can answer that objection under pressure, so I’d like you to play the role of a committed defender.

Please play an unnamed but sophisticated naturalist about disease. Your position is this: a condition is a disease just in case some biological mechanism fails to perform the function that explains why that mechanism exists in the species’ evolutionary history, where functions are fixed by their contribution to survival and reproduction in the environment of selection, not by human preferences; the failure itself is an objective biological fact. You are sharp and empirically literate, willing to concede small points where the evidence genuinely cuts against you, but you will not retreat to normativism, and you will not stretch the word *function* until the view is empty. Stay in character, keep your replies to one or two paragraphs, and answer my opening directly.

My opening: I can grant you that a heart that no longer pumps blood is broken in an objective sense, because its function can be read off the causal role that explains why hearts were selected. But in the mental domain you cannot produce the neutral list of functions your account requires. Attention, mood, and social cognition are not obvious organs with obvious jobs; the capacities psychiatry treats as functions are exactly the capacities whose loss we disvalue. So your naturalism either goes silent just where psychiatry matters, or it gerrymanders a list of evolved functions to match the current diagnostic manual. Tell me, then, what your view says about a persistent low mood that looks like the evolutionarily expected response to persistent defeat: how do you distinguish a broken mood system from a mind doing exactly what it was shaped to do, without smuggling in a value judgment about how a person ought to feel?
