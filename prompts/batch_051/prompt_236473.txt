Write the desiderata that an adequate theory of vagueness must meet, treated as a specification one would fix before choosing between epistemicism and supervaluationism. The purpose of the theory is to serve both as a semantics for vague language and as an account of what competent speakers are doing when they hesitate over a borderline case. I want the full requirements list rather than another defense of one camp. The list should cover the phenomena a theory must handle: clear cases, borderline cases, higher-order borderline cases, penumbral connections (for example, the claim that nothing can be both red and orange even for an object at the border), the forced-march sorites, and the fact that equally competent classifiers can place a sharp cutoff in different places while agreeing that the disputed region is borderline. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it binding. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any theory and which are preferences of a particular camp, such as the preservation of bivalence or the claim that vagueness is semantic indecision rather than ignorance. State which subset of the requirements, if any, you take to be jointly unsatisfiable — I particularly want the tension between retaining all classical inference rules and allowing truth-value gaps made precise, if you think it is real — and spell out the reasoning instead of just asserting the conflict. Finally, briefly score epistemicism and supervaluationism against the list, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates. Define any technical vocabulary you introduce (precisification, supertruth, bivalence, higher-order vagueness, penumbral connection) so the piece is self-contained. Write in American English.
