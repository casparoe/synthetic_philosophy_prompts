I have been thinking hard about the idea that excellence is a final value, and I have come to hold the perfectionist view that developing and exercising demanding human excellences—intellectual, technical, artistic, athletic—is good to some degree in itself, not merely as a means to welfare, status, or downstream benefit. On this view, a world in which someone privately attains and exercises a high standard of skill, deriving no pleasure from it and affecting no one else, is to that extent better than an otherwise identical world containing only mediocrity; I find myself moved by the thought that the best human lives involve serious engagement with standards of excellence even when nothing else comes of it. What I now want is a map of what would count against this view, ordered from what should overturn it down to what should merely force a qualification. Go beyond the obvious cases—pointless achievements, morally repugnant excellence, lucky or forged accomplishments—to the deeper pressure points: whether the view is really about value or about meaning and whether that distinction lets it evade tests a value claim should face, whether the posited value is agent-relative or agent-neutral and what each reading commits me to, how it handles the difference between a reason to admire some excellence and the excellence's being good, and what it predicts that purely welfare-based rivals do not. Distinguish the objections a committed perfectionist would themselves concede count against the position from those they would dismiss as misdirected, and say whether the view has been or will inevitably be qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is a special problem for claims of this kind.
