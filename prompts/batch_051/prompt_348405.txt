I am drafting a novel in which one character, an interpretability researcher, argues in a public debate that mechanistic anomaly detection is a research programme without a genuine object. For the scene to work as more than caricature, I need to know whether her argument is valid and, if it is not, where it first breaks. The argument she gives, as I have drafted it, is this.

(1) To say that an output was produced by a mechanism is to describe the components that causally produced it and the pattern of dependence among those components.

(2) In a fixed, already trained neural network, the units, layers, weights, and their connections are exactly the same from one forward pass to the next.

(3) Since the pattern of dependence among the components that produce an output is fixed by the standing connections and the weights, the mechanism that produces the output must likewise be the same across all forward passes.

(4) Mechanistic anomaly detection, by contrast, is the practice of flagging outputs of a fixed network that are produced by a mechanism other than the one the network uses for standard outputs, even when the outputs themselves look unimpeachable.

(5) If step (3) is correct, then no such output exists: every output of a fixed network is produced by the single mechanism constituted by the network itself.

(6) Therefore, mechanistic anomaly detection either has no real object of study or has silently shifted to a loose, merely behavioural sense of the word mechanism.

Is this argument valid? If not, which numbered step is the first to fail, and what is the most precise diagnosis of the error? If the failing step could be repaired in a way that would preserve the force of the argument, give the repaired step and say whether the argument would then go through.
