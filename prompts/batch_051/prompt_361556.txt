I was talking to a friend of a friend over dinner last week, and he laid out an argument about cheating that I haven't been able to get out of my head. He said that to make sense of sexual exclusivity as a moral requirement, the wrongness of infidelity has to come from somewhere fairly specific, and then he walked through the options like this. (1) It can't be breach of promise, because plenty of couples have never actually promised exclusivity—they just fell into it—and yet the hurt is treated as profound, whereas explicitly promising to water someone's plants and then forgetting doesn't rise to anything like the same kind of wrong. (2) It can't be the risk of disease or pregnancy, since a fully protected affair is still infidelity, and so is a non-penetrative sexual betrayal with no physical risk at all. (3) It can't be jealousy, because people get jealous over their partner's close friendships, jobs, or hobbies, and we generally don't think jealousy gives them a veto over those. (4) So it must be something about sexual intimacy itself: the idea that it is the kind of thing that may only be shared with one person. (5) But if that's true, it's mysterious why the same logic doesn't apply to the other forms of intimacy that are supposed to make sex meaningful—emotional vulnerability, confiding, deep mutual care. Almost nobody thinks a spouse is wronged in the adultery-like way by their partner having an extremely close friend they share everything with. (6) So either sexual exclusivity is just a convention that we've been dressing up in moral language, or we're wildly inconsistent about intimacy in general.

He said it like it was utterly obvious, but I think I want to keep both of the starting intuitions: that a sexual affair is a distinctive kind of wrong, and that a nonsexual best friend is not. I can't see the exit, though. Which step in that argument should I reject, and what do I have to give up to reject it?
