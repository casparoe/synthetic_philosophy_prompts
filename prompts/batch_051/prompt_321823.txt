I'm part of a working group designing a scenario exercise for a graduate seminar in planetary governance, and we've been handed a draft institutional design for governing stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) that I want you to stress-test. The draft is meant to manage three problems at once: the moral hazard whereby the availability of cheaply deployed SAI displaces emissions abatement; the risk of termination shock if deployment stops abruptly; and the question of who decides whether deployment begins and continues. Under the design, deployment above a stated global radiative forcing threshold may begin only on a "triple" authorization: (i) approval by 60 percent of protocol parties, one state one vote; (ii) approval by parties representing 60 percent of world population, weighted as such; and (iii) approval by parties representing 60 percent of average annual fossil CO2 emissions over the preceding five years. Each authorization lapses after four years, after which injection mass is automatically reduced by 10 percent of the originally authorized level per year unless the same triple vote reauthorizes; this pre-scheduled ten-year ramp-down is meant to make termination gradual rather than binary. Any party hosting injection equipment or flights must post a bond scaled to the cost of completing that ten-year ramp-down from the maximum authorized level, and the bond is forfeited if the party halts or interrupts the ramp-down unilaterally. While deployment is active, every party whose emissions exceed a baseline set to its average annual emissions over the five years before the protocol entered into force, ratcheted down 3 percent per year, pays a per-ton levy, split evenly between adaptation in the most climate-vulnerable parties and verified carbon dioxide removal. Finally, claims for compensation for climate losses, whether from background warming or from SAI side effects, are decided by an independent technical panel under pre-agreed detection-and-attribution rules, not by Council vote.

I want to know how this machine would actually behave once the relevant actors — great powers, petrostates, small-island coalitions, heavily emitting middle powers, private aircraft operators and aerosol suppliers, donor governments, carbon-removal developers, and the panel's own experts — treat it as something to be worked rather than respected. I have two standing suspicions: that the four-year reauthorization vote becomes a permanent hostage situation in which any blocking coalition can extort concessions by threatening to let the scheduled ramp-down proceed, and that the supposedly subordinate compensation panel becomes the real decision-maker by signaling which deployments count as compensable harm. But I want the full map, not just those two worries, and I want the analysis kept at the level of mechanism rather than overall desirability.

Please respond as a structured memo with exactly these sections, in order: (1) "Actors and baseline interests" (roughly 150 words): who the design empowers and who it irritates before anyone starts gaming it. (2) "First-order incentives" (roughly 250 words): how the triple vote, the sunset-and-ramp-down, the bond, the levy, and the compensation panel each pull actors. (3) "Gaming and failure modes" (roughly 300 words): concrete strategies actors could use to exploit the design, including but moving beyond my two hypotheses. (4) "Termination-shock dynamics" (roughly 200 words): how the formal ramp-down schedule actually behaves under threats of noncompliance, withdrawal, and precipitate halting. (5) "Second-order effects" (roughly 200 words): likely effects on mitigation, carbon removal, climate diplomacy, and the broader institutional landscape. (6) "Can it be fixed?" (roughly 200 words): which failure modes could be corrected by amendable changes without gutting the design's three aims, and which look baked in. Do not conclude with a recommendation on whether the protocol should be adopted; keep the evaluation internal to the mechanism.
