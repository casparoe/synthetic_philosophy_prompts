I've been thinking about a strange evaluative asymmetry in our judgments about pity. When someone is moved to pity by another person's suffering—say, a passerby is visibly affected by a displaced family on the news, or stops to give money to someone begging on the street—we tend to speak well of them. We call pity a humane impulse, a sign of moral sensitivity, sometimes even a credential of good character. Yet when someone is the object of pity, the same word turns sour. To learn that others pity you is humiliating, and few protests are as common as "I don't want your pity." We praise the very attitude whose receipt we treat as an insult. On its face that looks like a contradiction in our emotional vocabulary, and I want to know whether it is anything more.

The tension is an old one. Schopenhauer made compassion—understood roughly as what we call pity—the foundation of all morally significant conduct: on his account, I can be moved to relieve your suffering only because I participate in it so immediately that, for the moment, it becomes a motive for me as directly as my own suffering would. Nietzsche attacked exactly this picture. He suspected that what presents itself as selfless participation is often a disguised satisfaction in looking down: the pitier is elevated, made stronger or more fortunate by comparison, and pity distributes status at least as much as it shares suffering. If anything like Nietzsche's diagnosis is right, the asymmetry would be no mystery—we praise pity because we imagine ourselves as the giver, and resent it because we intuit that the giver's compassion carries a verdict on our condition.

What I am asking for is the strongest defense one can give of the asymmetry without buying all of Nietzsche's psychological story. Can someone coherently hold that pity is a genuine virtue in the person who feels it and simultaneously hold that the person pitied is right to recoil from being its object? What exactly would the two judgments be tracking—the difference between having an altruistic motive and having that motive publicly directed at one's weakness, the difference between how an act looks from the inside and how it looks from the outside, or something else? Whatever principle you propose, test it against nearby cases: pity offered anonymously, pity coming from someone who has suffered exactly the same thing and genuinely knows the recipient's position, and the person who says "I don't want your pity, but I would accept your solidarity." Then give an honest verdict: should the asymmetry be kept as it stands, reversed, or given up?
