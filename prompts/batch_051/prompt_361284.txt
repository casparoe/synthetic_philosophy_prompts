I direct grantmaking at a private animal-welfare foundation and hold a version of lexical threshold negative utilitarianism: below a threshold of suffering intensity, pleasures and lesser pains are commensurable and may be traded off, but above that threshold a single individual's suffering is lexically prior, so no quantity of milder pleasure or lesser pain can compensate for allowing one such episode. Our trustees want a written procedure, usable by non-philosophers at monthly meetings allocating between tens of thousands and several hundred thousand dollars, for evaluating interventions that trade off preventing intense suffering against producing moderate positive welfare or causing moderate discomfort as a side effect. Please turn the view into a step-by-step procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure returns a 'no' and where it hands the decision to a human because the step encodes a value judgment rather than a fact, and how to handle uncertainty about whether an expected harm crosses the threshold and about where the threshold lies. Make the theoretical commitments explicit: whether many sub-threshold harms may aggregate so that together they cross the threshold, whether the lexical priority attaches within a single life or across lives, and how to treat low-probability but very severe expected suffering. Then run the procedure on two proposals: (1) long-term environmental enrichment that modestly improves the lives of a very large number of animals, and (2) a veterinary intervention that eliminates episodes of acute pain in a small number of animals but causes brief, mild handling stress in many more. State which parts of the view the procedure has to simplify and what it cannot settle, and conclude with one case where the procedure yields an answer I will probably not like.
