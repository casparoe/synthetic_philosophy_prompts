I'd like a piece of sustained deadpan satire, roughly 650 words, in the form of a letter from a fictional government Behavioural Insights Unit to a resident who has just been silently defaulted into a new programme. You can choose the programme, though a retirement-savings scheme or an organ-donor registry would be natural; either way, the unit is writing only because a regulation requires it to notify people after they have been enrolled. The letter should be written in the cheerful, plain-English-but-slightly-evasive style such units favour, and it should spend the whole time reassuring the recipient that nothing has been decided for them, that opting out is always available, and that their freedom of choice has been fully preserved. The satire should work by having the details — the opt-out procedure, the deadlines, the framing, the choice architecture described as mere convenience — quietly demonstrate that the choice is not nearly as free as the letter insists.

The target is the practice of "libertarian paternalism" and the claim that influencing people through defaults and framing is unobjectionable because no option is forbidden and everyone may choose otherwise. The jokes should come from getting the actual mechanics of that position right: status quo bias, the asymmetry between opt-in and opt-out, the idea that a nudge should improve welfare as judged by the people being nudged, the fact that a designed default is itself a decision someone had to make, and the way seemingly trivial opt-out costs such as a form, a phone call, or a short window of time do real work. A specialist in the nudge literature should recognize that the author understands what is being satirized and finds the satire fair rather than a caricature, while a reader who has never heard the phrase "choice architecture" should still feel that they are being managed. Stay in the voice of the unit throughout, never break character, and let the reader infer what is being ironized without winking or editorialising. Please write continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — just paragraphs, the way an actual letter would read.
