I teach a seminar on art and political life, and in the Du Bois unit I've found myself drifting toward sympathy with the line I associate with him: roughly, that there is no neutral art for art's sake, that every artwork does political work whether its maker admits it or not, and that the real question is never whether art is propaganda but which interests it serves, wittingly or not. The class is a mix of philosophy majors and studio art students, so the studio kids keep pressing on cases they think are apolitical, and the majors keep wanting a tidy thesis statement. Last week a sharp undergrad asked me what I would have to see to give the view up, and I realized I had no real answer — I could feel myself defending it by quietly narrowing what it meant each time it was pressed.

So I'd like the thing mapped as honestly as if it were a view in metaphysics I didn't hold. What would count against the Du Bois line? I don't mean the easy stuff (Stalinist posters are bad, lots of art is decorative). I mean: which cases of art that seems to refuse propaganda would actually embarrass the view, which internal tensions matter, and does the position split into two claims — a descriptive one about how art functions socially, and a normative one about what artists in unequal societies ought to do — with different exposure? A committed defender will concede some things; which ones? Then rank the defeaters from fatal to merely akward, and tell me whether the view as I've actually stated it (not a straw version) risks anything, or whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, is that a problem for a position of this kind, or just what it means to hold a view in aesthetics?
