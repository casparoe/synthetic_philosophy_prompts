Act as a philosopher of death who also designs behavioral experiments. Take your time, brainstorm two or three candidate study designs, then commit to the one you'd actually run. I'm working through a conceptual claim: the Epicurean line that death cannot be bad for the person who dies, because a person can be harmed only while they exist, and when death arrives the person no longer exists. The standard reply is the deprivation account — death is bad because it deprives the person of the life they would otherwise have had. I don't need a recap of that dialectic. I want to know whether any piece of it can be brought into contact with evidence. Design a concrete study (behavioral, survey-based, longitudinal, whatever) that would distinguish the claim "death harms the one who dies" from the claim "death harms only the survivors, while dying as a process may harm the dying." Keep straight the process of dying, the event of death, and the state of being dead. Say what pattern of results would count in favor of the deprivation account and what would count against it. Then be explicit about what even a decisive-looking result would not settle — for example, whether a harm must be experienced, whether losses to a nonexistent subject count, and how far you're measuring people's attitudes rather than the badness itself. Seperate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of death, and don't present a contested view as consensus.
