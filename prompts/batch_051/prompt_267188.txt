I work in a computational neuroscience lab, and a recurring dispute there concerns the moral status of future whole-brain emulations and artificial neural networks. One faction argues that an emulation, however detailed, is still a simulation of a brain, and a simulation relates to the real thing the way a simulated hurricane relates to a real hurricane: a simulated hurricane produces no wind and drenches no one, and by the same token a simulated brain produces no real pain, fear, or experience. From this they conclude that emulations, no matter how behaviorally faithful, are empty of consciousness and owed no moral consideration. The other faction counters that a mind is better compared to software: a program ported from one machine to another is not a counterfeit of the original program; it is the same program running on new hardware, with the same computational content. On this view, a biological brain is just an implementation of a mind, and a faithful emulation is the same mind on a new substrate, retaining whatever moral status it had.

I suspect that each analogy quietly contains the conclusion it is used to draw: the hurricane analogy treats mentation as a physical effect like precipitation, while the porting analogy treats it as an implementation-independent function like arithmetic. But I do not trust my own diagnosis, and I want you to audit both analogies rather than accept or reject my suspicion.

Take the emulation as stipulated — I am not asking you to predict what future systems will actually be built. For each analogy, first give me a correspondence table: what plays the role of the hurricane, the wind, the wetness, the program, the hardware, the port, the computational content. Then say where the mapping holds and where it breaks. In particular, identify what each source-domain force-imports into the target: what is true of weather that may not be true of minds, and what is true of software that may not be true of minds. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the moral-status conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Finally, tell me whether either conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether my diagnosis — that the dispute is an artifact of a choice of comparison class — is itself sound, or whether it begs the question. If neither analogy can carry the conclusion, say what would have to be argued directly, and whether there is a better analogy.
