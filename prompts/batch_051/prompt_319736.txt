I write a science-and-ideas column and I'm doing a piece on the March 2023 run on Silicon Valley Bank. Treat it as a case study in information cascades and rational herding — the account where late decision-makers rationally imitate earlier actions because those actions may encode more information than whatever each person privately knows. I already know the standard story about the balance sheet and the regulators, so don't recap it. Give me a tight mapping instead: who is the late decision-maker, what action is being copied, what private information is supposedly being swamped, and does the model imply that an individual depositor should have pulled money even while privately doubting the bank was insolvent? Then turn the lens around: the run was driven by VCs and founders publicly urging portfolio companies to withdraw, through dense networks and group chats, with the balance-sheet facts largely public from the start. Where does the anonymous, sequential imitation story strain or outright misdescribe what happened, and how much of the run is better read as a coordination switch or self-fullfilling prophecy than a cascade? Finish by saying what SVB actually shows about rational herding: confirm, refute, or partly both — and defend your verdict.
