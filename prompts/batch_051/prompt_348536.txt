I'm taking a course on 19th-century political thought and we just went from On Liberty to Mill's Considerations on Representative Government, and I'm having trouble keeping the two Mills together. In Chapter 18, Of the Government of Dependencies by a Free State, he lays out the view, if I'm reading him right, that despotic rule over a dependency is legitimate when the subject people are not yet, in his categories, advanced enough for representative institutions, so long as the despotism aims at improving them rather than benefiting the rulers. He presents this as following from his whole developmental picture: representative government is the ideal for mature peoples, but the test of government is how well it cultivates capacities, and for some people the cultivation requires an illiberal transitional stage. This isn't just an abstract aside for him either — he worked for the East India Company and defended British rule in India in roughly those terms.

On one side, I find the underlying theory genuinely compelling. Government exists to develop people's capacities; his claims about progress and self-government hang together systematically and give a unified answer to why children, for example, are not entitled to the same liberty as adults. On the other side, my intution is pretty stubborn: a foreign power cannot legitimately decide that another people is too backward to rule itself and then rule them despotically for their own good. That feels less like a conclusion than a rationalization of colonial power, and I can't shake it even though the theory is elegant.

I don't want a "Mill was a racist" dismissal or a "man of his time" excuse. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be revised or abandoned, and whether there's a reading on which Mill's developmental liberalism and my intuition are actually compatible — not just verbally but in what they commit you to. Walk me through the costs of each option.
