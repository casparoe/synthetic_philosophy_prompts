Not sure if this is a proper distinction or just me re-inventing something. I've been trying to sort out consequentialist cluelessness, and I've come to use two labels. Standard cluelessness says: for nearly any act, once you follow the causal ripples far enough, the branches multiply and your expected-value calculation becomes guesswork. Fair enough.

My proposed distinction is between what I call front-loaded and back-loaded cluelessness. Front-loaded cluelessness is when the first-order effects are themselves murky but the act sits inside a familiar class with known baselines. E.g., I don't know exactly what my donation to a charity does, but I know something about how donations of that type usually behave; my uncertainty is smooth. Back-loaded cluelessness is different: the act only matters if it crosses some threshold, and I mostly don't know where the threshold is. Examples: mailing one extra leaflet in a marginal election, or adding one unit to a slow-building climate campaign. Below the threshold the act is wasted; above it the act is huge. The uncertainty is not about the shape of the effect but about my position relative to a trigger.

Here's the bit I want checked. I think much of the cluelessness literature effectively assumes front-loaded cluelessness, even in cases that are really back-loaded, and this makes the standard responses (robust satisficing, heuristics, sensitivity testing) less helpful than they look. I also suspect back-loaded cluelessness is better handled with threshold-uncertainty tools than with the usual cluelessness remedies. Is this a genuine distinction or a false binary? Has someone already made this point under another name? Give me the three strongest objections and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable.
