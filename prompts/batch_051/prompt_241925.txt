For a history class I have been reading the Westminster Confession of Faith, and I got stuck on a sentence in chapter 1, section 8, where it says:

"But because these original tongues are not known to all the people of God who have right unto, and interest in, the Scriptures, and are commanded, in the fear of God, to read and search them, therefore they are to be translated into the vulgar language of every nation unto which they come, that the Word of God dwelling plentifully in all, they may worship him in an acceptable manner; and, through patience and comfort of the Scriptures, may have hope."

I know the same confession later teaches that God has instituted the only acceptable manner of worshipping him and has limited it to what his revealed will prescribes, so that any way of worshipping him not prescribed in holy Scripture is forbidden. But I do not understand how these two statements fit together. Does the passage about translating Scripture mean that a worship service is acceptable to God only if the Bible is actually read to the people in their own language, since otherwise they cannot worship in an acceptable manner? If so, does that commit me to the broader principle that every part of a service — songs, prayers, musical instruments, the order of elements — is forbidden unless the Bible explicitly commands it? That seems too strong, because then a prayer spoken in a language the congregation does not understand would itself be a forbidden act of worship, and so would uninspired hymns. But the confession seems to say that translation is needed precisely so that worship may be acceptable, as if worship without access to Scripture in one's own language is defective. Is the second half of the sentence an application of the regulative principle, or is it making a different point about Scripture and worship? I cannot tell how much the passage is supposed to prove.
