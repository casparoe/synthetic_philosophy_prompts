I've spent twenty years teaching epistemology and I have a habit I'd genuinely like pressure-tested. When a first-year or a colleague wants to argue about whether the external world exists, or whether other people have minds, or whether the past actually happened, I don't give arguments. I treat these as hinges — the sort of thing Wittgenstein is on about in On Certainty — and I say, quite cheerfully, that they aren't claims we have evidence for but things on which our whole practices of evidence and doubt already lean. Then I move on. Over the years the habit has bled into much less comfortable territory. I'll catch myself treating some quite contestable thesis as a hinge because reopening it would wreck the seminar, or because I suspect the person raising it hasn't done the reading. My question is whether this habit is actually defensible, or whether I've built myself a machine for never facing the hardest questions and teaching my students to do the same. How would someone on the inside tell a geniune hinge from a belief they're merely shielding from scrutiny? And is there a version of my rule that keeps the good part — refusing to pretend every doubt deserves equal airtime — without the seminar becoming a place where hard questions get sneered at? Does it matter that I'm doing this as a teacher rather than as someone just trying to form his own beliefs? I'd like the answer in continuous prose, as an email to a colleague would be — no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables.
