I'm teaching a philosophy of science seminar and want to pair a formal idea with Stanisław Lem's Solaris, and I'd like a careful reading rather than a plot summary. The formal idea, as I understand it (correct me if I'm off), is ontology identification: a scientist normally works inside a fixed ontology — a ready-made list of kinds, states, and relations — and tries to figure out which particular configuration is actual. Ordinary underdetermination says the data might fit more than one configuration. Ontology identification is the deeper problem of figuring out whether you've got the right list of kinds at all, because the observations themselves were classified using you're current list. Inaccessible information would then be whatever facts can't be recovered under any observation strategy, because the instruments and categories you use are themselves part of the world being measured, not outside it.

Read Solaris through that. The planet-ocean has generated a discipline, solaristics, with a enormous literature and still no settled answer to what the ocean is — one organism, many minds, a language, something godlike, or just physics. And the visitors, including Kelvin's wife Rheya reconstructed from his memories, make it stranger: the only "contact" anyone gets is apparently built out of the observer's own private history, so it's hard to say where the ocean's message ends and self-projection begins. I want the reading to treat both sides seriously: where the novel genuinely shows an ontology-identification failure rather than just ordinary underdetermination, and where it resists or complicates the formal picture — maybe Kelvin's problem at the end is ethical or existential rather than a question of convergence to the truth, and maybe "information" is the wrong concept because it already presumes a shared ontology the ocean doesn't owe us. Also: what does the novel add to the philosophical debate that the formal models leave out? My students will mostly know the films, if that, so assume only broad plot knowledge and don't rely on exact scenes or direct quotation.
