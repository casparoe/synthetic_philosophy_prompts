I am taking a course in the philosophy of classification, and we have been discussing conceptual engineering and ameliorative analysis: the idea that concepts can be deliberately revised—fixed or replaced—rather than merely analysed as they are. I keep thinking that psychiatric classification is already doing this, though it does not exactly advertise it. The main diagnostic manuals in psychiatry are revised periodically, and many of the revisions do not look like straightforward empirical discoveries. Thresholds shift so that the same symptoms count as a disorder in one edition but not the next; categories are split, merged, or relabelled in ways that track reimbursement, treatment access, and stigma as much as new evidence; and some conditions have been demoted or reframed after the practical costs of classifying people that way were judged too high. At the same time, these manuals present themselves as neutral, evidence-driven catalogues of disorders that exist independently of the classification. What theory of psychiatric concepts does this practice actually presuppose? Is it treating disorder categories as natural kinds to be discovered, as tools to be engineered for clinical and social purposes, or as some hybrid? Are the different parts of the practice—the revision procedures, the official self-description, and the clinical use of the categories—philosophically consistent with one another, or does each presuppose something different? And if one fully accepted the engineering picture, what would have to change in how revisions are justified, how the categories are taught, and how the manuals describe themselves?
