I'm a retired ornithologist and I've recently gotten deep into philosophy of mind, mostly the animal cognition side of it. Could you act as a philosopher of cognitive science and think this through with me carefully? If you think my question is badly posed, say so before you try to answer it.

One summer, at my old field cabin, I habituated a pair of ravens and built a little puzzle box for them. The lid was held shut by a sliding bolt; you had to slide the bolt sideways, then flip the lid up, to get the food. They watched me work it twice, and inside an hour one of them walked up, slid the bolt, and flipped the lid. My old training kicked in immediately: this is Lloyd Morgan's canon territory — don't reach for a fancier psychological process when a leaner one will do. Maybe it's a chained sequence of reinforced movements, or at most a learned association between bolt-sliding and food becoming available.

But lately I've started to suspect the canon smuggles in a preference and then calls it parsimony. So here's what I'd like your help with. Suppose I wanted to show, not just assert, that the ravens have something causal in mind — like the bolt blocks the lid — rather than merely having the right associations at some level of abstraction. What experiments should I run? Please brainstorm a few concrete designs (probe trials, transfer tasks, whatever a thoughtful experimental psychologist would do). For each, tell me what the birds would do if the causal story is right and what they'd do if the associative story is right. Then be blunt about which part of the dispute no experiment can get at, and wether that says more about the limits of the methods or about the concepts themselves.

Let me add the thing I'm really stuck on. I once sketched a degraded-mechanism transfer: give the same birds a box where the bolt still slides around but no longer blocks anything. If they stop bothering with the bolt when it's causally irrelevant, that smells like understanding. But couldn't an associationist just say the birds noticed the bolt no longer predicts food and stopped? It seems like "having the right associations at the right level of abstraction" can be stretched to cover any behavior whatsoever. Is that unfalsifiability real, or am I just being unimaginative about experiments? And am I right that the canon runs together two different gradients — one about lower versus higher psychological processes, and another about simpler versus more complex models — when those aren't the same thing at all?
