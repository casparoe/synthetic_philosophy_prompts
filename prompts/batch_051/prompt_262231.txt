I am a philosophy PhD student preparing a discussion exercise for an undergraduate unit on population axiology. Please write a pantoum about Parfit's repugnant conclusion. In the opening quatrain, describe a world of ten billion people with rich, flourishing lives; call this world A. In each successive quatrain, advance one step along the chain of the mere addition paradox: add an equal or larger group of lives that are still worth living but slightly less good than the last, so that total welfare rises while the average quality of each life falls. The pantoum's interlocking repetitions should do the philosophical work — the second and fourth lines of one quatrain become the first and third lines of the next, so the reader can never leave the earlier, better world behind, and the first line should return as the poem's final line, now heard in the context of a world of hundreds of billions of lives barely worth living. Do not name Parfit, and avoid the labels repugnant conclusion and mere addition paradox, in the poem itself. A population ethicist should recognize both the total view and the person-affecting resistance to it; a reader coming to it cold should still find it a good poem.
