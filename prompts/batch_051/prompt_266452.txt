I recently served on a working group that drafted ethical guidelines for a philanthropic foundation, and one sentence from our deliberations has been bothering me ever since. We were deciding whether a trustee who systematically favors projects in his own city over more effective projects elsewhere should be recused for bias. He defended the local preference as one legitimate moral preference among others. Another member answered: "A bias is just a preference you don't happen to share," and concluded that our guidelines could not mark any trustee's preference as biased without simply imposing the majority's tastes on a dissenter. The final guideline papered over the issue instead of resolving it.

I am not asking you to adjudicate the foundation's policy. I want the slogan itself dismantled. Treat "a bias is just a preference you don't happen to share" as a compressed philosophical thesis, and work out what it could actually be asserting. Separate the distinct precise claims available to it — for instance, that biases and preferences are the same kind of psychological state; that the two words differ only in evaluative connotation; that no judgment can be called biased without an independent standard that preferences lack; that a bias is a preference exercised in a domain that requires impartiality; that a preference becomes a bias when it rests on defectively weighted reasons. For each claim, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false. Then commit: state which precise claim is the strongest defensible reading of the sentence, whether that reading is strong enough to secure the colleague's conclusion about the recusal guideline, and what further premise would be needed to get from the slogan to that conclusion. I want a definite, reasoned position, not a menu of options left for me to choose from.
