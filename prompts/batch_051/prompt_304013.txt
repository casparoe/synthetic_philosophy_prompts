Give me a working definition of "person." A healthy adult human and a three-day-old infant are clearly persons; a stone and a human corpse are clearly not. A corporation, a chimpanzee, and a split-brain patient whose two hemispheres seem to pursue independent goals are the harder cases: I lean toward treating the chimpanzee as a person for some purposes but not others, I take the corporation to be a legal fiction, and I have no settled view about the split-brain patient. Now the case that raises the stakes. On orthodox Trinitarian doctrine, the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are three persons and one God: the Father and the Son are not the same person, yet they are the same God, and the doctrine denies that there are three Gods. Your definition should not, just in virtue of its form, make those three persons into three Gods; but it also should not shrink "person" into something like "a role the one God plays toward us" merely to dodge that result, unless you will defend that shrinking as the right account of personhood. Propose the definition, run it against every case, note where it misclassifies or goes silent, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. Answer in exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: item 1 states the definition; items 2–4 apply it to the cases (grouping is fine, but every case must be classified); item 5 identifies the verdict you are least confident in and either revises the definition or defends that verdict.
