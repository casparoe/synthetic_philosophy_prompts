In a discussion group I attend, two people got locked in a debate about the decision-theoretic puzzle sometimes called counterfactual mugging, and I would like help adjudicating it.

The scenario they were using is this. Omega, a perfectly reliable predictor, flips a fair coin. If the coin comes up heads, Omega gives you $10,000, but only on the condition that Omega predicted you would pay $100 in the case where the coin came up tails. If the coin comes up tails, Omega tells you that the coin came up tails, explains the setup, and asks you to pay $100; if you pay, you lose $100, and if you refuse, you lose nothing. You are asked to pay only in the tails branch.

The dispute was about whether a person in the tails branch should pay.

Alice said: "You should pay. Before the flip, the policy of paying in tails has an expected value of $4,950: with probability one-half the coin is heads and you receive $10,000, and with probability one-half the coin is tails and you lose $100. The policy of refusing in tails has an expected value of $0. Since 'pay in tails' is the policy you would have wanted to precommit to before the flip, you should carry it out."

Bob replied: "That average runs together two branches, only one of which is actual and only one of which you are in. Once Omega asks you to pay, you know the coin came up tails. Given that information, paying has an expected value of minus $100 and refusing has an expected value of $0. You cannot cause the coin to have been heads, and you cannot cause the heads payout to be delivered after the fact. So a decision procedure that conditions on what you already know says to refuse. Precommitment only matters when carrying it out affects what happens in the world where the act occurs; here, it does not."

Alice responded: "But which policy your decision procedure instantiates is exactly what Omega used to set up the heads branch. If your procedure refuses when told the coin came up tails, Omega predicted that, and in heads runs it paid nothing. If your procedure pays in tails, Omega predicted that, and in heads runs it pays $10,000. Across many runs of the scenario, the paying procedure comes out ahead by about $4,950 per run. A decision theory that calls that procedure irrational is leaving money behind in the very branch where the money appears."

Bob responded: "The person in the heads branch is not me, and no choice I make here can put that branch's money into my pocket. You are asking me to pay $100 to secure a purely counterfactual fact about myself — that I am the sort of person who would have paid — while the actual causal consequences of paying are all negative. This is not like Newcomb's problem, where my choice is at least informative about an opaque box whose contents I do not know; here the coin and the options are already fully known, so the choice carries no new information and changes no actual outcome."

Alice answered: "The scenario is deliberately not about information. It is about which policy is best from the standpoint at which the prediction is made. If you let yourself update on 'the coin came up tails' before choosing, you turn a single choice of policy into a choice that is guaranteed to look losing in tails and thereby guarantees losing in heads as well. The right question is which procedure you should be, not which act looks best after the evidence is in."

At that point neither would budge. Their exchange raises a puzzle I have not been able to resolve: does the rationality of paying turn on whether expected utility is computed before or after learning the coin, and is that difference tied to what an agent should precommit to? I do not want to bias the verdict, so I will not say which of them I lean toward.

Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: for each specific claim Alice or Bob makes, say who is right and under what assumptions; identify where the disagreement is substantive and where it is merely terminological; and then give your own assessment of which position is stronger overall, stating the decision-theoretic commitments that assessment relies on. Please write your answer in American English.
