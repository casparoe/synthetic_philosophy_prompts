I keep noticing a parallel I can't quite settle. In the epistemic injustice literature people mostly distinguish two failure modes: testimonial injustice, where a hearer discounts a speakers word because of an identity prejudice, and hermeneutical injustice, where a gap in the shared conceptual resources leaves someone unable to make sense of their own experience. At work I build systems that ingest reports from the public, and I've started seeing the same two failure modes. A report either gets through but is discounted — the model or a rule assigns the sender low credibility, the way a spam filter disbelieves certain senders — or it never gets represented at all, because the schema has no field for that kind of thing. The second one isn't a rejection; it's an inability to record the report. It struck me that the first is structurally testimonial injustice and the second structurally hermeneutical injustice. Is that a real isomorphism, or am I just pattern-matching on 'two ways a message can fail'? I can see the obvious difference that a database has no interests and no power, but I can't tell wether that undermines the structural claim or is just the point where the analogy stops paying rent. What would have to be true of both sides for lessons to transfer, and would anything from the software side — auditing false-negative rates by group, widening the schema, adding fields — actually illuminate the epistemic side? And what does the parallel predict that then turns out false?
