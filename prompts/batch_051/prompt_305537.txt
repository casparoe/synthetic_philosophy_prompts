I've been taking a modern European history course and also falling down the rabbit hole of old WWI historiography, and I can't shake the feeling that this argument has been going in a circle since the 1960s. The Fischer controversy, I mean. One side keeps saying the documents show Berlin deliberately worked to turn the July crisis into a continental war, and they point to the blank cheque it gave Austria-Hungary in early July 1914, and to the September Programme, the statement of German war aims drafted a few weeks later that reads like a plan for hegemony. The other side says the documents show a system of alliances sliding into war, and they point to the Russian general mobilization and France's refusal to restrain its ally, and they accuse the Fischer side of reading the war backwards from its outcome and hunting for a single guilty nation-state in a way that's anachronistic. What gets me is that both camps have been poring over the same telegrams and memoranda for decades now, with more archives digitized every year, and nobody seems to change their mind.

My working theory is that this isn't really a dispute about the documents, or at least not mostly. I suspect the phrase responsible for the war is doing three jobs at once: a causal question about which decisions actually made war more likely, a counterfactual question about whether the war would have happened anyway without this or that move, and a moral question about whether a whole state can be blamed like a person. The two camps are answering different ones of these an then talking past each other. Is that the right diagnosis of why the debate has stalled? And if so, what would progress look like: a distinction that splits the question, an agreed way of doing counterfactuals, or just admitting that collective blame is a moral judgment historians can't adjudicate? If I'm wrong and there is a genuine archival disagreement still doing the work, tell me what it is and what evidence would settle it.
