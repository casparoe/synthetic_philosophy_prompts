so here's the thing. there's a practice that shows up at two very different scales and i want to know what picture of the self, autonomy, and rationality it quietly commits you to.

at the personal scale: advance directives / Ulysses-style pacts. someone writes down, while well, that if they later refuse treatment during a manic or psychotic episode the refusal should be overridden. the later person is scared and wants to leave; the earlier person's instruction wins. at the political scale: constitutional precommitment. a constitution is hard to amend—supermajorities, separate ratification steps, maybe some clauses that can't be amended at all—and a current majority can't pass a law the bill of rights forbids, even if they really want to. the dead (or at least absent) majority binds the living majority, on purpose.

the naive reading is that both presuppose a divided self: the present, competent self is the 'real' one and the future self is cognitively compromised or akratic, so it's fine for the former to bind the latter. that's also the obvious rationalist read—it's just self-binding against hyperbolic discounting, like deleting the app. but i'm not sure the political case actually fits that reading. a present electorate isn't obviously 'more you' than a future one, and a constitutional amendment is usually not made in a panic. so what does the political practice presuppose about whose preferences count, about autonomy, about whether a person (or a people) is one thing over time? does it presuppose that some preferences are more authentically yours than others, that you can be wrong about your own good, or just that you can rationally bind yourself against predictable future preference-change—and which of those are actually required to make the practice coherent vs. merely compatable with it?

also poke at whether the personal and political cases presuppose the same thing or just look similar. maybe the political version is really about making cooperation possible by removing some moves from the game, and doesn't need any story about a divided self at all.
