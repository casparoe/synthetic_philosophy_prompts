I'm on a deadline for a piece about why we let old crimes go, and a philosopher I interviewed handed me an argument that statutes of limitations are morally indefensible, and I can't shake it. It goes like this. (1) What it is about an act that makes it wrong — the harm, the intention, what the agent knew — is fixed at the time of the act. (2) Things that happen later can't change those earlier facts; a crime doesn't become less intentional thirty years on. (3) So the degree of blameworthiness of the act is exactly the same at year thirty as it was at year one; blame doesn't decay just because the calender moved. (4) A just legal system should make the availability and severity of punishment match the blameworthiness of the act. (5) So the state should be no more willing to let a thirty-year-old crime go unpunished than a thirty-day-old one. (6) Statutes of limitations let people escape punishment precisely because of how much time has passed. (7) Therefore statutes of limitations are unjust. I'm half-convinced there's a sleight of hand in here, maybe around step (4), but I can't say exactly what's wrong. Tell me: is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what would the corrected version of that step have to say? And if it is valid, how should someone who trusts the argument think about the fact that basically every legal system has limitation periods? Write it in continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold, like an email.
