I am currently taking an undergraduate philosophy of biology course, and after a seminar on natural selection I stayed behind and listened to two classmates argue about the old charge that the theory of natural selection is a tautology. I have been asked to write up my understanding of the argument for our study group, but I honestly could not referee it at the time, and I would like you to adjudicate the dispute point by point. Here is how the argument went, as best I can reconstruct it.

Maya began by saying that the tautology charge rests on a crude reading of fitness. If fitness is equated with actual survival and reproductive success, then the slogan that the fittest survive is obviously circular, but evolutionary theory need not define fitness that way. On the propensity interpretation, fitness is an organism's expected reproductive success, a probabilistic disposition fixed by its traits and its environment. Actual success is only evidence of fitness, not the same thing as fitness. Once fitness and actual success are separated, the claim that organisms with higher expected reproductive success will tend to leave more descendants becomes a substantive empirical claim, one that could be false for any particular individual and must be checked against the world.

Jonas replied that this only relocates the problem. He asked where the expected value comes from. If the expectation is estimated from the realized reproductive success of the type, then the type is being labelled fitter because it actually reproduced more, and the principle again says little more than that the type that reproduced more reproduced more. If, instead, the expectation is computed from the organism's traits, then fitness means something like having traits that tend to promote survival and reproduction, and the claim that organisms with survival-promoting traits tend to survive is still close to trivial. Either way, he said, the propensity has not clearly added empirical content.

Maya answered that the second option is not trivial once the propensity is specified independently of the outcome it is meant to explain. Fitness can be assigned from an ecological or engineering model, she said: metabolic efficiency, running speed, number of viable gametes, the performance of a phenotype on tasks that affect survival. These facts are conceptually distinct from whether a particular individual actually survives and reproduces. An unlucky organism with a high expected reproductive output can die young, and a lucky one with a low expected output can flourish. Because the propensity can come apart from actual success in individual cases, the theory is falsifiable at the population level: under specified conditions, a type with the higher propensity should tend to increase in frequency relative to a type with the lower one.

Jonas pushed back that the population-level test quietly reconnects the two. To check whether the type with the higher propensity actually increased, one must measure realized reproductive output, and if the expected pattern fails to appear, it is always open to say that the environment was not as specified or that drift, migration, or some other process intervened. In practice, he argued, the propensity is so underdetermined by observations that selection is protected from refutation. The tautology objection, he said, is at bottom a complaint about testability, and appealing to a disposition that cannot be observed directly does not by itself secure testability.

Maya responded that dispositions are tested all the time; solubility and fragility are not meaningless because they are not directly observed. Fitness propensities rule out a great deal, she said: if two types begin at equal frequency and one has the higher expected reproductive output under conditions that can be specified in advance and held roughly constant, selection predicts that it will increase, and if it declines instead, that is a serious anomaly. One can appeal to drift or immigration only by taking on the burden of showing those forces were large enough. The mere possibility of auxiliary hypotheses does not make a theory unfalsifiable, she said, or every empirical theory would be in the same boat.

Jonas conceded the general point about dispositions but said the analogy falters for a further reason. What counts as the environment in which a fitness propensity is defined frequently depends on the organisms themselves: the relevant conditions include competitors, frequency-dependent payoffs, and niches that organisms partly construct. So the environment is not an independent backdrop against which the propensity is specified; it is partly fixed by the very types whose fitness we are trying to explain. That, he said, makes the independent specification Maya advertised much harder than the solubility analogy suggests.

That is roughly where they left it. I can see pieces of both positions, and I do not have a settled verdict. Could you adjudicate the disagreement? I want to know who is right about which specific points, where each side overstates its case, and what the most defensible position would be on whether the propensity interpretation dissolves the tautology objection. Please also say whether one of them is more right overall, and, if the propensity interpretation only partly succeeds, what exactly that partial success shows about the status of natural selection as an empirical theory. I would appreciate a point-by-point treatment even though it should read continuously. For the format, please write your answer as continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter, and avoid headings, bullet points, bold text, and tables.
