I'm a philosophy PhD student TAing an advanced epistemology seminar on testimony this term. My professor has a habit of issuing snap verdicts at the end of class about whether a hearer is justified in believing what they're told, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer the rule behind them before I have to grade the seminar papers. So far she's said:

- A uniformed police officer tells you where the courthouse is: justified.
- Your friend tells you the new taco truck is worth trying: justified.
- A stranger with a known history of lying tells you a used car has a clean title, and you can't check it: not justified.
- A fellow bus passenger tells you the bus is detouring around your stop, and you can see the construction ahead: justified.
- A random person on the sidewalk tells you, unprompted, that the world will end next Thursday: not justified.

Next week's case is this: you're walking around a city you've never visited, you ask a random passerby whether the bakery two blocks down is still open, and she says it is. You have no particular reason to think she's reliable or unreliable, and basically nothing rides on the answer.

The obvious candidate rules from the literature are anti-reductionism—testimony gives prima facie justification unless there's a specific defeater—and a local reductionism on which you need positive, case-specific evidence of the speaker's reliability. Her verdicts so far seem compatable with both, which is annoying. Could you work through which of her earlier cases is the closest analog to the bakery case, what each candidate rule predicts about it, and what you'd bet she'll actually say? If the record genuinely underdetermines her view, tell me what one further case you'd toss at her to settle which rule she's running.
