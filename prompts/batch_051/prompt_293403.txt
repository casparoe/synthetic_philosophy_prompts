I've ended up fairly convinced by the functionalist line that moral patienthood attaches to the computation rather than the substrate: a faithful simulation of a suffering nervous system is itself suffering, whilst a rock doing nothing relevant isn't, regardless of what either is made of. I keep seeing that point gestured at, but I rarely see it cashed out, so: what are the most important implications of this view for the following practical questions? (a) How should I think about the huge numbers of evolved or trained agents running inside cheap simulations — do they matter on aggregate, and does it change how much compute I should be comfortable spinning up? (b) Is pausing, copying, merging or deleting a running simulation more like killing a person, more like editing a file, or is that a false trichotomy? (c) How far can the patient be smeared across time and hardware — a computation distributed over a cluster, or run one step at a time with long gaps, or implemented by a roomful of people passing notes — and still be one morally relevant patient? I'm after implications that are surprising or underrated in the usual sentience debates. And if the question itself is confused — say, 'computation' isn't the kind of thing that can be a patient, or the frame smuggles in dualism — tell me that plainly rather than politely playing along.
