I am a retired experimental physicist who has lately been reading philosophy of science, and I recently encountered a general-audience magazine essay that summarized James Woodward's interventionist account of causation. I would like to know how much of the summary I should trust. The essay presents the view roughly as follows:

"Woodward holds that X causes Y just in case there is some possible intervention on X that would change Y. An intervention is a manipulation of X that sets its value independently of other causes of Y, so that any resulting change in Y can be attributed to X alone. For example, pressing a light switch causes the light to come on because, if one intervened to flip the switch while holding fixed all other common causes of the light, the light would come on. On this view, causal relationships are just associations that remain invariant under such interventions. Since the definition of an intervention itself mentions causes, the account is often charged with circularity; Woodward replies that the circularity is harmless because he is not trying to reduce causation to non-causal terms but merely to clarify how causal claims are connected to manipulative practice. The essay then turns to objections. It says that on Woodward's view, a causal claim such as 'smoking causes cancer' would be true only if some actual or practically possible intervention on smoking exists. This, the essay concludes, rules out causal knowledge in history, astronomy, and economics, where interventions on the relevant variables are impossible or unethical. It adds that 'invariance' is also too weak, because any accidental correlation that holds across a few manipulated trials could count as causal even if it disappears under other interventions."

The first few sentences sound plausible to me, since they resemble the logic of controlled experiments, but the last two criticisms strike me as possibly overblown or as reading objections back into the view. I cannot tell whether the essay is fairly reporting Woodward or smuggling a refutation into its exposition. What I would like is an accuracy audit: which parts of this summary would Woodward accept as fair, which parts conflate claims he keeps distinct or overstate a more modest thesis, and whether the apparent implications for history, astronomy, and economics actually follow from the accurate core of his account. In particular, does Woodward's treatment of the circularity worry succeed, and does his view really require interventions to be practically available in the way the essay suggests?
