I spend a fair amount of time in rationalist and adjacent circles, and I have noticed the same example doing a large amount of work in arguments about whether to reform or remove existing institutions, rules, and practices. I would like a careful audit of whether it can carry the weight people put on it.

The example is usually called Chesterton's fence. As it is presented there, it runs roughly like this. A reformer comes across a fence or gate standing across a road and, seeing no use for it, proposes to clear it away. A more conservative interlocutor replies that the reformer should not remove it until he can say why it was put there in the first place; his inability to see a reason is not evidence that no reason exists, and the fence may encode some purpose or accumulated practical knowledge that is not currently legible to him. In its generalized form, the example becomes a principle: when an existing institution, rule, or practice looks pointless or burdensome to you, the fact that you cannot reconstruct its original rationale is no argument for removing it; investigate first, and leave the burden of proof on the would-be reformer.

In the places I read, this example is made to support several different conclusions at once: (1) a weak methodological precept, that one should learn what a thing was for before destroying it; (2) a stronger epistemic claim, that the bare survival of an arrangement is some evidence that it has been useful, at least to someone, and perhaps that it still serves people as a whole; and (3) a still stronger, Burkean conclusion, that there should be a standing default against redesigning long-lasting social institutions, and that ordinary participants' inability to state a rationale for a practice is no significant objection to it.

I want to know how far the analogy actually holds. Please give me an explicit mapping: what corresponds to what? What are the social-world analogues of the fence, the road, the reformer, the person who originally erected the fence, the original reason for it, the cost of leaving it in place, the act of removing it, and the knowledge available to the reformer? Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the three conclusions above from the ones that are merely superficial. I suspect some differences may matter, but I am unsure how much each can bear. A fence is a discrete functional artifact, built by identifiable agents for an identifiable purpose; institutions often arise gradually without any designer, can be captured by interested actors, can persist through coordination problems or inertia rather than through serving the people subject to them, and can silently change their function while retaining the same outward form. The reformer in the story is a single uninformed passerby, whereas real reformers are sometimes accountable, have large amounts of evidence, and may already know the relevant history. And removing a fence is local and cheaply reversible, while many actual institutional changes are not. The example may also quietly import assumptions about who has standing to remove the fence and whose knowledge counts, which already presumes part of what the Burkean conclusion is trying to establish.

Please say, for each of the three conclusions I listed, how much of it remains standing once the disanalogies are on the table. Is the weak version only epistemic hygiene? Is the strong Burkean version actually supported by the analogy, or is the analogy doing much of the argumentative work by presenting an accumulated institution as if it were an engineered artifact whose designer's purpose could be recovered? If the fence analogy is not quite right, what would be a better analogy or a cleaner statement of the principle that does the real work — for example, something about blind variation and selection rather than design and purpose?
