At the chemical plant where I work, the same substance can receive two apparently conflicting regulatory treatments. Its safety data sheet classifies it as acutely toxic on the strength of a standardized test in which laboratory animals are exposed to the vapour, and the entry describes this hazard as an intrinsic property of the substance. That classification is not revised when the substance is handled only inside a sealed reactor that would neutralize any leak within seconds, nor when it is shipped with an inhibitor that suppresses the toxic effect. The tension goes further: one intermediate product is classified as toxic because of what it does under the standard test, even though in the only circumstances in which it could possibly reach a worker—a leak into the plant's humid air—it changes within seconds into a harmless solid, so the toxic disposition is extinguished precisely along the path by which it would have manifested. Yet the same regulator's process hazard analysis asks us to rate the hazard of the substance in its actual setting, where these blocking and vanishing conditions count for a great deal.

I am trying to identify the theory of dispositions, or at least of hazardous properties, that the classification side of this practice presupposes. It sounds as though it could be committed to the idea that a substance can be poisonous even when it cannot poison anyone under the circumstances, because the power is still there but masked, and maybe even that a substance can be toxic when the very conditions that would lead to the harm also remove the toxic disposition. But perhaps the practice strictly presupposes far less: a decision to classify by one standardized test, for reasons of consistency and precaution, with no deep metaphysical commitment at all. Please reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, say whether the two regulatory approaches are philosophically coherent together, and explain what the agency would have to change if it adopted a purely conditional or manifestation-based view of toxicity and flammability.
