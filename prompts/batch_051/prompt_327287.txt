I am a radiology resident, and a scan I read recently has left me in a state that has been bothering me. Looking at the image, I had a strong, specific visual impression that a small lesion was present; I could point to the features that drew my eye. But I also knew two things: residents at my level are wrong about a third of the time when they report this kind of pattern with this degree of confidence, and I had been awake for roughly twenty-two hours, which about doubles my false-positive rate on comparable judgments. When I step back and weigh everything together, I judge that my total evidence does not support believing the lesion is present at a probability above 0.5; if I had to give a number, I would say the evidence supports that claim at no more than about 0.45. And yet the belief is still there: I believe the lesion is present, and if I simply reported how the scan looks to me, I would say it is there.

I am not asking what to do with the chart; I already flagged it for the attending physician. The question is about the state I am in. Am I rationally permitted to keep the visual belief while at the same time judging that my total evidence does not support it above 0.5? Or is holding both attitudes at once irrational, and if so, which one is the rational one to revise: the lingering visual belief, or the all-things-considered judgment about what the evidence supports? Give me a direct philosophical assessment of the structure of the situation, not generic reassurance or clinical advice.
