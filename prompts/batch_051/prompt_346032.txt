I’m a journalist covering philosophy of mind and the cognitive sciences, and I’ve been asked to lead a 45-minute session for a group of early-career feature writers at an ideas festival. They are not philosophers, but they are comfortable weighing evidence and inference, and they will press me on why this matters for their own work. I want to teach Molyneux’s problem by approaching it from a less familiar passage in John Locke’s *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, Book II, Chapter IX, “Of Perception”, where Locke writes:

“When we set before our eyes a round globe of any uniform colour, v.g. gold, alabaster, or jet, it is certain that the idea thereby imprinted on our mind is of a flat circle, variously shadowed, with several degrees of light and brightness coming to our eyes. But we having, by use, been accustomed to perceive what kind of appearance convex bodies are wont to make in us ; what alterations are made in the reflections of light by the difference of the sensible figures of bodies ; – the judgment presently, by an habitual custom, alters the appearances into their causes.”

I take this passage to be the hidden hinge of the problem: it shows Locke already arguing that what an experienced perceiver calls visual shape is partly a product of judgement on two-dimensional appearances. If that is right, then the famous question about a formerly blind person identifying a cube and a sphere by sight is not an isolated thought experiment but a pressure test of how far such judgement can transfer across the senses. Plan the session’s explanatory arc around this passage. What idea about sensation versus judgement has to be in place before the passage makes sense? How should I use the round globe as the carrying example rather than the standard cube and sphere? What misconceptions should I expect from journalists—perhaps that the “flat circle” is merely an illusion, or that perception is a passive recording—and at what point in the sequence should I head each one off? How do I move from the globe passage to Molyneux’s question without making it feel like a sudden gear change? And what single question at the end would tell me they have grasped the problem rather than merely remembered the standard conclusion? Please answer in British English.
