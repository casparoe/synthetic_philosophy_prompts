You are a historical linguist and a philosopher of language with an interest in how technical vocabularies are built. I want a candid, rigorous assessment of a thesis I have been developing, not praise. Think carefully; begin by brainstorming a few ways my thesis could be read, pick the strongest, and state it in your own words so I can check whether you have understood me.

I call the thesis the Lexical Mortmain view, after the legal idea of a dead hand continuing to control property from beyond the grave. The observation behind it is that technical fields keep coining new terms from Latin and Greek even though no one speaks those languages, and I think the deadness of the source is not decorative but the entire point. A term assembled from living words—guilty mind, fast heart rate—remains attached to ordinary usage and drifts as the vernacular drifts. A term assembled from classical roots—mens rea, tachycardia—has no native speech community to push back, so it arrives looking finished: its meaning is whatever the coining professional community stipulated, and it presents itself as a stable fixture before it has actually earned stability through long use.

I think this explains several facts at once: why law and medicine lean so heavily on classical coinage; why such terms feel more precise than plain-language equivalents even when their definitions are just as vague; and why they tend to be revised by committees and style guides rather than by the slow pressure of everyday speech. I am aware that classical terms do drift—atom was coined for something uncuttable and now applies to something that gets split—so I am not claiming that dead roots guarantee permanence. My narrower claim is that dead-language coinage is a strategy by which a professional community tries to assert custody over a term’s future, and that the deadness does real causal work by removing the one force that normally renegotiates meaning over time: a living community of native speakers.

Be willing to conclude that the idea is banal, false, promising, or some combination.
