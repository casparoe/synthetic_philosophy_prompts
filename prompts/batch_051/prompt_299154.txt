I have recently joined the priority-setting committee of a public health agency that allocates a fixed annual budget across interventions for conditions whose outcomes are so poorly measured that any probability assignment is, in effect, a constructed prior. I intend to defend a maximin allocation rule: when the evidence is that thin, we should choose the policy whose worst-case burden on any affected group is least bad, rather than optimize expected benefit under subjective priors or minimize maximal regret. My core argument is that under severe ignorance the prior does the moral work, so the expected-utility defense is circular; maximin is the only rule that refuses to let a made-up prior decide who goes untreated. The audience will be the agency's economists and biostatisticians, mostly Bayesian decision theorists who know Harsanyi's critique of maximin, plus one or two philosophers sympathetic to Harsanyi's impartial-spectator argument and skeptical of Rawls's slide from the original position to maximin. Prepare me for the question period: give the objections, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased as this audience would phrase them, each with what I should concede, what I should hold, a two-minute reply, and a note on where that reply is soft. Include the objection I should be hoping nobody raises.
