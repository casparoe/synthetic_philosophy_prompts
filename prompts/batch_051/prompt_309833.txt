The Church-Turing thesis leads a curious double life. In its mathematical form, the claim that every effectively computable function — roughly, every function computable by a finite mechanical procedure — is Turing-computable is usually treated as something close to a definition or a piece of conceptual analysis, not as a hypothesis that any experiment could overturn. In its physical form, the claim that any function computable by a physical system is Turing-computable is usually treated as a contingent, empirical conjecture about what the world permits. Yet both are routinely called the Church-Turing thesis. What, if anything, justifies this asymmetry in epistemic status? In particular, if some physical process were found to compute a non-Turing-computable function, would that refute the mathematical thesis, show that it was never a good analysis of effective computability, or leave it untouched? Or is the physical thesis simply the mathematical thesis plus a stipulation about what counts as computation in nature, in which case it is no more empirically vulnerable than the mathematical one? I want an assessment of whether the asymmetry should be kept, collapsed, or reversed. Please answer in American English.
