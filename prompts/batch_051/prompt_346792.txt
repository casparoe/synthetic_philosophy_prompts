I ended up as the person on my city's climate working group who has to defend a carbon price-and-dividend plan in front of the group that is most likely to kill it. The audience is mostly neighborhood environmental justice organizers who have been pushing for moratoriums, building-code changes, and public ownership of utilities, plus a couple of degrowth-leaning ecological economists who think pricing carbon is mostly a way of making continued growth feel responsible. I'm not walking in cold: I want to argue that a steadily rising carbon fee with the revenue returned per person, plus a border adjustment on imports, is the workhorse climate policy we should actually defend, because it changes millions of decisions at once without requiring the government to know the right answer to each one. But I also plan to concede that pricing is not a hard cap and will not by itself produce the planned reduction in energy and material use they want, and that is why I'm framing it as a compliment to regulation and public investment rather than a substitute for it.

I can already hear what they will say. They will say it treats the atmosphere like a commodity, that a flat dividend doesn't make up for the fact that rich households buy far more emissions, that rich people will simply pay to keep polluting while the refinery stays in the poor neighborhood, that any offsets attached to the scheme are greenwashing, and that the only honest framework is degrowth, not a price tweak. The two economists will make a sharper version of these points, probably about rebound effects, the difficulty of setting the right price, and where the money actually lands. What I need is a prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order I will actually hear them, phrased the way this group would phrase them, with what I should concede up front, what I should hold, the part of my own case that is weakest and that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss, and the one damaging objection I have not listed here that they are likely to raise. And tell me honestly whether framing it as a compliment to regulation and investment is a real concession or just a way of sounding like I agree with them.
