I'm giving a lunch seminar next month at an interdisciplinary AI institute, and I want help preparing for the Q&A. The thesis I'm defending is that you can't uniquely recover an agent's objective function from its behavior—not even with unlimited observations, and not even if you also get a transcript of which options it considered and rejected. I'm going to argue that the objective and the search space are jointly underdetermined: if you see an agent consider A and B and then pick A, you can't tell whether it preferred A to B, or whether B got pruned by a search heuristic before the objective really decided anything—and you never see the candidates the search process didn't generate in the first place. Reward function and search process are a package deal, and the data only pins them down together, and only up to a big equivalence class. I'll end on the claim that in practice the prior over objectives—or the architectural bias about which candidates even get generated—is doing more work then anyone admits.

The audience will be maybe 40 people: mostly ML grad students and researchers, many of them doing RL from human feedback or inverse RL; one microeconomist who works on revealed preference and keeps insisting utility is just a compact description of choice, not a hidden thing to discover; and a philosopher of mind who's allergic to behaviorism and will want to say I've confused epistemology with metaphysics. The host warned me the RL people get irritated when underdetermination talks don't engage with the actual algorithms, and that the economist will probably bring up consistency conditions on choices.

Write me a Q&A prep sheet. For each objection: who in the room raises it, the words they'd actually use, how likely it is, how much damage it does, what I should concede, what I should hold, and a two-minute reply. Also tell me where my own argument is softest, and include the question I should be most afraid of even if no one thinks to ask it.
