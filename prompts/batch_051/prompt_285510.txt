Large encyclopedic museums have long held, and in many cases still hold, objects acquired under colonial rule or foreign occupation — the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles are the standard examples — while presenting their own possession as legitimate custody: they stress preservation, universal public access, and scholarship, and they treat repatriation as a discretionary concession rather than the return of something to which another party already has a claim. The same institutions would generally treat a painting stolen in an ordinary burglary last year as subject to a straightforward claim for return. What theory of cultural property does this pattern of practice presuppose — a theory of how title is acquired, transferred, and lost, and of the moral relevance of an acquisition's circumstances? Reconstruct that implicit theory, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with; assess whether the two halves of the practice can be rendered mutually consistent; and say what would have to change in the institutions' practices of custody, display, provenance research, and public framing if title were instead understood to remain with source communities and their present-day representatives. Where the underlying historical and moral claims are disputed, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
