I'm a journalist working on a piece about why human extinction would be bad beyond the deaths it involves, and I keep coming back to Samuel Scheffler's "afterlife conjecture" — roughly, that a lot of what we care about in our own lives quietly depends on the assumption that humanity will continue after we're gone, so if we learned the species was about to die out, our projects and values would lose their point even if our own lifespans were untouched. I'd like a reading of Children of Men through that lens. The films premise — a world where no child has been born for 18 years, so humanity is winding down without a mass die-off — looks like a near-perfect thought experiment for the conjecture: it isolates the loss of the future from the loss of present lives. But I can't decide wether the film actually vindicates the idea or complicates it. The despair there is tangled up with politics (the police state, the camps), and the characters' motives are often more ordinary — fear, grief, protecting one actual person — than a philosophical point about the future. So read the film against the conjecture: where does it show people losing their grip on value because the future has emptied out, and where is it showing something else? Does the ending — the boat, the Human Project — endorse the thought that a retrievable future restores meaning? And what should I make of the odd moments where the state is still preserving artworks (the David) as if culture matters even when there may be nobody to receive it?
