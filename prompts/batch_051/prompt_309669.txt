In reading Joseph Bertrand's Calcul des probabilités, I keep returning to his treatment of what later came to be called Bertrand's paradox. As he presents it, the question is simple: a chord is drawn at random in a circle; what is the probability that it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? Bertrand lays out three equally natural ways of making that specification precise — choose the two endpoints uniformly on the circumference, choose the chord's distance from the centre uniformly along a radius, or choose the chord's midpoint uniformly inside the circle — and they yield one half, one third, and one quarter respectively. He takes the example to show that an unspecified random choice in a continuous space has no well-defined mathematical meaning.

I am drawn to the principle of indifference as a constraint on rational priors: when a set of possibilities is truly symmetric and exhaustively specified, equal probabilities are the only non-arbitrary assignment. And I largely accept Bertrand's deflationary lesson that an undefined physical phrase like at random is incomplete until a generating procedure is fixed. Yet I cannot shake the intuition that if someone actually performed a definite physical procedure — for example, dropping a long straight stick onto a circle and taking the chord it lands on — the question would have a unique answer, and any competing answer would simply be answering a different question. That makes me unsure what is really being challenged: the principle, the idea that probability must attach to a well-specified procedure, or the demand that random chord have a default meaning in the absence of one.

Give me the strongest version of both the principle-of-indifference defender's position and the intuition that there must be a unique privileged answer. Then tell me which should yield, and what exactly would be lost by giving it up.
