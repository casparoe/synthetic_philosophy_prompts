I am a novelist, and I am outlining a book in which a natural historian and a physiologist fall into a long argument about what a certain trait of a fictional animal is for. I want their quarrel to illustrate a genuine philosophical split rather than a misunderstanding, so I need a case built to specification.

The split is between two accounts of biological function. On the etiological, or selected-effects, account, the function of a trait is the effect for which the trait was selected in its lineage: the effect that explains why the trait is there. On the causal-role account, the function of a trait is the contribution it makes to some present capacity of the containing system, with no essential reference to history.

Construct for me a single fictional but biologically ordinary trait on which the two accounts assign two different functions, call them F1 and F2 — not merely function versus no function. Provide the organism and its ecology briefly; stipulate the trait's actual selection history as authorial worldbuilding, arranged so that F2 has not itself been selected for in the lineage; and specify the trait's present causal role and the containing system relative to which the causal-role verdict is being read. Then show the reasoning behind each verdict: which effect the etiological account marks as F1 and why, and which present contribution the causal-role account marks as F2 and why. Identify the one structural feature of the case that does the real work in forcing the two verdicts apart, say what the case would do if that feature were removed — whether the accounts would converge, and on what — and sketch the strongest objection a defender of the account that comes out worse would press against the case.

Avoid stock textbook examples and any named real taxon; invent the organism outright. The biology should stay within ordinary terrestrial chemistry and genetics, and the history may be stipulated, but it must generate each verdict from the stated facts rather than merely assert it.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — a single sentence naming the trait and the two rival verdicts the accounts deliver — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
