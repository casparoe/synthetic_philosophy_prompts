I'm preparing a handout for a graduate seminar in moral psychology on what we owe one another in attitude, as opposed to in action. Please write a decision tree, usable also as a sorting quiz with skip instructions, that classifies people by their answers to diagnostic questions about concrete cases rather than by which labels they recognize. The tree should work through the following fault lines: whether private, never-expressed attitudes can be morally wrong in themselves or only bad as character; whether we can be required to take up an attitude we cannot adopt at will, or whether control over time changes this; whether attitudinal obligations are owed to all persons equally or only within specific relationships; whether the fitting response to an attitudinal failure is blame or something else; and whether respecting someone is primarily an earned appraisal or an owed recognition. Use this case at a central branching point: a nurse who gives a patient flawless, non-discriminatory care while privately holding a settled contempt for people of the patient's background, where the contempt is not something she can switch off by a decision now. Use a second case somewhere in the tree: someone who has spent years trying, without success, to extinguish a phobic aversion she repudiates. Build the space from the answers rather than from a list of positions, and include as leaves at least a Kantian recognition-respect view, a Strawsonian reactive-attitudes view, a Darwall-style second-personal view, an appraisal-only view of respect, and a doxastic-involuntarist view, using "unnamed" where no standard label fits. For unoccupied combinations of answers, note whether you take the gap to be incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any adjacent positions the tree cannot separate. Close with a short note stating which question is doing the most contested theoretical work, what alternative answer to it would most restructure the tree, and where you are least confident that a leaf has been attached to the right named view rather than to its nearest neighbor.
