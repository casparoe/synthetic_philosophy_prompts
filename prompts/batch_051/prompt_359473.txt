I'm a third-year undergrad taking a formal epistemology seminar and I also volunteer as a radio coordinator for a campus search-and-rescue group. We get reports from hikers and rangers that are often conditional, like "if the missing person is in the creek drainage, they're probably in the north fork" or "if the subject went uphill, they would have crossed the logging road." Our team keeps a probability map of sectors and updates it as reports come in, sometimes every fifteen minutes during an active search, and the stakes are real because we send volunteers into different terrain based on the map. In class we did the Judy Benjamin problem — where a soldier gets a message like "if you're in the Blue zone, you're in the east part of Blue" and the question is how she should revise her probabilities. The formal solutions are weird: some rules (minimum relative entropy, Adams conditioning, whatever) change probabilities of things the message didn't seem to be about, and different rules give different answers depending on how you partition the space. I need to turn this into something I can actually use at the radio desk. Please give me a step-by-step procedure for updating a probability map when a new report is conditional. It should say: what questions to ask about the report before I do any math; how to choose a partition; when to just use ordinary conditionalization, when to use Jeffrey conditioning, and when to use one of the fancier rules; how to check whether the update changed probabilities it shouldn't have; and at what point I should stop and go back to the source or hand the decision to the team lead because the report is too ambiguous. The procedure has to be runnable in under five minutes per update, by someone who knows basic probability but hasn't taken the seminar I'm in, and it should leave an audit trail for the after-action review. Then apply it to two or three realistic cases, including one where it tells me something I won't want to hear (like the report is useless without more info, or we have to move probabilities we were confident about). Be honest about what the procedure cannot fix and where judgement remains. If possible make it a checklist I can tape to the desk. I'd definately appreciate it.
