I'm a graduate student in a seminar on the philosophy of mathematics, and after our session on computability two classmates got into an argument that I ended up listening to and was asked to referee. I couldn't decide who was right, so I'd like a written adjudication to circulate to both of them. The exchange, as I remember it, went as follows. (1) Rhea said the Church-Turing thesis is an empirical claim about the world: it asserts that the informal class of effectively computable functions coincides with the formal class of Turing-computable functions, and that is a substantive, in-principle falsifiable assertion about the reach of mechanical computation. (2) Tomás replied that the notion of an effective procedure is informal and vague, so the thesis functions as a definitional bridge between an informal notion and a formal one; a physical process that computed a non-Turing-computable function, such as the halting function for Turing machines, would refute the physical Church-Turing thesis, not the Church-Turing thesis itself. (3) Rhea answered that if the thesis were a stipulation, Church and Turing would not have argued for it: the convergence of general recursiveness, lambda-definability, and Turing computability, together with Turing's analysis of what a human computer does, looks like evidence for an empirical identification, not a decision. (4) Tomás said those arguments are conceptual analysis, not experiment; they show what we mean by effective procedure, so a purported counterexample would be rejected as not really an effective procedure rather than accepted as a refutation. (5) Rhea replied that this makes the thesis unfalsifiable by fiat and understates its content: the formalisms were developed independently and could in principle have come apart, and Turing's analysis could in principle have missed some procedure a finite human could carry out; that converging evidence closes off these possibilities is exactly what makes the thesis empirical. (6) Tomás responded that the possibilities Rhea mentions are logical possibilities, not something evidence about the world decides; any procedure that is finite, step-by-step, and checkable just is what a Turing machine captures, so the thesis is quasi-analytic, and only the separate claim that physics contains no hypercomputational process is genuinely empirical.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each numbered move, assign a rating on the scale clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, mixed, mostly right, clearly right, and justify the rating in a sentence or two. Then add a short paragraph identifying the strongest point each speaker should concede and whether the dispute is partly terminological. Please format the main verdict as a table with columns: Move, Speaker, Rating, Justification.
