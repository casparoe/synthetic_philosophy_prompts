Suppose the editors of a major biology journal get it into there heads to fix the problem of old evidence — you know, the Bayesian puzzle where a theory's best support is often some fact everyone already knew, so formally the evidence had probability 1 before the theory arrived and shouldn't move your credence at all, even though it intuitively feels like the theory nailed it. Their rule: from now on, any datum that was already in the public record before the authors deposited their hypothesis counts for zero confirmation. You can mention old data in the introduction as motivation, but it may not appear anywhere as support. Only data collected after the timestamped deposit counts, and even then only if the authors had no access to it beforehand. Once a year an author can re-base a theory, but everything they successfully predicted under the old base gets demoted to old evidence and stops counting. They also hire adversarial reviewers whose job is to figure out whether the authors actually saw the new data before depositing; if the reviewers think so, the paper is rejected and the authors are banned from depositing for a year.

I keep going back and forth on whether this would make science more honest or just more theatrical, so I want a cold read. Walk me through the incentives. Who wins and who loses? Where does it get gamed first — vague placeholder deposits, slicing hypotheses into tiny pieces, quietly sitting on a theory until the data is basically in? What does it do to fields where nearly all the available evidence is already old, like paleontology or much of cosmology? And can adversarial review ever really distinguish between thought of it before seeing the data and fit it after the fact? Don't tell me whether it's a good idea in the abstract — tell me how it would actually play out.
