Consider the convention of spoiler warnings. Readers and viewers take care not to learn in advance that a fictional character dies, and many will flinch at a horror scene even while knowing that nothing real is threatened; yet few people see anything odd about being told, without any such warning, that Napoleon lost at Waterloo or that a true-crime case ended in a conviction. What theory of our engagement with fiction does this combination of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit account of how we respond to events we know did not occur, distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit with, and say whether they would continue to make sense if our responses to fiction were quasi-emotions or acts of make-believe rather than fully fledged emotions. Please write your answer in British English.
