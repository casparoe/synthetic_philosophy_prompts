I direct food procurement for a network of fourteen public school districts serving roughly 110,000 meals per school day. Our board has already adopted an ethical commitment, and my job is now to turn it into routine purchasing decisions: we are to minimize expected animal suffering per meal, where the animals counted include not only farmed animals but also wild animals harmed indirectly in producing feed crops and plant-based ingredients. My team of three procurement analysts, none of whom has formal training in ethics, reviews our protein portfolio twice a year and scores each major product line for the coming two semesters: beef, pork, chicken, eggs, dairy, legume- and grain-based patties, and whole legumes and grains. The board will not approve any change that raises per-meal cost by more than ten percent unless it comes with a written animal-welfare justification that follows the procedure you give us. The analysts have about ten working days per review cycle, working from vendor data sheets that report species, stocking density, days to slaughter, feed conversion, and crop sources, but no welfare estimates.

The principle we are trying to translate into routine work is this: we may not dismiss a source of suffering merely because it is indirect, wild, individually small, or uncertain to exist. That principle collides immediately with our actual choices. Beef looks bad on feed conversion and therefore carries a large crop footprint through its feed; chicken looks bad on the number and duration of individual lives but better on feed conversion; plant-based patties avoid farmed animals but still depend on high-input monocrops whose cultivation and harvest kill small wild animals. Some of my analysts now argue that once crop deaths are counted, beef may outrank chicken, and plant protein may not outrank chicken. I cannot adjudicate these claims myself, and I do not want a procedure that simply assumes a number for field-animal deaths per hectare and mechanically applies it.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure — an ordered checklist with decision points and stopping rules — that my analysts could actually run on each product line without philosophical training. It must satisfy the following:

1. Every empirical quantity is treated as a placeholder: deaths per hectare of a given crop, duration and intensity of suffering per animal, the probability that invertebrates are sentient, and so on. For each such input, specify what data we would need to collect or estimate, and state how the beef–chicken–plant-protein ranking would change across a reasonable range of values, rather than pinning the whole procedure to one contested estimate. Do not rest the procedure on citations or named empirical estimates from studies; give us the reasoning and the data requirements, not a bibliography.

2. Every value judgment is marked as such rather than hidden inside the arithmetic. This includes at least: whether deaths of wild juveniles or small-bodied animals count for less than deaths of adult farmed animals; whether harms are offset by the fact that the animals would not otherwise have existed; whether many small losses may aggregate to outweigh one large loss; and whether chronic low-level suffering is comparable to shorter, more intense confinement.

3. The procedure has clear stopping rules: conditions under which a product is rejected outright or approved outright, and the precise point at which a decision must be escalated to me or the board because the remaining disagreement is evaluative rather than factual.

Then work the procedure through the following three cases, making one of them a case where the procedure returns an answer our board will not like:

(a) replacing the default beef burger with a chicken patty, under the supposition that beef's crop-footprint deaths through feed are substantial once counted;

(b) replacing that chicken patty with a legume-and-grain patty made from high-input monocrops, under the supposition that harvest deaths in those crops are substantial; and

(c) keeping whole eggs on the menu rather than switching to a low-welfare beef product, where the procedure must decide how to treat the lifetime welfare of layer hens together with the routine killing of male chicks.

Finally, tell me honestly what this procedure cannot do. Where will it force a decision that rests on a contested philosophical choice rather than on facts? Where will it most likely misfire? Which comparisons among our product lines will remain genuinely indeterminate even if the analysts follow it perfectly?
