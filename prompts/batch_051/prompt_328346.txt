So I'm giving a talk in two weeks to my department's formal epistemology / foundations of statistics reading group, and I've finally realized I need to spend less time polishing the slides and more time preparing for the Q&A, because this crowd will eat me alive if I'm not ready. The title of the talk is something like "Cox's Theorem Does Less Than You Think." My thesis: Cox's theorem, as it's usually pushed, does not actually establish that any rational agent's degrees of belief have to obey the probability axioms. The theorem only shows that if you've already agreed to represent plausibility by a single real number, to always be able to compare any two plausibilities, and to let conjunction and disjunction behave like ordinary Boolean algebra in the way the proof needs, then yes—out pop the sum and product rules and you're a probabilist. But those starting assumptions are exactly what's in dispute, and the theorem doesn't give you a reason to adopt them. So someone who is already sympathetic to imprecise probabilities, or to non-numeric ranks of belief, can just say "nice proof, wrong starting point" and walk away. The audience: mostly Bayesians who learned the theorem through Jaynes and treat it as the knockdown argument for why you have to be a Bayesian; two philosophers who work on imprecise probabilities and will be friendly but won't let me coast; and one logician who will care less about the epistemology and more about exactly which algebraic properties the proof quietly uses. Write me a Q&A prep sheet, not a restatement of the talk. Give the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way this particular group would phrase them, with for each one a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft, and a clear sense of what I should concede up front versus what I should hold. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Keep it under 900 words.
