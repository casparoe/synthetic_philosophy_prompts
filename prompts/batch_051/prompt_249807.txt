I direct the portfolio office at a mid-sized industrial manufacturer. Every quarter I chair a review in which a committee decides whether to continue, re-scope, or terminate each development project that is over budget or behind schedule — usually ten to fifteen projects at a time. The stakes are high: a wrong continuation ties up a year of scarce engineering capacity, and a wrong termination forfeits capabilities and customer relationships we cannot cheaply rebuild. The committee has ninety minutes per project, a one-page financial summary prepared by an analyst, and the project lead in the room; the CFO must personally approve any continuation that releases new money.

Our recurring mistake is the dollar auction. In that auction two bidders run the price of a dollar past a dollar because each new bid is treated as protecting the money already bid, and the second-highest bidder must also pay while getting nothing. We do the same: every quarter, the amount already spent on a project is offered as a reason to spend more. I want you to turn the underlying logic of the auction into a procedure my committee can actually run. The core of that logic is that sunk costs should not by themselves move a decision, and that only forward-looking costs and benefits count. But I do not want a slogan that says the past is always irrelevant. Past spending can be relevant when it has changed something about the future: it may have bought information, built a reusable capability, created obligations to customers or suppliers, or altered competitors' behavior. The procedure must separate those legitimate uses of history from the bare argument that we have already spent too much to stop.

Write the procedure as an ordered set of questions the committee must answer for each project. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer to move to the next question, where the procedure stops with a recommendation to terminate, where it stops with a recommendation to re-scope, and where it hands the decision to a named person because the question is one of values or unquantifiable judgment rather than fact. The procedure should be usable by committee members who have never heard of the dollar auction or sunk costs; it should not require them to learn the theory, but it should keep the distinctions the theory actually needs and drop the ones it can do without. Before the questions, state which features of a project would make escalation rational and which merely make it feel rational, so the committee does not apply the lesson of the auction to situations that lack its structure.

Then run the procedure on three projects:

1. A two-year-old software platform, 40% over budget. The remaining work is well understood and the expected value of completion is now negative, but we promised delivery to our largest customer, and walking away would damage that relationship.
2. A materials project that has missed its last three technical milestones. Each failure has narrowed the likely causes, and the team argues that the next attempt has a substantially higher probability of success than the original plan assumed.
3. A project that is on its original schedule and budget but whose business case has evaporated because a competitor launched a superior product; the team wants to continue because the work is nearly done and the money already spent is about to be converted into a finished asset.

Finish by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do: which inputs of judgment or values it still requires, what kinds of projects it will systematically mishandle, and which failure mode remains even if the committee follows it faithfully.
