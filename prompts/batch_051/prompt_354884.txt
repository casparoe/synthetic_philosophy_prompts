I am writing a paper on the empirical status of superrationality, and I have reached an impasse with a colleague that I would like your help in rendering testable. My inclination is that "superrationality," as Hofstadter introduced it, is not an identifiable mode of practical reasoning but a name for a pattern of choices that can be produced by several more familiar mechanisms, and that our usual demonstrations do not distinguish one from the other. I want to know whether any experiment could.

Consider the two canonical cases. In the Platonia dilemma, twenty people are told, with no possibility of cooperation or communication, that if exactly one of them sends a telegram by a deadline, that person receives a billion dollars; if none or several send, nobody receives anything. The superrational advice is that each person sends with probability one in twenty—the common mixed policy maximizing the chance that exactly one person sends. But this interior probability is never a best response for an expected-money maximizer who treats others' choices as independent: if you assign positive probability to the others' all refraining, sending for certain is strictly better than sending with any lower probability; if you assign that event probability zero, every probability of sending yields the same zero payoff. In the Luring Lottery run by Scientific American in the 1980s, readers could send a postcard requesting any number of entries; a one-million-dollar prize was to be divided by the total number of entries and awarded by a random draw weighted by entries. A reader who expects everyone else to submit y entries in total maximizes expected winnings by submitting about y entries, which, if everyone reasons that way, feeds on itself into runaway submissions; the superrational advice was instead to estimate the number N of likely responders, roll an N-sided die, and send a single entry only on a roll of one.

My colleague claims that if an incentivized laboratory version produced the restrained pattern—send rates clustered near one-twentieth in Platonia, and mostly zero- or one-entry submissions in Luring—that would be strong evidence that subjects reason superrationally. I doubt it. The same surface pattern could come from anticipated guilt about spoiling the common prize, from a fairness rule such as "everyone may take the same small chance," from a quasi-magical belief that one's own choice is correlated with others' choices through a shared procedure, or simply from the demand characteristics of being handed a famous paradox to solve.

Please design the strongest experimental test you can of the claim that superrationality is a distinct mode of practical reasoning. State the conditions, incentives, and outcome measures; say exactly what pattern of results would favor the superrational reading and what would favor one or another deflationary reading; and be explicit about what no behavioral data could settle. In particular, I want to know whether any observation could distinguish a subject who reasons "we will all converge on the same answer, so I choose the policy that is best if we all do" from one who reasons "my choice is correlated with theirs through a shared rule, so the same policy advances my private interest," and whether that residual difference is a real psychological difference or only a modeling idiom.
