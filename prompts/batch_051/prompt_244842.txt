All year I've been having versions of the same argument about dead men — Jefferson and the statues, Churchill and empire, Columbus and basically everything — and I keep noticing a move I don't have a word for. The sentence "he was a man of his time" seems to do two different jobs, and people switch between them mid-argument as if they were one. Sometimes it's a claim about what the person could have known: Jefferson grew up inside a slave society, nobody in his world was treating chattel slavery as the obvious evil we take it to be, so he couldn't have seen it the way we do. Other times — often the same person, five minutes later — it's a claim about our standing to judge: we can't grade anyone from the past at all, because our grandchildren will find us equally blind about something we're currently doing without a second thought.

Those don't look to me like the same assertion. The first is about a particular man's circumstances and knowledge, and it still leaves room to say that what he did was wrong even if he's less blameworthy for not seeing it. The second isn't about him at all; it's about how any of us is placed to blame the dead, and it seems to block even saying the act was wrong. The thing that genuinely bothers me is the glide. A friend will start with the first defence — Jefferson simply couldn't have known. Then I point out that Jefferson himself seems to have had private doubts about slavery, that he knew the arguments against it. And instead of defending the first claim, she slides to the second: "it's arrogant to judge an eighteenth-century man by our standards, we buy our clothes from appalling factories so who are we to talk." That isn't a retreat from a strong version of a claim to a weaker version; it's swapping one claim for another while keeping the slogan. The same pair shows up with Churchill — first people insist he just absorbed the racial hierarchy of his class and couldn't have known better, and when you push back they move to the standing point, "all eras have their blind spots, so no ranking is even possible."

I know people use "presentism" and "moral relativism" near this debate, but I'm after the specific mechanism of going from "he couldn't have known better" to "you have no standing to judge." Is there an established name for this glide, or for the umbrella phrase that hides the switch? If there isn't, how would you carve the two claims apart in principle, and what are the boundary cases where they come pre-separated? I'm not satisfied with just a label — I want to know why these two keep getting run together and what exactly the difference is.
