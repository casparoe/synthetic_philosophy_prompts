In my reporting I keep running into an argument about the evolution of cooperation, and I want to check whether it is sound before I let it structure a story. The backdrop is the prisoner's dilemma: cooperation produces a benefit that all can share, while each individual can do better by withholding cooperation. Some researchers in the multilevel selection tradition claim that analyzing only the individual incentives misses part of the explanatory picture. Their argument, as it was presented to me, runs like this.

(1) In any mixed group, a defector receives the benefit of the cooperators' efforts without paying the cost of cooperating, so within that group defection yields a higher payoff than cooperation.
(2) Therefore, within any single group, natural selection will tend to replace cooperators with defectors.
(3) But groups that contain many cooperators produce more of the shared good, and are more likely to survive, split, or found new groups, than groups dominated by defectors.
(4) Therefore, between groups, selection favors a larger share of cooperators.
(5) Since within-group and between-group selection pull in opposite directions, the net outcome is set by their relative strength: cooperation can spread provided the between-group advantage is large enough to outweigh the within-group disadvantage.

The flat conclusion is that a trait can be selected against within each group that contains it and yet still evolve, because the distribution of the trait across groups does part of the causal work. I find each step plausible when I read it in isolation, but colleagues give different diagnoses: one says the move from (1) to (2) overreaches, another says the move from (3) to (4) is where the error enters, and a third says (5) merely reasserts the conclusion. I want to know whether the argument as written is valid and, if not, exactly which step fails, what the most defensible corrected version of that step would be, and whether the flat conclusion follows from the corrected argument.
