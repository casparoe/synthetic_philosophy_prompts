I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and I've been poking at causal discovery packages — PC algorithm, BIC score, all that — mostly to figure out how much I should trust the little DAGs they draw. The thing bugging me is the standard story about why a minimal Bayes net ought to track causation: you assume the Markov condition, you prefer the simplest Markov-compatible graph, and you throw in faithfulness so that independencies in the data have to match the graph. I want a constructed, worked example where applying those criteria, without any hidden-variable caveats, leads a causal discovery algorithm to pick the wrong causal structure.

Set it up like this. The true system has exactly three observed continuous variables, X, M, and Y, related this way: X causes M, M causes Y, and X also has a direct causal effect on Y. The parameters are arranged so that the direct path and the indirect path through M exactly cancel, making X and Y marginally independent. No hidden confounders, no cycles, linear Gaussian equations, and pick an ordinary engineering setting for what X, M, and Y are (CI/CD pipeline, incident response, service load, latency, whatever feels natural). Give the structural equations with actual coefficients and confirm the cancellation is exact.

Now do the check properly. State the conditional independencies in the data — engineer it so the only one is that X and Y are independant unconditionally. Show that X and Y are dependent conditional on M. Explain why that pattern makes the distribution unfaithful to the true DAG. Then show that the minimal Markov-compatible graph for the same distribution is the collider X -> M <- Y with no X-Y edge, and that a PC algorithm with a perfect independence oracle would output exactly that collider. Here's the part I especially want: the distribution is actually faithful to that collider, even though the collider is causally false. Walk through how that can happen, and say what it means for the claim that faithfulness is what connects Bayes nets to causation. If the true process is unfaithful, does switching to the one graph that is faithful buy you anything at all?

Then the honest section. A defender of the faithfulness-and-minimality approach would say I rigged it with a knife-edge parameter cancellation — measure zero, never occurs in real data, so real causal graphs are generically faithful. Take that objection seriously. Where does it break down in an engineered system with a controller or a human operator deliberately tuning parameters to keep two quantities decoupled — say, a load balancer or autoscaler compensating so that a traffic spike doesn't visibly move latencies or error rates? And what about finite samples: once the cancellation is only approximate, what would PC actually do, and does that make the generic-faithfulness defense stronger or weaker? Finish by saying which single feature of the case is doing all the work, and what would happen to your conclusion if that feature were removed.
