Next month I present a seminar paper to a research group of formal epistemologists and philosophers of probability, joined by two Bayesian statisticians who work with probabilistic models daily but suspect that philosophical debates about updating demand more inputs than the evidence can supply. I shall defend the thesis that Jeffrey conditionalization is not a stopgap for anomalous cases but the correct general rule for updating on uncertain evidence: the normal case of learning is that an agent's probabilities shift over a partition of evidential propositions without any member of the partition becoming certain, and strict conditionalization on a proposition about how things appear cannot capture this without either relocating the uncertainty into a proposition the agent never formulated or attributing certainties the agent does not possess. My central argument is that this is a feature of evidence rather than a defect in the model, and that the attempt to force all learning into propositional certainty is what generates the familiar puzzles about the import of imprecise observations.
