Construct a short humorous scene to the following specifications. Superiority theory, incongruity theory, and benign-violation theory must all judge the scene funny, but each must locate the fun in a different feature of the scene rather than in the same feature described three ways. Relief theory must have no explanatory grip on the scene: it should contain no prior build-up of nervous tension, no forbidden impulse, and no repressed material whose discharge a relief theorist could point to. Constraints: the scene must be no more than four sentences; it may not use sexual, taboo, or scatological content; no one may be seriously harmed; and any butt of the joke must be a specific individual whose pretension or minor mishap keeps the derision benign.

The point of the case is to test whether these four theories track genuinely different sources of humour or merely redescribe the same cases in different vocabularies, so the verdicts must be generated from the theories rather than stipulated.

After presenting the scene, do the following. First, for each of the three theories that judge the scene funny, state in that theory's own terms the precise feature that produces the verdict. Second, for each of those three theories, specify the smallest change to the scene that would make that theory alone withdraw its verdict of funniness, or explain why the relevant feature cannot be isolated from the other two. Third, explain why relief theory cannot account for the scene, give the strongest reply a relief theorist could make (for example, by broadening the notion of released tension), and assess whether that reply preserves the theory or empties it of content. Fourth, identify the single structural feature that does the work in excluding relief theory, and say what kind of example the scene would become if that feature were added back.
