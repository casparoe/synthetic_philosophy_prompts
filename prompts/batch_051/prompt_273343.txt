I've been keeping a kind of notebook for my own beliefs, and I've found myself following a policy that I'm not sure I can defend. The policy is this: an observation gets to raise or lower my confidence in a general claim only if I made the observation while I was deliberately checking that claim, or at least while the claim was live for me as something to be tested. I do not give evidential weight to things I merely happen to notice in the course of the day.

The dispute that made me want to examine this came up over dinner. My housemate, who has read some philosophy of science, argued that because 'all ravens are black' is logically equivalent to 'all non-black things are non-ravens,' the red chair in our kitchen — a non-black thing that is not a raven — confirms that all ravens are black, and any rule that discounts it is irrational. I replied that the chair should not move me at all: I was not sampling non-black things or testing anything about ravens; I was just sitting down with coffee. Yet I do count a black raven as evidence when I go out deliberately to look for ravens with the hypothesis in mind. I can see that, at the level of logical form, Hempel's point is that nothing in the logic distinguishes the chair from the raven, and I don't want to claim the logic is wrong.

My question is about the policy rather than the logic. Is there a principled asymmetry, from the point of view of confirmation, between observations gathered under a deliberate test and observations noticed incidentally? What exactly would ground that asymmetry — something about the sampling process, my attention, what I would have done with a different outcome? Can the policy be restated so that it keeps whatever is right in it without becoming a license to ignore inconvenient evidence? And under what conditions, if any, should I let the red chair count, or refuse to let the deliberately spotted raven count?
