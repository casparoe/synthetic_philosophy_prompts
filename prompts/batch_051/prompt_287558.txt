I'm a novelist, researching a book about an art advisor who makes a living buying under-attributed paintings from sellers who don't know what they're sitting on. Here's the asymmetry I can't shake. A lawyer who trades on a confidential merger tip goes to prison. A dealer who spots that a canvas catalogued as circle of Rembrandt in a provincial auction is actually by Rembrandt himself, and buys it for a song, gets a profile in the art press. Both are pure trades on an informational edge: the seller's ignorance is doing a lot of the profit-making, and the trade probably wouldn't happen if both sides knew the same thing. I've been reading around no-trade theorems and the epistemology of markets, and they seem to make both cases look epistemically suspect, not just legally different. Is there a defensible principle that justifies treating these so differently, or is the asymmetry mostly a class-bound superstition about which kinds of knowing-more are admirable? If you defend it, defend it against the hard version where the only difference is the type of information and the venue of the trade.
