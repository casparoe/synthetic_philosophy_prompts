Hempel's The Function of General Laws in History is the theory side. He argues that explaining an event means showing it follows deductively from general laws plus initial conditions. Historians almost never do this fully; instead they give what he calls an explanation sketch, where the laws are left implicit because they're trivial or too complicated to state, but the sketch could in principle be filled in. That's really appealing to me because it gives one standard for explanation, it connects historical explanation to prediction, and it treats history as continuous with science rather then as some special mysterious narrative thing.

But here's the firm intuition I can't shake. When I actually read good narrative history, it doesn't feel like a sketch of a deductive argument. Take the standard explanation of why the First World War broke out: you trace the sequence of decisions, misperceptions, alliance commitments, the way each actor reasoned about their options. The narrative gives understanding, but there's no general law about great powers being dragged into war by alliance webs, and if there were it would be either false or vacuous. The explanation works by making the event intelligible as the result of human actions and choices, not by subsuming it under a law. So narrative explanation seems to do real explanatory work that the covering-law model can't capture.

I don't want to be told which side wins.
