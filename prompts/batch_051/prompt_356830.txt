In my environmental ethics PhD program, two people in my cohort got into a long argument at our reading group, and I honestly could not untangle it. We were reading John Stuart Mill's essay On Nature (from his Three Essays on Religion), where Mill claims, roughly, that nature is a scene of constant violent death and that the fact that something is natural gives us no reason to treat it as good or worth preserving. That prompted a fight about habitat destruction.

Jordan said Mill's point has an underappreciated implication: if a healthy ecosystem is not a peaceful garden but a war zone of predation, starvation, and parasites, then clearing a forest or draining a marsh might reduce the total amount of suffering among wild animals. Robin shot back that Mill's argument is about justification, not about welfare accounting; the fact that nature is cruel does not mean it is net negative, and destroying a habitat ends all the positive experiences of the animals who would have lived there. Jordan replied that this is exactly the kind of "nature is good" thinking Mill warned against, because you are assuming the lives produced by the ecosystem are on average worth living without evidence. Robin answered that Jordan is the one doing the assuming: the burden is on someone who wants to destroy an existing habitat to show that the animals it displaces or kills are not made worse off, whereas Jordan's argument just counts future possible lives as if they were a debit.

Can you adjudicate the exchange move by move? Specifically: who is right about what Mill's argument does and does not entail, and who is more right overall about whether habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering? Please answer in American English.
