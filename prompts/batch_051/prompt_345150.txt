When a government agency wants to know how much a safety regulation is worth, the standard move is to infer values from choices people already make. The value of a statistical life isnt based on asking anyone directly: it comes from stuff like the extra pay workers demand for riskier jobs, or how much more someone will pay for a car with a better crash rating. Choice ends up being treated as the raw data for utility. But then, in the same broad practice, analysts get skittish when the observed choices look self-defeating. For a smoker, they might quietly redo the calculation as if the person did not discount the future so steeply; for someone who passes up cheap safety equipment, they treat the observed behavior as not really revealing anything. So choice is the gold standard for utility except when it isn't. What view of utility and its relation to choice does this practice presuppose? Is it committed to utility being whatever people actually choose, or to something more idealized — utility as what people would choose if they were informed and rational — or can the practice stay silent on that? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the two halves of the practice hang together, and tell me what would have to change if you replaced the presupposed view with, say, a hedonist or objective-list account of wellbeing.
