I'm a PhD student in moral psychology and I've been stuck on the Browning/Goldhagen debate. Browning's "ordinary men" story seems to lean pretty heavily on the classic situationist psychology — Milgram's obedience experiments especially, but also the Stanford prison experiment — for the idea that ordinary people can be pushed into doing terrible things by the situation alone. But both of those have taken a beating over the last fifteen years: the Milgram archive suggests the subjects who went all the way were less blindly obedient and more trusting of the experimenter and invested in a scientific goal they saw as legitimate, and the Stanford prison study is now widely regarded as compromised, with Zimbardo nudging the guards rather than letting the situation do the work. What I want to figure out is whether Browning's argument actually survives this. Is the "situation turns anyone into a perpetrator" psychology load-bearing, or can the real explanatory work — incremental escalation, group conformity, not wanting to let your comrades down, the way the battalion broke the killing into bureaucratic roles — be salvaged without Milgram and Zimbardo? And if it can be repaired, does the repaired version still answer Goldhagen's claim that the men were willing executioners shaped by an eliminationist culture, or has the argument quietly shifted from "situation, not character" to something weaker like "ordinary people can acclimatize to killing when an organization makes it normal"? I'd like a sharp analysis of which premise was doing the real work, whether anything worth calling Browning's position survives, and what the strongest repairable version would still have to concede to Goldhagen.
