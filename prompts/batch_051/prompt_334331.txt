I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on centers on a private charter city and a seastead whose founders each believe they have exited the state. I've been developing a concept for the book that I call the conservation of governance: there is no exit from the state in the sense those characters mean, because every attempt to detach produces a replacement rather than a vacuum. A seastead without a territorial sovereign still has to handle dispute resolution, contract enforcement, defense, and shared infrastructure, and those functions are political authority no matter how contractually they are styled; a charter city remains tied into its host state's currency, emergency services, and judicial backstops. So political authority is not abolished by exit—it is redistributed, rescaled, or hidden inside private agreements. The corollary I want the book to dramatize is that the meaningful question is never whether one can opt out of the state but which governance arrangement one has opted into, and on what terms of accountability. Is this a genuine concept or just a metaphor in physics dress? Does it already exist in some other form, what does it get right, and where does it break down—in particular, what does it wrongly count as governance, and what kinds of exit really do reduce political authority?
