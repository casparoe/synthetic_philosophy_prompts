Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether all evidence is ultimately certain evidence. He held what I will call the certainty-first view: whenever I think I have learned something merely uncertain, I have in fact learned something else for certain — a proposition about my perceptual state, about what was said, about how a document looked — and the correct response is to conditionalize on that proposition in the ordinary Bayesian way. I held that there are cases in which the most honest description of one's evidence is a shift in the probabilities over a partition, with no element of the partition becoming certain, and that the right rule for such cases is Jeffrey conditionalization: the new probabilities on the partition are treated as inputs, and every other proposition keeps its old probability conditional on each element of the partition. We traded schematic examples of ambiguous glances and smudged documents all evening and reached no verdict, because each of us could always redescribe the other's case in his own terms. I want a case that actually separates the two rules.

Construct me an ordinary-life scenario — nothing stranger than a misread document or an ambiguous glance, no rare illusions, no contrived testimony, no demons or perfect predictors — in which an agent receives genuinely uncertain evidence over a small partition {E1, ..., En} whose new probabilities q_i are all strictly between 0 and 1. Then do the following.

State the agent's prior over the partition and over one downstream proposition H. Compute the posterior over H that Jeffrey conditionalization produces from the q_i. Then pick the most natural certain redescription available in the scenario — the proposition about the agent's perceptual state or about what was seen, heard, or read that a certainty-first epistemologist would say the agent learned for certain — and compute the posterior over H that standard conditionalization produces on that redescription. Show that the two posteriors diverge, and say exactly which feature of the case does the work: is it that the redescription's likelihoods under the partition elements cannot be made proportional to the q_i, or something else? State what would happen if that feature were removed, and under what condition on the likelihoods the two rules would coincide.

Then give whichever of the two rules the case counts against its strongest defense. If it is the certainty-first view, must its defender enrich the redescription, and, if so, at what price — and does the enriched move amount to abandoning the certainty-first view in all but name? If it is the Jeffrey view, must its defender admit that the q_i are not rationally held unless they were themselves generated from the likelihoods of some redescription? Finally, state whether a version of the scenario survives that defense, or whether every attempt to separate the rules ends up showing only that Jeffrey conditionalization is as good as its inputs.

The point of the case is to settle whether irreducibly uncertain evidence is a real epistemic kind or only evidence with a missing redescription. A case that simply stipulates the q_i and the redescription so that the numbers come out different, without showing how the scenario generates both, will not settle our argument; the divergence has to arise from the setup, not from definition.
