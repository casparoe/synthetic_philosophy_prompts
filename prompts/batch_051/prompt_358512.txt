I cover science and food policy for a magazine, and I've written a fair amount about the welfare question in aquaculture — whether salmon, shrimp, and crabs are conscious pain-feelers or just showing nociceptive reflexes. A few years ago I adopted a rule: if a study about crustacean pain or fish suffering is funded by aquaculture companies, I treat its conclusions as basically non-evidence. I'll still read it, but only to find the flaw. If it's funded by a welfare group or a government research council, I treat it as my bottom line.

Then I caught myself the other week. I was complaining to a colleague about an industry-funded trial that used a small sample and an unusual pain test. But I'd covered a charity-funded study the month before that used an even smaller sample and a weirder pain test, and I'd written it up dead straight. I realized my rule isnt symmetrical. My question: is it ever defensible to discount findings by funder interest like this, given that I can't run my own experiments and the people paying for the work do seem to get the results they pay for? If so, how should I set the discount so it's something more principled than whichever side I happened to trust first? Or should I just read the methods every time and drop the funder check entirely?
