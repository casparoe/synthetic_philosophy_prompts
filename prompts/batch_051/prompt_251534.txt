I have a standing rule about forecasts: I refuse to acquire a piece of information, no matter how reliable or how cheap, unless I can say in advance which action it could change. This past quarter that meant not opening free analyst reports on our sector and not reading the central bank's latest monetary policy statement, because prices, hiring, and our capital plan were already fixed until the next review; the only effect of the new information would have been to make my settled decisions feel less settled. I know the standard theorem that free information cannot reduce a rational agent's expected utility, since she can always choose to ignore it after seeing it. But I doubt that theorem applies to the agent I actually am. Is my rule rationally defensible, or is it motivated ignorance dressed up as policy? Specifically: what assumptions must hold for value-of-information reasoning to go through, which of those assumptions is doing the real work in my case, and is there a restated version of the rule that preserves whatever protection it gives without becoming a license to avoid relevant evidence?
