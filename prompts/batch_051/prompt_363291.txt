In my seminar on Kant’s political philosophy we have been studying the First Supplement to Perpetual Peace, where Kant argues that the guarantee of perpetual peace is supplied by nature itself. As I understand the text, his thought is that nature, working through the self-interest, vanity, and mutual antagonism that drive human beings into conflict, also produces the very order they refuse to bring about from duty: within states, the unsocial inclinations force people to accept coercive law as the only way to secure their own ends; among states, the same pressures push them toward a federal league, even though no state would submit to such a league from moral motives alone. Kant adds that this guarantee is not sufficient to prophesy future peace with theoretical certainty, but that it is sufficient on practical grounds and makes it a duty to work toward that end.

A classmate defended this as a version of the invisible-hand analogy: just as private vices can produce public benefits in a market, the selfish motives that realists say make moral hope naive are the very instruments through which nature produces peace. On that reading, the realists are right about the inputs and the idealists are right about the outcome, so hoping for progress need not be wishfulness.

I want to audit that analogy properly. What maps onto what between nature’s guarantee in Kant and the invisible-hand picture, and where does the mapping break down? I am especially interested in the status of the guarantee: does the invisible-hand analogy import a view of historical necessity or of the transparency of self-interest to its own consequences that Kant is careful not to claim? And once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion — that the idealist’s hope is rationally warranted rather than merely comforting — survive, or was it being carried by the analogy the whole time?
