I am a PhD student in the anthropology of religion, and in two weeks I have to lead a seventy-five-minute seminar for third-year undergraduates on ritual substitution and the transfer of guilt — the family of rites in which a community's fault is laid on a victim, an animal, or an object that is then expelled, destroyed, or otherwise removed, and the community treats the matter as closed. I do not want you to explain the material to me; I want a plan for the session. The students have already read on sacrifice and on purity and pollution, but nothing on substitution or scapegoating. Please give me the order in which the ideas must be introduced and why each depends on the one before it; the one concrete example that should carry the whole session and what it must have and what it must lack; the misconceptions I should expect — I suspect the main ones will be that guilt is a kind of substance that gets literally moved from one being to another, and that the victim is being punished rather than substituted — and the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off; and a single question I can ask at the end to check whether they have grasped the difference between a transfer that changes what the community is entitled to do or believe and a transfer that merely redescribes a change in how the community feels. I had thought of using the day-of-atonement goat, the one over which sins are confessed and which is then sent into the wilderness, as the carrying example, but I worry it brings too much theological baggage; tell me whether to keep it or replace it and with what. I also want the plan to make one puzzle land rather than get smoothed over: the substitute is so often required to be intact, pure, or otherwise set apart even though the rite then treats it as the bearer of the community's fault. Finally, be explicit about what would change your mind about the plan — which alternative understanding of how the transfer works would force you to reorder the session or change the example, and where your map of likely student misconceptions is most likely to be wrong — and say what I should openly leave out or decline to settle at this level. Please write in continuous prose, as in an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
