I recently spent a day helping my kid build an elaborate sandcastle, with towers and a crenellated wall and everything, and then watched a wave flatten it in about ten seconds. My father-in-law, who studied physics, joked that the wet pile of sand afterward was actually more complex than the castle, because its exact configuration was more disordered and improbable. That sat wrong with me, but I couldn't immediately say why. The castle needed a long sequence of deliberate steps (packing, carving, stacking) and the pile needed basically one. I later read a bit about assembly theory, which argues that complexity should be measured by the minimal number of assembly steps needed to build an object from a fixed stock of basic building blocks, the assembly index. By that measure the castle comes out high and the heap comes out low, even if the heap is harder to describe in information-theoretic terms. I want to understand what follows if we actually took this view seriously. Please describe the most important implications of the assembly-index view of complexity for how we should think about designed objects, living organisms, and random-looking arrangements, and for what we should count as complex at all.
