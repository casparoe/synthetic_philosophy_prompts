Something that's been bugging me. The criminal justice system is loudly committed to impartiality: jurors get dismissed for any personal tie to the defendent, and judges are supposed to recuse themselves if there's even the appearance of partiality. But at the same time the law is full of carve-outs for loyalty — you can't be forced to testify against your spouse, and communications with your lawyer or clergy get protected, but your best friend gets no such shield. Outside court, meanwhile, we mostly treat someone who rats out a close friend as a rat, not as a model citizen. What theory of impartiality and loyalty does this whole setup presuppose? Does it actually presuppose that impartiality is the default virtue and loyalty is just a bias we tolerate for pragmatic reasons, or does it presuppose that there are distinct roles where loyalty is the right call and others where it isn't? And if its the role-based story, what exactly is the difference between a juror and a spouse that makes loyalty a disqualification in one and something the law protects in the other? I can't tell if the system is coherent or just a pile of compromises.
