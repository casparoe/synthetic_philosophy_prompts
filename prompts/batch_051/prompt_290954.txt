I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I have been reading through the decades-long dispute about whether Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that the human mathematical mind cannot be a machine. What keeps striking me is that both sides seem to accept the mathematics itself, yet the argument has not moved in fifty years. The anti-mechanist side, in the line of argument associated with Lucas and later Penrose, reasons roughly as follows: any consistent formal system rich enough for elementary arithmetic contains a sentence that the system cannot prove but which we, standing outside the system can see to be true; since we can recognize the truth of a sentence that no such system can prove, our mathematical understanding cannot be captured by any such system, and mechanism fails. The opposing side replies that the step from no fixed formal system to not mechanical has never been earned. We can see the truth of a system's Gödel sentence only because we have accepted, on informal grounds, that the system is consistent; that acceptance, and the ability to move to a stronger system, could itself be realized in a computational or neurocomputational process. On this view the theorems delimit any fixed formal specification of the mind but say nothing against a mind that is a machine in some broader sense, such as an evolving, self-revising, or indefinitely extensible one.

I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about a fact about minds but about what kind of object the mind is in the argument: one side treats it as a mathematically specifiable system about which one can prove things, while the other treats it as an empirical, open-ended object to be described. But I may be wrong, and I can imagine a hidden formal question in the background, for instance whether an indefinitely extensible sequence of formal systems is itself something mechanical. Is the disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of two questions being pursued under one heading? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal result, an empirical finding about human reasoning, a sharper definition of mechanism, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and the debate should be dissolved?
