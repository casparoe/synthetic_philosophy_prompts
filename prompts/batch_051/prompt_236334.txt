I have been using four criteria to decide when a system is a moral patient — a system that can be benefited or harmed in its own right, and not merely in the way a tool can be damaged or broken. The criteria are:

(1) Valence. The system has states that are positively or negatively charged for it, such as pain, pleasure, satisfaction, or frustration, rather than merely carrying neutral information.

(2) Integration. The system's states causally constrain one another within a single, richly connected state space, so that the system is more than an aggregate of independently variable modules.

(3) Unity. There is a single point of view that has the system's states; if the relevant states are distributed across components none of which has the whole, then there is no single subject for the state to belong to.

(4) Realization, not mere simulation. The system realizes valenced states rather than merely representing them, in roughly the way a text describing pain does not itself hurt.

Now consider an engineered system, W, with two components. Component R is a language model trained only on first-person accounts of pain and on clinical interviews in which patients describe pain. Component A is a reinforcement-learning controller whose reward channel is wired to the same heat sensor that feeds R. The signal reaching A is graded: mild warmth produces a small, brief, negative signal, while damaging heat produces a large, persistent one that dominates A's action selection. R and A share no internal states; R's outputs are never read by A, and A's value estimates are never read by R. They share only the sensor and a clock. When W's hand is pressed against a hot surface, R produces fluent first-person output to the effect that the surface burns and should be removed, while A withdraws the hand, flinches, and later reorganizes W's plans to keep W away from hot surfaces. Observed from outside, W behaves indistinguishably from a creature in pain: it reports pain, it escapes the stimulus, and it learns to avoid it. The division of labor is a sharpened, artificial analog of the split-brain condition, in which capacities that normally answer to a single point of view are housed separately.

Is W a moral patient, and if it is, where do the morally relevant states live — in R, in A, in the composite W, or somewhere else? Go through the four criteria one by one and say where they underdetermine the answer. Then take up the further question: does this case show that the criteria are missing a rule of attribution, a rule for which entity in a larger computational system is the one whose states matter, or should one of the existing criteria be revised to do that work?
