My flatmate has an argument that seems to settle the bivalence debate in about two minutes flat, and I can't figure out where it goes wrong. It runs:

(1) Excluded middle holds: for any statement S, "S or not-S" is true.
(2) A disjunction is true only if at least one of its disjuncts is true.
(3) So for any statement S, either S is true or not-S is true.
(4) If not-S is true, then S is false.
(5) Therefore every statement S is either true or false: no truth-value gaps.

I'd most like to keep (1) if I can, since rejecting excluded middle feels like it should be a last resort, but I don't want to kid myself about what the other exits cost. So wich premise should I give up, why did it look so secure, and what does each of the standard positions have to swallow once they take their exit? Cover the people who reject excluded middle outright, the supervaluationists if they're the ones who take the other exit, whoever would attack the step about negation, and anyone who thinks the argument is actually sound.
