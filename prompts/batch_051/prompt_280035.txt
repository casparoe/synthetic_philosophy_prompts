We have been arguing about meritocracy, and someone at our seminar laid out the following line of thought. It appears to prove that in a meritocracy nobody deserves their earnings — and, worse, that for parallel reasons nobody deserves praise, blame, reward, or punishment at all. We are unwilling to accept that conclusion, but each step, taken alone, looks plausible to us. Here is the argument.

(1) In a meritocracy, a person's earnings are supposed to track her contribution, and her contribution is a function of two things: her talents and her effort.

(2) Nobody deserves her talents. Talents are fixed by genetic endowment and early environment, neither of which she chose.

(3) Transmission principle: if nobody deserves a factor, and an outcome is produced by that factor, then nobody deserves the outcome.

(4) Nobody deserves her own effort. To exert effort, a person must already possess traits that make sustained effort possible — focus, energy, conscientiousness, the capacity to defer gratification — and those traits are themselves unchosen endowments, so by (3) any effort they produce is undeserved. If effort is instead an uncaused act of will, not produced by any trait or circumstance, then whether a person exerts it is a brute fact about her, and a brute fact is no more a desert basis than the genetic lottery is. Either way, effort is not something she can deserve.

(5) Therefore, by (2), (3), and (4), nobody deserves her contribution, and by (1) nobody deserves the earnings that track it.

(6) The same reasoning applies outside the economy. A person's character is given, and her actions flow either from that given character or from brute acts of will. So by (3), nobody deserves praise, blame, reward, or punishment either.

(7) Therefore there is no desert of the kind meritocracy requires, and more broadly no moral desert at all.

Tell us where the argument goes wrong. Which premise or step should we reject, and why did it look as though it had to be accepted? We would most like to keep (2) — the thought that people do not earn their starting endowments — and the parallel between economic and moral desert drawn in (6). What do we have to give up to keep those, and what does each main way out of the argument cost? Please answer in American English.
