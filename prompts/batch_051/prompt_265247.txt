In my Bayesian epistemology class we read Jaynes's paper Prior Probabilities, and he has this bit where he says roughly that when all you know are some expectation values, the maximum-entropy distribution is the only one that uses that information and nothing else—any other distribution would secretly be assuming extra facts you don't have, so maxent is the unique "least biased" prior. I get the intuitive pull, but when I tried to turn it into an argument against the arbitrariness objection, the whole thing started to feel less solid.

For my paper I'm looking at four ways someone could cash out this idea that maxent priors are non-arbitrary because they don't smuggle in ungiven information. Could you give each one a score out of 10 for how well it actually answers the worry that maxent is just one convention among many, and rank them from best to worst with a sentence or two each?

a) The information-theoretic reading: maxent maximizes entropy subject to constraints, and entropy is mathematically the unique measure of "missing information," so the prior is privileged.

b) The epistemic reading: rational degrees of belief are supposed to be as close to uniform as possible given the evidence, and maxent captures that, so it's the uniquely rational prior.

c) The decision-theoretic reading: maxent priors minimize worst-case expected loss when you don't know the true prior, so they're justified by conservative betting norms.

d) The pragmatic reading: maxent priors aren't uniquely rational or uniquely non-arbitrary, but they have nice convergence and invariance properties, and that's enough.

I don't want a literature review—just your honest ranking and which reading you'd lean on if you wanted to defend maxent against someone who says it's still arbitrary.
