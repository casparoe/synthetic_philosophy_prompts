Practically every democracy organises political life through parties: parties select candidates, impose programmes, discipline voting, and run adversarial campaigns in which each side arrives with a settled position on nearly every issue. Simone Weil argued that the party form has a logic of its own that is hostile to truth. I will give you the substance of her view so that you need not rely on recall. On her picture, a party's first and defining aim is its own growth; it will therefore tend to suppress independence of mind among its members, since open dissent strengthens rival parties, to punish members who follow their own judgement, and to collect and inflame collective passions rather than letting individual consciences deliberate. Yet our institutions still treat parties as the normal, neutral machinery of democratic representation.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose about where sound political judgement comes from, about the relation between individual conscience and collective action, and about whether organised partisan conflict helps or hinders the discovery of political truth? Reconstruct the most defensible implicit theory behind party politics, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Then give your assessment: if Weil's description is even partly accurate, does party politics fail by its own best lights, and what would an alternative political form have to look like if the presuppositions fail?
