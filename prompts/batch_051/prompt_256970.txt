I have been trying to decide whether a parallel I keep noticing between a point about rational belief and a common software practice is a real identity of structure or just a coincidence of wording, and I would like your help.

The point about belief is Van Fraassen's Reflection principle, which I understand in plain terms as follows. If I now consider the possibility that tomorrow I will be 80% certain of some proposition P, then conditional on that very possibility, I should now be 80% certain of P myself. My future opinion is not merely another piece of evidence to be weighed against my present one; it is authoritative in advance. I gather the standard objection is anticipated irrationality: if I know that tomorrow I will be drunk and will be 95% sure I can safely drive home, Reflection seems to require that I be 95% sure now, which is plainly wrong.

The software practice is the automatic update. A device set to auto-update follows a deferential policy: when the next version of a program becomes available, the device adopts that version's behavior wholesale and retires the current version, without independently re-adjudicating the change. That looks like the same structural move at the level of a machine: the present configuration treats the future configuration as the authority.

Here is the mapping I have in mind. My present beliefs correspond to the currently running version, and my future beliefs to the next installed version. A proposition corresponds to a claim about how the system should behave or respond. Reflection corresponds to the auto-update default of deferring to the next version. Anticipated drunkenness corresponds to a known bad forthcoming update. Pinning a version, or disabling updates, corresponds to Ulysses binding himself to the mast against a future self he does not trust. Rolling back an update corresponds to later selves concluding that an earlier version had been right after all. And things like version history, reproducible builds, and canary releases seem like they should have epistemic counterparts, but I cannot work out what those counterparts would be in a principled way.

I can already see one possible disanalogy: auto-update does not form an expectation about what the next version will do and then match that expectation; it simply runs whatever arrives. A future version is also written by other people rather than produced by the device's own future self, though a person's future beliefs are likewise shaped by outside forces. I do not know whether these differences are fatal.

What I want to know is whether this parallel is a genuine identity of structure, a useful analogy, or a confusion. If results are meant to transfer, what exactly would both sides have to share? What is the strongest thing the parallel predicts that turns out false on one side or the other? And does the software side tell us anything about when a present belief should be protected against one's own future revisions, or is that just a metaphor?

At the very end, make the last line begin with "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of GENUINE, PARTIAL, or SUPERFICIAL, and then a one-sentence summary of your assessment.
