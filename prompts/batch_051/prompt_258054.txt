I keep catching myself using "preference" and "bias" in ways that don't map onto anything I can articulate. Here are my gut calls on a bunch of cases:

- I think my brother's refusal to eat octopus because they're intelligent is a legitimate moral preference, but my aunt's refusal to eat any animal because she finds meat gross is a bias.
- I think someone who opposes legal prostitution because they think it degrades women has a real moral position, but someone who opposes it because they find the idea of selling sex icky is just biased. Yet both would say they're acting on a gut feeling of wrongness.
- I think a voter who favors strict immigration rules because they worry about social cohesion has a preference, but a voter who favors the same rules because they grew up in a mostly white town and are uncomfortable around outsiders has a bias. But if you ask both for reasons, the second one might say the same thing about cohesion.
- I think my own aversion to eating insects is just a preference about my own plate, but if I tried to stop a whole culture from eating insects I'd call that a bias.
- I think a doctor who refuses to perform assisted suicide because they believe life is sacred has a legitimate moral preference, but a doctor who refuses to treat gay patients because their religion says homosexuality is wrong has a bias. I want to say the difference is that the first is about an action and the second is about a person, but that doesn't feel like the real line.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? I keep going back and forth on one case I didnt list: my strong opposition to capital punishment. I think it's a preference because I can give a whole argument about state power and irreversibility, but I also know the feeling came first and the argument came after. What does the principle you propose say about that case, and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop?
