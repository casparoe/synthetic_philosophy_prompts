Does the empirical evidence support the claim that what someone believes or expects can alter the contents of their perceptual experience? Be clear about what cognitive penetration would have to be, which effects would not count, and what the philosophical stakes are.

And here's the anonymised student answer:

Cognitive penetration is standardly defined as a direct, semantically coherent effect of a subject's cognitive states on the content or phenomenology of perception. If my belief that bananas are yellow makes a banana-shaped patch look yellower than a neutral patch with the same physical colour, that would be the real thing. The effect has to be direct: if I infer from my belief that the patch must be yellow and then judge that it looks yellow, that is a post-perceptual inference, not a change in the perception itself. Attention effects also don't count, because attention changes which parts of the scene get selected rather than how the selected parts are represented. Perceptual learning is ruled out too, since it is a slow, diachronic change in the system rather than a short-term effect of an occurrent cognitive state.

The memory-colour effects are the best evidence we have. In these experiments, participants match a patch against a comparison stimulus, and they set the comparison differently when the patch is shaped like an object with a typical colour (say, a banana or a heart) than when it is a neutral shape, even though the physical colour is the same. Because the neutral shapes do not show the same shift, the result can't be explained by a general response bias. That looks like high-level knowledge about what colour things typically are reaching down and changing visual processing directly. So what the subject sees is genuinely different in virtue of what they know.

The philosophical upshot should be taken seriously. If vision is not informationally encapsulated, then Fodor's modularity thesis about perception is false. And if beliefs can shape what we see, perception can no longer act as an independent tribunal for checking those beliefs: we'd risk just seeing the world the way we already think it is.
