I retired from a career in the atmospheric sciences — most of it spent on numerical weather prediction and the limits of forecast skill — and have lately been working through philosophy of science and metaphysics in a more systematic way than I could while employed. That combination has produced an argument that troubles me. Each step seems sound, but the conclusion is one I would never have accepted in my working life, and still do not accept now. I would like you to find the failure, if there is one, and to price the available ways of fixing it.

(1) A deterministic system is one whose state at a time, together with its dynamical laws, fixes exactly one state at every later time.

(2) Chaotic deterministic systems exhibit sensitive dependence on initial conditions: two initial states that differ by an arbitrarily small amount separate exponentially in time. In a bounded system, this implies that any finite initial uncertainty eventually grows until it saturates the system's strange attractor; after that horizon, the states consistent with the measurements are spread over effectively the entire range of accessible states.

(3) No real measurement possesses infinite precision, and no physically realizable device can record an exact real-valued state. Every observer we can build, and arguably every observer the physical world can contain, begins with finite uncertainty.

(4) Therefore, for a real chaotic system, no physically realizable observer can predict the individual trajectory beyond a finite horizon. Beyond that horizon, the observer's best forecast is no more informative than a random draw from the system's long-run statistical distribution over the attractor.

(5) If presented with two systems — one deterministic and chaotic, the other genuinely stochastic — that share the same long-run distribution, an observer could hope to tell them apart only by possessing exact initial states or by successfully predicting an individual trajectory beyond the horizon. Premises (3) and (4) close off both avenues.

(6) A distinction that no physically realizable observer could draw, even with arbitrarily large but still finite resources, is not a distinction in nature itself; it belongs only to the mathematical descriptions we choose.

(7) Hence, for the atmosphere and similar systems, the claim that the underlying dynamics are deterministic rather than stochastic is not a factual claim about nature; it is a preference for one formally equivalent model over another.

I regard (7) as a serious overreach, and I cannot locate the step that overreaches. I am prepared to be told that the argument is valid and that one of its sober premises must go. I would most like to keep premises (1) and (2).

Please provide a diagnosis in no more than two paragraphs. Then append a JSON object with the following fields:

- verdict: either "valid" (no inference step fails; the correct response is to reject a premise) or "invalid" (a step in the reasoning fails).
- failing_step: if the argument is invalid, the number of the premise at which the failure first appears, or the inference between two premises (for example, "from 5 to 6"); if valid, null.
- flaw_kind: a short category such as "false premise", "invalid inference", "equivocation", "modal slide", "quantifier shift", or "other".
- flaw_statement: a one- or two-sentence statement of exactly what goes wrong.
- why_it_looked_right: why a competent reader would find the faulty step plausible.
- cost_of_revision: what one must surrender if the problematic premise or step is rejected or revised.
- least_costly_alternative: if there is a different premise whose rejection would be cheaper or more defensible, give its number and say why.
- surviving_claim: the strongest nearby thesis that survives your diagnosis, in one sentence.
