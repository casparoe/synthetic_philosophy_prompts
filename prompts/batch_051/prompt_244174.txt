Before I read yet another theory of courage, I want the spec instead. Most of what I've read feels built for soldiers facing death in battle, and I want to know what a theory of courage — and maybe valor — for a mostly peaceful, civilian society has to get right. So give me the desiderata: the cases it has to handle (a firefighter going into a burning building, a whistleblower who knows it will cost them there job, a patient enduring years of treatment, someone telling a hostile crowd a truth they don't want to hear), the distinctions it can't collapse (courage vs. fearlessness, vs. recklessness, vs. mere endurance, physical vs. moral courage, doing a risky thing once vs. sticking something out for years, and what if anything is left of valor once you take away the battlefield), and whatever the psychology of fear and risk-taking has established that a theory can't contradict. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and say whether it's negotiable. Then score the usual contenders — Aristotle's doctrine of the mean, courage-as-overcoming-fear, courage-as-willpower/self-control — against the list, and tell me which single requirement does the most damage to the most popular view. Make the main deliverable a table with columns: requirement, why it's required, negotiable?, and which views it threatens. Keep any prose around the table short.
