Here is a reconstruction of the classical problem of the first angelic sin. I want a precise diagnosis of the argument, not a survey of the literature.

Assume the traditional picture: a wholly good God creates purely spiritual creatures, the angels, with clear intellects and free wills. They are created good, and yet before any human being exists, the first sin occurs — the fall of Satan and the rebel angels. The problem is to explain how a creature that begins wholly good could be the first origin of evil.

Consider the following argument that the fall is impossible.

(1) A wholly good cause cannot be the source of evil.
(2) Therefore, because every angel is created by a wholly good God, every angel is wholly good in nature and will at its first moment.
(3) Nothing prior to the first evil act could have corrupted the angel who commits it; so at the moment immediately before that act, that angel is still wholly good in nature and will.
(4) An evil act can proceed only from a defect already present in the agent at the moment before the act.
(5) A wholly good agent contains no such defect.
(6) Therefore, no angel could perform the first evil act.
(7) But by hypothesis the fall of Satan and the rebel angels is the first evil act; hence the fall could not have happened and did happen.

Is the argument valid? If it is not, identify the first step at which it fails and explain exactly what that step assumes without entitlement. If it is valid, state which premise is false and give the most defensible replacement. I am especially uncertain about premise (4): it seems to require that the defect from which an evil act proceeds already exists before the act, but a first sin looks like an act in which the defect and the act arise together. Am I missing a way to keep (4), or is that where the argument breaks?
