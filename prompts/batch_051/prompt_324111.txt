I have been reading Abelard's ethics alongside some criminal law, and I am struck by what may be a genuine structural parallel or may be my own conflation. Abelard seems to work with a three-layered picture of the moral act: there is the inclination or suggestion, which is not yet sin; there is the consent by which the will endorses it, which alone is the sin; and there is the outward deed, which follows the consent and adds nothing to the guilt. If you consent to a killing, you are fully guilty even if the blow never lands. Criminal law looks to me as though it runs the same threefold division with different emphases: it separates motive, the mental element, and the physical act; motive is mostly irrelevant, the mental element determines which crime the act is, and the same physical movement can be an accident, a lesser offense, or murder depending on what was in the agent's head. Attempt doctrine seems to meet Abelard almost exactly: when a plan fails, what is punished is the intention together with the steps taken toward it, not any harm actually done.

But then the parallel starts to pull apart. The law also requires an outward act, and generally will not punish a decision that never leaves the head, whereas Abelard's account locates the entirety of the sin in an inward consent. And most legal systems grade completed offenses more severely than attempts, treating the result and the harm as aggravating, while Abelard's consent-only account seems to deny that the deed alters the degree of guilt. I am asking for a diagnosis rather than a verdict on either side: is this resemblance deep — wrongness genuinely located in the will in both — or superficial, with the law driven by deterrence, harm, and public order in a way Abelard's account is driven by guilt before God? What would both sides have to share for results to transfer between them, and what exactly does the divergence over grading completed versus attempted wrongs get wrong on the legal side if the parallel were taken literally?
