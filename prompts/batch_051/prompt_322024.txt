My philosophy-of-biology professor has been handing down verdicts all term on what she'll call a single organism, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer the rule. So far:

- A human: yes, obviously.
- A Portuguese man o' war: yes. She got a little annoyed about it, said it's one embryo that buds off all those weird specialized zooids, and the zooids cant survive on their own, so calling it a colony is just a visual prejudice.
- A lichen: yes. Her reason was that the fungus and the alga are so metabolically stitched together in the thallus that they run one economy, and when it reproduces asexually they send off little clumps that carry both partners.
- An ant colony: yes, straight-up superorganism. She said the colony has its own life cycle from founding queen to the next round of winged reproductives, and a lone worker is a part, not a whole.
- A single worker ant: no.
- A flock of starlings: no.

The next case she's making us think about is a reef coral colony plus the zooxanthellae living inside its cells, taken as one system. The algae do most of the photosynthesis and hand the coral a lot of the product; the coral provides shelter and nitrogen; if the algae go, the coral bleaches and usually dies in time unless it can reacquire them. But in the coral species she described, the larvae start out algae-free and have to pick the zooxanthellae up from the surrounding water — the algae aren't bundled into the larvae or eggs.

Predict her verdict on that case. Say which rule or rules she's actually running, what rival rule fits her old verdicts just as well, and give your best guess with reasons. Then tell me the cleanest extra case you'd ask about to catch the two rules giving different answers again, ideally one that pushes them apart in the other direction.
