I've been going down a rabbit hole about how the Roman Republic fell, and the office of tribune of the plebs really stuck with me. From what I can tell, it started as a way for ordinary people to check the senate — they could block actions and were supposed to be physically untouchable — but by the end ambitious politicians were using it to paralyze the state or ram things through outside the senate. So I got thinking: what if a modern country built an office like that, but with a few new rules?

Here's my design. A country creates a Tribune, one person elected each year, but only people in the bottom third of the personal income distribution can run, and only people in that bottom third can vote in the Tribune election. The Tribune has one power: within 30 days of any law, regulation, or executive order taking effect, they can formally interpose against it. That immediately suspends the measure and sends it to a binding vote of the same bottom-third electorate within 60 days. If that vote rejects the measure, it dies and the Tribune stays in office. If the vote upholds it, the Tribune loses office and immunity on the spot, any other interpositions they've filed are void, and they can be prosecuted like any ordinary citizen. There is no limit on how many interpositions the Tribune can make during their single one-year term, they can't be reelected, and they cant hold another elected office for five years after. While in office they are also immune from arrest and prosecution except for treason.

You are an expert in institutional design with a good knowledge of Roman history. I want not just a verdict on whether this is good or bad, but a careful analysis of how the thing would actually behave. Before you answer, brainstorm three or four failure modes or ways it could be gamed. Then walk me through: who would run and who would win such an election, how parties and rich interests would try to use or capture the Tribune, what this would do to the legislature's incentives to draft laws, whether any of those failure modes can be fixed without gutting the whole idea, and whether the referendum plus the reelection ban actually stops the office from drifting into the kind of chaos people blame for Rome's collapse — or just produces a new kind of mess.
