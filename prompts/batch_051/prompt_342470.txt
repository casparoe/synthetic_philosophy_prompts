I am not an academic, and I came to this through amateur astronomy rather than formal philosophy, but I have been turning over a question about what the laws of nature actually are. Two positions keep emerging. According to the first, a law like Newton's law of gravitation is not something in the world that pushes planets around or compels them; it is only a concise description of a very regular pattern in how things in fact move. According to the second, laws are genuine features of reality that govern or constrain how events unfold, and that is why new events so reliably fall in with the old pattern. I gather the first position is often called Humean and the second anti-Humean or governing, though I do not want to lean on labels. I have been trying to separate the two with a concrete case instead of intuition, and the discovery of Neptune keeps looking like the right test.

The story as I know it: by the 1840s, Uranus was repeatedly deviating from where Newtonian calculations said it should be once the pulls of the sun and the known planets were accounted for. Working backward from that stubborn mismatch, Urbain Le Verrier in France, and along a parallel track John Couch Adams in England, inferred that an unseen planet of a particular mass and orbit was disturbing Uranus; in 1846 Johann Galle in Berlin pointed a telescope at the spot Le Verrier's arithmetic indicated, and Neptune turned up almost exactly there. I would like you to take that episode and analyze it through the two positions I described. Tell me how each side would characterize what actually happened: what made the prediction possible, what Newton's law was doing in the reasoning, and whether the law was doing any work beyond compactly recording the regularities already observed. Then reverse direction and say what Neptune does to the two positions themselves: does the episode tip the balance, can each side absorb it without strain, and are there parts of the story that neither view especially wants to talk about? I am less interested in a final verdict than in a disciplined account of where each position has a natural explanation, where it has to stretch or borrow from its rival, and where it should stay silent.
