I'm doing a food ethics module this term and we keep coming back to what "vegan" actually means, because it matters for the arguments but no-one will pin it down. The usual defintion is about avoiding animal products where this is possible and practicable, but that last clause does loads of quiet work and it never gets cashed out properly. Could you give me a working definition of veganism (or of "acting vegan" if you think the label matters less than the practice) and run it through these cases?

- A student in London who eats a fully plant-based diet, buys no leather or wool, and checks medication ingredients. I'd count this as clearly vegan.
- A Maasai herder in a drought-prone part of Kenya whose diet is mostly milk, blood and meat from his own cattle, because growing crops there is not really viable. I want to say this can still count as vegan, but I can't make the usual definition do that without cheating.
- Someone who eats oysters and mussels because they think bivalves probably can't suffer.
- Someone with a serious chronic condition whose only affordable medication contains lactose, and who has asked for an alternative and been told there isn't one.
- A lone parent in a UK food desert, on a very tight budget, who takes food-bank parcels that include dairy and tinned meat, because turning them down would mean the kids go hungry.
- Someone who eats a plant-based diet purely because they prefer the taste, but happily wears a second-hand leather jacket and doesn't think animals matter morally. I'd say this is clearly not vegan even though the diet matches.

I'm after a definition that gets as many of these as possible right, or an honest verdict on which ones it can't handle and why. Also, is veganism better thought of as a diet, a set of consumer choices, or a moral commitment? I've got a feeling the right answer to that changes what the definition has to do.
