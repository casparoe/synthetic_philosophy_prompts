I cover science and ideas for a living, and I'm thinking about doing a short explainer on two-dimensional semantics—specifically how "water is H2O" can feel informative while still being necessary. A explainer I read in a magazine I generally trust put the view like this: "The basic move is to give an expression two intensions. The secondary intension is the old story: it picks out the same thing in every counterfactual world, which is why water is H2O in every possible world. The primary intension is the innovation: it tells you what the word would pick out if you didn't yet know which world was the actual one—roughly, the clear drinkable stuff in the lakes and rivers, whatever its underlying chemistry turns out to be. That primary intension is the descriptive, a priori component of meaning, the bit a competent speaker has in their head just by knowing the word. It's what makes 'water is H2O' informative: you can picture a world where the watery stuff is XYZ, so the sentence isn't knowable a priori, even though it's necessary." I'm inclined to trust the writer, who is a professor, but the phrase about the primary intension being "the bit a speaker has in their head" sounds suspiciously like the descriptivism I thought Kripke killed. Can you go through the summary and separate what a careful two-dimensionalist would accept, what's a harmless simplification, and what they'd wince at or flatly reject? In particular, is it fair to call the primary intension the descriptive, a priori part of meaning, or something a speaker has "in their head"? And if treating two-dimensional semantics as a single settled doctrine with uniform terminology is itself part of the problem, tell me that rather than playing along.
