I retired from a career in the natural sciences a few years ago and have been working through the metaphysics of time at the level of the journals rather than the survey courses. I find that the presentism–eternalism debate keeps circling back to a distinction I first met in J. M. E. McTaggart's 1908 paper "The Unreality of Time": his "A series" of positions running from the far past through the present to the far future, and his "B series" ordered only by earlier and later. The passage that most unsettles the block-universe picture I absorbed as a scientist is this one:

> It would, I suppose, be universally admitted that time involves change. A particular thing, indeed, may exist unchanged through any amount of time. But when we ask what we mean by saying that there were different moments of time, or a certain duration of time, through which the thing was the same, we find that we mean that it remained the same while other things were changing. A universe in which nothing whatever changed (including the thoughts of the conscious beings in it) would be a timeless universe.
>
> If, then, a B series without an A series can constitute time, change must be possible without an A series. Let us suppose that the distinction of past, present and future does not apply to reality. Can change apply to reality? What is it that changes?

I would like you to read Kurt Vonnegut's *Slaughterhouse-Five* through this passage, and conversely to read the passage through the novel. It seems to me that the Tralfamadorian picture—all moments permanently coexisting and surveyable at once by a four-dimensional observer, death treated as one moment among the rest, the level refrain "So it goes." that follows each death—is close to McTaggart's B series without an A series, and that Billy Pilgrim's being "unstuck in time" is what that world would feel like from the inside. But McTaggart's challenge in the quoted passage is that a B series without an A series cannot contain genuine change. Does the novel actually present a world without an A series, or does it quietly depend on one—through Billy's passivity as something that develops, through the reader's own felt succession, through the historical mounting toward Dresden—and thereby confirm McTaggart's point despite itself? Where does the novel genuinely push back on McTaggart, or on presentism, or on eternalism? I want the reading to be faithful in both directions: say clearly where the metaphor breaks down, and close by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical dispute that the papers do not.
