I am pulled in two directions about whether it can be right to care less about a good merely because it lies further in the future—what is usually called pure time preference. On reflection, I am attracted to temporal neutrality. Discounting a future benefit because it is uncertain, because a dollar now could be invested and grow, or because the would-be recipient may not exist is not pure time preference and is not my target. Temporal neutrality says that after those factors are set aside, the bare position of a good in time—earlier rather than later—gives no reason to weight it differently. The principle associated with Sidgwick, that the mere difference of earlier and later is not a reasonable ground for caring more about the earlier consciousness, strikes me as the right default. The standard consistency argument points the same way: only constant exponential discounting avoids predictable preference reversals, and it is hard to see what could justify any particular positive rate rather than zero once uncertainty has been removed. Impartiality across generations—of the kind any broadly utilitarian or sufficientarian view seems to require—presses in the same direction.

But I also have a firm intuition that will not yield. I care considerably more about a painful migraine tomorrow than about an equally severe, equally certain migraine thirty years from now, and I would not be indifferent if forced to schedule one of them. This does not feel like a mere error of arithmetic or a failure to compound probabilities; it feels like a permissible way for a temporally extended creature to be specially related to its present and near-future stages. At the social scale, a zero rate of pure time preference seems to let the interests of enormous numbers of possible future people dominate each present decision to an extent that I cannot accept as an account of what morality actually demands of finite agents like us.

I do not want to be told which side the literature favors. I want to know which should yield and why. Give me both positions at their strongest, and then press the conflict harder than the familiar debate does. In particular: does the conflict look the same within a single life as it does across different lives? If my future self is, in relevant respects, a different person from me—a matter of overlapping psychological strands rather than one persisting subject—then is my discounting of my own far-future pain really a brute time preference? Or is it better redescribed as an agent-relative partiality toward a near self, of a piece with the partiality I may show toward my own children? Would that redescription let the theory and the intuition both survive? Also consider whether temporal neutrality should be read as a constraint on what an impartial moral calculus may count, leaving room for agent-relative near-bias as a permission, or as an all-things-considered requirement—and say what turns on that choice.

Throughout, say what would justify each possible move. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes—for example, a debunking explanation showing that near-bias is produced by the same mechanisms that generate hyperbolic preference reversals and present bias, mechanisms we already regard as sources of error? What would it take for the theory to be amended or abandoned—for example, an argument that person-like partiality across one's own temporal parts is not an arbitrary discount but a defensible deep partiality, and that temporal neutrality overreaches by flattening it? And is there a reading on which both survive?
