I should like your judgment on an argument I witnessed at a recent workshop on the ethics of risk. Two colleagues — I shall call them Rahim and Vogel — became entrenched in a dispute over a case, and neither I nor the others present managed to referee it to anyone's satisfaction. The case was this. A chemical plant released a substance over the course of a year, exposing one hundred thousand nearby residents to a one-in-one-hundred-thousand increase in their chance of developing a fatal cancer. The exposure occurred without the residents' consent; they were not informed in time to refuse or to move. As it happened, no one developed the cancer. The question under dispute was whether the company wronged any of the residents.

Rahim argued that it plainly did. Wrongness, he said, must be assessed ex ante, from the standpoint of the agent at the time of acting. At that moment the company knowingly imposed a risk of serious harm on each resident, without consent. Being subjected to that risk was itself a setback: each person has an interest in bodily security and in deciding what risks to bear, and the company treated that interest as a cost it could override for its own convenience. The fact that the costs did not materialize does not undo the wrong, any more than a reckless driver who happens to hit no one thereby becomes blameless.

Vogel replied that Rahim was conflating the standpoint of rational decision with the standpoint of wronging. A rational agent must weigh outcomes ex ante, but wronging is a relation between persons, and it requires a victim. In this case no resident was made worse off; no one's life went differently than it would have gone had the plant never operated. The release may have been dangerous, even grossly negligent, but danger is not the same as wrongdoing. If harm never materialized, no one has a complaint grounded in having been harmed, and without such a complaint the language of "wronging" is misplaced.

Rahim answered that Vogel was ignoring the difference between harming a person and wronging a person. One can wrong someone without harming them: a burglar who breaks into a home and takes nothing has still wronged the occupant. What the residents are owed is not compensation for a harm but acknowledgment that the company treated each of them as if their consent did not matter. The complaint is not "you made my life go worse"; it is "you made me bear a risk I never agreed to bear."

Vogel countered that the burglary example cuts the other way. The burglar's wrong lies in the violation of the home and in the wrongful intent, not in exposing the occupant to a risk of loss. Consider instead a factory that installs a faulty guardrail on a walkway: if no one falls, we rightly criticize the safety procedures, but we do not say that every pedestrian who walked past was wronged by the guardrail. Rahim's view, Vogel continued, would make ordinary life impossible to live morally, since driving a car, maintaining a building, and running a hospital all impose small unconsented-to risks on unidentified others. What is needed is an account of when risks are acceptable — a threshold or principle of fair social cooperation — not the blanket claim that every unconsented-to risk wrongs the person who bears it.

Rahim replied that the guardrail and ordinary-activity examples smuggle in the very thing under dispute: a background of reciprocity and shared benefit that makes the risk reasonable. The plant enjoyed all the benefit while the residents bore all the risk, with no fair scheme of reciprocity in view. Moreover, Vogel's ex post view carries a morally strange implication. Imagine the identical release in a second town, where by chance one resident does develop the cancer. Vogel's view says the company now owes that one resident a full remedy and owes the other ninety-nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine nothing, even though the company did the same thing to all of them. Whether an act wrongs nearly everyone it touches should not be settled by a lottery over outcomes.

Vogel responded that this is not a reductio but an accurate description of how responsibility for harm works: remedies track harm, and we cannot distribute compensation for harms that never occurred. The claim that the one unlucky resident and the others were all wronged in the same way sits uneasily with the fact that only one of them has been made worse off. Both of us, he added, are circling around a deeper question: whether a risk that does not materialize is itself a harm to the person who bears it, or merely a danger of harm. On that question, Rahim has been assuming rather than arguing his conclusion.

I found the matter genuinely difficult, and I would value a careful adjudication. Please work through the exchange point by point: on which specific claims is each of them right, where does each argument fail, and what, if anything, are both parties missing? Then state which overall position you judge to be the stronger, and why. Do not advance a third view without first showing where the two presented positions break down or can be reconciled.
