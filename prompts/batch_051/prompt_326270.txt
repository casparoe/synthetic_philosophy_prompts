I'm a science and ideas journalist working on a piece about how the word sentience gets made to do policy work—in animal welfare law, research ethics, and increasingly AI governance—even though the underlying phenomenon looks continuous and multi-dimensional rather than like a clean threshold. I keep coming back to a stack of hard cases and finding that no definition I try handles all of them consistently, so I'd like you to construct one.

Give me a working definition of sentience, or tell me if you think the relevant concept for moral status is better captured by some other term. Then run your definition explicitly through the following cases and report a verdict for each, including the ones where it returns no determinate answer instead of forcing a yes or no:

- A healthy adult human being.
- A conventional digital thermostat.
- A honeybee, which avoids damage and can learn arbitrary color and odor associations, but has a brain of roughly a million neurons.
- An octopus, with its large partly distributed nervous system, sophisticated problem solving, and conspicuous injury-avoidance behavior.
- A human cerebral organoid in a dish, which shows spontaneous electrical activity but has no sensory organs, no body, and no behavioral output.
- An adult human in unresponsive wakefulness syndrome, with sleep–wake cycles and eyes open but no reliably interpretable behavioral response to commands or stimuli.
- A large language model that produces fluent first-person reports of feeling, without a continuous stream of states or an embodied sensorimotor life.

I take the first as clearly sentient and the second as clearly not. Beyond that I have only leanings: the octopus probably is, the honeybee is a genuine maybe that I'd rather not settle by intuition, and the others are open. If your definition conflicts with a leaning or with one of the "clear" cases, don't quietly adjust it—say so, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict.

I'd also like the definition to confront the degree problem head-on. If sentience comes in degrees, a binary definition may misrepresent the cases; if it is scalar, then law and policy still have to draw a threshold somewhere along the gradient. Say how your definition handles that tension, and whether it is best understood as drawing a line or assigning a position along a continuum.

Throughout, separate what is empirically or philosophically settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested view—about the neural correlates of consciousness, the evidential weight of behavior, or the status of language models—as if it were the consensus.
