For a seminar I teach on the metaphysics of language, I have been trying to work out whether the notion of a grammatical rule with exceptions is even coherent. A colleague in linguistics gave me the following argument, which purports to show that it is not. Each step looked, in isolation, almost impossible to deny, yet the conclusion would mean that the entire practice of describing morphology as rule-governed — including the contrast between productive sound patterns, suppletion, and lexically specified irregularities — rests on a mistake. I would like a precise diagnosis, not a restatement of the conclusion.

The argument runs as follows.

1. If English has a rule forming the past tense, then there is a true general statement to the effect that every English verb has a past tense obtained by suffixing an allomorph of -ed.

2. A general statement of that sort is true only if every English verb conforms to it; a single standard nonconforming form falsifies it.

3. English has many standard nonconforming forms. Go/went and be/was are suppletive: the past form is not produced from the stem by any operation of English phonology. Buy/bought, sing/sang, and keep/kept are likewise not obtained by suffixing -ed.

4. So the past-tense rule, as a claim about all verbs, is false.

5. The only way to preserve the rule is to restrict its domain to the regular verbs.

6. But a verb is regular, in this context, just in case its past tense is formed by the rule in question.

7. So the restricted rule says only that the verbs whose past tense is formed by the rule have a past tense formed by the rule. That is vacuous.

8. Therefore the past-tense rule is either false or vacuous; in neither case is it a substantive rule of English.

9. The same dilemma arises for every inflectional rule with exceptions — plural formation, comparative and superlative formation, subject-verb agreement — and, more generally, for any description of a language as containing rules alongside exceptions.

10. Hence no grammatical rule can genuinely have exceptions. Since linguistics constantly describes rules with exceptions, including the contrast between regular sound change and suppletion, the argument proves too much and something must give.

Which step, if any, is the one to reject, and why did it look undeniable before it was pressed? Then explain what a correct account of a grammatical rule must look like once that step is rejected, and in particular what content the English past-tense rule has and in what sense go/went is an exception to it. I should say that I would prefer to keep the ordinary claim that English has a substantive past-tense rule and that go/went is an exception to something; if that is the step that must go, tell me what the cost is.
