I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I am on a small team building a structured deliberation platform for civic education. One of our pilot modules concerns secessionist movements within stable, rights-respecting democracies, with Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec as the standard cases that motivate the design. The platform works by presenting users with two opposing positions and asking them to role-play a continuing exchange, and we have learned that the exercise only works when both positions are genuinely strong and neither is a caricature. I would like you to write a benchmark dialogue for this module.

The dialogue should be between two equally competent interlocutors who disagree about whether a majority in a sub-state territory has a legitimate claim to independence in a well-functioning democracy. One should defend the view that secession is justified, if at all, only as a remedy for serious injustice: annexation, systematic violation of basic rights, or the sustained denial of genuine self-government. On this view, a bare majority vote for independence is not by itself sufficient; the territorial integrity of a legitimate democratic state carries independent weight, and a regional majority is not the only constituency affected by redrawing the border. The other should defend the view that a self-identifying people with a territorial base has a democratic claim to choose its own political status, and that a free and fair majority vote for independence is therefore a strong prima facie case for secession rather than something that must be earned through oppression. The two should press each other on the hard cases: a narrow result of fifty percent plus one, the position of minorities inside the seceding territory, a wealthy region attempting to exit in order to avoid fiscal transfers to poorer areas, and the problem of determining who counts as the relevant people. Have them respond to each other's actual points, revise or clarify where it would be honest to do so, and end without either side conceding. The aim is a dialogue a general reader can follow without a background in political philosophy.
