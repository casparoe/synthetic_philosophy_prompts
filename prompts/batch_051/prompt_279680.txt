I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about how mathematics keeps expanding what it will count as a real object — from the old straightedge-and-compass standard, through coordinate geometry letting in algebraic curves, to calculus admitting transcendental curves, and finally to set theory admitting objects no one can actually specify, like a well-ordering of the real numbers. I want a sidebar quiz for readers, something like "which philosophy of mathematical legitimacy do you actually hold?", that sorts people by what they say about cases rather than by which label they like.

Please write me the quiz itself, not just a plan for one. Nine or ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options. The cases I want included at minimum are: doubling the cube (the cube root of 2), squaring the circle (or some other way of getting at pi), the graph of sin(x) or another transcendental curve, a continuous nowhere-differentiable function, a well-ordering of the real numbers, the pieces from the Banach-Tarski decomposition, an uncomputable real like Chaitin's Omega, and the imaginary unit. You can add cases if you need them to seperate positions, but each question should be answerable by a curious general reader in one or two sentences, no jargon. No question should just ask "are you a formalist?" or "is math discovered or invented?"

The leaves I want the quiz to sort people into are: a strict straightedge-and-compass constructivism; an algebraic/equation-based permissivism; an analytic/infinite-series permissivism; a set-theoretic permissivism; a proceduralist view where an object has to be effectively specifiable or computable; and a formalist/instrumentalist view where consistency or usefulness is the whole story. These should come apart where they genuinely do; if two of them end up indistinguishable on my cases, say so instead of faking a distinction.

I'd also like you to build the thing from the logical space rather than from the list of names. So first lay out the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, locate the six positions inside that space, and flag unoccupied combinations with a note on whether each looks incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored. Then provide the final quiz with a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the positions, including what to do with mixed patterns.

Its for a general science-interested audience, so the tone should be light and the setup for each case should make the object vivid (e.g., a curve you can't actually draw smoothly, a number no algorithm can compute). Keep it fun; the underlying point is that the old fights about what counts as a legitimate mathematical object are still live choices, not settled history.
