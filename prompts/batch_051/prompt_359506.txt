In his first letter to the Corinthians, Paul tells women who pray and prophesy to keep their heads covered, and he declines to rest the instruction on local custom alone: he reaches back to the order of creation (the man was not made from the woman but the woman from the man, and the woman was made for the man) and adds that nature itself teaches that long hair is a woman's glory and a shameful thing for a man. In the letter to the Galatians he tells his readers that those baptized into Christ have been made one in him, so that the distinctions of Jew and Greek, slave and free, and male and female no longer mark the decisive difference. Read flatly, the two passages pull in opposite directions: the first gives male–female difference a continuing, theologically grounded role in worship, while the second announces that the distinction has been superseded in the body of Christ. The familiar reconciliation is to call the head-covering instruction a culturally bound application, intelligible in first-century Corinth but not transposable, while treating the Galatian claim as a transcultural truth about salvation. But that division is under strain, because the head-covering argument itself appeals to creation and nature—exactly the considerations usually invoked to show that a command is transcultural rather than local. Is the familiar reconciliation available except by fiat? Lay out the candidate readings: the two passages govern different domains; the Galatian claim concerns standing before God rather than practice; the Corinthian passage is Paul taking a local honor-code and improvising a theological rationale for it; or his own view of sexual difference is genuinely in motion. Then state the least ad hoc criterion that keeps the Galatian declaration transcultural while treating the Corinthian creation-order argument as culturally contained, and test it on the slavery case, where the Torah regulates slaveholding as a stable institution while Galatians likewise declares the slave–free divide no longer decisive in Christ. Conclude with a verdict on which reading requires the least special pleading.
