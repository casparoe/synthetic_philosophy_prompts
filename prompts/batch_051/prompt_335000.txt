I am writing a moral psychology paper and, before I try to defend the following set of views, I would like them audited for consistency. I genuinely hold all five, but I have no clear sense of where the strain lies, and I would rather find out now than in the seminar.

My commitments are these.

(1) Psychological egoism as an empirical thesis: every action is ultimately performed because the agent expects it to secure some advantage for themselves—pleasure, safety, money, standing, or the avoidance of guilt. Seemingly altruistic behaviour is always best explained by expected reciprocation, social reward, or the warm glow of self-approval.

(2) Ethical egoism as a normative thesis: the only non-arbitrary moral requirement is that one maximise one's own long-term wellbeing. Rival moral theories either rest on unargued duties or cannot explain why their requirements bind me.

(3) A Humean account of practical reason: reason can criticise means but not final ends. I therefore do not claim ethical egoism is rationally compulsory; I claim it is grounded in a deep and nearly universal desire for one's own flourishing, which is simply a fact about the kind of creatures we are.

(4) A doctrine about rational error: there are people we rightly criticise as making rational mistakes—not merely having unusual tastes—when they knowingly act against their own long-term interests. The gambler who understands the odds and still bets the mortgage, or the person who refuses a beneficial treatment out of stubborn pride, has gone wrong in a way reason can identify.

(5) A methodological scruple: any theory of motivation that can meet apparent counterexamples only by attributing hidden, unconscious, or self-deceived motives, with no independent evidence for those attributions, has insulated itself from falsification. I accept this as a general constraint on theory choice.

Could you tell me whether this set is jointly tenable? Please treat the answer as an audit rather than a lecture: identify which commitments pull against which, rank the tensions by severity, and distinguish genuine tensions from ones that dissolve once a distinction is drawn. For each genuine tension, say which commitment, or which distinction, would have to give, and what the cheapest repair would cost.
