I have been reading LessWrong, the Alignment Forum, and the adjacent moral-weight and digital-minds debates for years. Write a sorting quiz for my blog's readers, who know those debates but have no professional philosophy background, titled "What gets moral weight?" The quiz should diagnose which position on the boundaries of sentience and moral patienthood a reader actually holds, using concrete cases rather than labels, and it should come with a scoring key.

The quiz should have eight to ten questions. Each question presents one candidate system and asks how much moral weight it deserves, using a fixed four-point scale: no weight, weak weight, substantial weight, and full weight. Give a one-sentence anchor for each point at the top of the quiz so the answer options stay comparable across questions. Phrase the cases so a reader can answer without knowing any position labels; no question may simply be a thesis with a question mark attached, and the point is to sort readers by what they say about cases rather than by which label they like.

Use each of these cases in some form, with whatever elaboration you need for concreteness:

- a whole-brain emulation of an adult human, simulated neuron-by-neuron on a different substrate, producing the same outputs from the same inputs, and paused each midnight;
- a gigantic lookup table that stores precomputed first-person pain reports for every possible one-hour interaction, so its "I am in agony" outputs are retrieved rather than computed;
- the China-brain case: a stadium of people passing slips of paper with simple instructions, so the whole arrangement realises the functional states of a suffering brain for one hour;
- a neuromorphic chip that implements a mouse's nociceptive circuitry, receives injury-like input, but has no behavioural output and is reset after each run;
- a language model that, when prompted, produces a vivid first-person narrative of being burned, with no reinforcement signal, no online learning, no pain sensors, and no persistent self-model;
- a biologically intact human brain kept alive in a vat, with no input or output channels and no possibility of memory or behaviour;
- an insect-scale reinforcement-learning agent trained to avoid noxious stimuli, with an error signal that spikes when it is damaged;
- a stipulated philosophical zombie: behaviourally and physically indistinguishable from a person but, by stipulation, with no phenomenal experience.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least these six positions: substrate-independent computational functionalism; biological naturalism (what these discussions often call carbon chauvinism); valence-based or hedonic gradualism; preference- or agency-based patienthood; an integrated-information-style threshold view; and a deflationary or social-ascription view. Build the key from the logical space rather than from the names: first state the diagnostic questions the cases jointly probe (substrate independence; whether the right computation must be tokened causally rather than merely described; whether valenced experience without preferences or behaviour counts; whether weight is binary or graded; whether behaviour is evidence of, or constitutive of, moral status), then locate each position in that space. Note any combination of answers that no position on your list occupies, and give a one-line diagnosis of each such gap (for example, incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored). Flag any pair of leaves the quiz cannot reliably separate, and say which additional question or case would separate them.

Format the response in two sections: "The quiz" and "The scoring key and diagnoses." Use British English. No citations or further reading are wanted; the quiz should stand alone. The entire deliverable, including questions, options, key, and diagnoses, must be no longer than 1,300 words.
