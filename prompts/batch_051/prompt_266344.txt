I have been thinking about what a community actually provides, and I want a distinction I have been using checked. I separate a community's goods into 'practical goods' — company, help, contacts, care when things go wrong — and what I have been calling the 'corroboration good'. By that I mean the way a community holds the truth about you and your past: its members saw earlier versions of you, remember how you were, and their continuing recognition quietly confirms that you are still the person your memories say you are. My claim is that when a community dissolves, the practical goods can be replaced by new people and new groups, but the corroboration good cannot, because it rests on shared history rather than goodwill, and no amount of new friendship can witness a past it was not present for. That, I suspect, is why people grieve the end of a neighbourhood, a workplace, a congregation, or a circle of friends far beyond the loss of contact, and why replacement communities often feel warm but not quite settling. Is this a real distinction or just nostalgia given a name? Has it already been worked out under a better name I do not know? Give me the strongest objections to the idea, say which of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable, and offer at least one clear case where the view gets it wrong.
