For the past year I have been working on a novel whose narrator restores old photographs, and the research has left me stalled on a distinction I do not know how to name. Looking at a snapshot of my late mother as a child, I find myself saying that I have seen her, not that I have seen a picture of her; the photograph feels less like a depiction and more like a small window, or a relay through which my attention reaches the person on the far side. The sensation is strongest with old contact prints and with the blurry, badly framed pictures no one would call good: even a scratched and fogged snapshot seems to vouch for the fact that that laughing woman on the beach was really there, in a way that a careful charcoal portrait of the same scene, drawn by an eyewitness, does not. And yet the sensation fails me with certain photographs. Airbrushed fashion spreads feel closer to paintings than to windows, and a heavily composited digital image, however sharp, does not seem to let me see through it. Nor does the difference reduce to the mechanical character of photography, since a camera obscura tracing and a photorealist oil painting each feel, to me, like a picture rather than a view.

I keep wanting a term for this odd property: the way some images present themselves as an encounter with the thing itself while others present themselves as representations of it. Is there an established concept for this, and can someone lay out its boundaries instead of merely supplying a label? I would like to know whether the distinction survives digitization, whether it can coexist with heavy manipulation, whether it belongs to the image or to my attitude toward the image, and what exactly falls on each side.
