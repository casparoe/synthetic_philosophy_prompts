You are a scholar of statutory interpretation and institutional design who studies how legal rules shape the behavior of legislatures, courts, agencies, and litigants, and who is comfortable reasoning about long-run institutional equilibria. Please think carefully before answering, and begin by identifying the main mechanisms you expect to operate.

Some background. In ordinary statutory interpretation, courts generally treat the plain text of a statute as controlling unless applying it would produce a result that is not merely unwise or surprising but absurd: so plainly contrary to the legislature's evident purposes that no reasonable lawmaker could have intended it. The absurdity doctrine is usually understood as a narrow safety valve, not a general license to correct or improve statutes.

Now the counterfactual. Suppose that, starting tomorrow, the following rule is binding on every court in the United States, federal and state, at every level, and remains in force for at least thirty years: whenever a judge concludes that the literal application of a statute would produce a result the judge regards as absurd, wasteful, or unjust, the judge is required to set the text aside and instead implement what the judge reconstructs as the legislature's reasonable purposes. The rule contains no requirement that the absurdity be extreme, obvious, or beyond reasonable disagreement. There is no independent check on the judge's judgment about what counts as absurd or unjust, and no cost to the judge for getting that judgment wrong beyond ordinary appellate reversal.

I am not asking for a story about what happens, and I am not asking whether you think the rule is desirable. I want a reasoned causal prediction of how the legal and political system would change over those thirty years, with explicit attention to mechanisms, incentives, and constraints, and with a stated degree of confidence for each major link in your argument.

Please address at least the following, though you may reorganize or add to them: how legislatures would draft statutes differently, if at all, including their use of detail versus standards, purpose clauses, and legislative history; how litigation, settlement, and prosecutorial behavior would change; how lower courts would behave in the shadow of appellate review; how the rule would interact with administrative agencies and with judicial review of agency action; the effects on predictability, the rule of law, and the separation of powers; and whether the system would converge on a stable equilibrium or instead generate escalating instability.

For each prediction, distinguish what is robust from what depends on contestable assumptions. In particular, say how your conclusions depend on assumptions about the ideological composition and homogeneity of the judiciary, the speed with which legislatures can adapt, the public's tolerance for judicial rewriting, and the appetite of appellate courts for policing their own rule. Where you are genuinely uncertain, say so and identify the fact that would settle the question.

Please write your answer in American English.
