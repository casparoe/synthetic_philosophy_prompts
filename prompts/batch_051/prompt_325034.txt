I've spent enough years reading rationalist blogs and their comment sections that terms like "preference," "bias," and "value" have picked up fairly precise jobs in my head. But there is one recurring move whose exact content I cannot pin down. In arguments over substantive moral disagreement, someone will offer a considered moral judgment—an anti-consequentialist constraint, a claim about what makes a distribution unjust, a conviction about the moral status of some practice—and a reply will arrive with the tone of a refutation: "That's not a moral preference; that's just a bias." The one-liner is treated as ending the discussion: once the view is labeled a bias, it no longer has to be engaged as a moral claim. I can see that this sentence could be asserting several very different things: a claim about statistical miscalibration; a claim about the causal origins of the judgment; a claim about its insensitivity to evidence or argument; a claim about self-interest, partiality, or motivated reasoning; perhaps a claim that the view is not really a moral preference at all. Those claims do not all license the same downstream move, and they range from nearly vacuous to substantive and probably false. I want a systematic breakdown. Lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that "That's just a bias" could be making when aimed at a moral judgment. For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. Then identify exactly which readings would justify treating the targeted view as no longer entitled to consideration in moral argument, and which readings leave the preference/bias distinction intact rather than collapsing it.
