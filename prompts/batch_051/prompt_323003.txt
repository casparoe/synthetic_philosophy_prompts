I'm in a media law seminar and we spent a whole week on Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co., mostly on the syllabus's line that says "Simply couching a statement—'Jones is a liar'—in terms of opinion—'In my opinion Jones is a liar'—does not dispel the factual implications contained in the statement." The professor keeps coming back to ratings and forecasts—like a bond rating, a product review, a sportswriter's prediction that a coach will be fired—and how they sit exactly on the line between factual implication and pure opinion.

So heres the counterfactual I keep turning over. Imagine an alternate 1990 where the Supreme Court goes the other way in Milkovich. Instead of rejecting a separate opinion privilege, the Court reads Gertz broadly and announces a categorical First Amendment "opinion privilege": any statement that is framed as an opinion, or that cannot be proved true or false, gets absolute immunity from defamation liability, even if it implies a defamatory factual assertion. So saying "In my opinion Jones is a liar" would be protected, and so would a credit agency saying "In our opinion, this bond is junk" if it's dressed up as an evaluative judgment rather than a balance-sheet claim.

Specifically: how do credit rating agencies, securities analysts, product reviewers, and election forecasters change their behavior under this broader shield? Does defamation law get displaced by other legal tools—fraud, negligence, consumer-protection statutes, securities regulation—and where would the pressure be strongest to re-narrow the privilege?
