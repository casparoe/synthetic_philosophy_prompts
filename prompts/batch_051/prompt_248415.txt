i've been trying to get the benthamite line on sexual morality straight, and I want to pressure-test a criteria set against a case rather than just the usual "is it harmful" hand waving. So here's the position I want you to work with, a cleaned-up Benthamite sexual ethics stated without any of his historical examples. (I know Bentham himself cared at least as much about what the law should punish as about private moral wrongness; for this exercise I'm asking about the moral verdict, not the legal one.)

The position says an arrangement is morally wrong just in case it fails the utility test, meaning it lowers the total of pleasure-minus-pain across everyone affected, compared to the best feasible alternative. But the view also comes with four ground rules for reading that test:

1. Harm to someone who didn't genuinely consent to the risk always enters the ledger as a real negative, and serious nonconsensual harm is the clearest case of wrongness.
2. The distress of third parties who merely disapprove of, or are disgusted by, other people's private conduct does not enter the ledger. Otherwise any disliked practice could be made wrong by enough bigotry.
3. Asceticism is neutral: renouncing a pleasure has no positive or negative value except through its actual effects on the ledger.
4. There's no standing requirement that a person's desires must have been formed reflectively or autonomously for their satisfaction to count as a benefit. If someone stably wants and enjoys a way of life, that counts, even if the want was shaped by peer pressure or shame.

Now the case. The Order of Quiet Joy is a small, legal, well-documented community of adults. Members take a permanent vow: no sex, no masturbation, no deliberately cultivated sexual fantasy. When someone seems restless or admits a fantasy at the weekly check-in, the rest hold a "mirroring" session: the member stands in a circle while the others describe, calmly and at length, exactly what the member's longing looks like from the outside, until the member reports that it has subsided. Members are free to leave and some do — about one every two years — but most stay for decades, and they describe the result as a deep, quiet contentment they rank above any sex they ever had. There's no physical force and no financial trap; everyone joined as an adult and reaffirms the vow yearly.

The data are messy in a way I want you to lean into rather than smooth over. On momentary affect, members record fewer peaks and more low-grade stings — they miss sex sometimes, and the mirroring sessions are genuinely uncomfortable — so if you sum moment-by-moment pleasure minus pain, the Order comes out slightly below the comparison group over a year. On global measures, members report higher life satisfaction, more felt meaning, and a calm they describe as more pleasant than their previous state, and they say they'd rather stay than leave. So whether the Order passes the utility test hangs on what counts as a pleasure and how you aggregate it.

Is the Order's sexual regime wrong under this position? Work through the ground rules one at a time and be explicit about how you aggregate the pleasure data. Then commit: wrong, not wrong, or the criteria cant decide. If you pick "cant decide," say exactly which ground rule is doing the breaking and propose the smallest amendment that fixes it. If the criteria say "not wrong" and you find that verdict a little repulsive, say whether that repulsion is a reason to revise ground rule 2 or 4 (or the whole master test) or just the position working as intended.

End with a table. Columns: ground rule (number and one-line restatement); what the Order looks like under it; verdict (met / violated / underdetermined); confidence (low / medium / high). Add a final row for the overall verdict.
