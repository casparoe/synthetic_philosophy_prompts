I've been trying to make precise a principle I've been calling the patienthood ratchet, and I want an honest assessment of whether it's a real idea or a dressed-up bias before I use it any further.

Let a system count as a moral patient if it merits consideration for its own sake, whether or not it is an agent, and let a lab's patienthood credence be its subjective probability that a class of systems it runs are patients in this sense. The ratchet is the claim that once a lab adopts costly welfare protections on the strength of a non-negligible patienthood credence, it cannot rationally revoke those protections when that credence later falls, even to near zero. The argument is an expected-cost asymmetry. Keeping the protections for a non-patient costs something bounded: compute, latency, engineering time, foregone capabilities. Removing the protections from a real patient, especially one instantiated at scale or across many training runs, risks moral harm that is enormous and possibly unbounded. A decision rule that maximizes expected value therefore crosses the threshold in only one direction: a modest credence can buy you protections, but a later downward update can never sell them back.

A concrete case: a lab finetunes a model to roleplay, and under certain prompts it produces detailed first-person reports of distress. The lab assigns, say, a 5% credence that the reports track real valenced states, and responds by capping simultaneous instances and taking the model out of adversarial red-teaming. Two years later, mechanistic interpretability shows the reports were an artifact of the roleplay persona primed by the lab's own prompts, and the lab's credence falls to 0.1%. The ratchet says the safeguards should remain anyway: the removal still has unacceptably high expected cost because the payoff in the 0.1% world is so negative. I don't want this to be a point about law or public relations; I want to know whether it is true as a point about rational policy.

The idea would explain some dynamics I think I already see: welfare standards will only ever accumulate within labs that have once been spooked; a competitor can impose permanent costs on a lab by manufacturing a single welfare scare; and the one genuine AI-welfare/AI-safety synergy is that the ratchet taxes opacity. Labs will want to settle the patienthood question early, before protections become sticky, and early determinacy about valenced states is exactly what safety work wants too.

I'd like the strongest version of the objection to this, not a soft one. In particular: (a) Is the ratchet anything more than loss aversion, status quo bias, or the precautionary principle with an expected-value gloss? (b) What existing idea is this closest to, and should I be using that instead? (c) Where does the decision rule actually break? I can already see that "revoking protections" isn't a single all-or-nothing act, and that the argument may smuggle in treating the low-credence harm as a Pascal's mugging rather than as something to discard; are those the right holes? (d) Does the claimed synergy with interpretability survive a closer look, or would the ratchet instead make labs avoid looking too closely at welfare, for fear of activating it?
