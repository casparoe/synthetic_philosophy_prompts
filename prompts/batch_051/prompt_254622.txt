One of my old professors kept a running list of little vignettes about whether a belief counts as justified, and she'd rule on each one. I've been trying to reverse-engineer her epistemology from the list. Here are the verdicts I remember.

1. A mathematician believes a lemma she just proved from standard axioms. She can walk through every step, and the axioms strike her as self-evident. Professor: justified.

2. A detective believes the butler did it. His reasons: the butler's fingerprints are on the glass, the butler lied about where he was, and the timeline fits. Each reason traces back to something he directly observed, and the reasons all hang together. Professor: justified.

3. A man believes the moon landing was faked. Whenever you challenge one of his reasons, he produces another reason for that reason, and so on without end, and the whole pile conflicts with physics and history he otherwise accepts. Professor: not justified.

4. A student believes her senses are generally reliable. Asked why, she says they've been reliable in the past. Asked how she knows that, she says the same thing again: the past reliability is known through the senses. There's no independent support, just this tight little circle, though the belief doesn't conflict with anything else she believes. Professor: not justified.

5. A physicist believes a new particle exists because the Standard Model predicts it, and the model is backed by decades of independent experiments, each of which bottoms out in direct observation or math she can check herself. Professor: justified.

Now here's the new case. A philosopher believes time doesn't really flow; the block universe is right and past, present, and future are all equally real. She can't trace this to any observation, experiment, or self-evident starting point; it isn't built on a foundation of that sort. What it has going for it is that it makes a huge range of otherwise puzzling things cohere: memory, change, the direction of physics, time-travel paradoxes, free will. The belief is recommended entirely by how much coherence it buys across her whole system of beliefs, and when foundations come up she shrugs and says she doesn't need one.

What would my professor say about the block-universe belief if she's trying to stay consistent with her own track record? Which earlier case is the closest precident, and does it actually decide the new one? If her rulings genuinely underdetermine this, spell out the principle each possible verdict would commit her to. And if more than one epistemology is still in the running — foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism, whatever — say which ones still fit her record and which side each would take on the block-universe case.
