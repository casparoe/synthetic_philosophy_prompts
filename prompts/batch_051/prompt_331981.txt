For a handout in my moral psychology seminar on self-control, write a branching decision tree that sorts someone into a position on the question whether self-control is best understood as a virtue, as prudence, or as a form of repression. Build the tree from the logical space first, not from the labels: identify the load-bearing questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the named positions in that space, and mark the combinations that nobody serious occupies. The leaves must include at least: Aristotelian temperance (desire brought into line with practical wisdom), continence or enkrateia (lingering contrary desire overridden by reason), a Stoic-style extirpation view (troublesome desire is to be eliminated, not managed), a prudential self-regulation view (self-control as an instrumental skill serving the agent's own ends), and a repression critique (the effort to stamp out desire drives it underground at psychological cost). Phrase each branch node as a concrete vignette or an easily answered claim, not as a request to identify a label. Draw the vignettes from this set: someone who declines dessert while still wanting it, a former smoker who still craves cigarettes years after quitting, a person who deliberately schedules indulgences to stay disciplined the rest of the month, and a person who white-knuckles through anger and later erupts. Distinctions the tree should press on include whether a lingering contrary impulse is itself a defect or merely a fact about the agent; whether the standard of success is set by the agent's own goals or by an independent account of flourishing; and whether inner conflict is inherently costly or can mark a healthy plurality of motives. For any unoccupied combination, state in one clause whether it is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored; and flag any two named views the tree cannot cleanly separate. End with a short methodological note saying which one or two distinctions are doing most of the classificatory work, which single change of answer would most alter the tree's verdict for a typical respondent, and where the tree is least confident.
