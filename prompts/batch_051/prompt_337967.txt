At the reading group I go to, the conversation last week somehow came down to a standoff between two people over the evil-demon thought experiment — the one where an all-powerful demon says he'll make your life unbearable unless you admire him. (1) Dana said the threat obviously gives you a reason to admire the demon; if you wouldn't even try to bring the admiration about, that's practical irrationality, and that's just what "having a reason" comes to. (2) Evan said no: the threat gives a reason to *try* to admire the demon, or maybe to want to admire him, but not a reason *to admire* him. It's like being paid to believe it's raining — a reason to get yourself to hold the belief, but not a reason for the belief itself; admiration is answerable to features of the person, not to payoffs. (3) Dana replied that the belief analogy is doing all the work and it's a bad analogy, because you can't decide to believe but you can cultivate admiration over time; so the only workable sense of "a reason to admire" is a reason to do whatever self-manipulation produces the admiration, which the demon's threat plainly is. (4) Evan came back: even after that self-manipulation succeeds, if someone asks "why do you admire him?" then "to stop him hurting me" reads as a causal explanation of the admiration, not as something counting in favor of it; only features of the demon himself do that, and those alone make the attitude correct. Someone else in the room muttered "wrong kind of reasons" as we packed up, but we didn't get to sort it out.

Can you go through the four moves one by one, grade each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, and then say where the two of them are both missing something important about how reasons for an attitude work?
