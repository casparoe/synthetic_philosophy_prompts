I am a graduate student in the humanities, and a recent family crisis has forced me to think carefully about a pattern I had previously only half-noticed. My younger sister was diagnosed with a serious illness a few months ago, and several relatives have responded by offering a form of consolation built around the idea that no suffering is ever ultimately undeserved. They do not mean that God is testing her; they mean something closer to a cosmic accounting. Every action, they say, leaves behind a debt or a credit, and over a long enough period — perhaps across more than one life — the books balance. When I pressed one of them on how this could apply to my sister, who by any ordinary standard has done nothing to deserve her illness, the reply was that the relevant acts may have occurred before this life and that the balancing may not be complete for several more.

What unsettled me was not the metaphysics but the flexibility of the explanation. Each time a case seemed to resist being read as deserved, the timeframe quietly expanded to absorb it. A house fire, a cancer diagnosis, a sudden financial windfall, a child born into poverty — each was explained by the same underlying mechanism, because the unit of accounting could always be re-specified to fit the case. I have also noticed a slide within single conversations through three distinct claims: first, that good and bad deeds tend to produce matching outcomes within an ordinary lifetime; second, that the universe must balance out in the long run; and third, that the existence of suffering is itself evidence that some wrongdoing must have occurred somewhere in the chain. The third claim closes a circle in a way I can feel but cannot yet articulate.

I am not asking for pastoral advice, for a verdict on whether the underlying worldview is true, or for a survey of religious ethics. I want to understand the conceptual structure of what is done when a desert claim is protected from counterexamples by extending its timeframe. Is there an established name for this specific move, or for the kind of theory that can absorb any possible outcome by adjusting its scope? I know the just-world fallacy is somewhere nearby, but that seems to name the end-state belief that people get what they deserve. I am after the structural feature: the expanding ledger. How exactly does this move relate to karma understood as a theory of moral desert, and where is the line between a substantive theory of desert and an explanation that no possible outcome could disappoint?
