I am currently taking a course on collective responsibility, and I keep returning to a legal practice that seems to me to pull in two directions at once. In United States federal criminal law, a corporation can be convicted of a crime for the acts of its employees. Under the doctrine of respondeat superior, the act and mental state of a single employee, including a low-level one, can be attributed to the corporation as long as the employee was acting at least partly to benefit the company, even when senior management had a compliance program forbidding exactly that conduct. Federal courts have also permitted juries to aggregate the knowledge of several employees under the collective knowledge doctrine, so that a corporation can be convicted of a knowledge-based offense even when no individual employee knew all the elements of the offense. The usual punishment is a fine, often set through a negotiated deferred-prosecution or non-prosecution agreement. But the corporation cannot be imprisoned; the fine is ultimately borne by shareholders, employees, or customers rather than by the specific decision-makers; and the employees whose acts created the liability are frequently not prosecuted at all.

This seems inconsistent on its face. Criminal punishment of a natural person is normally taken to require a culpable individual, someone who had a guilty mind, and the sanction is meant to fall on that individual. Here the law constructs a guilty mind out of pieces scattered across several employees, punishes an entity that cannot be imprisoned, and lets the cost fall on people who did nothing blameworthy, while often leaving the actual wrongdoers untouched. Yet the law insists on calling this a criminal conviction, with the stigma and collateral consequences that carries, rather than a tax or a regulatory penalty.

What theory of corporate moral agency, blameworthiness, and punishment does this practice presuppose? Is it committed to the view that a corporation is a genuine moral agent with its own intentional structure, or is it better understood as a deterrence scheme that borrows the language of blame while remaining neutral about whether the corporation can be blameworthy in the strict sense? Which parts of the practice strictly presuppose corporate culpability, and which parts are compatible with several different theories? If we instead adopted the view that only natural persons can be morally blameworthy, would this practice need to be abandoned, or could it be redesigned as purely regulatory while dropping the retributive language? Please reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and indicate how confident you are in the reconstruction and which point you are least sure of.
