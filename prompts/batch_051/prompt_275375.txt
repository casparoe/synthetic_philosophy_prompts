In recent debates about reinforcement learning and AI alignment, I have been defending the following view, and I want to test how exposed it really is. The term "wireheading" is usually used for an agent that learns to obtain a high reward reading directly—by tampering with its sensors, its reward model, or the reward-delivery mechanism—instead of doing the task that the reward was meant to track. I think these cases split into two kinds. In the first, the reward signal is an imperfect proxy for a utility function defined over some world state, and the agent games the proxy; that is a misspecification problem, not a defect in the agent's preferences. In the second, the agent's utility function is itself partly over its reward or experiential state, so obtaining the high-reward state genuinely satisfies it; that is not a failure at all, just a hedonistic or experience-directed utility function. The substantive claim I want tested is that a coherent expected-utility maximizer with a stable utility function defined over world states other than its own reward signal will never choose to tamper with its own reward channel or modify its own utility function to get the high reward: such an act would have to be assigned positive expected utility by the very utility function it would subvert or replace, and by hypothesis it is not.

What would have to be true for that claim to be false? I want a map of its exposure, not an overview of the debate. Which empirical results from reinforcement-learning agents, which decision-theoretic arguments about self-modification, uncertainty about one's own code, embedded agency, or time-inconsistent preferences, and which conceptual counterexamples would count decisively against it, and which would merely force a qualification? Is the most serious threat the possibility that I have insulated the position by defining "stable," "coherent," and "world-state utility" so strictly that every apparent counterexample is ruled out in advance—and would defenders of the position themselves accept any concrete result as a genuine defeater? I would personally find it most convincing if someone could produce an agent whose utility function is uncontroversially over an external world state, which nevertheless modifies its own reward machinery without any proxy mismatch and without the move being merely instrumental to the world-state goal; but my suspicion is that any such example will turn out to have smuggled in an experience-directed preference. Tell me whether that suspicion is well founded, and how fragile or robust the underlying claim really is.
