Consider the following two-player variant of Newcomb's problem, designed to bring out the structure of what is sometimes called acausal trade.

An experimenter has already run simulations of two agents, you and B, each facing this same game, and has used the simulated choices to fill the boxes. You are in a causally isolated room; so is B; neither of you can communicate with or causally affect the other. In your room there is a transparent box containing $500,000 and an opaque box that contains $1,000,000 if and only if the simulation of B one-boxed. In B's room there is a transparent box containing $500,000 and an opaque box that contains $1,000,000 if and only if the simulation of you one-boxed. Each of you must now choose either to take only the opaque box ("one-box") or to take both boxes ("two-box"). Each knows the complete setup and cares only about maximizing his or her own money. You are certain that the simulations were run before your actual choice and that your choice cannot causally affect their contents, B's actual choice, or B's box. You are also certain of the following symmetric evidential correlations: conditional on your one-boxing, the simulation of B one-boxed with probability r; conditional on your two-boxing, the simulation of B two-boxed with probability r (equivalently, one-boxed with probability 1 − r). By symmetry, B has the matching conditional probabilities about the simulation of you. Treat the case in which r is strictly between one half and one.

Work through the case in detail. (i) State the causal dominance argument as it applies to this variant, and say what it recommends. (ii) State the evidential expected-utility calculation explicitly in terms of r and the stated dollar amounts, and solve for the values of r for which evidential decision theory recommends one-boxing. (iii) Do causal and evidential decision theory still diverge in this variant, and if so, for exactly which values of r do they part ways? (iv) Compare the variant with the standard one-player Newcomb problem. Is the replacement of the predictor by a simulated symmetric co-player merely cosmetic, or does it change which arguments are available to the one-boxer — for instance, by adding a reason to regard one's own choice as selecting, in a non-causal sense, the choices of other agents who run the same decision procedure? Be explicit about where, if anywhere, the analogy between the two cases breaks down.
