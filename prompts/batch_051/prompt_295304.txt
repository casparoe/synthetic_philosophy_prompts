At a reading group I attended, two people argued about what it is for something to be a law of nature, and I left unable to sort out who was right about what. I wrote the exchange down as best I could.

Elena began by defending the view that a law of nature is nothing over and above the regularities it summarizes. If every actual electron behaves in a certain way, she said, that is all there is to the law; saying the law makes the electrons behave that way adds a mysterious extra item that does no work.

Marcus replied that this gets the direction of explanation backwards. The law is the reason the electrons all behave the same way. A statement that every coin in some bag is copper does not explain why any particular coin in the bag is copper. So if laws are just summaries, they explain nothing.

Elena answered that general facts can explain their instances even when they are just facts about a collection. A coin is copper because of how it got into the bag, and the general fact that the bag contains only copper coins does explain why this particular coin is copper, given how the bag was filled. In the same way, she said, the law that all Fs are G is a general fact about the whole history of the world, and it can explain why this F is G. Explanation does not require a governing force.

Marcus said that on her view the universe's orderliness is a massive coincidence. The only reason there is a simple, exceptionless pattern to summarize is that the mosaic of particular facts happened to be arranged that way. On his view the law is what constrains the mosaic, which is why a simple summary is possible at all.

Elena replied that on his view the law is an extra cosmic enforcer sitting on top of the facts and making them fall in line, and that is exactly the kind of occult thing we should refuse. Her view needs only the facts; his view needs the facts plus something that pushes them around.

Marcus then offered a case. Consider a possible world, he said, in which the fundamental laws say that a certain kind of particle, if it existed, would decay in exactly three seconds, but no such particle ever happens to exist. On his view there is still a law there, and it makes true a counterfactual: if such a particle had existed, it would have decayed in three seconds. On Elena's view, he said, there is no law there, because there is no regularity to summarize. But what would happen in that unactualized situation is a real feature of that world, not merely a feature of our description of it.

Elena responded that a law that never manifests is an idle wheel. If it never touches the actual history of the world, we have no evidence for it and no need for it. Counterfactuals about unactualized particles, she said, are consequences of the model we use to reason, not reports of a real governing relation in the world.

Marcus answered that her own view needs counterfactuals too. A regularity is lawlike only if it supports predictions about the next instance, including instances that have not occurred yet. Where, he asked, does that modal reach come from? A mere list of what has happened so far does not tell you what would happen next. So she is quietly helping herself to the very thing she calls mysterious.

Elena said this is exactly what the best-system view handles. The laws, she said, are the axioms of the deductive system that best balances simplicity and strength over all the particular facts of the world. Such a system earns its keep by being simple and informative, and it supports predictions because it is the best compression of all the facts, past and future. No extra push is needed.

Marcus replied that best is relative to our taste in simplicity. A different mind with different standards would pick a different system, and then what is a law would depend on the describer. Laws of nature should not be anthropocentric or vague; his governing laws are part of the world whether or not anyone finds them simple.

Elena said his primitive governing relation is a blank check: he never says what it is beyond the job it is supposed to do. At least the best-system account says something concrete: laws are what you get when you compress the facts in the most economical way.

Marcus answered that clarity is not the same as truth. A clear false story is worse than an account that posits a primitive because the phenomena require one. The phenomena here, he said, are lawlikeness, counterfactual robustness, and the non-accidental character of regularities; his account explains them, while hers re-describes them.

They kept going after that, but this is the core of it.
