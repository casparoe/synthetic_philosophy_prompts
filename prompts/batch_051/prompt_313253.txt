I've been thrown into this topic by accident: my sister was recently accepted onto a trial for a speech-decoding brain-computer interface after a brainstem stroke left her with locked-in syndrome. The device picks up signals from her premotor cortex as she attempts to speak and turns them into text on a screen. The consent form talks endlessly about 'neural data', the surgeon says it decodes intended speech, and the company's press release says it 'reads her mind'. I'm meant to write an assessed essay for a neuroethics course on whether this sort of technology gives us reason to treat mental privacy as something stronger than ordinary data protection — specifically, as a right of cognitive liberty over our own neural signals — or wether that framing is overblown rhetoric that would wreck sensible regulation. I keep going round in circles.

For the essay (roughly 2,000 words), please weigh the strongest version of each side. The cognitive-liberty side should say something like this: neural signals are not just behaviour, because they get captured before the person has committed to anything — including before self-censorship — so normal consent and privacy frameworks miss the distinctive harm. The other side should say something like this: a decoder that reconstructs attempted speech is closer to a very good keylogger or lip-reader than to mind-reading, and 'thought-reading' is a category mistake, because most of what the implant outputs corresponds to speech the person was trying to produce, not private reflection. Try to draw a defensible line between (a) decoding what someone is trying to say, (b) decoding inner speech or other thoughts they were not trying to communicate, and (c) raw neural activity that isn't propositional at all. Then commit: do we need a new right of cognitive liberty, or should this be folded into existing privacy and consent protections? Defend your conclusion properly rather than ending with 'both sides have a point'.
