I've ended up co-teaching a three-session unit on what, if anything, makes a deductive argument force you to accept its conclusion, for an undergraduate logic-and-argument seminar -- fourteen students, 75-minute sessions, mixed majors, a few with no philosophy background at all. The topic got under my skin last month after I reread Lewis Carroll's little dialogue where the Tortoise accepts the premises, accepts that if the premises are true then the conclusion is true, but still refuses to take the step to the conclusion; when Achilles writes the "if then" down as another premise, the Tortoise accepts that too and simply asks for the next one, and so on. I can see it on the page, but I don't think the students actually feel why adding more written rules doesn't help -- half of them think the obvious fix is to write the rule down in bigger letters, so to speak.

What I'd like is a single 75-minute structured game or role-play for the seminar where the group runs into the problem themselves before it gets named. Here is my rough image: one participant plays a Tortoise whose only job, in good faith, is to comply with exactly what the written rules say and nothing more, while everyone else plays Achilles and has to write a rulebook that forces the Tortoise to reach a target conclusion from some accepted starting claims. I want the rules of the game, the roles, a timeline with rough minute marks, what the facilitator does and does not say at each stage, and an explicit debrief that maps specific moments in the game (the first fix, the second fix, the point where they realize it's the same move again) onto the idea that the rule of inference might not be the kind of thing you can put in the notebook as just one more premise. Please also say what predictably goes wrong when this is actually run -- my guess is the Tortoise gets blamed for being obstinate when they're actually playing the role faithfully -- and how the facilitator should handle it. And be honest about which feature of the idea a game with a cooperative-but-literal Tortoise can't reproduce; I suspect it can't reproduce the feeling of actually being compelled by a valid argument and may overstate the skeptical case, but flag anything else.

Two more constraints. Please keep the activity self-contained and not require outside readings or sources; the students should be able to run it with just the rulebook and the rules of the game. And in the debrief notes, separate what is basically settled in how people read and teach this material from what is still genuinely contested among people who work on the justification of deduction, and don't present a contested reading as if it were the consensus. I'd rather tell my students "this is one live way of seeing it" than accidentally turn it into a tidy moral.
