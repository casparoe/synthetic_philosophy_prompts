I've been going around in circles on the paradox of blackmail and want a definitoin of blackmail that actually sorts the cases. The paradox, as I understand it, is that blackmail seems to be built out of two things that are each perfectly legal on their own: you're allowed to reveal embarrassing-but-true information, and you're allowed to ask someone for money. But when you make the money the price of not revealing, suddenly it's a crime. So I want a definition of 'blackmail' that captures what goes wrong in the combination, not just 'threatening something bad' because that would catch way too much.

Here are my cases. Give me the definition, run it through all seven, then tell me which verdict is least secure and whether you'd defend it or revise the definition.

A. You threaten to send photos of a married coworker's affair to their spouse unless they pay you $5,000.

B. A prosecutor tells a defendant: plead guilty to the burglary, or I'll also charge you with the assault from that night—and both charges are ones the prosecutor could legally bring.

C. A union votes to strike unless the employer raises wages by 6%; the strike itself would be legal.

D. A store owner settles a lawsuit: they'll drop a claim they have a right to pursue in exchange for $3,000 from the customer.

E. A customer emails a restaurant: 'I found a live bug in my soup. I'll post photos and a one-star review unless you refund the meal and throw in a $100 gift card.'

F. A journalist is about to publish an embarrassing true story about a politician. The politician's staff offers her $50,000 to not publish it, and she takes the money. She never demanded anything.

G. You know your neighbor has been stealing from the till at work. You tell him: turn yourself in by Friday or I'll report you to your boss.

My gut says A is clearly blackmail, B, C, and D clearly arn't, E is sort of blackmailish, F is on the journalist who takes the money rather than demands it, and G I keep flipping on.
