Next week I'm presenting at my school's law-and-philosophy club (mostly debate kids, plus our faculty advisor who used to be a litigator and only really cares what this would mean in a actual courtroom). My thesis is that legal fictions aren't the weird exception to how law works—corporate personhood, a ship being sued like it's a person, that kind of thing—they're the clearest example of how all legal categories work. Law doesn't discover categories like 'person,' 'property,' or 'contract'; it stipulates them, and the only difference between a 'contract' and a 'corporate person' is that we've stopped noticing the first one is also an 'as if.' So any theory that tries to ground legal rights in pre-legal facts (real promises, natural persons, whatever) falls apart on it's own basic vocabulary. The club has a mix: one kid is basically a legal positivist, another comes from natural-law debate and thinks I'm saying actual human beings don't anchor the law, and the advisor will probably bring up criminal liability and piercing the corporate veil. Give me the objections in roughly the order I'll get them, phrased the way those three would actually ask them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my argument I should prepare to defend instead of hoping nobody notices. Also include the objection I'm secretly hoping nobody asks. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the ranking and the replies, and what you're least sure about.
