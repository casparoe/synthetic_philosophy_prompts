I serve on the Commission on Public Memory of a mid-sized U.S. city. The commission must decide what the city should do with a recently discovered basement archive of roughly 4,100 civilian information reports filed with the city police between 1953 and 1977. The reports, written by ordinary residents about their neighbors' politics, private associations, and family lives, were used for surveillance and blacklisting and contributed to at least ninety documented firings. Several people named in the files are still alive, as are many of their children. The archive is intact, legible, and currently unknown to the public.

The commission has split into three camps. One holds that the city owes the people named in the archive, and their descendants, a duty to remember, and that the only way to discharge that duty is to preserve the files and make them publicly searchable once a release protocol is in place. A second camp argues that public release would open old wounds, turn pressured confidences into permanent public property, and let the informants' judgments continue to define their targets; on their view the commission should affirm a positive case for forgetting: seal the files for restricted research use, allow a memorial record to be drawn from them, and destroy the originals after a fixed period. A third camp would preserve the files only in anonymized, aggregate form, on the ground that what the city ought to remember is the surveillance system itself rather than specific people.

Draft the commission's decision memo. It must contain an actionable recommendation to the city (public access, restricted research access with eventual destruction, anonymized preservation, or some specified variant), the reasons for that recommendation, and an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. Treat the disagreement as a substantive dispute about whether remembering is something the city owes and whether forgetting can be a legitimate communal act. Do not settle it by recommending that all three approaches be pursued, and do not rest the recommendation on what most current residents would prefer.
