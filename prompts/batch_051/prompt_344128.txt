I have been going back and forth on whether recursive interpretability has a principled stopping point, and I would like an essay that commits to an answer rather than settling into a both-positions-have-merit posture. The question can be put as follows. If we use one AI system to interpret another, we can trust the interpretation only to the extent that we understand the interpreter; the interpreter's own behavior can therefore seem to need an interpretation, and so on upward. One side holds that the regress is benign: each level of interpretation can render the target less opaque, and the interpreter one level up can be strictly simpler than the system it explains, until we reach something boring enough to audit by hand — for example, a small formally verified checker. The other side holds that opacity is exported upward rather than eliminated, since a claim that a system does some particular thing is never stronger than our grip on the interpreter that produced it, and the relevant kind of simplicity can only be bought by giving up the explanatory power that made the interpretation informative in the first place. Give each position its strongest form, then argue for a definite conclusion. In particular, make clear which notion of interpretation is doing the work in your answer: a behavioral guarantee that outputs remain inside some described class, or a mechanistic explanation that would let a competent engineer anticipate and repair failures. Commit to a verdict about whether the regress bottoms out, and under what conditions.
