I am a doctoral student in sociolinguistics and the graduate-student member of our university's Academic Appeals Committee. We meet next week on an appeal, and I have been asked to circulate a short written decision memo.

The case. A master's student was removed after twice failing the thesis-proposal milestone. Her supervisor, who was educated in Japan and has run this research group for fifteen years, wrote on the first failed proposal: "The comparative chapter would benefit from a fuller treatment of a contrasting case"; "The committee may expect the argument to be arranged differently"; "The proposed timeline is perhaps optimistic." At a progress meeting she also told the student the draft was "broadly on the right track." The student, who did her undergraduate degree in the Netherlands, read these remarks as mild encouragement and made only light revisions. The supervisor has testified that, in her mentoring style, "would benefit from" and "perhaps" are strong warnings — the face-saving equivalent of "this must be fixed or you will fail" — and that she said "on the right track" to preserve the student's confidence. The student's appeal argues that she was entitled to be told the true state of the work, that the reassurance functioned as a white lie with material academic costs, and that the institution should not let a student's fate turn on unmarked cultural skill in decoding indirect speech. She requests one further funded semester and a resit with severity-labeled feedback: every written comment tagged as suggestion, recommendation, or required for passage.

The supervisor and the department chair oppose severity labeling. They argue it would degrade mentorship into a bureaucratic checklist, that explicit severity tags can wound students who depend on face-saving encouragement, and that a university-wide rule of Dutch-style directness merely replaces one cultural communication norm with another. They also note that the student could have asked what the phrases meant.

Draft the memo. It should recommend a concrete decision on the appeal — uphold the removal, grant the resit unconditionally, or grant it with conditions — and may attach policy changes if they follow from the reasoning. The memo itself should state the recommended decision in its first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preface and no restating of the facts or the question. It should engage the strongest argument on the losing side seriously rather than dismissing it.
