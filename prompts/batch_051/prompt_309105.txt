I'm a philosophy professor, and at our weekly ethics work-in-progress lunch last Thursday, two of my PhD students, Maya and Oliver, got into a proper row about a friend of theirs. The friend — a recent PPE graduate — has taken a high-paying data science job at a firm that builds personalised adverts for online gambling sites, and she plans to donate something like sixty per cent of her salary to global health charities, mostly bed-net distribution and vitamin A supplementation. The argument ran through most of the lunch and by the end they asked me to referee. I had a supervision straight after and honestly couldn't give them a decent answer on the spot.

Maya said it was a textbook case of earning to give: the job is legal, the friend is replaceable, and if she doesn't sit in that seat someone else will, and that someone won't donate sixty per cent. So the marginal harm of taking the job is close to zero and the marginal good is enormous — you can count the lives saved, and refusing doesn't change the gambling industry one bit. Oliver said this is exactly the mistake effective altruists make. Skilled people aren't perfectly replaceable, and even if her work won't single-handedly expand the firm's reach, it does make their adverts better at pulling people into gambling. He said she's funding easily measured good with diffuse, unmeasured harm, and treating the unmeasured harm as if it were zero isn't a virtue, it's a bias. Maya shot back that Oliver's line would rule out almost every job: the university happily takes money from banks and oil companies, and if complicity means working somewhere that does some serious harm at scale, nobody is clean. At least her friend is offsetting with actual bed nets rather than polishing her conscience with a seminar on systemic injustice. Oliver said that's precisely the institutional critique: the whole frame is individualistic. It treats the main moral question as 'given this system, how do I personally maximise good?', when the question ought to be 'how do we change the system?'. He added that the reason bed nets show up as the answer so reliably is that they can be counted, while the harm of entrenching predatory industries — and normalising the career path that does so — can't be, and that measurability is doing all the epistemic cheating. At that point Maya asked what concrete harm the friend causes by taking a job that would otherwise be filled by someone who wouldn't donate, and Oliver asked whether that counterfactual actually makes the betting market any less predatory. Neither would move.

I've heard alot of this before from both undergrads and colleagues, but I've never seen it staged so cleanly. Could you walk through the exchange turn by turn and say who's right about what — which claims hold up, which overreach, and where they're talking past each other — then give a bottom-line verdict on who is more right overall?
