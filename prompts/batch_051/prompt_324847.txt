OK here's the situation. Through a weird series of events I've just become the studbook keeper for a small captive-breeding program for an endangered frog. The director is worried that our whole breeding strategy amounts to picking whichever frogs happened to leave the most surviving offspring last season, and she wants me to turn it into something defensible before the next breeding season. A philosopher friend of mine called this the old tautology objection to natural selection: you can't explain or predict reproductive success if fitness just is the reproductive success you observed. The usual fix is the propensity interpretation, where fitness is a probabilistic disposition to survive and reproduce in a given environment, not the actual number of offspring an individual happened to leave. I want the decision procedure built on that distinction, not just wearing it as decoration. So here's what I need: a step-by-step procedure for deciding, from the records we actually keep (pedigree, clutches, offspring number, survival to metamorphosis, maybe three seasons of data), whether an observed difference in offspring counts warrants treating one frog as having a higher fitness-propensity rather than just having had a lucky year. Work two or three cases through the procedure, and make at least one of them a case where the procedure says we shouldn't pick the frog we'd currently pick, like the one with the highest single-season count. Flag the points where the procedure can't settle it from these data and a judgement call has to be made. Assume the person doing this has no philosophy background and only a spreadsheet. Begin with the procedure itself, no preface or commentary.
