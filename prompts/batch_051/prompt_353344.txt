In a typical zoning hearing, a developer proposes replacing a single-family house with a modest four-story apartment building, and the planning commission takes testimony from neighbors about parking, shadows, noise, and the character of the street. Those concerns can delay, shrink, or kill the project. The people who would live in the new building, however, get no vote, no speaking slot, and no formal standing: their interests are not weighed and found less important, but are simply absent from the process. This creates a structural asymmetry in whose interests count. The losses of current residents, including comparatively small ones such as a slightly longer search for parking, are treated as serious constraints on what may be built, while the gains of would-be residents, sometimes as large as an affordable home, a much shorter commute, or escape from overcrowding, are counted at zero.

Elsewhere we treat it as a basic requirement that a decision imposing a significant harm on someone should at least count that person's interest and usually give them a voice. Is there a defensible principle that justifies giving current residents this privileged procedural standing while denying it to future residents, or is the asymmetry an accident of municipal boundaries that we should not endorse on reflection? The strongest answer should identify the best candidate justification—self-government belongs only to current members; homeowners have bought a particular local environment; outsiders can settle elsewhere while insiders cannot opt out of the change—test it against a nearby case such as a private club voting on whether to admit new members, and say whether the principle survives or should be given up.
