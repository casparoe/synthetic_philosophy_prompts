I keep seeing people argue about what some agent 'actually wants' from a few observed choices — a rat in a maze, a chess engine, a recommender system — and it always seems to turn on hidden assumptions about what the agent could even have searched over. I want the full spec first. Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of inferring an optimizer's objective and search space from its behavior: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse, and the constraints it has to respect, with the consideration behind each. It should cover at least: telling a genuine optimizer apart from something running a reflex, a fixed policy, or a lookup table; seperating the objective from the agent's beliefs, constraints, discounting, and planning horizon when different mixes of those produce identical behavior; dealing with the fact that any finite behavior is consistent with infinitely many objectives and search procedures, and saying what priors or simplicity biases break the tie; saying what can still be inferred when you only see a few trajectories and maybe not the agent's inputs; and what to do when the agent is better at optimizing than the observer is, so 'what would I do in its place' is exactly the wrong heuristic. For each requirement, mark it non-negotiable or a camp preference. Then flag which subsets of the list can't be jointly satisfied — I especially want to know which combinations of 'recover the agent's objective,' 'stay agnostic about the search space,' and 'use only observed behavior' are mutually incompatible. Skip the scorecard; if you want, one line each at the end on how inverse RL, revealed-preference theory, and plan recognition do against it.
