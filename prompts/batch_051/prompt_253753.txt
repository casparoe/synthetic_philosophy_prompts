I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about paternalism, by looking at a series of cases and asking which principle would catch my intuitions. I am aware that my verdicts pull in different directions, and I would like to know whether there is a coherent view behind them, or whether I need to give something up.

Here are my cases. I am sure about the first few and much less sure about the later ones.

A friend is about to cross a bridge that I know, from a structural report I have just read, is unsafe. He does not want to hear my reasons. I grab his arm and stop him. Right.

A friend is about to text his ex whom he is trying to get over. It is two in the morning and he has been drinking. I take his phone out of his hand. Right, but less confidently.

A government requires cigarettes to be sold in plain packs with graphic warnings. I think this is acceptable.

A government bans trans fats in restaurant food outright, rather than requiring disclosure. I also think this is acceptable, though the food only harms the person eating it, and she could in principle have asked what was in it.

A university automatically enrols its first-years in a retirement-savings scheme unless they opt out. I think this is acceptable.

A social media platform makes the ‘delete account’ option genuinely harder to find, with the stated aim of preventing impulsive deletions. I think this is not acceptable, even though the company says it is protecting users from a decision they might regret.

A friend hides my cigarettes and tells me I have run out, to help me quit. I think this is not acceptable.

A friend, while I am drunk, tells me the bar has closed so I will go home. I think this is probably not acceptable, even though it produces the outcome I would want when sober.

A doctor tells a patient, who has asked for his prognosis, a softened version of it because the doctor judges the full truth would destroy the patient’s remaining hope and his family agrees. I think this is not acceptable, but I cannot explain why it differs from stopping someone on a bridge.

A mother tells her adult son that his estranged brother did not phone on his birthday, when in fact the brother did not phone, in order to spare the son pain. Actually he did not phone, so she tells the truth. I am confused about why this feels different from the case above, and I am not even sure it does.

I also have a tentative principle, which I borrowed from a lesson we did on John Stuart Mill, but it does not get all of these, and I would like it tested and repaired rather than defended at all costs. Mill writes, in the fourth chapter of *On Liberty*:

Neither one person, nor any number of persons, is warranted in saying to another human creature of ripe years, that he shall not do with his life for his own benefit what he chooses to do with it.

But he also says, a little earlier in the same chapter:

As soon as any part of a person’s conduct affects prejudicially the interests of others, society has jurisdiction over it, and the question whether the general welfare will or will not be promoted by interfering with it, becomes open to discussion.

I used to find this compelling, but it cannot seem to explain the trans fat ban or the savings default. I would like a principle that does the best job of capturing all of these verdicts, states its verdicts on cases I have not listed, and tells me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why.
