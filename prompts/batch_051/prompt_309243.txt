I am a student in an upper-level undergraduate seminar called Coordination, Competition, and the Shape of Social Life. For my final project I have to design and run a 50-minute structured activity for the seminar (14 students, mostly philosophy and economics majors) on multipolar traps — the kind of situation the course discusses under the heading of Moloch, in which each player's locally rational move, given what the others are doing, produces an outcome that is predictably worse for everyone, including the players themselves, and in which no single player can unilaterally exit or improve things by acting alone.

The instructor has two constraints. First, no lecture up front: participants should run into the trap through the rules and payoffs, with the name and analysis supplied only in the debrief. Second, the activity must not be a standard two-person prisoner's dilemma or a voluntary contribution game. It should isolate the feature that makes a trap multipolar rather than a coordination failure that cheap talk or a monitor could fix. With three or more players, even full mutual knowledge of the payoff structure and an unenforced discussion round should fail to stabilize the cooperative outcome, because each player's best response is to deviate just enough to get ahead, and the defector's advantage persists while the others hold to the agreement. Ideally the activity also lets participants try the obvious fixes — first a promise, then some penalty or monitor — so they can feel how the trap reappears at the next level up once the enforcer is just another self-interested player, rather than being told this afterwards.

Please give me a complete session plan: materials (points or tokens, no real money), the exact rules, a payoff scheme with the numbers worked out so that the cooperative outcome is visibly better than the result the group will actually reach and the bad equilibrium is genuinely tempting, a 50-minute timeline, and a way to run the discussion round so that it predictably fails in the designed way. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the idea — local rationality, best-response reasoning, unilateral inescapability, and the difference between a multipolar trap and a commons problem that a binding rule can solve. By the end, participants should be able to say, about their own behavior, why the bad outcome was produced by individually rational choices rather than by ignorance or bad character. Also tell me what is likely to go wrong the first time I run it (for example, a participant playing dead or donating points, or the group actually holding an agreement), how I should handle each failure without breaking the instructional point, and end with an honest note on which feature of these traps a classroom simulation cannot reproduce and what I should say to supply it.
