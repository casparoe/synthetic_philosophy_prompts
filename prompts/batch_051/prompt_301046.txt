In a discussion group on democratic theory, someone defended the British and Scandinavian monarchies by saying that the monarch is purely symbolic, and added, as if it were obvious, that no one would want a hereditary chancellor of the exchequer or a hereditary chief justice. That concession struck me as more interesting than the defense itself. Citizens of functioning democracies are generally appalled by the idea of hereditary seats in a legislature, hereditary judgeships, or a hereditary military high command, even where the office in question would be stripped of real decision-making power. Yet a hereditary head of state in the United Kingdom, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, or Japan is treated as normal and often as a source of nonpartisan continuity and national dignity. A new democracy that created a hereditary speaker of its upper house—one who opens the session, reads a prepared speech, and holds no substantive powers—would be met with ridicule. What, if anything, justifies this asymmetry?

I would like the strongest principle you can find that distinguishes a hereditary constitutional monarch from these other hereditary offices, and then an honest test of that principle against nearby cases: a monarch who retains a real but narrow reserve power, say the power to refuse assent only in a genuine constitutional crisis; a ceremonial president chosen by lot for a single fixed term; and an appointed-for-life ceremonial president selected by a nonpartisan commission. Does the principle survive those cases, or does it collapse into tradition, symbolism, and status quo bias? Please give a verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, extended, or given up, and say explicitly what would change your mind—which argument or institutional arrangement would push you the other way—and where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
