The author's summary of the field runs roughly as follows. Over the past two decades, experimental philosophers have shown that the intuitions analytic philosophers treat as universal are in fact culturally local: Western and East Asian participants regularly give different verdicts on classic thought experiments. East Asian participants, for example, tend to give compatibilist responses to cases of agents acting in deterministic universes, judging that those agents act freely and are morally responsible, while Western participants are more likely to give incompatibilist responses. Work with people in small-scale societies reportedly shows that many of them do not share the Gettier intuition that a justified true belief falls short of knowledge. Since philosophical theories are built from just such intuitions, and since those intuitions vary by culture, the theories analytic philosophers have constructed from the intuitions of mostly Western undergraduates are, the author concludes, best understood as ethnographies of one demographic. The book's final chapter then argues that most of analytic philosophy's claims to culture-independent truth are unsupported, and that the field should either restrict its claims to the populations whose intuitions it sampled or defer to cross-cultural psychology. I suspect the empirical picture is tidier here than the underlying studies warrant and that the move from descriptive variation to local ethnography is too quick, but I do not know the literature well enough to audit the chapter precisely.
