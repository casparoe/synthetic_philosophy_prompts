Since retiring from a career in the natural sciences, I have been reading moral philosophy, and I keep circling around a distinction I want to get straight: the difference between being honest with someone and being readable by them. In ordinary talk we use sincerity, transparency, and legibility almost interchangeably, but I suspect they come apart, and I suspect the following argument slips from one to the other. I cannot, however, locate the exact place where it goes wrong—or say for sure that it does go wrong.

Please act as a professional philosopher working in the ethics of assertion and the epistemology of testimony. Before giving a verdict, spend some time distinguishing the possible meanings of sincerity, transparency, and legibility, and say how they relate. Then examine the argument step by step. For each step, state the strongest reading under which it is plausible and the reading under which the argument is most likely to fail; do not settle for a bare yes-or-no verdict.

Here is the argument.

(1) Sincerity is the norm by which a person presents to another person the attitude they actually hold—what they actually believe, want, or intend—rather than a counterfeit.

(2) Presenting an attitude to another person through words or deeds is not something that can happen wholly inside oneself; it requires making that attitude available to the other person through the communicative act itself, not merely through special knowledge of one's personal history.

(3) Making an attitude available to another person through the act itself means that the other person could, in principle and with the relevant shared background, read the attitude off the act—and that is just what it is for the act to be legible to that person.

(4) Therefore, sincerity in communication requires legibility in communication: to speak or act sincerely with respect to some attitude is to make that attitude legible to one's addressee.

(5) To the extent that an act is illegible—ambiguous, private in idiom, or deliberately opaque—it fails to make any attitude available to the addressee, and so fails to be a sincere presentation of any attitude.

(6) Since we owe others sincerity whenever we present ourselves to them, we owe them the degree of legibility required to make that sincerity possible.

(7) Therefore, there is a general duty of transparency or legibility: whoever addresses another person must, as far as feasible, speak in terms that the addressee can interpret correctly.

My question is this. Is the argument valid? If it is valid, are all the premises true? If it is not valid, tell me exactly which step, or which transition between steps, fails, and why. I am especially uneasy about steps (2), (3), and (5), which seem to run together what sincerity is with how others can tell that one is sincere, but I want to see whether that suspicion can be made precise. Also address the following: if the argument fails, is there some weaker but sound version of the conclusion—for example, a duty to avoid deliberate strategic opacity in asymmetric relationships, or an epistemic virtue of making one's reasoning examinable rather than a full moral duty of legibility—or does the argument fail so thoroughly that no such thesis survives? Please be as exact as you can about what the argument establishes, what it does not, and where its edges lie.
