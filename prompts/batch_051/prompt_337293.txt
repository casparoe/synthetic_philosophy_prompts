I'm working on a peice about offence and the sacred, and I want to hang it on a rewatch of Monty Python's Life of Brian. The distinction I keep circling is one that philosophers of religion and free-speech types sometimes draw between blasphemy in the strict sense — wronging or insulting God directly — and sacrilege or offence against the sacred — violating the fences a community builds around holy texts, rituals, places, and figures. The film is obviously irreverent, but I suspect its targets are mostly the fences rather than the god inside them: the squabbling revolutionary fronts, the crowd at the back that mishears the sermon, the followers who turn a dropped sandal and a gourd into holy relics. Even the final mass crucifixion looks to me like a grim joke about how humans keep their spirits up, not a shot at divinity.

Read Life of Brian through that lens for me. Which scenes should I point to in the essay, and what exactly do they show about the difference between insulting God and offending the community that guards the idea of God? Where does the film resist my tidy reading — where would an honest critic have to admit it does something stronger than poke at religious institutions? And what does the film get right philosophically about why people feel offence at the sacred, that the academic debates about blasphemy tend to miss? I dont need quotations and I'll rewatch for the details; what I need is the interpretation, plus an honest note on where it strains.
