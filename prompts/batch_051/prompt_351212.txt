Ok so a constitution-design idea ive been kicking around. Suppose a country writes this precommitment rule into its constitution: any change to the law that mostly benefits the parties or coalition currently in government takes effect only after the next general election. Any change that mostly benefits the side out of power — or is genuinely neutral, or burdens the current government's own supporters — takes effect immediately. The rule explicitly covers constitutional amendments, and a constitutional court decides which bucket each change falls in, with no appeal. The pitch is straight Ulysses-contract stuff: a majority has to legislate behind a partial veil of ignorance, because by the time the law bites it might not be the majority anymore, so self-serving rigging gets slow and visible.

Walk me through how this actually plays out. Who fights over what counts as "mostly benefits the current government," and what does that fight look like when the court's word is final? What kinds of laws get delayed, and does the delay mostly change substance or just timing? Say a party passes a law that clearly hurts its own base just to get it into force immediately — bug or feature? What about a party that quietly stalls an opposition-friendly change it actually supports, just to keep it out of force for now? Where does the system get gamed first: fake neutrality, delegating the real decision to a regulator (and how are the regulator's own rules classified?), calling a snap election, the court classifying a law about its own jurisdiction, or amending the constitution to kill the rule? What are the worst failure modes, and can the classification problem be patched — citizen juries doing the classifying, say — without making the whole thing toothless?
