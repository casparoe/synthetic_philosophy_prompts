I have been trying to work out whether our ordinary judgments about liberty are governed by a single principle or by a set of comfortable but ad hoc distinctions, and I keep coming back to the following asymmetry. Consider three cases. In the first, a highwayman confronts a traveler and demands his money on pain of death; the traveler surrenders his purse, and everyone, including the law, regards this as a paradigm violation of the traveler's liberty. In the second, an employer in a one-industry town offers a destitute worker the only available job on harsh terms: twelve-hour shifts, wages too low to save, no right to organize. If the worker accepts, few people describe this as a violation of the worker's liberty, and the law does not ordinarily treat the offer as coercion. In the third, an injured climber drags himself down a mountain because no one is there to help; no one calls this a loss of liberty either, only a misfortune.

On the surface, all three people end up with their options narrowed, but by different things: a threat, an offer, and natural circumstance. The negative-liberty tradition handles the first and third cases naturally by defining unfreedom in terms of interference by another agent, but it must then explain why the employer's offer does not count as interference, since the employer is deliberately shaping the worker's option set from a position of vastly superior bargaining power. The republican tradition, with its emphasis on arbitrary power and dependence, seems built to capture the second case, but it may then be forced to describe a rich benefactor's conditional gift as domination simply because the beneficiary depends on it. Positive-liberty accounts, for their part, seem to treat the worker and the injured climber as instances of the same underlying condition, which cuts against the sharp ordinary contrast between the second and third cases.

I am looking for the strongest principle you can give that either generates the ordinary three-way pattern — threats violate liberty, offers of this kind do not, and natural necessity raises no question of liberty at all — or shows that part of the pattern should be revised. Test your principle against two nearby cases: a monopolist who sets take-it-or-leave-it prices for a good the buyer cannot do without, and a charitable offer of rescue made by the only person in a position to help. Then tell me honestly whether the ordinary distinction between the highwayman and the employer should be kept, revised, or abandoned, and which tradition — negative, positive, or republican — comes closest to justifying it.
