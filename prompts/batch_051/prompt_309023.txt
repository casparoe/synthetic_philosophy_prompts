My friend has a bunch of very firm views about who ends up entitled to what, and I've been keeping a list because they seem to hang together but I can't quite pin down the rule. So far:

- She says if a million people each voluntarily pay a quarter to watch a famous basketball player, and he walks away with an extra $250,000 while everyone else is a quarter poorer, the resulting holdings are completely just, even though the distribution is now much less equal than before.
- She says if a mugger threatens to shoot someone unless they hand over their wallet, the mugger hasn't come to justly own that money, because a transfer made under that kind of threat isn't a real transfer.
- She says if I freely give my car to a stranger as a gift, and the stranger ends up richer than me and richer than most people, that's fine: I was entitled to the car, so now they are, no matter what it does to the pattern.
- She says if the state taxes wages that were earned legitimately just to make the distribution more equal, that's not automatically okay; taking a chunk of what someone earned is, she says, more like forced labor than charity.

Next week I want to ask her about a new case. A woman has inherited a painting that her grandfather bought in good faith in the late 1930s, at a normal price, from a dealer who had in fact stolen it; the family of the original owner has now come forward asking for it back. Neither the woman nor her grandfather had any idea it was stolen.

What's your guess about her verdict, and what principle is she actually running? I have a hunch it's some kind of historical or entitlement view, but "historical" could mean a few different things, so I also want to know which other rules fit her track record just as well and which further case would best seperate them.
