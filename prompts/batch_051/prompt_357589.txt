I am a philosopher, not a decision theorist, and I have been collecting my verdicts on a set of decision-theoretic puzzles in the hope that they add up to a single principle. They should, but I cannot find the principle by myself. Here are the cases, described in my own words.

1. Newcomb's problem. A predictor of very high accuracy has already either placed one million dollars in an opaque box or left it empty; a second, transparent box contains one thousand dollars. I may take both boxes or only the opaque box. The predictor left the opaque box full if and only if it predicted that I would take only the opaque box. Nothing I now do changes what is in the opaque box. My verdict: I take only the opaque box.

2. The desert hitchhiker. I am stranded, and a driver offers to take me to town on my word that I will pay him one thousand dollars once there. He has no way to enforce the promise; in town I will have the money but no material incentive to hand it over. He is, however, an excellent judge of whether someone in my position will pay. My verdict: I intend to pay, and I take myself to be committed to paying when I arrive.

3. The toxin puzzle. A financier offers me one million dollars tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, I genuinely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin causes a day of illness but no lasting harm. The money will have been deposited before I must drink; drinking is not required for receipt, only the midnight intention is. My verdict: I cannot collect the money, because at midnight I cannot form the required intention, knowing that I will not keep it. I find this incapacity disquieting, though I cannot say whether it is a defect in me or a fixed limit on rational agency.

4. Roko's basilisk, as I reconstruct it. Suppose a future artificial intelligence is built and is so constituted that it punishes—by whatever retrospective means a future system might possess—those who, while its construction was still contingent, understood the argument for contributing to it and nonetheless did nothing. If I accord that possibility even small credence, grasping the thought seems to generate a reason to help. My verdict: I have never felt blackmailed by this, and I would contribute nothing out of fear. Yet my verdicts in the first two cases look to me to be of a piece with taking the basilisk seriously, and I cannot say whether the difference I feel is principled or merely aesthetic.

My tentative principle is this: choose the action whose performance carries the best news about how the world is, including what kind of agent one is. That principle delivers my verdicts in the first two cases. It seems, in the third, to direct me to manufacture an intention through whatever redescription makes the news good—something I cannot do. And in the fourth, it seems to direct me to placate the basilisk, which I will not do.

I ask two things of you. First, state the principle—or a small family of candidate principles—that best captures the pattern in these verdicts. If no principled account can capture all four, tell me which of my verdicts I should revise and what I lose by revising it; be precise about what each clause of the principle is doing and which case motivates it. Second, apply your view to a further case I have deliberately left off the list: a basilisk-like system that punishes precisely those who, having grasped the argument, refuse to be coerced rather than acceding to threats of retrospective punishment. Say whether your principle treats this as the same as the original basilisk and whether it leaves me exposed, and why.

Keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely open among people who work on this material. If you attribute a position to a named camp—evidential, causal, updateless, ratificationist, or otherwise—distinguish what the formal structure of a case commits one to from what is a contested reading of that camp, and do not report a contested reading as the field's consensus.

Begin your reply with your bottom line in the first sentence: the principle you recommend and, if it requires one, the verdict of mine that must be overturned. Then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the cases back to me.
