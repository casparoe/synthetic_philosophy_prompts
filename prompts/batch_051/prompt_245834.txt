Right, here's one I've been chewing on. You're a professional philosopher with a background in the ethics of biography and historical fiction. Think through this carefully rather than giving me a snap verdict — start by brainstorming two or three candidate verdicts and the strongest objection to each, then work through the criteria one by one.

I've been trying to get clearer about what, if anything, a dramatist owes to a real person who's dead. (I'm setting aside the sceptical view that the dead can't be wronged at all — if you think that's actually the crux of the case, say so.) Here's a rough set of criteria someone might offer for when a film or novel wrongs its real, dead subject. It wrongs them when it: (i) asserts, or invites the audience to believe, a damaging falsehood about what they did or were — call it the falsehood condition; (ii) uses them as raw material in a way the living person would probably have refused, exploiting the fact that the dead can't object — the consent condition; (iii) distorts the meaning of their actual, documented choices, say by recasting a public act as a private grudge — the meaning condition; and (iv) foreseeably causes serious distress to people who actually remember the person — the survivors condition.

Now here's the case I keep going back and forth on. A prestiege film is made about a real woman — call her Elena Sarto — a minor but genuine figure in a 1970s strike. The historical record shows she built the strike committee, held it together through a long dispute, and was known to contemporaries as blunt and a bit ruthless in meetings. The film gets nearly all of that right. But the screenplay invents a scene in which, the night before the key vote, she privately threatens to expose a colleague's affair unless he votes to strike. There's no evidence anything like this happened; the screenwriter says the scene is there to dramatise her ruthlessness. The film is marketed as 'based on a true story'. Elena died twenty years ago. She has no surviving partner or children; one adult niece, who never knew her well, says the scene makes her aunt look like a blackmailer and has publicly asked for the scene to be cut.

Does the film wrong Elena Sarto? Go through the criteria honestly. Where you think the answer is no, say whether that's because the criteria are too demanding or because the case doesn't meet them. Keep the question of whether the niece is wronged seperate from the question of whether Elena is wronged. And tell me whether the criteria themselves need amending — for instance, should there be a condition about genre or marketing, or about how much time has passed, or about whether the invented scene concerns a private vice rather than a public act?
