I have recently found myself responsible for defending a decision in which a single policy produced clear benefits for one group and concentrated costs for another, and the experience has unsettled me about how far cost-benefit reasoning can be pushed before it stops being obviously legitimate. That is what led me into a reading group on distributive ethics, where one participant pressed the objection to utilitarianism known as the separateness of persons. He argued it primarily through an analogy. Classical aggregation, he said, operates as if the whole of society were a single person: it adds up everyone's pleasures and pains into one running total and accepts a loss to one individual whenever it is outweighed by gains to others. Inside a single life we accept exactly this structure, because the same subject who bears a cost now receives the benefit later, and so we say the sacrifice was rational. Across lives, he insisted, there is no corresponding subject who endures one person's loss and enjoys another person's gain, so the analogy illicitly imports a single point of view, a single set of preferences, and a single consciousness that the aggregate simply does not have. On the strength of that analogy he concluded that the move from individual prudence to social maximization is not just unappealing but structurally invalid.

I am less interested in a final verdict on utilitarianism than in an audit of this analogy. I would like the correspondence table made explicit: what in the utilitarian framework is supposed to play the role of the single person's momentary pains and pleasures, their preferences, their memory and anticipation, their willingness to trade the present against the future, and the coherence of their choice situation? Where does the mapping hold up, and where does it break? More importantly, which of the disanalogies matter for the separateness-of-persons conclusion and which are incidental to it? And once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion survive, or was it being carried by the picture of society as one person all along? If the analogy is misleading, I would also want to know whether there is a better analogy that captures what aggregative utilitarianism is actually doing across distinct persons, or whether the very idea resists being pictured in this way.
