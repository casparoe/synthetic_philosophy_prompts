I've been thinking about where, if anywhere, consciousness switches on in the animal kingdom, and I keep coming back to the following argument. It feels too strong, but I can't pin down the step that goes wrong.

(1) Normal adult humans are conscious: there is something it is like to be one.
(2) The features we take to be relevant to consciousness — behavioural flexibility, learning, sensitivity to stimuli, nervous-system complexity — vary more or less continuously across the animal kingdom. For any animal we confidently regard as conscious, there seems to be another animal, slightly simpler on these measures and close to it in ancestry, that we would be reluctant to declare wholly non-conscious.
(3) If two animals differ only slightly with respect to the features relevant to consciousness, and one is conscious, then the other is conscious as well — perhaps less richly, but not wholly lacking consciousness.
(4) Starting from humans and repeatedly applying (3) along the gradient described in (2), we arrive at animals of the simplest organization, such as sponges.
(5) So sponges are at least minimally conscious.
(6) But that conclusion cannot be right: a sponge has no integrated point of view; there is nothing it is like to be a sponge.
(7) Therefore one of the earlier steps must be false. The only serious candidate is (3). Yet if (3) is false, there must be a sharp line in the animal kingdom — a first wholly non-conscious animal as we move down the gradient — and no one can say where that line falls or what would determine it.

Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step (or transition between steps) fails, what the correct version of it would be, and whether rejecting (3) really forces a sharp line. I want to avoid both the conclusion in (5) and the sharp-line view in (7), and I can't see a third way.
