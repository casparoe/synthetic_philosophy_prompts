After a long career in the natural sciences—chiefly physical chemistry, with later work on biological systems—I have recently, since retiring, begun to work through the philosophical literature on reduction and emergence, and I am struck by how much work a single sentence is made to do: ‘the whole is more than the sum of its parts.’ In a departmental seminar I heard it used to dismiss, as obviously misconceived, the view that the behaviour of a many-body system is fixed by the properties and arrangement of its constituents. In a reading group on complex systems, the same sentence was used for the much weaker thought that bulk behaviour cannot be predicted from a parts-list without doing the calculation or simulation. Elsewhere it has been treated as a claim about explanation—that higher-level vocabulary such as temperature, fitness, or selection cannot be paraphrased away—and elsewhere still as a claim about causation, to the effect that genuinely new causal powers appear at higher levels of organisation. I would like these uses separated from one another. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims the slogan could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, drawing on concrete scientific examples (for instance from thermodynamics, condensed matter, or ecology) that support or expose it. Then state which reading actually does the work in each of the four uses I have described, and indicate whether any of those arguments could get by with a weaker reading or would collapse if only the stronger readings were available.
