Last night a friend and I argued for several hours about Emmanuel Levinas's claim that ethics, rather than metaphysics, is first philosophy, and that the face of the Other resists my powers and places a demand on me before any theory or choice on my part. He left me with an argument that I cannot get out of my head, because it seems to prove too much: it either reduces the face to something I construct or turns it into something I never actually meet. The argument runs as follows.

(1) If ethics is grounded in the face of the Other, then the face must be something I can in principle encounter.
(2) Whatever I can encounter is something that is given to me.
(3) Whatever is given to me is given within the horizon of my own meanings, projects, and categories; there is no given that arrives without being taken up into the sense I make of things.
(4) Therefore, when I encounter the face of the Other, the face is already taken up into my own sphere of meaning, and so it cannot be the radical exteriority that breaks into my self-enclosure and calls my freedom into question, as Levinas insists it does.
(5) Hence either ethics is not grounded in the face of the Other, or the face is not something I encounter at all.
(6) The first option abandons the heart of Levinas's position; the second leaves the face as a merely theoretical posit that can do no ethical work.
(7) So Levinas's account is either false or ethically empty.

I find the conclusion intolerable, but I could not locate the step at which the argument goes wrong. I would most like to keep the thought that the face is genuinely encountered and genuinely other, because otherwise the whole idea collapses into a metaphor. Which premise or step should be rejected, why did it look plausible, and what is the cost of rejecting it — and if the argument is valid after all, which premise is least costly to give up?
