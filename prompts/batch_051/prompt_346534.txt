Having retired from a career in the physical sciences, I have lately been reading about a puzzle in the philosophy of action and decision: whether a person can, while genuinely deliberating about what to do, assign a probability to her own future choice. A member of my reading group argued that she cannot. His case rested on an analogy with measurement: trying to predict your own choice while you are still making it is like trying to measure the temperature of a bath with a thermometer that is itself immersed in and altering the bath. The thermometer does not passively record the bath's temperature; it perturbs it, so the final reading is not a reading of the unperturbed system. By the same token, he claimed, a probability assignment made during deliberation feeds back into the deliberation and changes the very choice it was meant to predict. His conclusion was that genuine deliberation crowds out prediction, and that decision theory errs when it represents agents as holding credences over their own possible acts.

I would like this analogy audited the way a good experimentalist would audit a measurement protocol. Give the correspondence table: what plays the role of the bath, the unperturbed temperature, the thermometer, the immersion, and the final reading? Then identify the points where the mapping breaks. A deliberating agent, unlike a bath, is aware of the prediction being made about her, can reason about its effects, can adjust her criteria, and can settle the question by deciding rather than predicting. Which of these differences are cosmetic, and which of them remove features the analogy was silently importing—for example, that the target quantity exists determinately before one measures it, or that the perturbation and the measured quantity are independent? Finally, state whether the conclusion survives once those imported features are removed, and offer the best improved analogy you can, from measurement, control, or elsewhere, with a note on where that improved analogy breaks as well.
