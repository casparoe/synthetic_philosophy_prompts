"Dr. Alvarez, an oncologist, has a patient who asks, 'Did the scan find a tumor?' The pathologist's report says the scan shows an ambiguous mass that is 'concerning but not diagnostic of malignancy.' Dr. Alvarez knows the patient will take 'we didn't find a tumor' to mean everything is fine, and she wants to spare the patient anxiety, so she says: 'The scan didn't find a tumor.' Is Dr. Alvarez's action morally on a par with simply lying to the patient that the scan was clear? First explain the distinction between lying and misleading, then take a position on whether that distinction carries moral weight in this case. Defend your position."

Here's the student's answer, word for word:

"Dr. Alvarez did not lie. Lying requires asserting something the speaker believes to be false, and her statement was literally true: the scan did not identify a tumor in the official sense, since the report only describes a concerning mass. So she misled rather than lied. The question is whether this matters morally. I think it does. Kant's absolute prohibition on lying is usually interpreted to leave room for misleading speech, because the categorical imperative rules out false assertions but not true evasive ones. In addition, the patient is partly responsible for the false belief, since she drew an inference that went beyond what Dr. Alvarez actually said; she could have asked whether the scan was normal. Because Dr. Alvarez asserted something true and let the patient complete the false inference on her own, her action is still wrong—she was paternalistic and withheld important information—but it is clearly less wrong than lying to the patient that the scan was clear. So the distinction does carry moral weight in this case."
