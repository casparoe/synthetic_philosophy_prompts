I've been rereading the preface to Mill's A System of Logic, and there's a passage there I keep coming back to. He writes:

"Whether moral and social phenomena are really exceptions to the general certainty and uniformity of the course of nature; and how far the methods by which so many of the laws of the physical world have been numbered among truths irrevocably acquired and universally assented to, can be made instrumental to the formation of a similar body of received doctrine in moral and political science."

Now here's the case I want you to reason through. A historian makes the following claim about the collapse of the Roman Republic: it was overdetermined. The concentration of land and wealth, the transformation of military commands into instruments of personal ambition, and the Senate's refusal to absorb new elites were each, on their own and given the standing background conditions, sufficient to bring about the constitutional breakdown; they did not merely jointly produce it, and you shouldn't treat any one of them as "the" cause. That's a fairly standard overdetermination-style explanation, but I want to know whether Mill's stated ambition in that preface can handle it. If moral and political phenomena are not exceptions to the uniformity of nature, and if the methods that worked for physics are supposed to produce received doctrine in history, then what does Mill's own method of difference—or, for that matter, his method of agreement—actually do with a case where a phenomenon has several independent sufficient causes? Does the historian's overdetermination claim count as the sort of law-like result Mill hoped for, or is it a repudiation of the whole project, and why? Don't tell me what commentators have said; I want you to work out what the passage commits Mill to, and whether an overdetermined historical explanation can make good on that commitment. If your answer is that Mill can't have both his preface and his later discussion of plurality of causes, say exactly which one has to give and what the historian should say instead. And if you think he can have both, explain how the inductive methods are supposed to seperate a sufficient cause from a merely contributing one when the event happened only once.
