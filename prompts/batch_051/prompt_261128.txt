I'm trying to stress-test a view in the aesthetics of games, and I've reached the point where I need an objection I haven't already catalogued. Let me state the view carefully, because I want the new objection to engage with exactly this target and not with a neighboring one.

I'll call it the sculpted-agency view. It holds that games are an art form whose medium is agency itself. The designer's material is the player's practical situation: which choices are live, what counts as a reason or a cost, what the player notices, risks, and feels answerable for. The aesthetic object, on this view, is not primarily the image, the music, or the story, but the shaped experience of acting — the way a game lets a person inhabit a way of deciding, desiring, and evaluating — and the player's aesthetic engagement occurs from inside that activity rather than through contemplation of an external object.

I have already collected five objections, which I'll put in my own words:

1. The control objection. An artist determines the features of the work; the player determines much of what actually happens in play. So the designed object and the encountered object come apart, and agency cannot be a medium in the way paint or sound is, because the "artist" does not control the work's actual qualities.

2. The instrumental-attitude objection. In play, attention is aimed at winning, solving, or progressing, not at attending to the experience for its own sake. That is a practical stance rather than an aesthetic one; appreciation requires something like disinterested attention, and goal-seeking undercuts it.

3. The over-inclusiveness objection. If sculpting a practical point of view counts as an artistic medium, then airport layout, queuing systems, and casino architecture are also art whenever they shape agency effectively. The view cannot say why games are the art of agency rather than any well-designed exercise of behavioral control.

4. The borrowed-content objection. The features we actually praise in games — the visuals, the score, the dialogue — are achievements of older art forms. The rule-systems and affordances are admired the way a well-made tool is admired, and the view mistakes good design for art.

5. The unstable-object objection. A painting or a poem is a stable public object that can be revisited and shared. The agential experience of a game is private, ephemeral, and different across players and playthroughs, so there is no single work for a community of appreciators to encounter and criticize.

I want a sixth objection that is genuinely different from these five — differing in the kind of consideration it raises, not just in the example it uses, and not a rewording, special case, or combination of anything above. Then give me your honest verdict on it: does it do real damage that the five don't, or does it only force a clarification the view can absorb? If you conclude that the list already covers the ground, or that the underlying question is confused — for instance because two senses of "art" are being run together — I would rather you tell me that flatly than supply a cosmetic sixth objection for the sake of completeness.
