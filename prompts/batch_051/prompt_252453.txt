I keep noticing a structural parallel between the institutions-vs-geography-vs-culture debate about why some nations are rich and the old nature-vs-nurture debate in behavior genetics, and I cant decide whether it's deep or whether I'm just pattern matching on "there are three boxes and a messy outcome." Here's the mapping as I see it. Geography plays the role of genes: a slowly changing endowment that's fixed before the outcome, that no country chooses in the short run, and that does much of its work through the other variables. Culture plays the role of the shared environment: transmitted from the people around you, sticky across generations, and not the product of a single deliberate decision. Institutions are the odd one out, because they're the part of the environment that agents actually design, but they're also inherited from colonizers, wars, and accidents, so maybe they sit somewhere between shared and nonshared environment. The methodological structure also looks alike to me. Both debates try to partition variance in a complex outcome across correlated causes using observational data; both face the problem that the "endowment" cause selects or shapes the "environmental" cause (genes select environments via gene-environment correlation; geography selects institutions via disease and settler mortality); and both have to answer the objection that a variance decomposition doesn't tell you what an intervention would do, because the decomposition is population-specific and the correlations can change.

So my question: is this a real identity of causal structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion? If it's real, which results actually transfer? For example, behavior genetics found that shared family environment explains surprisingly little of adult personality and IQ variance once genes are handled; does that predict that "culture," the shared inherited context, explains surprisingly little of national income once you handle the geography-institutions confound? And does the gene-environment correlation result transfer as "geography selects institutions, so controlling for institutions understates geography's total effect"? I'd also want to know where the mapping breaks: countries arent a population of exchangeable individuals, there's no random recombination or twin/adoption design, and institutions are chosen strategically with foresight. Name at least one thing the parallel predicts that is probably false, and say what would have to be true on both sides for the transfer to be more than metaphorical.
