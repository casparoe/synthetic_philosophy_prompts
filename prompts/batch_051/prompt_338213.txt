Design a thought experiment in which an endlessly extended human life escapes Bernard Williams's Makropulos dilemma, and then demonstrate that the case meets the specifications below. This is a design task, not a request for scholarship; do not quote Williams or any commentator and do not cite secondary literature. Explain the relevant concepts fully in your own words.

Williams's dilemma, restated plainly: he argues that an endless human life cannot be coherently wanted. Either the categorical desires that give a person reasons to go on living—desires whose objects are valued independently of the bare fact of being alive rather than conditional on it—are eventually exhausted and the life descends into tedium; or, to avoid that tedium, the person's desires and character change so much that what continues is no longer recognizably the same person's life. Endless life is, on this picture, either boring or not the continuation of the same life.

Your task is to construct a case that slips between the two horns.

Specifications

1. Same person constraint. The immortal must remain recognizably the same person in the sense Williams presses: a stable character and a connected set of categorical desires must survive the entire span. You may not lean on memory erasure, periodic personality resets, uploading, supernatural transformation, or any device that removes boredom by replacing, numbing, or dissolving the agent whose life this is.

2. No infinite content. Do not stipulate infinite novelty engines, preloaded infinite libraries of experience, or superhuman cognition that never repeats. Whatever novelty the life contains must be generated by a finite set of ordinary human capacities engaging with a changing ordinary world—new people, new problems, new performances, new local circumstances.

3. Genuinely desirable, not merely bearable. Say what the person looks forward to at roughly century one thousand, and explain why it is not the same thing they looked forward to at roughly century one hundred.

4. Concrete narrative. Present the case as the life of one specific person with a specific practice, role, or set of relationships, not as an abstract argument that some possible psychology might work.

5. Keep the scenario itself to no more than two substantial paragraphs; the walkthrough has no length restriction.

After presenting the scenario, run the check in prose:

(a) List the categorical desires in play, marking which are stable across the whole life and which are recurrent or generative.

(b) Show step by step why the case evades the first horn: why the stable desires are not simply exhausted, and why the recurrent or generative ones do not collapse into tedium.

(c) Show why the case evades the second horn: why the ongoing novelty does not add up to a new person.

(d) Identify the single structural feature of the case that does the anti-tedium work.

(e) Run the removal test: remove that feature and say whether the case collapses back into the Makropulos dilemma, and why.

(f) State the strongest attack a defender of Williams would make against the case, and answer it honestly.
