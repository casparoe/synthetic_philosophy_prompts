I am a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I am preparing a long essay on the moral arithmetic of population. Something in my own reporting keeps striking me as an asymmetry I cannot defend, and I want to know whether it can be defended.

When a public-health program prevents the deaths of a thousand infants, we describe the result as a thousand lives saved and treat the program as an unambiguous good. When a government enacts a non-coercive pronatalist policy—say, a child allowance and paid parental leave explicitly designed to raise the birth rate by a thousand—the same kind of description does not appear. We do not speak of a thousand lives added; we speak of demographic engineering, of nationalism, of instrumentalizing women's choices. Yet if the comparison is taken at the strictly impersonal level, both interventions leave the world with a thousand more living people than it would otherwise have had. The asymmetry grows stranger when the direction is reversed. A couple who choose to have a fifth child, explicitly for the sake of their community or their country's demographic future, are regarded as exercising a private right. A government that adopts the same reasons as public policy is regarded with suspicion.

I want the strongest principle that could justify these asymmetries. State it precisely, and then test it against the hard cases: a policy that merely removes financial obstacles to childbearing rather than rewarding it; a stillbirth-prevention campaign, which sits somewhere between saving and adding; a couple who have a large family for collectivist reasons; and a government that declines to fund family planning while insisting it is neutral on population. Be honest about where the principle fails. Conclude with a clear verdict: is the asymmetry worth keeping, should it be reversed, or should it be given up? Write this as analytical material I can use in the essay, not as a survey of the literature.
