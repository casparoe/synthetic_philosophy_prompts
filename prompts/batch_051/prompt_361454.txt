I maintain a small open-source library, and a group of other maintainers has been circulating a license text that tries to settle the derivative-work boundary with a bright-line rule. Under the proposal, a work is a derivative of a covered library only if it either (a) includes a source file copied from the covered library, or (b) copies twenty or more consecutive lines of the covered library's source in a form that survives preprocessing. Merely linking against a compiled version, importing a public header without copying from it, calling the library through an API, or exchanging data over a network or separate process does not trigger copyleft. Treat this as a mechanism rather than taking a position on its merits: what would it actually incentivize downstream developers, the library's maintainers, and the surrounding tooling to do? Where would it be gamed first — for example through vendored packages, build-time code generation, automatic refactoring, or services that relabel the boundary — and which of those games could be fixed without abandoning the bright-line idea? Please distinguish what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on open-source licensing, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus.
