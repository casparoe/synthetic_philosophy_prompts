I was having one of those long conversations with a friend who works at a research foundation, and she's been reading accuracy-first epistemology—the view that credences should be evaluated by how close they get to the truth, using scoring rules, and the epistemic utility theory that goes with it, where norms like probabilism get justified by showing that violating them doesn't improve expected accuracy. Her foundation is floating a proposal for how to fund replication studies. Every lab that takes money has to pre-register a set of claims and attach a probability to each one (replicates or not, sign of the effect, effect size falls in some interval, and so on). The payout isn't based on whether the lab turns out to be "right," but on the accuracy of those probabilities, scored with a strictly proper scoring rule like Brier or log score—which, I'm told, means your expected score is maximized by reporting your genuine credence, so you can't game it by nudging your numbers. Then there are three twists. Part of the payout depends on beating a panel of outside shadow forecasters who see the same pre-registration but don't run the study. Labs choose most of their own claims from a menu, though each lab is also assigned at least one generic replication claim. And there's a public market where anyone can trade on the lab's claims before the results are known.

I'm supposed to help think through this not as whether it's a good idea but as a mechanism. How would it actually behave if rolled out? What would it incentivize among labs, among shadow forecasters, among traders, and among the foundation staff who select which studies get funded in the first place? Where does it break—for instance, how do labs game claim selection, coordinate or copy predictions, or handle the interaction between the scoring payout and normal publication incentives? What happens with risk-averse researchers, with nearly unfalsifiable claims, or when the market lets people bet on someone else's results? And are the failure modes fixable by tweaking the scoring rule, the menu, or the market, or does it all drift toward some stable pathology? The specific thing I'd like answered: does the fact that Brier and log score are both strictly proper actually matter for the institutional behavior, or does that equivalence get swamped once you add the comparative payout and the market? Underneath that, I'm curious whether the whole setup is a fair test of the accuracy-first picture—whether making accuracy the explicit currency of a real institution produces a truth-tracking community, or whether the social dynamics detach the scores from actual accuracy.
