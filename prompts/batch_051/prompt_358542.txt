At the university where I teach, the statutes of 1907 contain a disciplinary clause that has suddenly become contentious. It reads: 'Any member of the University who, in any public part of the University or in the sight of any person assembled there, deliberately defiles, defaces, or treats with manifest contempt any book, image, vessel, or other object which the adherents of any religion hold sacred, and thereby gives grave offence to those adherents, shall be liable to expulsion.' The clause's drafters were fairly clearly thinking of a student spitting on an icon, trampling a scripture in a lecture hall, or emptying a consecrated chalice.

A final-year student has mounted a piece in the student centre called Afterimage. It shows high-resolution digital models of objects she knows many students consider sacred, including a Torah scroll, a copy of the Qur'an, and a consecrated host. Nothing happens until a visitor presses a button; the image then burns slowly and realistically until only ash remains on the screen. The originals are untouched and never leave their keeps. The programme resets after an hour, and the entrance carries a written warning about the work's content. Three religious student groups have complained that the piece treats what they hold sacred with contempt and that they are gravely offended. The artist responds that no object was damaged, that a projected image is not a thing at all, and that her aim was not ridicule but a serious enquiry into whether the sacral quality of a thing survives its reproduction.

I have been asked to say what the clause requires in her case. I am not after a guess about how the disciplinary panel will in fact vote. I want to know what each of the four standard approaches delivers: the ordinary meaning of the words, the original purpose of the clause, the probable judgment of the 1907 drafters if they had been asked, and the reading a reasonable current member of the university would now take away. When they diverge, which should control and why? In particular, if the clause does not reach the afterimage, is that because it draws a defensible line at physical things, or because it was never really protecting things at all but only protecting against grave offence—in which case the digital detail might not rescue the artist? Please resolve that ambiguity as part of the adjudication.
