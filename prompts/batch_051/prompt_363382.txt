I'm trying to get a handle on when I actually trust base rates, because my gut keeps coming down different ways on cases that feel like they should be the same kind of thing. A friend tells me her startup has maybe an eighty percent shot of making it five years, and I have no trouble thinking no, most small businesses fail, that's just denial. A couple we know gets engaged after six weeks and I'm quietly happy to apply the divorce-rate-for-fast-marriages to them. Predicting whether the new bistro on our corner survives two years: the citywide restaurant failure rate feels too broad, restaurants in this specific neighborhood feels right, but restaurants of that exact cuisine in this neighborhood feels too narrow, like I'm slicing until I get the answer I wanted. But when someone in the forecasting server I'm on says the United States has been a hegemon for roughly eighty years, and hegemons as a class tend to decline before they hit a century, so the smart money should be on American decline, I want to throw the whole reference class out. Those old empires weren't nuclear liberal democracies with these institutions, and the sample is tiny. Still, the startup friend could say the same thing about her company—it's not like the median small business, it has this team, this market. I don't buy it from her, but I'm making the exact same move in the hegemon case.

So I'm trying to work out what principle is actually doing the work for me. Something like: use a base rate when the case was drawn from the class by a process you trust and the class is big enough, but don't when... that's where it falls apart. What's the principle, or small set of candidate principles, that best captures the pattern of my verdicts? Be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, and tell me what it says about the one I keep going back and forth on: using the historical lifespan of empires to put a number on how much longer the current US-led order has left.
