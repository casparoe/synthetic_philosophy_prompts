I am a retired evolutionary biologist who has spent the past few years reading philosophy of mind, and I have been drafting a short essay on teleosemantics. I would be grateful for comments on the following notes; I have reached a point where I cannot tell whether my central analogy is doing real work or merely restating a familiar objection in biological vocabulary.

The essay presses on a difficulty I keep encountering in teleosemantic accounts of mental representation: the move from selected-effects functions to determinate content. The standard story says, roughly, that a state represents whatever condition it is the system's proper function to indicate, and that a token which fires in the absence of that condition is a misrepresentation. From a biologist's perspective, this seems to conflate two things that signal detection theory keeps apart: the detection criterion a mechanism has been selected to apply, and the actual state of the environment. Many evolved detection systems are tuned to tolerate a high rate of false positives when the cost of a miss is large, or to tolerate misses when false positives are costly. A token firing in response to a competitor stimulus is not always a breakdown of the device; it can be the device's normal, statistically expected operation given the error-cost trade-off under which it was selected.

The case I want to make central is Batesian mimicry. A predator's visual avoidance system is selected, in part, by encounters with both model and mimic. The receiver's behavior is maintained by the whole statistical mixture: the mimic's frequency, the cost of attacking the model, the value of eating the mimic. When the receiver avoids a hoverfly, it is doing exactly what its detection criterion tells it to do in that region of stimulus space. Teleosemantics wants to call this a misrepresentation of a wasp. But from the standpoint of selection history, the system was selected to get this very class of cases wrong relative to the underlying category, because the underlying category is not the quantity the system was selected to track. The quantity it was selected to track is something closer to a likelihood ratio or a risk-weighted decision variable. If that is right, the content of such an internal state is not a proposition about wasps at all, but something graded and probabilistic.

From there, the essay would generalize: rather than taking the bearer of content to be a state whose content is fixed by a mapping to a stimulus category, we might take the bearer of content to be the detection criterion itself, or the position of the decision boundary in an error-cost space. Content, on this view, is not a set of distal conditions but a kind of weighting over possible states of the world, and misrepresentation is not a category error so much as a high-entropy sample from the system's own assessment of the environment. I want to argue that this is friendlier to the project of naturalizing content than it first appears: it keeps normativity out of the foundations, but it gives up on the idea that determinate propositional content is the fundamental unit.

I can see the obvious reply: a teleosemanticist may simply refuse to assign wasp-content to the predator's state and say that the actual content is something disjunctive or coarse, and that this bullet, while costly, is not fatal. So the essay needs to explain what the mimicry cases add beyond that familiar pressure. My present thought is that they add a constraint on any acceptable account of misrepresentation: a good account should not classify a system's expected, design-consonant operation as error merely because the environment is hostile to the categories the theorist has chosen. But I am unsure whether this is a genuine constraint or only a restatement of my own intuitions.

I would particularly welcome comments on the following: (a) whether the signal-detection framing accurately characterizes the teleosemantic notion of misrepresentation, or whether it attacks a straw man; (b) whether the move to graded, likelihood-like content actually solves anything, or merely relocates the indeterminacy problem; and (c) whether the Batesian mimicry example carries weight for the human case, or whether it relies on treating very simple perceptual states as representative of mental content in general. I would also be glad for suggestions about how to tighten the argument, and about places where a more widely read philosopher would recognize this point as already made and in need of engagement.
