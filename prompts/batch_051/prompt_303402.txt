I'm a science writer working on a piece about the block universe, and I keep coming back to Minkowski's 1908 lecture "Space and Time." Right after he introduces the world-line, he says:

The whole world appears to be resolved in such world-lines, and I may just anticipate, that according to my opinion the physical laws would find their most perfect expression as mutual relations among these world-lines.

I read this as: the whole history of a particle is already there as a line, so the future segment of a world-line is no less real than the past segment. That reading has led me to a shelf of claims I'd like to run in the piece, but I honestly can't tell if their a coherent set. Here they are:

(1) Past, present, and future events are all equally real; the universe is a four-dimensional block.

(2) There is no objectively privileged now. The feeling that "now" is special is an indexical illusion, like "here" being special.

(3) The Rietdijk–Putnam argument is basically sound: since simultaneity is relative, an event that is future for me can be present or past for another observer, so future events are already determinate.

(4) But the past–future difference is still an objective feature of the world: entropy increases one way, and causes run earlier-to-later, not the reverse.

(5) And I still want to say my future decisions are open in a serious sense: before I decide, there is no fact of the matter about what I'll choose.

Can you go through these and tell me which pairs actually clash and which only look as if they do? For each real tension, say how severe it is and what the cheapest fix would be. Then tell me the single smallest revision — which of (1) through (5) to amend or drop, and how — that would make the whole package hang together. At the very end, put a line in the form \answer{...} with the number(s) of the commitment(s) to amend or drop, or "none" if you think it already hangs together.
