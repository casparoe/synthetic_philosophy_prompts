I'm in a high school philosophy club, and for our ethics unit a friend of mine sent the group chat his summary of a chapter about supererogation. His summary went roughly like this: "Supererogatory acts are actions that are morally good but not required, like running into a burning building to save a stranger or donating a kidney to someone you don't know. On the standard view, these acts are optional, so you're allowed not to do them, and that means there are no grounds to criticize someone who sits them out. Most defenders of supererogation think it can only exist in deontological theories, because those are built on rules like don't kill that leave space to go beyond duty, whereas utilitarianism, which says you must always do whatever produces the most good, can't allow supererogation at all. And in any case the whole category is probably self-contradictory: if an act really is morally better, then morality prefers it, and if morality prefers it, then it is just a duty, so supererogation collapses into ordinary obligation and the idea is an illusion."

I have to admit the last step sounds kind of convincing to me — if an act is better, why wouldn't it be required? — but the rest feels too quick, especially the claim that deontological theories are the only place supererogation makes sense, since don't deontologists also think you have a duty to help people? Could you go through the summary basically sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher working on this would accept, where my friend is overstating or conflating things, and wether the conclusion that supererogation is contradictory actually follows from whatever he got right? I dont need reading suggestions; I just want to know which parts I can trust and which parts I should push back on at our next meeting.
