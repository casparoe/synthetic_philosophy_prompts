I'm taking a seminar on free will this term and we just did Frankfurt cases, and I keep feeling like the whole debate is running on a equivocation. You know the standard setup: Black wants Jones to perform some act, but he only intervenes if Jones shows a sign he's about to do otherwise; since Jones does it on his own, Black never has to act, and the usual lesson is that Jones is responsible even though he couldn't have done otherwise, so the principle of alternative possibilities is false. But then people argue about whether Jones still had some little "flicker" of freedom left (like doing it on his own rather than being forced), or whether only "robust" alternatives matter, and I suspect those arguments are really about three different things that all get lumped together as an alternative possibility: (1) an alternative action being available, (2) an alternative source or initiation of the action being available, and (3) an alternative the agent could have chosen for reasons, not merely by luck. My half-formed idea is that seperating these out might dissolve the standard stalemate, or at least force each side to say which one it actually needs.
