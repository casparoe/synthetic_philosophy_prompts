I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. I work on a team that builds risk-scoring models used for decisions like credit pre-approval and which applications get routed to a human underwriter. Every time the company asks whether our model is fair, someone proposes a metric — demographic parity, equalized odds, calibration, predictive parity — and then someone else produces a plausible case where that metric conflicts with another one we also had good reason to care about. I have since learned that this is not just our confusion: there are known impossibility results in algorithmic fairness showing that some combinations of these properties cannot all hold at once when the base rates of the outcome differ across groups.

I don't want another survey of metrics. I want the specification that a notion of fairness for a predictive system would have to satisfy in order to count as adequate, with those impossibility results taken seriously. Please act as a professional philosopher who specializes in the foundations of fairness and in formal, mathematically informed philosophy. Before you write the final answer, take some time to brainstorm the different jobs a fairness concept might be asked to do — auditing an already deployed system, guiding a modeling decision, assigning responsibility for a particular bad outcome, allocating a scarce benefit — because I suspect some requirements look mandatory under one job and optional under another.

Then write the desiderata, with the concrete case or consideration behind each requirement, and say for each whether it is non-negotiable or a demand only one camp treats as fairness. In particular:

1. Which of the standard candidate requirements are genuine desiderata and which are camp-specific: calibration (roughly, a score means the same thing for a person in any group), balance or equalized odds (roughly, the rates of false positives and false negatives are similar across groups), predictive parity (roughly, the proportion of positive classifications that are correct is similar across groups), and demographic parity (roughly, decision rates are similar across groups)?

2. Which combinations of those requirements are jointly unsatisfiable given the impossibility results? I need the conflicts separated into two kinds: formal necessities that cannot be removed by more data or a better model, and empirical trade-offs that might shrink or disappear with a stronger model.

3. Which single requirement does the most work to rule out the standard candidate notions of fairness, and should the impossibility results push us toward giving up on a single unified notion of fairness, or toward a contextual account where the right notion depends on the purpose and on the relative costs of different errors?

I would also like an honest assessment of whether there is one underlying thing the word fairness is tracking in these systems at all, or whether we should treat fairness as a label for a set of partially conflicting engineering constraints. A table summarizing which combinations are satisfiable is welcome, but the requirements and their motivating cases should carry the weight.
