It strikes me that the species problem in biology and the problem of community detection in network science may be closer than they are usually taken to be. In both, one has a large set of items joined by a tangle of pairwise relations—gene flow or reproductive compatibility in the one case, edges of varying strength in the other—and one wants a principled partition into groups. In both, the available definitions (interbreeding, monophyly, ecological role; modularity, conductance, stochastic block models) each capture part of the intuitive notion of a group yet disagree on hard cases: ring species and chronospecies in biology, overlapping or nested communities in networks. Are the two problems genuinely isomorphic in structure, or is the analogy merely loose because biological species are not just clusters in a static similarity graph but populations with their own history, boundaries maintained by real processes, and perhaps a claim to be natural units? More pointedly: network science contains the observation that a partitioning method can fail to detect small but well-defined communities when the network as a whole is large—a ‘resolution limit’. Is there a species-level analogue of that limit, and if so, what would it show about the project of finding one true species concept? In answering, state the mapping precisely, say what would have to hold on both sides for results to transfer, and name at least one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false.
