I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've been reading Descartes's Meditations on First Philosophy, specifically the passage in the First Meditation where Descartes introduces the thought of a malicious demon who is supremely powerful and cunning and directs all its efforts to deceiving him. I keep thinking of the demon as a hypervisor that intercepts every input and writes frames directly to my framebuffer. But I have an argument that seems to prove too much, and I need help locating the bad step.

(1) If I know I am sitting here typing, then I can rule out the possibility that I am a brain in a vat being fed exactly this experience.
(2) I cannot rule out that possibility, because any experience, memory, or piece of reasoning I could use to rule it out is itself part of the simulated stream.
(3) So I don't know I am sitting here typing.
(4) The same move applies to every empirical belief: I don't know any empirical fact.
(5) But my premises in (1) and (2) are themselves empirical—about what can be simulated, what knowledge requires, what experience is like—or at least were acquired through ordinary experience and testimony.
(6) If I don't know any empirical fact, then I don't know the premises from which (3) and (4) are derived, so the argument cannot establish its conclusion.

This makes the skeptical argument look self-defeating, which seems too convenient. Which step should I reject, and what do I lose by rejecting it? If the argument is valid after all, which premise is least costly to give up?
