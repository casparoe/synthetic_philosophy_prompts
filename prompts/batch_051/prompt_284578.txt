You are a professional philosopher who works at the intersection of ethics and economics and who is experienced at presenting to interdisciplinary, adversarial audiences. I am giving a colloquium talk next month in a PPE program (philosophy, politics, and economics), and I need you to prepare me for the question period.

My talk defends the claim that the familiar debate about whether greed is a vice or an engine of prosperity is misframed. I argue that "greed" and "acquisitiveness," as used in that debate, cover two separable dispositions. The first is a bounded, institution-responsive concern for one's own material well-being and relative position; this disposition, suitably constrained by law and competition, does most of the work in the standard prosperity story. The second is an unbounded, comparative appetite for having more than others as an index of one's worth; it is a vice in roughly the Aristotelian sense, and whatever prosperity it produces is contingent and tends to undermine itself unless it is restrained by non-market norms. I conclude that the practical project should not be to condemn or celebrate greed wholesale, but to design institutions and cultural signals that cultivate the first disposition without licensing the second.

The audience, in roughly equal parts, will be: (1) neoclassical economists who work on growth and institutional design, who are inclined to treat self-interest constrained by competition and law as the mainspring of prosperity and to hear "vice" talk as unscientific moralizing; (2) virtue ethicists who think of greed as a settled defect of character and are suspicious of any view that treats a vice as acceptable social architecture; and (3) historically minded political philosophers who know Mandeville's paradox about private vices and public benefits and Smith's distinction between self-interest and benevolence, and who will punish any caricature of those positions. Assume the audience is smart, quick, and willing to press on soft distinctions.

Please begin by brainstorming the objections each of the three groups is likely to raise, in their own vocabulary. Then consolidate that into the prep sheet I will actually bring to the talk: the eight to ten objections I am most likely to receive, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way this particular person would put it, with a roughly two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is softest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks and explain why it is dangerous. Tell me which one objection I should preemptively concede in the talk itself, and which one I must hold firm on even though it will cost me.
