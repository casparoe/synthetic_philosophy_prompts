I am leading a book-club discussion of Dostoevsky’s The Idiot and want to set it against Susan Wolf’s essay Moral Saints. Wolf’s target is the idea that a person who is as morally good as possible would be an unambiguous ideal. On her account, the moral saint — the person whose every action is as good as it could be — pays a heavy price: the single-minded cultivation of moral virtue crowds out the non-moral excellences that make a life rich, such as wit, aesthetic sensitivity, play, and deep partial attachments, and the resulting person may be admirable in one register while being bland, humorless, or faintly inhuman in another. Prince Myshkin looks like an obvious test case. He is radically compassionate, nearly incapable of resentment, and willing to absorb the chaos of everyone around him, which makes him read alternately as a kind of secular Christ and as a holy fool. Yet the novel does not simply illustrate Wolf’s worry. Myshkin is not dull — his innocence is strange and magnetic — and the wreckage he leaves behind, Nastasya Filippovna murdered by Rogozhin, Aglaya humiliated, his own mind shattered, suggests the problem is less that his goodness is boring than that it is somehow unfit for a world of erotic jealousy, pride, and strategic self-interest.

Read The Idiot through Wolf’s critique. Where does Myshkin exhibit the costs of moral perfection, and where does the novel push back on or revise the critique — for instance, by locating the saint’s failure in a refusal to see certain motives as real, or by suggesting that the danger of maximal moral goodness is not dullness but a beautiful passivity that others experience as both a lure and a judgment? I would like the reading to be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the novel to an illustration of a thesis, and do not soften Wolf’s argument to flatter the novel. It is fine to conclude that the novel contributes something to the philosophical debate that the argument alone does not capture.
