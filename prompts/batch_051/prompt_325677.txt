I retired six years ago from a career in physical chemistry (mostly spectroscopy and reaction dynamics), and I've been spending my retirement reading philosophy of computation, which is thrilling and maddening in equal measure. Something bothers me: in debates about whether the mind is a computer, or whether physics could compute something uncomputable, people treat the Church-Turing thesis as one settled claim. But the further I read, the more it looks like several theses travelling under one name, with quite different statuses — one is a quasi-definitional claim about which formalisms capture the intuitive notion of an effective procedure, one is a mathemathical claim about the equivalence of the various formalisms (Turing machines, recursive functions, the lambda calculus), and one is a physical claim that no process in our universe can compute beyond the Turing limit. I've also gone down a rabbit hole lately about Malament-Hogarth spacetimes — solutions of general relativity in which one observer's worldline has infinite proper time inside another observer's finite past, so that in principle a computer could run an infinite computation and still report the result. That has made me suspicious that the physical version is less a law of nature and more a bet about what kind of spacetime we happen to occupy.

What I'd like from you is a short research proposal — six months, about five thousand words, for a small independent-research grant I'm applying too — on the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis. Please state one precise, answerable central question (something like: which versions of the thesis are empirical, which are stipulative or mathematical, and what could count as evidence for or against the empirical one?). Then explain the approach: why the project is mostly conceptual rather than empirical, where results from computability theory and general relativity genuinely have to be brought in, and why the standing positions (roughly, the view that it is just a definition, versus the view that it is a law of nature) leave the interesting questions open. Say what the possible conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them, and be honest about failure — what would a project that merely sorts the theses into boxes look like, and how would a better version earn its keep, perhaps by showing that many ordinary appeals to Church-Turing in philosophy of mind and debates over hypercomputation secretly trade on conflating the versions? Please don't include a bibliography or ask for citations; I'll fill those in myself. Start directly with the proposal, no preface or commentary.
