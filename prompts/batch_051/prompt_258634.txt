I have a set of verdicts about the timing of conscious experience, drawn mostly from thinking about the color-phi phenomenon, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them—or which of them must give.

Here is the case. A red spot is flashed briefly at one position, then a green spot is flashed briefly a short distance away, in quick succession. Observers do not report two stationary flashes; they report one spot moving across the gap and changing from red to green roughly halfway along its path. Since the second flash occurs after the first, the reported color change seems to happen before the second flash itself is presented.

My verdicts:

1. I take the observer's report at face value. The moving, color-changing spot is the content of the experience itself, not an inference drawn from two discrete flashes and not a later memory error.

2. I also accept that visual processing is slow enough that by the time a stimulus becomes consciously available, later events have already been registered by the brain. So I cannot hold that the experience of the red spot was already finished before information about the green spot arrived.

3. I reject the picture on which the observer first consciously sees a red spot already in motion and then, an instant later, forgets that first episode and comes to believe the color changed halfway along the path. The red-only moving phase was never there to be forgotten. Call this the memory-rewrite picture; it is sometimes labeled Orwellian.

4. I also reject the opposite picture, on which consciousness waits until the green spot has arrived, assembles a coherent segment offstage, and only then releases it as the first thing the observer experiences. There was no hidden red-only first draft. Call this the editing-before-entry picture; it is sometimes labeled Stalinesque.

5. I do not want to generalize any of this into skepticism about temporal experience. When I watch a slow, attended event such as a traffic light changing, the experienced sequence tracks the objective sequence; it does not feel like an edited reconstruction.

6. When a stimulus is presented and then masked so quickly that a person can report nothing about it, I think the stimulus never became conscious at all, rather than that it was conscious for a moment and then instantly forgotten. So I do think there is a line, however hard to state, between what reaches experience and what does not.

7. Yet I also doubt there is any possible experiment, however fine-grained, that could decide between the memory-rewrite picture and the editing-before-entry picture. Any report or behavior that the first picture explains by saying that an original was in consciousness and then forgotten, the second can explain by saying that the editing happened before anything reached consciousness.

I can already see that verdict 6 seems to sit awkwardly with verdicts 4 and 7, but I cannot tell whether that is a genuine conflict or only an artifact of a bad taxonomy.

My tentative principle is this: the contents of consciousness should not be dated by the moment at which they cross some threshold in the brain; an experience carries its own represented temporal frame, and there is no further fact about when the experience itself occurs beyond what the content represents and what the subject can do and say. If that principle is right, the opposition in verdict 7 is not an empirical question at all but a distinction without a difference.

What I need is a precise account that either delivers all seven verdicts together with this tentative principle, or shows which of them I must revise. If there is such an account, state it, say which clause does the work for which verdict, and tell me what it predicts about cases I have not listed—for example, the color-phi setup with the two flashes at the same location, a subject who is distracted and reports the display only several seconds later, or a later flash that modifies what a person seems already to have seen. If there is no such account, tell me which verdicts have to go and whether the memory-rewrite/editing-before-entry distinction is still doing useful work or should simply be dropped.
