I cover AI and ideas for a living, and I keep running into a line of argument I don't know what to do with. People say an agent can't ever have proper grounds to trust its own reasoning because of something called the Löbian obstacle. As I understand it, the argument assumes the agent's mind is a consistent formal system strong enough to talk about arithmetic. A system like that, by Löbs theorem, can't prove its own soundness: if it proved its own outputs were true, that would imply it can prove its own consistency, and a consistent system that strong can't do that. So any check the agent runs on itself is just the same machinery vouching for itself, and the whole thing is circular.

But the systems people actually build now don't look anything like that. There's no axiom list, no proof search, theyre stochastic, they contradict themselves across sessions, and they were produced by gradient descent on text. I get that the formal theorem still holds for Peano arithmetic or whatever. What I want to know is whether the argument—not the theorem—survives contact with these systems. If it does, which premise is doing the real work, and what would it even mean for a neural net to be blocked in that way? If it doesn't, is there a repaired version that keeps the original worry alive without the formal machinery?
