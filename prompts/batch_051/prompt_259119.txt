In my decision theory course we have been working through the idea that deliberation crowds out prediction. The rough argument is this: while I am genuinely deciding whether to do A, I cannot coherently assign a probability to my doing A, because assigning a high probability would be tantamount to already having settled in favour of A, and assigning a low probability would be tantamount to already having settled against it; treating one's own action as an event to be predicted is the standpoint of an outside observer, not of someone still choosing. Now suppose a neural decoder, trained on brain activity preceding conscious choice, can display—before I have made up my mind—a probability, say 0.8, that I will choose A, and I can see this readout while still deliberating. Does the original argument survive this development? Identify which premise the neural readout puts pressure on, and say whether there is a repaired version of the argument that remains true, or whether the argument should be abandoned.
