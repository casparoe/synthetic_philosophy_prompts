You are a philosopher of law working through a contested concept. Begin by brainstorming several candidate criteria for fair notice and stress-testing them against the cases below before you commit. Do not simply fall back on the maxim that ignorance of the law is no excuse; I want the deeper condition that makes that maxim sound in some of these cases and wrongheaded in others. Then give me your best working definition of fair notice: the condition under which a person who never actually knew a legal rule may nonetheless fairly be held to it. The definition should get these cases right:
(1) someone who commits murder without ever reading the criminal code;
(2) a minor regulatory offence buried in a thousand-page statute book;
(3) a rule published only in an official gazette read by lawyers;
(4) a judicial interpretation that unexpectedly broadens a statute and is applied to conduct that occurred before the ruling;
(5) an unpublished administrative rule used as the basis for a penalty;
(6) a visitor prosecuted for a local regulatory offence that has no counterpart in their home country;
(7) a rule available only in a language the accused cannot read.
I am confident that (1) can fairly bind and (5) cannot; the other five are the hard middle, and I have no settled view on them. Propose the definition, apply it to all seven cases, and tell me which verdict you are least willing to defend; where a case exposes a gap in the definition, revise the definition rather than ignoring the case.
