I was in the break room at our Berlin office last week when two colleagues — Jonas, from Duisburg, and Marcus, from Michigan — got into an argument I've been turning over a lot since. It started when Marcus mentioned he crosses against the light at a totally empty intersection on his walk to work, and Jonas said that was exactly the difference between German and American folk morality: Jonas thinks rules are what make collective life possible, and crossing against the light when you privately judge it safe is a small act of freeloading on everyone else's compliance. The back-and-forth went roughly like this.

Jonas: A traffic signal only works because people obey it even when it looks pointless. If everyone treated "no cars coming" as a private override, the shared expectation that green means safe to cross would erode. Your private judgment isn't smarter than the rule; it just can't see the side effects.

Marcus: That's backwards. The light is a tool for safety, and once the safety condition is already satisfied, obeying it isn't public-spirited — it's a category mistake, treating a means as an end. And the claim that a few sensible violations will erode the whole system is an empirical prediction with no evidence. Decent people distinguish the empty street from noon traffic.

Jonas: It's not about any single crossing. Rule-following is a habit, and habits leak across contexts. A culture where people treat rules as binding by default produces lower corruption, less cutting in line, bureaucracies that actually work. A culture where everyone thinks they're the one person smart enough to override the rule produces breakdown. You can't run a society as a million little cost-benefit analyses.

Marcus: Fine, but binding-by-default also produces the neighbor who reports you for putting a glass bottle in the wrong recycling bin, and worse. A grown moral agent doesn't outsource judgment to a rulebook. That's exactly what American skepticism of authority is for: laws are instruments, not masters.

Jonas: "Individual conscience" is doing a lot of work there. Most of the time, when someone says "I used my judgment," they mean "it was more convenient." You can't tell — in the moment, or even from the inside — the difference between the person who overrides a rule on principle and the person who overrides it because their lazy. That's why we have rules in the first place.

Marcus: That's exactly why the distinction has to be practiced instead of avoided. A culture that trains people to suppress judgment in favor of the rule gets its orderly streets, sure, but it also gets people who later say they were only following orders. I'll take the occasional lazy jaywalker over that.

I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it, which probably says something about my own folk morality. I'd like you to act as a moral philosopher who works on the ethics of rule-following and adjudicate the whole thing. Take your time: start by laying out the different kinds of rules and the different values each side is implicitly leaning on (order, safety, trust, autonomy, conscience, coordination), then go round by round and say who is right about which specific claim, who overstates, and whether the claim actually lands against the other person. End by saying what both of them are missing — for instance, anything about whether the argument is partly empirical, or about which moves depend on which kind of rule. Write the whole answer in American English, for a reader who wasn't there.
