I'm a novelist. The book I'm working on is about an older mathematician whose late-career work is all done with a machine-learning system that proposes conjectures and, increasingly, supplies full proofs. I can't decide whether my protagonist actually knows the mathematics this thing produces, or wether he's just become a very good proofreader. A philosopher friend pointed me to Benacerraf's dilemma. As I understand it, the dilemma is roughly this: if mathematical statements are literal truths about abstract objects, then the usual causal story about knowledge can't explain how we ever come to know them; but if we fix the epistemology by giving up that semantics, we seem to lose ordinary mathematical truth. Now I keep thinking: Benacerraf set that problem up for a human knower, back when mathematics was something a person did in their own head. Suppose my novel assumes that a growing share of new mathematics arrives the way it sometimes does now — a model produces a result or a proof, and the human can check pieces of it but can't hold the whole thing in mind or trace the line from intuition to theorem. Does Benacerraf's dilemma survive that development? Does it get worse for my protagonist, because the machine is plainly a causal system with no contact with abstract objects either; or does it just dissolve, because the machine shows you can produce reliable mathematics without grasping anything at all; or does it relocate somewhere else, into questions about justification or understanding rather than knowledge? I'm less interested in whether the machine knows anything. I want to know what the development does to Benacerraf's original argument — which premise it bears on, and whether that premise can be rebuilt or replaced. And feel free to tell me if the honest answer is that my mathematician should be haunted by something entirely different.
