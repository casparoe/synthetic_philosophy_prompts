I've been working through the split-brain literature in a course on the unity of consciousness, and I keep wanting to use the classic commissurotomy experiments as a pressure test for the integrationist view I keep encountering. The episode I have in mind is the body of work done by Roger Sperry, Michael Gazzaniga, and their collaborators from roughly the 1960s onward on patients whose corpus callosum had been severed to control otherwise intractable epilepsy. The salient results, as I understand them, are these. A stimulus delivered to one visual half-field could guide a response by the opposite hemisphere while the other hemisphere remained unable to report what had been seen or why the body had acted. In most of these patients only the left hemisphere had speech, so when the right hemisphere directed the left hand to do something, the speaking hemisphere would supply a fluent but fabricated explanation of an action it had not in fact chosen. And yet the patients were not visibly "two people" in ordinary life: with both hemispheres receiving largely the same information during normal activity and with subcortical connections intact, they mostly functioned as a single person in everyday circumstances.

I want to analyze that episode through the integrationist account of the unity of consciousness: the claim that a single subject of experience exists just to the extent that experiential and cognitive contents are functionally integrated into one system for joint access, report, and action, and that the felt unity of normal consciousness is nothing over and above such integration. Be disciplined about the mapping. What exactly would the integrationist have predicted, and which of the experimental results fall out of the view naturally? Where does the view have to strain — for example, with the confabulated reports, with residual information flow through subcortical routes and external cuing, and with the fact that patients seemed unified in daily life? What does the view treat as decisive evidence of one subject rather than two, and where is it simply silent? Then run the inference in the other direction: does this episode confirm the integrationist account, does it become an anomaly the view can tolerate only by adding qualifications, or does it show that "integration" is too coarse a notion to track the boundaries of a subject of experience?

I would rather you flag uncertainty than flatten the case. Please say how confident you are in the main interpretive claims you make, and identify the single point you are least sure about.
