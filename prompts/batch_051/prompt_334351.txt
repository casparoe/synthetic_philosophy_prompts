Last month two friends of mine, both of whom have lived in both Canada and the United States, got into a long argument about which country is more liberal, and I still haven't worked out who was right about which points. Neither was being evasive about the word; they genuinely disagreed about what could settle the question. I'll reconstruct the exchange as faithfully as I can:

(1) One opened by saying Canada is plainly the more liberal country: it has universal health insurance while the U.S. does not; same-sex marriage became legal nationwide there about a decade earlier; assisted dying is legal nationally rather than only in some states; there is no capital punishment; and gun regulation is far stricter. "If 'liberal' means anything in ordinary North American usage, it means something like this."

(2) The other replied that those examples only establish that Canada is more liberal in the American left-right sense—roughly, more left. By the older and still common sense of the word, liberal means individual freedom from state coercion. On that criterion the United States looks more liberal: stronger constitutional protection for speech, while Canada criminalizes some hate speech and regulates broadcast content; fewer restrictions on markets and occupations; lower tax and regulatory burdens; and higher rankings on standard economic-freedom indices.

(3) The first pushed back: "Your 'freedom from state coercion' isn't a neutral standard. Choosing to count low corporate taxes and weak product regulation as freedom while ignoring freedom from medical bankruptcy or from dependence on an employer for health insurance is a libertarian weighting, not a definition." They added that even if one accepted the classical sense, the U.S. comes out ahead only because the index's components encode a prior policy preference.

(4) The second responded: "And your ordinary North American sense encodes the opposite preference. That's exactly the point: the bare question 'which country is more liberal' is underdetermined until we agree on the concept and on how to weight its dimensions. If we mean social protection and the use of the state to secure equal standing, Canada wins; if we mean non-interference in speech, commerce, and lifestyle, the U.S. wins. Neither of those is the comparative claim."

(5) The first answered that this is a counsel of despair: we routinely say some countries are more democratic or more egalitarian without solving every conceptual issue in advance. "We don't need a complete theory; we need convergence among competent observers. And in ordinary English, the claim that the U.S. is more liberal than Canada is made mostly by libertarians, which suggests whose definition is being smuggled in."

(6) The second came back: "Now you want to settle it by a poll of word use, which is a fact about speakers, not a fact about the countries. I'm happy to say 'more left' if that's all you mean. But I don't see why saying the U.S. is less liberal has to follow, when its institutions leave more room to speak and trade without answering to the state. My problem is with presenting a contested weighting as though it were a descriptive ranking."

I'm not asking for a bottom-line call, and you don't need to take sides overall. Please go through the exchange move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, say which specific points each of them is right about, and say what, if anything, both are missing about what would actually settle this sort of cross-country comparison. Where expert opinion is genuinely split—for example, over the role of value judgments in constructing indices, or over how much weight to give different dimensions of freedom—please flag that as contested rather than presenting one view as the consensus.
