I retired from a research career in the natural sciences and have only recently begun reading philosophy seriously, so I may be reinventing a wheel or merely pattern-matching; I hope you can tell me which. Throughout my career I drew a sharp line between a model that is predictively adequate and a model that captures the actual mechanism. A fit that saves the phenomena can be remarkably accurate—epicycles, Fourier series, regression terms—while licensing no claim that the world contains the entities the model names. Recently I have been reading about the debate over whether large language models genuinely understand language, the dispute often conducted under the heading of stochastic parrots, and I cannot shake the sense that it is the same argument with new nouns. The skeptic says that a system trained to minimize next-token prediction error will soak up any statistical regularity in its corpus, so fluent, appropriate output on held-out prompts is no more evidence of understanding than the predictive success of an epicycle model is evidence for real epicycles. The defender replies that repeated success on novel compositional tasks would be a miracle of memorization unless the model had latched onto something like the real structure of language—which sounds to me like the no-miracles argument for scientific realism. This has left me wondering whether the two disputes are structurally identical, loosely analogous, or only superficially similar.

What I would like from you is a verdict on the parallel, and help seeing where it holds and where it breaks. Specifically: (1) What is the exact mapping—are the LLM's learned representations the analogue of unobservable entities, or does that way of setting things up already beg the question? (2) What would need to be true on both sides for the standard arguments to transfer? For instance, does the no-miracles argument carry over to language models only under assumptions about the relation between training and test distributions that have no clear analogue in scientific inference? Does the underdetermination argument against realism transfer in the opposite direction, and if so, what would it predict about LLMs? (3) Is there something the parallel predicts that turns out false? One candidate has been nagging me: in science, the realist claims the theory's central terms refer to things existing independently of the theory, whereas a language model is an artifact whose entire causal history—weights, objective, data—is known in principle; so the question may not be whether to posit a hidden mechanism but whether a fully observed mechanism deserves the name "understanding." I cannot tell whether this disanalogy is fatal, or whether it is exactly the kind of point an instrumentalist would make and a realist would reject. Which is it?
