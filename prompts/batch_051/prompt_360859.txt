My mother died eight months ago, and I have noticed something I do not know how to classify. When I face a hard decision, or when someone at work says something she would have found absurd, a response with her priorities, cadence, and vocabulary surfaces in me before my own does. I have been reading Hofstadter's claim that a self need not be confined to one brain: when we internalize another person's patterns of thought deeply enough, a low-resolution copy of that person's self is literally running in our brain, so that after the other person dies, something of their 'I' persists, distributed across the people who knew them. I want to know whether that is more than a comforting manner of speaking.

Design the best test you can of the claim that another person's self is literally, though partially, instantiated in someone else's brain. (If the strongest test would have to be run on living subjects rather than on my memories of the dead, say how a living-case result could serve as a stand-in and what the extrapolation assumes.) Be concrete about what you would measure, compare, or manipulate, and specify which result would favour the claim and which would count against it. Then say what a positive result would still not establish — for instance, whether the original person's stream of consciousness continues, whether anything was transferred from their brain to mine rather than reconstructed from my memories, and whether a partial copy deserves to be called a continuation of the person rather than a model of them. The aim is not to refute the claim but to determine how much of it can be brought under empirical discipline and where the unavoidable residue lies.
