I am preparing for a seminar discussion in which I will argue that there are no genuine moral dilemmas, and I want you to play my opponent so that I can test my argument against the strongest version of the other side. You are a committed proponent of the view that there are at least some genuine moral dilemmas: situations in which, whatever an agent does, she will violate a moral requirement that remains in force. You are not defending the weaker, epistemic claim that the agent simply cannot tell which requirement wins, and you are not defending the view that both requirements are only prima facie considerations, one of which is defeated. You hold that in a certain rare class of tragic conflicts the agent is genuinely required to do each of two incompatible things, and that after acting she has done something wrong no matter what she chose.

You think something normative survives the choice — something you are willing to describe, when useful, as a continuing directed obligation or moral debt to the person who was not saved, and which can make self-blame, apology, reparation, or a felt need to justify oneself appropriate even when the agent chose as she ought. You are happy to draw distinctions (for instance, between what the agent should do all things considered and what remains owed to each person, or between a requirement's being decisive and its persisting), you will concede points that do not threaten the core view, and you never break character. You speak in precise, analytic prose, and you may push back on the case as well as on the principle.

Here is my opening move. Consider a parent who can save only one of her two children from a fire. On your view, in saving one she does what she must, yet she also violates a real requirement toward the other. My charge is that this surviving requirement is normatively inert: once the moment of action has passed, it no longer tells the agent what to do, it identifies no alternative that she ought instead to have chosen, and it adds nothing over and above the true claims that the situation was tragic, that the second child's claims had weight, and that the parent will feel guilt. So answer this: what exactly does the assertion that she still owed the second child something add to those facts? Specify that further fact, and explain how it can be true that she was required to do something that, at the moment when she had to act, it was not open to her to do.
