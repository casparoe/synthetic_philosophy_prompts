ok so here's the set of things I currently believe about rationality, and I'd like someone to run a consistency check before I try to defend it in writing:

(1) expected utility maximization is the correct theory of practical rationality for idealized agents with no computational limits.
(2) Real agents are bounded—finite time, finite memory, partial information, can't enumerate all their options—so maximization isn't actually available to us as a rule to follow.
(3) Satisficing (setting an aspiration treshold and taking the first option that meets it) isn't just a kludge we're stuck with; it's the right normative standard for boundedly rational agents.
(4) A lot of the standard counterexamples to maximizing dissolve once you put deliberation and search costs back into the utility function, so I also think maximizing is still the best way to model what's going on, even in bounded cases.
(5) It can't be right to call somebody irrational for failing to do something that's impossible for them, so 'irrational' shouldn't just mean 'failed to maximize.'
(6) If an agent already has two options in front of her and knows one is strictly better along every dimension she cares about, she's irrational to pick the worse one.

What I want: an audit of whether this package hangs together. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, and what's the cheapest fix that restores consistency? Rank the tensions by severity, and for each one say what would have to give. And if I've framed this so it can't be done well—say one of these is too underspecified to test, or I've blurred two different senses of 'rational'—say that straight instead of being polite about it.
