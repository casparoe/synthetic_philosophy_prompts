Recently, in a discussion group I attend, an argument about tipping etiquette came to an abrupt close when someone quoted the proverb, "When in Rome, do as the Romans do." The case we were disputing concerned a New York visitor to Japan who tips 20 percent at every meal, even though tipping is not customary there and can be read as rude or as bungled change. One side insisted he was imposing his own norms on people who hadn't asked for them; the other insisted that tipping is a moral convention his own community practices, and that traveling doesn't release him from it. The proverb was treated as the decisive move against the second view.

I have since noticed the same proverb doing similar work across a wider set of disputes about whether everyday conventions are moral obligations and whether visitors have special reason to follow local ones: a tourist who jaywalks because locals do, a visitor who refuses to queue at a bus stop in a queueing culture, a guest who brings a host a gift that local etiquette finds insulting. I want to get clear about what the proverb actually amounts to, because it seems to cover several different claims at once. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses "When in Rome" might express, from the trivial to the radical? For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false.

Most importantly, tell me which of those readings would actually get the arguer to the conclusion that the New Yorker should abandon his tipping practice rather than impose it, and whether the proverb is doing any independent work in that argument or merely restating a conclusion that needs to be earned on other grounds. It would also help if you said whether the force of the proverb, under the best reading, varies across tipping, queueing, and jaywalking as cases — for example, because they have different functions, such as reciprocity, coordination, and risk-management — and what that variation implies for the general question of whether everyday conventions bind visitors.
