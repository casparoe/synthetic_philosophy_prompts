I have been trying to work out what I actually think about the ethics of depicting war in games by recording my reactions to particular cases before consulting any theory. Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A deliberately farcical multiplayer shooter set in a caricature of the Napoleonic Wars. The soldiers are almost cartoonish, death is slapstick, and fallen players respawn within seconds. The game makes no claim to represent real battles, real soldiers, or actual suffering. Verdict: permissible, or at most subject to whatever general objection one has to violent play.

2. A slow, punishing single-player game set on a meticulously researched stretch of the Western Front in the First World War. The player mainly digs, carries messages, and watches companions sicken and die; there is no score, no victory condition, and little that a player would call fun. Verdict: not merely permissible but admirable.

3. A modern military shooter in which the player may call in an airstrike on a village to clear an objective. Civilian characters are rendered as dim, barely distinguishable shapes; killing them is neither rewarded nor penalised, and the after-action report does not count them. The game's tone treats them as part of the scenery. Verdict: wrong, despite the fact that no real person is harmed.

4. An abstract strategy game about industrialised warfare. Occupied regions are governed through a menu with a slider marked Civilian Requisitions, running from lenient to severe. The severe setting yields more resources and produces only a small numerical penalty to a civilian unrest value. Nothing is depicted graphically. Verdict: wrong, though I find the wrongness harder to articulate here than in case 3.

5. A historically serious wargame in which the player may choose to command a unit whose real-world counterpart is documented to have carried out mass killings of civilians or prisoners. The game does not depict those acts; it is tactically rigorous and offers only a sober note in the briefing. Verdict: I lean towards calling this impermissible, but I am unsure whether my unease is moral or merely aesthetic.

6. A cinematic single-player campaign that pairs a serious narrative about a soldier's guilt with combat sequences engineered to be thrilling: slow-motion breaches, dramatic music, and rewards for chained kills. The story insists that war is ruinous; the mechanics insist that it is exhilarating. Verdict: the case I keep reversing on. Sometimes it seems the most objectionable of all; sometimes it seems the only way a mass audience is made to sit through the serious part.

7. A game in which the player serves as a combat medic in a historical battle and carries no weapon. The central mechanic is triage under time pressure, and the game penalises the player for refusing treatment to enemy wounded. Verdict: permissible, perhaps the best of the set.

I ask: is there a principle that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts, or must some go? State the principle precisely, identify which case motivates each of its clauses, and then apply it to cases I have not listed—particularly these: a farcical multiplayer game set in a real and recent conflict rather than a remote historical one; a sober historical game about a battle currently being fought; and a game in which the player is a civilian trying to get their family out of a besieged city while the player's own side is the one shelling it. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether the principle should be repaired or my verdict revised.
