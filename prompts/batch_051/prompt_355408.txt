I'm a journalist who covers education fights, and I keep hitting a conceptual wall. Years ago someone gave me a tidy four-part test for when something counts as a *public school system*: its funding comes from broad-based taxation, it is answerable to democratic institutions, it serves all children without selection or ability to exclude, and it has some curricular responsibility for civic formation. I have been using that ever since. But in the school choice debates I now cover, I keep seeing arrangements that break the package: tax money goes to families as restricted-use education savings accounts, parents can spend the money at private schools, co-ops, microschools, online providers, or at home, and providers can be religious or follow pretty idiosyncratic pedagogies, yet the state still requires annual assessments and withholds funds from programs where kids consistently fail. So the money is public, and the accountability is real, but the system is not run by a school board, teachers don't have public employee status, and there is no single curriculum.

John Stuart Mill in *On Liberty*, Chapter V, lays out the principle I'm trying to use as a lens. He writes:

"The maxims are, first, that the individual is not accountable to society for his actions, in so far as these concern the interests of no person but himself. Advice, instruction, persuasion, and avoidance by other people if thought necessary by them for their own good, are the only measures by which society can justifiably express its dislike or disapprobation of his conduct. Secondly, that for such actions as are prejudicial to the interests of others, the individual is accountable, and may be subjected either to social or to legal punishment, if society is of opinion that the one or the other is requisite for its protection."

By that test, maybe the real question is not whether the system is "public" but whether the state is doing the right kind of thing when it compels education at all. So: is the system I described a public school system in every sense that matters? I want an answer that goes criterion by criterion and also says where the criteria themselves break down. If you think I'm using the Mill passage badly, or that the category "public school system" is doing more rhetorical work than analytical work here, I'd rather you say that plainly than try to force the case to fit.
