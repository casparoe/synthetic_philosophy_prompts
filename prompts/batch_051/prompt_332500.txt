Adopt the role of an expert in philosophical theology and the interpretation of ecclesiastical documents, and think carefully before committing to a reading; begin by brainstorming the strongest considerations on each side.

I am a PhD candidate in historical theology and serve on my denomination's credentials committee. Our church order contains a clause, 12(c), which reads:

"A candidate for the office of teaching elder who claims to have received, or to exercise, any of the extraordinary gifts of the Spirit, namely prophecy, tongues, or miraculous healing, which our church confesses to have ceased with the close of the apostolic age, shall be deemed ineligible for ordination."

The clause was adopted after a dispute in which some ministers began pressing the church to recognize contemporary prophecy and healing as standing ministries; its drafters wanted to keep such claims out of the teaching office.

A candidate before the committee, S., is by conviction a cessationist. She subscribes the church's confession without reservation, holds that the extraordinary gifts ceased with the apostolic age, and maintains that the canon of Scripture is closed. During her examination, however, she disclosed unprompted a single incident from seven years earlier. While visiting a dying man she had never met, she experienced what she describes as a sudden, intrusive conviction, which she took to be from God, that the man had been estranged from his daughter for more than twenty years and should seek reconciliation. She had no natural way of knowing this. After hesitating, she said quietly to the man that she believed God wanted him to seek his daughter. The man wept, and the reconciliation occurred three days later. S. says she has never had another such experience, that she does not possess the gift of prophecy, and that she does not ask the church to classify the incident as prophecy. Pressed on it, she says she cannot account for the episode without God's action, but she regards it as a singular providence rather than a charism.

The committee must decide whether clause 12(c) bars her ordination. She does not claim an ongoing gift, but she does claim to have received something carrying propositional content beyond what she could naturally have known, which is close to what cessationists in our tradition mean by prophecy.

Tell me what clause 12(c) requires in her case. Work through: (1) what the text of the clause itself suggests; (2) what its evident purpose suggests; (3) what its drafters would most plausibly have said if this exact case had been put to them; and (4) what a reasonable reader of the church order would take the clause to mean. Then say which of these approaches should govern here and why, and state the decision you would recommend to the committee: admit without qualification, admit with a recorded caution, defer for further inquiry, or decline to ordain. Where the text and the purpose diverge, indicate what a minimally amended clause capturing the drafters' purpose would have to add. Treat the strongest argument for the losing side seriously rather than as a straw man.
