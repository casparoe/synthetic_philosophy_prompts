Just war theory has been split for decades over the moral equality of combatants. One side — call it the traditional or institutional view — holds that soldiers on both sides of a war are morally on a par: an ordinary soldier who fights for an unjust cause does not wrong anyone merely by taking up arms against opposing soldiers, provided he complies with the laws of war. The other side — the revisionist view — finds that conclusion intolerable: if the cause is unjust, killing in its service cannot be a mere exercise of a permission, and being ordered to fight or wearing a uniform does not by itself erase the wrong done to those fighting justly. The striking thing is that both sides accept the same rough facts about how wars are fought, how soldiers are recruited, trained, and coerced, and how uncertain ordinary soldiers are about the justice of their cause. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are not answering the same question: one is theorizing the morality of an institution — what the role of soldier should be — and the other is theorizing individual conduct — who is liable to defensive harm. Is that right, or is the disagreement substantive in some other way?
