I am a novelist, and for years a particular working theory of narrative possibility has quietly governed how I plot. I hold that an event in a story is possible just in case a competent reader could, at that point in the narration, assign it a non-negligible subjective probability; that could and might differ only in threshold and in what the prose has made salient, not in kind; and that the lingering impression of a turn being open in some deeper, non-probabilistic sense is an effect of successful narration rather than evidence of a distinct modal fact. I want the case against this view, not a defense of it. Which readerly intuitions, which cases in plotting and characterization, and which arguments from the semantics of could and might and from formal epistemology does the position get wrong or strain to accommodate? Are there real narrative possibilities that cannot be represented in any probability space, or that are unmistakably open to a reader even when no subjective probability—even an imprecise or vanishing one—can sensibly be assigned? Where would an intelligent defender of my position concede that something has gone wrong, and where should they hold the line? I would personally be moved most by an example of the first sort: an event clearly possible within a story and yet not capturable by any probability judgment. Conclude by telling me honestly whether the view as I have stated it risks anything at all, or whether it has been surrounded by qualifications that spare it from every counterexample—and, if the latter, whether that insulation is a flaw in a view whose job is to guide craft rather than to state truths.
