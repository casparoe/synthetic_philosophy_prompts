I've been trying to get clear on what Russell's theory of descriptions commits him to about sentences involving fictional names, and I keep going in circles. In "On Denoting" he writes:

Take as an instance 'the father of Charles II was executed'. This asserts that there was an x who was the father of Charles II and was executed. Now the, when it is strictly used, involves uniqueness; we do, it is true, speak of 'the son of So-and-so' even when So-and-so has several sons, but it would be more correct to say 'a son of So-and-so'.

Here's the argument I want checked:

(1) Russell is analyzing "The father of Charles II was executed" as asserting that there was an x who was father of Charles II and was executed, with uniqueness built in.
(2) By the same pattern, "Sherlock Holmes lived at 221B Baker Street" should assert that there was an x who was Sherlock Holmes and lived at 221B Baker Street, with uniqueness built in.
(3) There is no such x: Sherlock Holmes is a fictional character and does not exist.
(4) So on Russell's analysis, "Sherlock Holmes lived at 221B Baker Street" is false.
(5) But it is true in the stories that Sherlock Holmes lived at 221B Baker Street.
(6) So either Russell's analysis mishandles fictional discourse, or "true in the stories" isn't really truth, or one of the earlier steps goes wrong.

Is the argument valid? If it isn't, which step fails and why? If it is valid, which premise should a Russellian reject, and what does rejecting it force them to say about "true in the stories"?
