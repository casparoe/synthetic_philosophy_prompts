Lately I've been reading about weakness of will (akrasia) in philosophy and hyperbolic discounting in behavioral economics, and the two keep looking like the same shape seen from different sides. In the philosophical case, an agent judges that A is better than B, resolves to do A, and then does B once B is immediately available or tempting. In the discounting case, an agent prefers a larger-later reward while both rewards are distant, but as the smaller-sooner reward approaches, their preference reverses and they take it. The resemblance looks deeper than a shared curve: both phenomena explain why people bind themselves in advance through precommitment devices and Ulysses contracts, and both locate the trouble in the gap between the planner and the chooser.

What I can't decide is whether this is a deep structural identity or a loose analogy that papers over the main difference. A hyperbolic discounter, at the moment of choice, seems to genuinely value the sooner reward more, so their choice is not against their current better judgment. The akratic, on the picture I was taught, acts against a better judgment that is still theirs at the moment of choice. So the two models appear to locate the failure in opposite places: the discounting model describes a preference reversal that is formally a rational response to new proximity, while the akrasia model describes a failure to execute a judgment that remains in force.
