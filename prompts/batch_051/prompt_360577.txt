I'm in a reading group working through Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein on rule-following — the Kripkenstein skeptic — and instead of a final paper we've decided too close the seminar with poems, which sounded like a joke at the time and is now my actual assignment. I'd like you to write a sestina. The poem should move through the skeptic's challenge: all I have is finitely many past uses of a word or rule, and those examples are compatible with indefinitely many different ways of going on — the test case being a function that agrees with addition on every sum I've ever actually computed but gives a different answer on the next one, the not-yet-computed case, so that no fact about my past seems to settle that I meant the one rather than the other. It should actually be a sestina: six six-line stanzas with the standard permutation (each stanza's end-words are, in the previous stanza's order, the 6th, 1st, 5th, 2nd, 4th, and 3rd), then a three-line envoi that uses all six end-words.

The real constraint is that the form has to carry the philosophy. Pick the six end-words so that the sestina's habit of re-using the same words in shifting grammatical roles enacts the skeptic's point — that 'plus', 'mean', 'same', 'next', 'rule', or however you choose them, can be made to point in different directions each time they return, just as the same past examples can be squared with any continuation. By the fourth or fifth stanza I want a reader to feel the words sliding out from under them. End with the skeptical-solution move: there's no private fact that makes my meaning determinate; what licenses me to go on is agreement with others in a shared practice, not something in my head. But keep it as a poem — someone who knows Kripke's reading should recognize the stations of the argument, and someone who doesn't should still get a person trying to be sure they've been following the same rule all along.
