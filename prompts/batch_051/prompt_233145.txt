I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and there's one recurring move I've never been able to weigh properly. Someone announces a deliberately unusual way of living — a strict elimination diet, a high-variance career plan, a polyamorous arrangement, a group house run on explicit rules — and a defender will say that the rest of us should leave the person alone, and perhaps even admire them, because the practice is an experiment in living. Sometimes Mill gets cited, sometimes not, but the phrase is made to carry most of the argument: it is supposed to turn a private lifestyle choice into something the community is obliged to tolerate and something bystanders should expect to learn from.

I want to know what that sentence could actually be claiming, reading by reading. Separate the distinct theses someone might be asserting when they say a practice is an experiment in living and infer that society should therefore tolerate it: a thesis about what the experimenter is doing epistemically, a thesis about whether there is a discoverable fact of the matter about the best way to live, a thesis about the positive or negative externalities for nonparticipants, a thesis about the grounds of toleration, and a thesis about whether the value of the practice is intrinsic to self-expression or merely instrumental. For each reading, give a verdict (trivial, substantive and defensible, plausible but contingent, or false), and say whether that reading by itself licenses the step from experimentation to toleration. My own suspicion is that the label does almost none of the work, and that the step to toleration is really being carried by a harm principle or an autonomy claim that would apply to badly designed experiments too, but I want the menu of options rather than one answer.

Reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, with no introduction or conclusion. Each item should be at most two sentences: the first sentence should state one precisification, your verdict on it, and whether it alone licenses toleration; the second sentence should name the specific argument or consideration that would most likely overturn that verdict, or, if the verdict seems secure, the point where your reasoning about it is weakest.
