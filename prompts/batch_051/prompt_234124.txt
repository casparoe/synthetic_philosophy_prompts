I keep having the same argument with a friend who does field archaeology, and I'd like a definition I can actually use. You're an archaeologist with a head for conceptual analysis who also reads LessWrong, so you'll know what I mean when I say that 'ritual' in site reports is usually a stop sign: the assemblage is weird, nobody can think of a practical explanation, so it gets called 'ritual' (or 'ceremonial', or 'votive', which I'm fairly sure is the same shrug with a fancier hat).

I want a working definition of 'ritual' — or 'ritual deposit', or whatever you think the useful unit is — that could in principle be scored from the material remains alone. No appealing to what the people believed unless the objects themselves already get you there. It has to handle these cases:

1. A midden: meters of ash, broken bone, potsherds, and charcoal all mixed together. This should come out not-ritual, or the definition is useless.
2. A flexed burial with a few shell beads. This should come out ritual.
3. A small pit beneath a house threshold with a single deliberately smashed pot, its sherds laid flat across the bottom.
4. A deep cave chamber with forty bear skulls arranged in a rough circle, each facing inward; no debris, no hearth, no sign anyone lived there.
5. A bog deposit of twenty bronze axe heads, unweared, neatly stacked but not wrapped, no human remains nearby.
6. A rubbish pit full of bird bones from a single species, all the wing bones missing, mixed in with ordinary domestic trash.
7. A ring of postholes with no floor, no hearth, no roof-fall, and no artifacts, but the gap between two posts lines up with the solstice sunrise to within a degree.
8. A burned house with its grinding stones, pots, and stored grain still inside, and the doorway blocked with stones from the outside.
9. A cave floor where a child's footprints trace a tight spiral twice over in soft clay, with no adult prints nearby. I have no idea about this one; could be play.

Maybe the term is doing two jobs — one about formality or repetition, and one about aim or function — and the mess comes from switching between them. If you end up needing two definitions, fine; I want to know which cases move between them.

Before you settle on one, think through the two or three cases you expect to cause the most trouble. Then start your answer with the definition itself in the first sentence — no preamble, no restating the question, no 'here is my definition'. After that, run it through all nine cases and say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, which verdict is least secure, and whether any of them are undecidable in principle from material evidence alone.
