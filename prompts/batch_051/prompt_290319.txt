I have a hunch that what people call deference to authority is actually two different things that get lumped together. Somtimes it looks genuinely epistemic: I treat the person as a better detector of the right answer than I am -- the surgeon, the air-traffic controller, the master electrician -- and I'd update my own private judgment because they said so, even if nobody was watching. Other times it looks like purely social compliance: I go along because of the rank or the lab coat, my private estimate doesn't move, and I quietly stop trying to have an estimate of my own. Milgram's setup always looked to me like the second one, but people sometimes describe it as the first -- that subjects were endorsing the experimenter's epistemic authority, not just crumbling under the situation.

So here's the claim I'd like to test: when people defer to an authority figure's judgment, some of that deference is genuinely treating the authority as evidence. Design the strongest test you can of that claim. Give me an experiment (or an observation in the wild, if you'd rather) that would separate "I updated because they know better" from "I complied because they outrank me." What result would favor the epistemic reading, what result would favor the rank-compliance reading, and what would a null result tell us? Then be honest about the residue: even if the cleanest possible result comes out, what would that still leave unresolved about whether the epistemic kind is ever a virtue, as opposed to just a useful shortcut?
