I recently found myself writing, in a professional self-assessment, a sentence of roughly the form "the project succeeded, but I don't believe that it did," and I have not been able to stop thinking about exactly what was wrong with it. The episode led me to put a question on Moore's paradox on the midterm for the philosophy of language course I am now grading. Below are the question and one student's answer.

Question:

Moore's paradox is standardly illustrated by sentences of the form "p, but I don't believe that p." Such a sentence can be true, yet asserting it seems absurd. (a) State precisely where the puzzle lies, and explain why the sentence is not simply a contradiction. (b) Distinguish the omissive form ("p, but I don't believe that p") from the commissive form ("p, but I believe that not-p"), and discuss whether an account of one form should automatically extend to the other. (c) Set out a norm-of-assertion explanation of the oddity, being explicit about which norm (knowledge, belief/sincerity, or some other) is doing the work.

Student's answer:

Moore's paradox concerns the oddity of asserting a conjunction whose conjuncts are individually assertable and jointly consistent. "The train is late" and "I don't believe that the train is late" can both be true at once, so the sentence is not a contradiction; the problem lies not in what is said but in the act of saying it. This is confirmed by the fact that the third-person version, "The train is late, but she doesn't believe that it is," is unproblematic to assert, as is the past-tense version, "The train was late, but I didn't believe it at the time." The paradox therefore arises only for first-person present-tense self-ascriptions of the absence of belief.

There are two forms. The omissive form is "p, but I don't believe that p"; the commissive form is "p, but I believe that not-p." The commissive form is simply the omissive form with a stronger second conjunct, since believing that not-p is a way of not believing that p. An account of the omissive form therefore automatically covers the commissive form.

The best explanation is that assertion is governed by a norm. On the knowledge norm of assertion, a speaker should assert p only if the speaker believes p. When a speaker asserts "p" while also asserting "I don't believe that p," the second conjunct concedes that the speaker lacks the belief that the first assertion requires; the two assertions cannot both be proper, and that is why the conjunction is defective. The same reasoning applies to the commissive form, since asserting "I believe that not-p" likewise concedes that the speaker's attitude toward p is not the straightforward belief that assertion requires.
