In a philosophy of science seminar, a classmate argued that the underdetermination of theory by evidence leads to a sweeping skeptical conclusion about scientific belief, and I have been trying to reconstruct his reasoning in numbered form so that I can check each move individually. I would rather have a precise diagnosis of the inference than a general objection to the conclusion.

Here is the reconstruction. (1) Assume for the sake of argument that hard underdetermination holds: for any scientific theory T, one can construct a rival theory T* with exactly the same observational consequences as T by adding the hypothesis of an unobservable compensating mechanism that cancels, for all actual and possible observations, any difference the two theories would otherwise produce. (2) If T and T* have exactly the same observational consequences, then every possible observation is equally likely under the two theories, so no possible observation could discriminate between them. (3) If no possible observation could discriminate between two theories, then all possible evidence supports each theory to exactly the same degree. (4) If all possible evidence supports two theories to exactly the same degree, then neither can rationally be preferred to the other on evidential grounds. (5) Rational belief in a scientific theory requires an evidential ground for preferring that theory over its rivals. (6) Therefore, since every scientific theory has such an empirically equivalent rival, no scientific theory can ever be rationally believed.

Please grant step (1) as stated; my unease lies somewhere in the later steps. Is the argument valid? If it is not valid, tell me which is the first step at which it fails and explain exactly why that step fails rather than merely rejecting the conclusion. If it is valid, explain why the skeptical conclusion follows. Please answer in American English.
