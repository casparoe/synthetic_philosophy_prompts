i've been going in circles trying to figure out what a theory of reasons for attitudes—reasons to believe things, admire people, be angry, be afraid, hope—actually has to do, because every argument I read just grabs one weird case and acts like it settles the whole question. I want the full spec instead. I don't have any academic background so don't assume I know the vocabulary, but I can take technical stuff if you explain it.

The thing I care about: a theory of when there's a reason to believe or feel something, where the reason is supposed to tell us whether the person genuinely should change their mind or their feeling, not just whether doing so would be useful to them. Write me the list of requirements such a theory has to meet, with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

At minimum work through the cases I keep tripping on, and add any I'm missing:

- The bribe case: I offer you ten grand to believe the sky is green, and I can make you really believe it. That gives you a reason of some kind, but it clearly isn't a reason that makes the belief the one you should have. Any theory has to seperate "there's something in it for you to be in the state" from "the state is appropriate to how things are."

- The dictator case: someone with total power over you says he'll have you killed unless you admire him. Whatever that gives you, it doesn't make him admirable. But then is it a reason to admire him or not? If it is, then with high enough stakes there's a reason to admire literally anyone, which seems absurd; if it isn't, then why does "you'd better do it if you want to live" sound like a reason?

- The useful anger case: being angry at someone who actually mistreated you can be appropriate even when it costs you something, while being angry at someone just because the anger helps you finally leave a bad situation is useful but doesn't track anything about them. Same feeling, two different relations to the world. The theory has to keep "matches the situation" from collapsing into "helps you."

- The liked-parent case: your spouse wants you to like their parent, and you'd be happier if you did. Do you have a reason to like the parent, or only a reason to want to like them? This looks like a reason that never touches whether the person is likeable, and a theory has to say whether and how reasons can move between levels like that.

- The demon-who-wants-the-real-thing case: a demon threatens you unless you admire him for his actual qualities—you have to find something genuinely good about him and admire that, and he'll know if you're faking it. Now the threat is pointing you at real features. Is that the right kind of reason, or still the wrong kind wearing a disguise? I honestly don't know what a theory should say, but it seems like it has to say something.

- The price of cheap reasons case: if a five-dollar prize for believing something barely under-supported counts as a reason, then reasons are so cheap they stop meaning anything; if no incentive ever counts, the death threat doesn't either. The theory owes an answer about where the line goes and why.

For each requirement, tell me if it's non-negotiable—something every account has to get right or it's stopped being a theory of reasons at all—or whether it's more a preference of one camp. And if some of these requirements can't all be satisfied at once, say so: that's the part I most want to see, because each one sounds right to me and I suspect you can't have all of them. Also tell me which single requirement does the most work, meaning it rules out the most views people actually defend.

From what I've gathered this is the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, but I don't know the lay of the land well enough to know which bits are settled. One thing though: I know people who've thought about this for decades still disagree, so keep it honest—mark clearly what's settled and what's still genuinely contested, and don't present a camp's position as if it were the consensus.
