I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I keep getting the same analogy waved at me while I research my next book. The book has a character who's had their corpus callosum cut, and the line everyone gives me is that a split-brain patient is basically two people sharing one skull — that the left hemisphere, the one that usually does the talking, is like a press secretary improvising explanations for things the silent right hemisphere actually did. From there it's a short hop to the conclusion I actually want: the unity of consciousness is a story the narrating hemisphere tells itself, and there never was a single self in there to being with. That conclusion is doing a lot of thematic work for me, so before I build a whole book on it I'd like someone to kick the tires on the analogy.

Here's what I need. First, give me the mapping explicitly — a table of correspondences is fine: what plays the role of the two people, the shared skull, the press secretary, the silent partner, the story. Then take it apart. Where does the mapping break? I especially want the disanalogies that matter for my intended use separated from the ones that don't. What does the analogy quietly import from our ordinary idea of a "person" — memory, a point of view, preferences, the ability to give an account of itself — that a hemisphere might not actually have? And is the supplied analogy actually doing the argumentative work here, or is the conclusion about there never having been a unified self really coming in from somewhere else? Once the disanalogies are on the table, tell me whether the conclusion survives, survives in weakened form, or was being carried by the analogy the whole time. If there's a better analogy for what's going on — one that captures the real weirdness without smuggling in two full persons — I want it.

End with a line beginning "Verdict:" giving me the one-sentence bottom line about how much weight the analogy can actually bear for my character.
