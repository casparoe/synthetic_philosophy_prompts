I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and in a seminar on consequentialism and population ethics we briefly discussed negative utilitarianism and the wider family of suffering-focused views. I left confused about a point I haven't been able to resolve on my own. The advertised difference from classical utilitarianism is supposed to be that the reduction of suffering has priority over the promotion of happiness — not merely that suffering receives a very large weight in a common currency. But I cannot see how "priority" can amount to anything other than a steep exchange rate.

If a unit of suffering can be outweighed by some sufficiently large quantity of happiness, then the position is just classical utilitarianism with an unusual coefficient; if it cannot be outweighed by any quantity of happiness, then suffering and happiness appear incommensurable, and incommensurability would seem to leave most actual policies undecided, since nearly every policy does some of both. I keep seeing the phrase "lexical priority" used as though it names the missing third option. How does a lexical ordering of values differ from a finite exchange rate on the one hand and from incommensurability on the other, and does introducing it actually give the suffering-focused ethicist a stable position, or does it just rename the difficulty?
