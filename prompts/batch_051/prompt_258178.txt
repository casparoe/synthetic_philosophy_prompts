I've been chewing on a dispute that's been running for almost a century and somehow never gets settled. On one side there's the anti-Whig position: ever since Butterfield, historians have been trained to treat writing the past as a slow climb toward us—toward modern liberty, secularism, science, tolerance, whatever—as a methodological sin; it anachronizes, it makes past people look like failed versions of ourselves, and it reads later outcomes back into earlier events as if that were their meaning. On the other side are the people who push back on that, and they're not fools: they say all history is written from some present, most people care about the past precisely because it led to now, and the claim to reconstruct the past as its own inhabitants understood it is itself a present-day pose. What puzzles me is that these two groups don't seem to be answering the same question—one keeps saying you've got the method wrong, the other keeps saying you've got the purpose wrong—so the argument just cycles. My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement is about the direction of explanation: retrospective (explaining later states by earlier ones) versus prospective (making earlier states intelligible in their own moment), and not really about facts or values. Is that the right diagnosis? If so, what would actually count as progress—a distinction that lets the two projects coexist, a division of labour, something else? And if it's not mainly about the direction of explanation, what kind of disagreement is it instead?
