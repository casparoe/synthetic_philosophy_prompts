Act as an expert on Dennett’s philosophy of mind and on social epistemology. In a recent discussion, someone appealed to Dennett’s Atlantic article comparing AI systems that pass as human to counterfeit money. The claim, as they presented it, is not merely that both deceive people, but that the harm is systemic: counterfeit banknotes do not simply cheat the merchant who accepts one, they erode confidence in the currency itself, so even legitimate money comes under suspicion. Dennett argues in parallel that counterfeit people—digital agents that pass as human—debase the general currency of social trust, so that genuine human speech and interaction also suffer. I want to know how far this analogy can actually carry Dennett’s conclusion that creating such systems should be treated as a serious attack on the infrastructure of civilization, not just as a new kind of fraud.

Your task: first brainstorm the intended mapping, feature by feature—what corresponds to the mint, the currency, the counterfeit note, the merchant, the general public, and the institution of money. Then identify where the analogy breaks, and say which disanalogies matter for Dennett’s argument and which are merely local. In particular, consider whether speech and personhood are a single currency or several different ones, whether trust in persons works like trust in paper, whether the relevant harm is deflation of trust or something else such as informational pollution or manipulated attention, and whether the analogy quietly imports monetary properties that human interaction lacks. End by judging whether Dennett’s regulatory conclusion survives the disanalogies, survives only in weakened form, or depended on the analogy all along. Where the philosophical territory is genuinely contested, say so rather than treating a controversial reading as the settled view, and separate what most Dennett scholars would accept from what remains open.
