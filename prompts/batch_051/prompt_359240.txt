I help run the moderation side of a mid-sized online community, and the issue I keep getting pulled into is what to do when one member posts something that treats another member's sacred object, name, text, or practice with open contempt. The options we have are crude: either we remove the post, which turns the poster into a free-speech martyr and invites copycats, or we leave it up, which reads to the targets as endorsement of a desecration and starts a weeks-long outrage cycle. I have been thinking about blasphemy and sacrilege on my own for some time, and the conclusion I keep reaching is that the worst damage usually comes not from the statement itself but from how it moves through the system—how it is amplified, how the people it targets are made to see it, how each side uses the incident as a banner.

So here is a proposal we are considering. Any self-identified religious or ethical community may submit a public register of the sacred: a list of the objects, names, texts, images, seasons, and practices it regards as due special reverence, each entry accompanied by an explanation of the boundaries—whether a particular name may be written, whether an image may be shown, what counts as mocking or defiling rather than merely disagreeing. Nothing on the register becomes unmentionable. When a post is reported as violating a registry entry, the report goes to a rotating panel of six members, three drawn from the reporting tradition and three from outside it. If the panel finds that the post treats a registered item in a way the entry's own authors would recognize as contempt or defilement, whatever the poster intended, the post is left up but: it carries a notice describing the entry and the reason it was flagged; it is excluded from algorithmic promotion and from default search; and the poster receives a desecration mark, which reduces the reach of their next posts unless they withdraw the post or post a good-faith response to the registry's explanation. Registrations can be challenged by other groups that say the same item is sacred to them and that the entry misdescribes its boundaries; while a challenge is pending, the stricter submitted boundaries apply.

I do not want a verdict on whether this is a good idea. I want to know how it would actually behave. What does it incentivize among ordinary members, among communities deciding what to register, and among panelists? How would it be gamed, and by whom first? What happens with items that are sacred to one group and a target of satire or veneration for another, or where members of the same tradition disagree about what counts as contempt? What second-order effects should we expect—on what gets posted, on who joins, on how communities relate to one another, on what the register itself becomes over time? And which failure modes, if any, can be fixed without gutting what the scheme is intended to do?
