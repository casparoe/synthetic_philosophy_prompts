Act as a philosopher of history who works on explanation and the psychology of retrospective judgment. I keep noticing a very specific move in historical writing and I don't have a name for it, though I know the general area (hindsight bias, the narrative fallacy, creeping determinism). The move goes like this. A big event—a war, a market crash, a revolution, the fall of a state—gets explained after the fact by piling up conditions: the coalition was fragile, the currency was overvalued, the elites where divided, there had been a drought, and so on. Each condition on its own is a real cause, and the explanation feels strong precisely because there are so many of them. But many earlier moments had nearly the same profile of conditions and nothing happened, and beforehand almost nobody treated those conditions as predictive. The same thing shows up in election postmortems: a couple of years before the vote, the exact factors that later explain a landslide were being described as electorally trivial or even as strengths. So the sheer number of causes is doing hidden work: it manufactures a feeling that the event was overdetermined, when really the causes are mostly being selected and narrated backwards. I'm not looking for hindsight bias or creeping determinism in general—I want the label, if there is one, for this specific mechanism, where a long list of individually plausible retrospective causes inflates the apparent inevitability of an outcome. If there's no established term, coin one, define it, and give one or two boundary cases that distinguish it from plain hindsight bias and from the narrative fallacy. Please start by brainstorming the nearest concepts and saying exactly how each fails to capture the thing; then give your honest verdict on whether this is a genuinely distinct phenomenon or just one of the standard ones in disguise.
