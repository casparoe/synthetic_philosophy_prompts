I've been reading up on punishment theory lately and I keep tripping over the same decades-old stalemate. Retributivists say an offender deserves to suffer in proportion to what he did, and that desert alone can justify punishing him, whatever the future brings. Consequentialists say punishment is just a forward-looking instrument: it's justified only when and because it deters, incapacitates, or reforms. What frustrates me is how stable the standoff remains after the standard concessions. The consequentialist grants that we must not punish the innocent and must not punish the guilty beyond what they deserve. The retributivist grants that whether to maintain a criminal justice system at all depends partly on whether the system does any good. Once both sides concede that, the dispute shrinks to one stubborn question: can desert ever give a positive reason to punish, or does it only set limits on punishment that forward-looking considerations already justify? On that question neither camp moves. Negative retributivists say desert only constrains. Positive retributivists say desert can require punishment even when no good will come of it, and they think the constraint-only view is unstable—if desert matters at all, why wouldn't it matter as a reason to act? Consequentialists answer that positive retributivism is just vengeance dressed up as a principle, and retributivists answer that consequentialists are smuggling in retributive constraints they can't derive from their own theory.

The argument always parks itself on two thought experiments. The consequentialist asks why we should punish a murderer on a desert island where nobody will ever find out, there's no deterrent signal, and no one to be incapacitated: what good would it do? The retributivist says his deserving it is itself the point, then asks about the sheriff who could prevent a riot that will kill twenty people only by framing and executing one innocent person. Everyone, including the consequentialist, says the sheriff must not do it, and the retributivist wants to know what consequentialist principle rules it out without secretly borrowing from desert. At that point each side treats the other side's favored case as an eccentric edge case, and there's no agreed method for deciding whose intuitions are the data and whose are the anomolies.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? It doesn't look empirical—nobody thinks the messy deterrence data, which seem to cut both ways, will settle it—and it doesn't look merely verbal, because the negative and positive retributivists genuinely disagree about the desert-island case. Is the impasse really about which cases get to count as data? Is it a hidden disagreement about whether backward-looking considerations can be reasons at all, where the two sides just have bedrock intuitions that neither can argue for? Are they answering different questions under one name—one about the general justifying aim of the institution and one about the permissibility of this particular act? Or is the whole thing downstream of whether "desert" names a primitive or a folk heuristic? I'd rather have a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement than a verdict for one side. And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a change of question, an empirical finding—or should we just accept that this one won't move and stop expecting it to?
