I teach a unit on what makes dispute resolution count as adjudication, and I give my students four criteria: an impartial third party with no stake in the outcome; each side gets a real chance to be heard; the decision is made by applying general rules rather than by haggling or raw power; and the decision is supported by reasons that the parties and the public can inspect. For years I used the obvious examples, but I want a hard case. Take a mandatory binding arbitration clause in a consumer contract, the kind you click through with your cell phone plan. The arbitrator is a retired judge paid by the company, the hearing is confidential, the award is final with no appeal, there's no written opinion unless the company asks for one, and class proceedings are waived. Go through my four criteria and tell me: does it qualify as adjudication? Where it passes and where it fails, say whether that reflects the criteria or the practice. And here's my suspicion: maybe the real issue isn't my criteria at all, but consent and the fact that the state will send the sherrif to enforce the award. If I had to add or remove one criterion to make the verdict come out right, which should it be, and would that be principled or just gerrymandering?
