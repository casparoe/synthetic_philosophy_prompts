I'm putting together a session for a philosophy of mind course that uses film, and I want to pair the debate over the cognitive penetration of perception with Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men. The philosophical issue is whether what we believe, expect, want, or fear can alter perceptual experience itself — not merely where we direct attention, not what we infer from what we see, and not how we remember or report it later. The film seems an obvious fit because so much of the jury's work consists in dismantling the testimony of two eyewitnesses: the elderly man who says he heard the boy threaten his father and saw him fleeing, and the woman who says she saw the killing through the windows of a passing elevated train. The dissenting juror's counterarguments often sound as if they are showing how expectation and need colored what the witnesses took themselves to see, but I am not sure the film actually commits itself to cognitive penetration rather than to less controversial top-down effects on attention, interpretation, or memory. That distinction is exactly what the philosophical debate turns on, and I want the session to keep it sharp.

What I would like is a close reading of the film through that lens: which scenes, testimonies, and arguments would a defender of cognitive penetration most plausibly cite, and which are better described as showing attention, perceptual learning, memory reconstruction, confabulation, or judgment? The woman across the street seems to invite a reading in terms of vision plus expectation, while the elderly man seems to invite one in terms of memory plus a need to matter; the jurors themselves, meanwhile, keep perceiving the same evidence through their own biographies and prejudices. I want the reading to be honest in both directions. It should not reduce the film to an illustration of a position, and it should not stretch the idea of cognitive penetration to cover things no one in the literature would count. Where does the film genuinely dramatize a claim about perception that the philosophical debate would recognize? Where does it resist, complicate, or even preempt the reading? And is there anything in the way Lumet stages seeing — the close-ups, the cuts, the heat, the timing of the train — that the argument-based literature tends to miss?
