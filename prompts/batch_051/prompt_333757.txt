I work as a data scientist, and my home vocabulary for reasoning under uncertainty is Bayesian statistics and probabilistic machine learning: priors, posteriors, and likelihoods; model classes and model evidence; hierarchical models and Bayesian model averaging; calibration, sharpness, and the epistemic/aleatoric uncertainty distinction. I am fluent in that vocabulary, so please take it as given and do not explain its basics.

Last night I had a long argument with a friend who has read a good deal of Keynes. As I understood him, his position had three claims. First, probability is not a subjective degree of belief but a logical relation between a body of evidence and a proposition: the evidence itself confers a given probability on the proposition. Second, these evidential relations are often only partially ordered. For many pairs of propositions, the evidence neither makes one more probable than the other nor makes them equally probable, so that relative to a body of evidence some propositions simply have no numerical probability at all. Third, even when a numerical probability exists, the weight of evidence behind it is a distinct and irreducible feature of an evidential situation. A probability of 0.7 resting on one thin, homogeneous sample is not the same state of knowledge, and should not license the same decisions, as a probability of 0.7 resting on a long, varied record. He repeatedly insisted that responding by placing a second-order distribution over the first-order probability is question-begging, since it assumes at a higher level the very thing Keynes denied: that all uncertainty can be represented by exact probabilities.

I would like you to do two things. First, restate each of the three claims as precisely as you can in my vocabulary. Where my vocabulary has a natural counterpart, give it; for instance, I suspect the third claim is close to the difference between a sharp posterior obtained under a mis-specified model and a posterior that is properly spread across model space, or perhaps to the calibration-versus-sharpness distinction or to epistemic uncertainty, but I am not committed to any of those mappings. Where a claim can be translated only with distortion, say exactly what gets distorted. Where a claim has no expression in my vocabulary at all, say so explicitly. Second, give me the residue report: claim by claim, what translates cleanly, what translates only by fudging, and what has no counterpart. Then offer your honest assessment: is the residue a sign that my friend's Keynesian claims are less precise than they looked and mostly dissolve under translation, a sign that my Bayesian vocabulary is missing something real, or a mix — and if a mix, which claims fall on which side?
