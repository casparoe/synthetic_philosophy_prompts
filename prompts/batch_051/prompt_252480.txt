I cover machine learning and cognitive science for a general-interest magazine, and I'm trying to write about the claim that word embeddings have turned meaning into geometry — the idea that a word's sense just is its position in a high-dimensional vector space, and that semantic relations are directions in that space. To keep myself from parroting the hype, I went back to Tomas Mikolov and his coauthors' 2013 paper "Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space." There's a sentence early in it I keep re-reading. After saying they expect similar words to land near each other, they describe the representations as built on the expectation that "not only will similar words tend to be close to each other, but that words can have multiple degrees of similarity." That feels like a bigger concession than the paper treats it as: if similarity isn't one thing, then a single distance between two vectors can't be doing the semantic work the "geometry of meaning" story wants it to do.

So before I write the piece, I want the full spec: what would a theory of meaning built on embedding geometry have to get right to be more than a demo? I mean the requirements list — the cases it has to handle (polysemy, antonyms that end up as neighbors, relatedness without synonymy like coffee and cup, phrases, negation, sense change over time), the distinctions it can't collapse (similarity vs. association vs. entailment, and whatever else you'd put on that list), and the things linguistics and psycholinguistics already know that it can't contradict — with the reason each item earns it's place. I don't want a bibliography, I want the criteria. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's tastes, and tell me which single requirement does the most to seperate the serious from the hand-wavy. And if some of these can't be jointly satisfied in a flat vector space, that tension is the part I most need to explain to my readers — especially given that "multiple degrees of similarity" line.
