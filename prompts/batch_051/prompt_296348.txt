I am preparing to teach a short course on interpretations of probability, and before I assign the usual survey pieces—frequentism, subjective Bayesianism, propensity and similar—I want to stop and write the specification first. I keep noticing that each camp begins by taking one kind of case as paradigmatic: the long-run frequency of a repeated trial, the degree of belief in a one-off proposition, the objective chance of a decay event. I would like the full list of requirements before I start, so that I can test each interpretation against something other than its own favourite example.

Write the desiderata that any adequate interpretation of probability should have to meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Include at least the following: repeatable events and long-run frequencies; single-case probabilities where there is no obvious reference class; the distinction between probability zero and impossibility; the difference between chance and rational degree of belief, together with a plausible account of how the two should align; and the formal constraints—additivity, for example—that the concept is usually expected to respect. Flag any subsets of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does most to exclude the standard interpretations. Then, in no more than a sentence or two on each, say how frequentism, subjective Bayesianism, and a propensity account fare against the list. Please write the answer in British English.
