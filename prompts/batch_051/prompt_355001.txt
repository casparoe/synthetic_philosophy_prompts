I'm a philosophy lecturer, and in the common room the other day a colleague from religious studies was pooh-poohing the old question 'what is a religion?' He said the category is a family-resemblance one, just like game: there is no single feature shared by chess, patience (the card game) and football, but the word works perfectly well. Same, he says, with Buddhism, Confucianism, and even the more obviously secular things people call religions — nationalism, the local football crowd, the cult of the market. Overlapping similarities, no essence, and anyone demanding necessary and sufficient conditions is stuck in a hopeless bit of Platonic essentialism.

I want to see how far this analogy can actually carry that conclusion. Treat it as an argument: the category game hangs together by family resemblance, therefore the category religion can too, and can stretch to godless and secular cases without our ever having to define it. Give me the correspondence table first — what in the religion case is meant to play the role of the game itself, the rules, the players, the scoring or winning, the boundary of the pitch? Then work through the disanalogies: which features of games does the analogy quietly import into religion (or of religion into games), and which of those matter for the intended conclusion and which are merely incidental? In particular I want to know whether calling something a religion is relevently like calling something a game once you think about how the category is used — opt-in, self-contained, bounded, and so on. Does his conclusion survive the audit, or does the analogy turn out to be doing all the work? If it partly survives, say what a better analogy might be; if it dies, say where exactly it gives out.
