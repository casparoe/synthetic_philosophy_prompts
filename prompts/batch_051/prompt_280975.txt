I've been developing an account of modesty that I call non-circulating self-assessment. The familiar accounts say that a modest person either underestimates her merits or is to some degree ignorant of them; both make modesty depend on false belief, self-deception, or a failure of self-knowledge, which seems wrong for the person who knows exactly how good she is and remains modest anyway. My proposal is that modesty concerns not the content of one's self-evaluation but its circulation. On this view, the modest person may privately hold an accurate, even high, opinion of herself; what makes her modest is that she declines to export that opinion into shared space — she does not make it a premise others are expected to adopt, does not trade on it in conversation, and treats it as a private holding rather than a claim on others. Intellectual humility, for example, would not be low confidence but the refusal to let private confidence do the argumentative work that evidence should do; social modesty would be the same restraint about one's achievements; moral modesty, the same restraint about one's own rectitude. Consider a scientist who says 'I may well be wrong' while privately thinking the evidence is decisive. The understatement account reads her as reporting a lower credence than she has, or as lying politely. Mine reads her as choosing not to place her self-assessment into the pool of considerations others are meant to rely on. I want an honest assessment rather than a defence. Is 'circulation' doing real work here or is it just a metaphor? Does the account get the obvious cases right, and where does it overgenerate or undergenerate? Is there already a position like this in the literature under another name — for instance about assertion, conversational norms, or the ethics of self-presentation? Give me the three strongest objections, say which, if any, are fatal, and then indicate whether what survives is a virtue or merely a useful social convention. Please answer in British English.
