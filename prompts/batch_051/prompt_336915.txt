Consider the absent-minded driver problem. A driver is on a road with two identical exits. He is absent-minded: when he reaches an exit, he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second. His payoff is 0 if he exits at the first exit, 4 if he exits at the second exit, and 1 if he continues through both exits. Before setting out he can choose a plan: at each exit, exit with probability p and continue with probability 1−p.

Here is an argument that seems to show rational planning and rational execution come apart in a way that cannot be right.

(1) The ex ante optimal plan is p = 1/3. Expected payoff under a plan p is (1−p)(1+3p), which is maximized at p = 1/3 and yields 4/3.

(2) Now suppose the driver actually finds himself at an intersection while following this optimal plan. Conditional on being at some intersection under p = 1/3, the probabilities are 3/5 that it is the first intersection and 2/5 that it is the second.

(3) The expected payoff of exiting at this intersection is therefore (3/5)(0) + (2/5)(4) = 8/5.

(4) Since 8/5 > 4/3, the current expected value of exiting exceeds the value of the plan he set out with. So at the point of action, exiting is better than sticking with the plan.

(5) The same reasoning applies at whichever intersection he reaches. So he should revise toward exiting at the current intersection, perhaps with probability greater than 1/3.

(6) But if he actually does that, he raises the chance of exiting at the first intersection and lowers his expected payoff: if he always exits, he gets 0. Thus a sequence of locally better choices makes him worse off than the original plan, and the argument seems to prove too much.
