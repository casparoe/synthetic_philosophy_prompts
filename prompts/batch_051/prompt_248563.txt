I've been trying to make luck egalitarianism's option-luck/brute-luck distinction do actual work and I keep hitting a wall with one case. Suppose we operationalize it like this: a disadvantage counts as option luck only if it came from a risk the person chose to run, could reasonably have declined without serious cost, could reasonably have foreseen, and their ability to assess the gamble wasn't itself compromised by bad brute luck. Everything else is brute luck. Now the case: someone grows up in real poverty (uncontroversially brute luck), and because of that he ends up reading a bunch about how early scarcity makes people more present-biased and more willing to gamble on small chances. As an adult he can choose between a stable local job and joining a startup with maybe a 70% chance of folding. He can state the odds, knows he's the kind of person who underestimates tails, and still picks the startup. It folds; he's left with debt and a CV gap. Does the resulting disadvantage qualify as option luck or brute luck under the criteria? Work through each one honestly; where the criteria don't force an answer, say so, and then say whether the option/brute distinction needs a further criterion like 'the preference behind the gamble must not itself have been formed under brute luck,' or whether adding that would just collapse the distinction into 'everything is brute luck.' Whatever else you say, end with \answer{...} where ... is one of 'option luck', 'brute luck', 'undetermined', or 'criteria need changing'.
