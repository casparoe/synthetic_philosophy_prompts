My spouse and I are deciding whether to have a third child, and I recently moved to a city that has been growing quickly; the conjunction has made me notice something unstable in my own moral reactions. I treat a larger family as a genuine good even when no member ends up better off than before, but I cannot bring myself to treat a larger city as any better when its average quality of life is unchanged. I want to write an essay about this, and I would be grateful for comments on the following working notes rather than a finished draft.

The claim I am testing is that ordinary moral thought is neither straightforwardly totalist nor straightforwardly averaging, but locally comparative. It evaluates a possible added life against the average achieved by the people who already populate the relevant unit — the family, the neighborhood, the city, perhaps the species — rather than against a fixed neutral level at which a life is neither a gain nor a loss, and it takes the relevant unit to be the one closest to the decider. This would explain why the same person can sound like a totalist about a third child and like an averagist about a doubling of the city, and it would also explain my uncertainty about whether a larger species matters at all.

The normative rationale I am sketching is that a decider can more reliably compare a possible new life to the lives already at stake than to an abstract neutral level, so the local comparison is not mere parochialism but a kind of epistemic modesty. My main worry is path dependence. If each addition is judged against the incumbent average, then whether a given added life counts as good depends on the order in which earlier additions occurred. Two growth sequences can reach the same final population with the same average, yet one can make every increment count as an improvement while the other has a step that fails, solely because the local average differed at the time of that step. I suspect ordinary thought does not track this order-dependence, which suggests it is not really applying the locally comparative principle I described.

I would welcome comments on three points. First, is the asymmetry between family-size and city-size judgments described fairly, or would people in fact judge the two cases alike if the cases were matched properly? Second, is the path-dependence objection fatal to the normative version, or can the view accept that the value of an addition is order-relative in something like the way some decisions are? Third, is there a more economical explanation I am missing, for example that people value their own projects and their members rather than the value of the world at all?
