I sit on the policy committee of our state's environmental quality board, and the staff has drafted a rule for a new water-quality trading program. Under the relevant state and federal scheme, only discharges from a point source—roughly, a pipe or a ditch—are regulated under permits and can be traded as credits, while fertilizer and manure running off farm fields counts as nonpoint-source pollution that mostly sits outside the permit system. The draft rule says that, for purposes of the trading program only, a measured reduction in agricultural runoff shall be deemed to be a reduction in a point-source discharge, so a farmer could generate credits and sell them to a wastewater plant instead of the plant doing its own costly upgrade. Half the committee says this is exactly the kind of thing lawyers do all the time—they keep citing corporate personhood and constructive notice—and that without the deeming clause the whole trading program is dead on arrival. The other half says the state would be declaring an outright falsehood true: runoff is not a pipe, its load reductions are diffuse and rain-dependent and only partially measured, and calling it a point source is not a harmless shorthand but a lie that will infect every number the program produces. We need a decision memo for the full board recommending approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with reasons the losing side can actually respond to.

Two things would make this actually usable. First, please seperate what is genuinely settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on legal fictions and the ontology of legal rules—for example, whether a deeming provision is best understood as a rule of inference, a stipulation, a status assignment, or a false assertion that the law chooses to give effect anyway, and whether fictions tend to stay innocent or harden over time into things officials mistake for facts. Don't present one school as the consensus if it isn't. Second, lead with your bottom-line recommendation in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. If you land on approve-with-conditions, make the conditions concrete enough that we could draft them that afternoon; and give real weight to the strongest argument on the side you reject.
