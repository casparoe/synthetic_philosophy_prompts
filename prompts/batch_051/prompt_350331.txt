I've been going down a rabbit hole reading about which animals are conscious or have "minds," and I keep noticing the same move in otherwise careful scientists and commentators. The pattern goes like this. Some capacity gets treated as the mark of a real mind — tool use, say, or mirror self-recognition, or planning for the future. Then a species nobody expected clears the bar: crows bend wires into hooks, a fish apparently passes the mirror test, scrub jays stash food in ways that look like they're thinking ahead. And instead of the conclusion "okay, so that species has the thing," the criterion quietly gets redefined: real tool use requires making tools rather than just using found objects; the mirror test doesn't count if the species' ecology might involve mirrors or if the behavior could be a reflex; future planning requires episodic memory, not just learned caching. Each redefinition sounds sensible and scientifically cautios on its own, and I'm not claiming any particular animal definitely has a mind. But across the literature the effect is that the boundary between "conscious" and "mere behavior" never actually moves, no matter what the animals do. It feels less like a principle and more like carving the definition so the line stays where it already was.

I got into this because a friend in biology keeps insisting fish can't feel pain because their responses are "just reflexes," and I started wondering how anyone could ever tell, and whether the criteria are doing the work. I'm trying to figure out whether this pattern has a name or a literature. I know the general "moving the goalposts" fallacy, but this seems more specific and not always dishonest — sometimes the new criterion really is better motivated. I also know there's an old principle about preferring simpler explanations for animal behavior, but I'm not sure whether that's the same thing or the opposite of what I'm describing. What I'm after is: does this exact dynamic have an established name in comparative cognition or philosophy of mind, what are the nearest concepts and how does it differ from them, and how do you tell legitimate scientific caution from a bias toward keeping humans (or mammals, or vertebrates) on the special side of the line? If it doesn't have a name, I'd rather hear a good definition and a proposed label than just "it's moving the goalposts."
