For the past two years I worked on the design team of a wellbeing app whose central feature was a daily happiness check-in. We removed the feature last quarter after our own data showed that the most frequent users reported the slowest improvement and left soonest, while weekly check-ins caused no measurable harm. I have spent the months since trying to work out what that finding means, and I have arrived at a view I would like assessed honestly.

On my view, happiness is a state that is degraded by being audited. I call this the audit tax. The core claim is that any deliberate act of turning one's attention to the question of how happy one is at this very moment withdraws attention from the activity one is living through, and the withdrawn attention is not returned; the audit consumes a small portion of exactly the state it is trying to measure. Happiness, then, is not destroyed by wanting it but by verifying it. The more frequently one checks, the less there is to find, because each check thins the experience out.

I think this sharpens the familiar paradox of hedonism. The usual explanation is that aiming directly at one's own happiness tends to miss it, because pleasure is a by-product of aiming at something else. My claim is meant to be stronger, or at least different: the self-defeating element is not the wanting but the checking. On my view, a person who wanted nothing but their own pleasure but never paused to ask whether it had arrived would mostly be fine, whereas a person who had fully renounced wanting pleasure but still audited themselves constantly—rating, journaling, comparing the present against the last entry—would still drain it. The paradox is therefore a paradox of measurement, not of desire.

An example: a listener who asks themselves every few minutes whether they are still enjoying a concert is, on my account, enjoying it less than a listener who never asks—not merely because they are distracted, but because each audit converts lived enjoyment into a verdict about enjoyment, and the verdict is thinner than the activity it replaced.

Tell me candidly and concretely: (1) Is the audit tax coherent, or does it rest on a picture of attention as a fixed pool that I have not earned? (2) Is this already in the literature under another name—I know the by-product view, and I do not want a reading list, only your judgement about whether I have said anything distinct. (3) Reconstruct the view in its strongest defensible form, then say what it gets right and where it breaks. I particularly want the false predictions: cases where ordinary experience or careful reasoning should make me abandon or at least curb it.

Please commit. I would rather have a single, argued verdict—coherent or not, distinct or not, salvageable only under such-and-such a revision—than a survey of possible positions. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the question.
