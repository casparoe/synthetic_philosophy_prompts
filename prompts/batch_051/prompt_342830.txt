For a continuing-education seminar I'm leading this spring, I've paired Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day with a couple of readings on perfectionism and the value of achievement, and the question I keep coming back to is this: is it good to be excellent even if no one benefits? Stevens is, by his own account, an excellent butler — the dignity business, the polish, the big dinner that goes off without a hitch while his father is dying upstairs. But once you actually list the beneficiaries, the ledger gets wierd: Lord Darlington is a deluded appeaser doing actual damage; Miss Kenton is made unhappy; and Stevens himself ends up alone, having traded his whole emotional life for the job. His excellence looks like perfectionism with the beneficiaries deleted. I don't think the novel wants us to simply write him off, though; there's something Stevens gets right about doing a thing well for its own sake, and the book refuses to sneer at it.

Could you read the novel through that lens for me? Take the role of a moral philosopher who works on value theory and perfectionism — the sort of person who distinguishes excellence-as-part-of-the-agent's-own-good from excellence-as-impersonal-value that can cut against welfare — but also someone who's faithful to the actual text, not just using the novel as a cute illustration. Start by brainstorming a couple of candidate readings and then commit to one. Tell me whether the novel takes a position on whether Stevens's excellence was good, all things considered, given that nobody benefits from it in any morally load-bearing way. Point me to specific scenes — the "dignity" discussions, the silver, the night his father dies, the ending on the pier — and also say which scenes fight the reading rather than support it. And finish by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that a perfectionism paper doesn't: what does the fiction dramatize that the arguments leave out?
