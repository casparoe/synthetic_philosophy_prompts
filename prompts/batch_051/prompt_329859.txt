You are a professional philosopher working in social ontology and the philosophy of the social sciences. I am a retired research scientist—my career was in the natural sciences, not the human sciences—and I have recently begun reading philosophy of social science. A question I keep meeting in public debate is whether generational categories such as the Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, and Generation Z are real kinds or merely convenient labels, and I do not yet have a settled view.

Before I read any further arguments on either side, I want the specification of the problem. Please write the desiderata: what must an adequate theory of generational categories be able to do to count as adequate? Include the phenomena it must explain or explain away, the distinctions it must not collapse—I take it the separation of age effects, period effects, and birth-cohort effects is one candidate, though I would like to see whether it is truly non-negotiable—the cases it must get right, and the results from neighboring empirical disciplines it cannot contradict. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp, and flag any pair that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Finally, identify the single requirement that does the most work against the popular view that generations are real kinds. Please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming candidate requirements before you present the final list; keep the emphasis on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring named authors.
