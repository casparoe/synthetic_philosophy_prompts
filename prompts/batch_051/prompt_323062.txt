In my European history class we read a short selection from Rousseau’s *The Social Contract*, Book II, Chapter 1, where he writes: “I hold then that Sovereignty, being nothing less than the exercise of the general will, can never be alienated, and that the Sovereign, who is no less than a collective being, cannot be represented except by himself: the power indeed may be transmitted, but not the will.” Our teacher took this to mean that Rousseau rejects representative government, but a classmate built the following argument from it.

(1) The sovereign is the people acting as a body in the exercise of the general will.  
(2) In a representative democracy, citizens transmit the power of making laws to representatives, who will on their behalf.  
(3) If representatives will on the people’s behalf, the people themselves are not exercising the general will.  
(4) Therefore the laws made by a representative body are not genuine acts of sovereignty.  
(5) But a legitimate state must be governed by the general will alone.  
(6) Therefore no representative democracy can be a legitimate state.

He then said this is clearly too strong, because Rousseau also says the power may be transmitted while the will may not, and he wants to allow something like a republic with magistrates. But the passage seems to rule out the very institutions Rousseau wants to permit. Where exactly does this argument go wrong? If none of the premises can be rejected without giving up something Rousseau needs, what should a Rousseauian say about modern representative democracies, and does the doctrine that citizens can be “forced to be free” make the problem harder or easier?
