You are a moral philosopher who works on virtue ethics and the empirical psychology of character. Think through the problem carefully, and begin by briefly mapping the conceptual landscape before you turn it into a procedure.

I am the dean of students at a residential secondary school. Our disciplinary committee writes evaluative language into internal records and recommendation letters: "she is honest," "he acted out of cruelty," "this shows integrity under pressure." I have read enough of the situationist critique of virtue ethics to worry that we are doing something the evidence may not license: inferring stable character traits from one or a few behaviors in highly structured situations. But we cannot simply stop using character language. The school is explicitly committed to character education, and the families, universities, and employers we report to expect it. I need a reusable decision procedure, not a lecture.

Please write a procedure our committee could actually run when deciding whether to attribute a trait to a student on the basis of observed conduct. The procedure should:

- state what must be true of the evidence before a trait attribution is licensed, including cross-situational consistency, behavioral diversity, the role of incentives and surveillance, and the baseline rate of the behavior in that setting;
- be written as an ordered set of questions or tests, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each;
- say where the procedure must stop with a finding of insufficient evidence for a character attribution rather than allowing a diluted attribution;
- say where it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts; and
- flag the cases it cannot settle.

Then run the procedure on these three cases:

1. A student returned a wallet containing cash last term, but was caught cheating on a physics test this term.
2. A student has intervened to stop bullying across four different settings over two years, but last month joined peers in vandalizing a rival school's locker room.
3. A student is consistently polite to teachers and coaches, but multiple new students report that the same student humiliates them when no adult is present.

Make sure at least one of the worked cases reaches a conclusion the committee would not like, and say which one it is and why the procedure forces it.

Throughout, be explicit about where the empirical situationist challenge runs out and a genuinely normative question about responsibility and desert takes over. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the procedure itself: which argument or empirical finding would lead you to revise the decision rule you have given, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
