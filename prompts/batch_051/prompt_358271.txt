Me and a couple of friends are running a tiny reading group on formal epistemology, and we got tangled on a point about Solomonoff induction that I'd like you to turn into a clean, made-to-order example. The background, put informally: Solomonoff induction is the idea that when you have observed a finite sequence, your prior weight on each possible continuation should be set by the shortest program that outputs it; simplicity is defined as having a shorter program on some fixed universal machine, and shorter programs get exponentially more weight. Kolmogorov complexity of a string is just the length of the shortest program that prints that string on a given machine. The standard reply to the worry that this all depends on your choice of programming language is an invariance theorem: switching from one universal machine to another only shifts complexity by an additive constant, so the dependence should wash out as the data gets long.

What I want you to build is a thought experiment in which Alice and Bob both run Solomonoff induction correctly but on two different universal machines (different built-in primitives or base languages), and they end up with sharply opposite, high-confidence predictions about the very next observation from the same finite data. Constraints: the data has to be something an ordinary person could actually encounter — rain/no-rain days, a card predictor flashing red/green, a traffic light pattern, your call — no exotic physics, no superintelligent predictors, and the stage-setting should be about one paragraph. Keep the observed history on the shorter side, a dozen or two dozen items at most, so the additive constants actually matter; the one-sentence gist of each machine's special primitive should be enough for a reader to see why the two agents diverge.

After the scenario, do the real work: state the observed history, give the two shortest programs Alice and Bob are respectively leaning on, say what each predicts for the next item and roughly why its probability is high for that agent, and explain why the invariance theorem doesn't stop the disagreement even though it's true. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the actual work — my guess is it's the short history relative to the additive constants, but I want your honest read — and what happens if you remove that feature, for instance by making the history much longer or forcing both agents to use a shared plain-language Occam's razor instead of raw Kolmogorov complexity. Finally, if you had to defend a hardcore Solomonoff fan against someone who says the whole approach is just a dressed-up choice of language, what would you say? Wear your algorithmic information theory teacher hat, think carefully, and start by brainstorming a couple of candidate scenarios before you settle on the one you run with.
