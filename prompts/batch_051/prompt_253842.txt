I've been rereading some Kripke and, on the side, tinkering with an interpreter for a small language I'm writing, and I keep noticing a parallel that might be completely daft. On the Kripke side: a proper name is what he calls a rigid designator — it refers to the same individual in every possible world — whereas a definite description refers to whichever thing satisfies the description in the world being evaluated, so its referent can shift from world to world. That's what makes Hesperus = Phosphorus necessary even though we had to learn it empirically: the two names are anchored to the same object in every world, but competence with the names doesn't by itself tell you that their anchors coincide.

On the programming side: an immutable variable bound to a particular object at its definition site, with lexical scoping, keeps referring to that same object no matter what context it gets evaluated in later, while an expression that resolves through the current environment (a dynamic-scope variable, or a lookup by property) can pick out different objects when evaluated in different states. And identity between two module-level constants looks to me like the necessary a posteriori: whether `a` and `b` are the same object is settled by their bindings once and for all, but you might still have to run the program, or at least inspect the heap, to find out.

So the mapping would be: possible worlds roughly correspond to evaluation contexts or runtime states, and in both cases something whose referent is fixed in advance comes apart from something whose referent is resolved on the fly. My question: is this a real structural parallel, or am I just being taken in by the fact taht both sides use words like 'bound' and 'evaluated'? What exactly maps to what, and what would need to be true on the programming side for the analogy to be more than cosmetic? Can the analogy be made precise, or is there a specific point where it fails? If it does fail, where exactly, and does it lead the parallel to predict something false?
