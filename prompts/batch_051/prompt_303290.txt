Provide a working definition of 'survival' in the personal sense — the sense in which a person can ask, before undergoing some process, whether they will survive it — that correctly classifies the following cases. Treat my stated verdicts as fixed where I give them, and otherwise let the definition do the work.

1. Anaesthesia. A person is drugged, operated on, and wakes the next morning with the same body, memories, plans, and character. I take this to be survival.

2. Cremation. A person dies and is cremated, and no duplicate, record, or successor of any kind is produced. I take this to be non-survival.

3. The teletransporter. A machine records the complete physical and functional state of a person's brain and body, destroys the body, and transmits the information to a receiver that assembles, from new matter, an exact physical duplicate. The duplicate wakes with all of the person's memories and concerns and goes on with their projects.

4. Branching. The same machine as in (3), except that it records the state without destroying the body: the original steps out of the machine unharmed while the duplicate simultaneously wakes elsewhere. Both share every memory up to the moment of scanning and thereafter lead separate lives.

5. Division. The same machine as in (3), except that it destroys the original and assembles two exact duplicates, in different rooms, both possessing all of the person's memories and concerns.

6. Approximate copying. The same machine as in (3), except that the duplicate is built with only about two-thirds of the person's memories; the rest are absent or distorted, and some characteristic desires and abilities are gone. The missing material cannot be recovered.

7. Total amnesia. A person suffers a brain injury and wakes in the same body with no memories of their earlier life and with radically changed values and abilities.

I find (1) and (2) obvious, and I give them only to anchor the definition. I am inclined to count (3) as survival but cannot see what would license that verdict if survival requires a later person to be me. I have no settled view about (4), (5), or (6). Case (7) pushes me toward the thought that 'survival' is doing two jobs — one concerned with whether some later person is the same person, and another concerned with whether what matters to me, such as memory, agency, and projects, continues somewhere, perhaps in more than one place at once or only partially.

Propose the best definition or definitions you can find, run them through all seven cases, and say where each definition gives a wrong answer or no answer. Then either revise it or defend the verdicts it yields, including wherever it contradicts the leanings I have reported.
