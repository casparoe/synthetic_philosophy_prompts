Ok so in my philosophy of law class we've been doing the void for vagueness doctrine, and after class two people from my section got into this whole argument at lunch about a disorderly conduct ordinance from the city where one of them grew up. I was just eating my sandwhich but they made me referee, and I honestly didn't know what to say.

The ordinance basically makes it a misdemeanor to do something that, in its context, tends to disturb the peace or quiet of people of ordinary sensibilities.

(1) Sara said it's obviously void for vagueness. How is anyone supposed to know in advance weather singing on the bus or arguing with their partner will "disturb" some hypothetical "ordinary" person? You'd have to guess, and the entire point of the doctrine is that you shouldn't have to guess about whether your conduct is criminal.

(2) Dev said that can't be right, because every law has fuzzy edges. He brought up the classic line-drawing cases — bald, heap, vehicles in the park. If edge cases voided laws, nothing would be enforceable.

(3) Sara said this isn't just an edge case problem. "Ordinary sensibilities" doesn't pick out a stable property you can check your conduct against; it's contested all the way down, so the law has no core of clear cases, only a fuzzy penumbra.

(4) Dev said she's basically describing every standard in law. "Reasonable person," "undue risk," "offensive" — courts apply evaluative terms like that all the time. And he said a context-sensitive standard is actually more precise than a bright-line rule, because it tracks what's really disruptive in the situation instead of slicing blindly.

(5) Sara said that's exactly the problem. The more you say "it depends on context," the more you've handed the content of the offense to the officer on the scene. That isn't a defense to the arbitrary-enforcement prong of vagueness; it's a confession of it.

(6) Dev came back that on that logic, every police standard — including "reasonable suspicion" — would be void too, and courts obviously don't think that.

(7) Sara said those aren't the same thing. Reasonable suspicion isn't a criminal law a citizen has to conform their conduct to in advance, and it's anchored to facts an officer can point to. This ordinance makes me predict in advance what a community of strangers will find intolerable.

At that point they turned to me to settle it. Can you go through this move by move and say who's right about which specific point, where each of them overreaches, and who comes out more right overall?
