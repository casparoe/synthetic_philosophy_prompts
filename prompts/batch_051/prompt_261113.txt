Act as a professional philosopher who works on value theory and moral psychology. I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so I care about getting the concepts right but I dont know the jargon. Please think carefully — maybe brainstorm two or three candidate principles before settling on your best one.

Lately I've been pulling apart values that feel like they matter for their own sake but might actually just be useful tools we're wired to treat as ends. The three I keep tripping over are truth-seeking, tradition, and purity. I'll give you my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases. I want you to find the principle, if there is one, that captures which of these feelings count as real intrinsic values and which are misfiring or covertly instrumental. Also tell me which verdicts I'd have to give up if the principle gets too complicated, and what the principle says about a case I can't decide.

Truth-seeking:

- After a production incident is mitigated, the root cause is never found and my manager wants to move on. I keep digging in my free time because I can't stand not knowing what actually happened. Verdict: good, even if we never see that bug again.
- A coworker says a movie came out in 2012 when it was actually 2011, and I feel a real urge to correct them. Verdict: not good; it's pedantry and I try to suppress it.
- I could read back through my old code-review comments to check whether my past criticisms were actually right. There's zero practical payoff now and the answer might be embarrassing. But I want to know if I was the sort of person who was right. Verdict: worth doing.
- A former coworker was laid off for "performance," but my manager hinted it was really office politics. Nothing actionable follows. I still want to know the truth. Verdict: the knowing matters.

Tradition:

- We have an ancient build script at work. It works, everyone hates it, and the replacement wins on every metric. I feel a weird pull to keep the old one. Verdict: that pull is just loss aversion; replace it.
- An open-source project I'm in writes a haiku in every changelog. It adds friction and no functionality, but long-time contributors treat it as part of the project's soul and new people start doing it too. Verdict: keep it; it actually is worth something.
- A family keeps making an objectively bad turkey recipe every Thanksgiving because it was the grandmother's and replacing it would feel like losing her. Verdict: keep making it, but I think that's a different kind of reason.
- A company refuses to retire a legacy admin panel with known security holes because "that's how we've always done it." Verdict: wrong; tradition is doing no real work there.

Purity:

- We need to ship a feature. The clean refactor takes three weeks; the hacky version takes two days and leaves manageable mess if we add a good comment. I feel a strong pull to do the clean version anyway. Verdict: the pull mostly overstates it; cleanliness matters downstream, not in itself.
- I'm looking at a house that's perfect and underpriced because the previous owner died there. I notice a creeped-out hesitation. Verdict: superstition, not a reason; buy the house.
- A friend refuses to eat at a restaurant that failed a health inspection two years ago, even after it was re-inspected and cleaned up. I don't just think "that's irrational." Part of me gets it — there's a feeling that the place is contaminated in a way re-inspection can't fix. Verdict: I suspect the feeling tracks something, but I can't say what.

The hard pairs for me are: root-cause digging (good) vs movie-year correction (bad), and old build script (replace) vs changelog haiku (keep). Each pair looks like the same underlying drive with the practical payoffs stripped out, so I can't explain why I come down differently. My working theory is something like: a value is intrinsic if it would still feel worth having in a world where it paid off nothing. But that just restates the problem, and it predicts the wrong thing for the build script. I'm also flirting with the extreme view that all three are just instrumental and the "matters for its own sake" feeling is a motivation hack our brains or cultures use to keep us doing useful things. But that view tells me to ignore the haiku ritual, and I don't want to.

Can you give me a principle that actually sorts these cases, even if it means telling me one of my verdicts has to go? Then apply it to an undecided case: an open-source team refuses to add anonymous crash telemetry to their library because "spying on users feels impure," even though it would help them fix bugs faster and no user is identifiable. Does my pattern say that purity reaction is a value or a misfire?
