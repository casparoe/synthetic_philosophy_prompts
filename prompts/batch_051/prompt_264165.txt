A colleague in the philosophy of physics has been circulating the following argument, and I have not yet found a diagnosis of it that I can trust. I would like a written assessment.

The argument, as numbered by my colleague:

(1) A theory is final just in case it would survive every possible future inquiry: no future measurement, at any energy, resolution, or scale, would overturn it.

(2) Our only source of evidence for a physical theory is a finite body of observations, made with particular instruments under particular conditions.

(3) For any finite body of evidence and any theory consistent with it, there are indefinitely many conceivable rivals that fit the same evidence but differ in their predictions for some regime not yet examined.

(4) Therefore, at no stage of inquiry can the accumulated evidence have eliminated every rival to a candidate final theory.

(5) To be warranted in judging that a theory would survive every possible future inquiry, one's evidence would have to eliminate every rival to it.

(6) So, from (4) and (5), no inquirer is ever warranted in judging that any theory is final, even if some theory actually is.

(7) But (5) is not a principle about finality specifically; it is a principle about what warrant requires. Apply it to any general physical theory about unexamined regimes — the atomic hypothesis, quantum electrodynamics, the quark model — and the evidence likewise never eliminates every rival.

(8) Hence no inquirer is ever warranted in judging any general physical theory true, not merely in judging one final.

(9) That conclusion I cannot accept. It makes the epistemic difference between a theory that has survived a century of deliberate, severe testing and a speculation that merely accommodates the same observations rationally invisible, and it convicts the ordinary practice of physics of one long unjustified leap rather than explaining its manifest reliability.

The argument therefore seems to prove too much, and I want to know where it goes wrong. Which numbered claim should be rejected, or which inference fails? Why does that step or premise look like a harmless piece of the epistemology of inquiry, and what makes it less innocent than it looks? Then say what the main positions in the vicinity — roughly, a scientific realist, a constructive empiricist (one who accepts a theory's empirical adequacy without asserting its truth about unobservables), and someone who takes underdetermination to be decisive — each have to pay either to block the argument or to live with its broader conclusion, and how much of ordinary physics each position leaves warranted.

I should declare my own commitments. I am strongly inclined to keep (1), which I take merely to unpack the notion of a final theory, and to keep (9) as a fixed point. But I do not want those commitments to take the place of a diagnosis.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis overall, and identify the specific point or step in your diagnosis about which your confidence is lowest.
