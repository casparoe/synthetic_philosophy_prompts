A colleague has asked me to set down the commitments that guide my work in epistemic utility theory, so that our seminar can pressure-test them as a set. Here they are.

I am a veritist: accuracy, understood as closeness to the truth, is the sole fundamental epistemic good, and the rational requirements on credence all flow from it. I hold that any legitimate measure of accuracy must be strictly proper: every probabilistic credence function must expect itself to be more accurate than any alternative. I accept the dominance argument for probabilism: any credence function that violates the probability axioms is accuracy-dominated (some alternative is at least as accurate at every possible world and more accurate at some) and is therefore irrational. I prefer the Brier score to the log score as the measure of inaccuracy for real agents. I think a rational agent updates by conditionalising on the strongest proposition she learns, even when the learning event was not antecedently represented as a cell of a partition. I think some rational doxastic states are best represented by a set of precise probability functions rather than a single function (for instance, after learning only that a coin is biased but not whether it is biased towards heads or towards tails). Finally, I think epistemic risk attitudes are irrational: expected accuracy is the whole story, and a concave transform of an accuracy score is not an admissible epistemic utility.

Go through each pair of these commitments and say whether the pair is genuinely in tension or only apparently so. For each genuine tension, say how severe it is, where the pressure comes from, and what the cheapest fix would be. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please indicate how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point you are least sure of, and write your answer in British English.
