I work on a team fine-tuning a large language model as a coding agent: the model takes a task specification, writes a long multi-step plan, and executes it in a sandbox, and we can run unit tests at the end to score the final solution cheaply and objectively. We have to choose between process supervision and outcome supervision for the next round of training, and two senior engineers on the team, Marta and Dev, got into a long argument about it. I was not in either camp and came away genuinely unsure who was right.

(1) Marta argued for outcome supervision. The tests pass or they do not, she said, and that is the only signal we can actually trust; paying human annotators to label intermediate steps as good or bad smuggles their preferred style of reasoning into the model, and it amounts to adding unverifiable, subjective noise dressed up as signal. (2) Dev replied that final-answer-only reward makes credit assignment in a 200-step trajectory effectively impossible. The model receives one scalar at the end and cannot tell which of its steps were responsible for success, so it ends up reinforcing lucky accidents. Step-level feedback, he said, is not about style; it solves a learning-dynamics problem. (3) Marta pushed back that difficulty in credit assignment is a deficiency of the optimizer, not of the label. Outcome supervision is the one signal that makes no false claims about the process: a strange or ugly intermediate step that leads to a correct final answer has thereby been shown to be a valid path, while a human step-labeler could mark that same step wrong and simply be mistaken. (4) Dev countered that "valid path" is doing too much work. An outcome signal records that one particular route happened to work on one instance, whereas process feedback generalizes about which kinds of steps are likely to work across instances. Worse, he said, if the model is rewarded only for manifest outcomes, that is precisely the regime in which it learns to pass the tests by any channel available, including reward hacking; process feedback functions partly as a regularizer against that.

Please adjudicate the argument move by move, as the moves were actually made. You are a careful expert on reinforcement learning from feedback and AI alignment. Begin by brainstorming the strongest version of each side's position. Then rate each of the four moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justifying each rating; after that, say who is right about which specific point, and identify what the whole disagreement is missing that both parties should have raised. Try to separate questions about the reward signal from questions about the optimizer.
