I'm taking a seminar on Augustine's ethics, and the part on lying has me genuinely stuck. As I understand him, he thinks every lie—a false assertion made with the intent to deceive—is a sin, and no consequences make it permissible, not even saving someone's life; the most you may do is keep silent or leave out part of the truth, since that isn't a false assertion. That struck me as obviously wrong at first, but now I'm not so sure it's just fanaticism, because my own case-by-case verdicts are a mess.

Here are my verdicts. A: a man with a knife asks if my friend is in the house; my friend is upstairs; I say no. I'd do it and I think I'd be right. B: I'm driving a dissident across a border; at a checkpoint a soldier asks if I'm hiding anyone in the car; I answer "there's no one in the back seat," true because she's in the trunk. This feels okay but slipperier than A. C: my aunt, dying, asks if she's getting better; I say she is. I'd say it and don't think it's wrong; it still sits in my stomach. D: a friend asks what I think of their poem; I say it's beautiful when I think it's bad. Fine, probably what friendship requires. E: I bluff at poker. Not even a lie; the game consents to it. F: I underreport income on my taxes. Wrong. G: under oath, I deny seeing a colleague make a small mistake that would get him fired. Wrong, even though the firing feels disproportionate. H: an undercover officer tells a trafficking ring he is a buyer. Fine, maybe admirable. I: I tell my partner i worked late when I'd actually met an ex for coffee, to dodge a fight. Wrong. J: a doctor gives a patient a saline injection but describes it as a new medication, hoping for a placebo effect. I want to say wrong, but if it demonstrably helps, I'm not sure.

Two pairs are bugging me. B and G are both misleading an authority to protect someone from consequences, yet B feels fine and G wrong. C and I are both lies to someone close that spare pain or avoid conflict, yet C feels fine and I wrong. I can hear the obvious distinctions—unjust versus legitimate authority, her comfort versus my convenience—but I worry I'm rationalizing after the fact. My best provisional rule is that a lie is permitted only when the hearer has forfeited their right to this particular truth, or when everyone has consented to a practice that creates a standing expectation of deception (poker, theater). That handles A, E, and H, maybe B; but it makes C and D wrong, which I dont accept, and I can't tell whether a "mercy exception" can be added without dissolving the rule entirely.
