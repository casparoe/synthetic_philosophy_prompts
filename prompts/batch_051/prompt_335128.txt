I'm a PhD student working on philosophy of programming / cognitive science, and I keep going back and forth on something. The functional programming crowd has a story about why mutation is so hard to reason about: once you allow assignment, referential transparency goes out the window, and you can't substitute equals for equals — an expressions value depends on the hidden history of the store, so you have to track identity over time (is this the same object as before? which x am I even talking about?) just to do the simplest thing. As an explanation of why programs with mutable state are bug-prone, I find it really compelling. But I also can't shake the intuition that people track a mutable world all the time without any of this machinery: I watch my coffee cup slide across the desk, I know its the same cup, I don't confuse it with another cup, and I don't feel like I'm doing anything like referential-transparency reasoning. So either everyday cognition is doing a lot more identity-and-state tracking under the hood than it feels like, or the story about why mutation is hard is a story about notation and languages rather than about thinking. I honestly don't know which should give way. What would it take for the intuition to go — what would debunk the feeling that reasoning about a mutable world is effortless? What would it take for the theory to go, or to get narrowed until it only explains a tiny corner of programming practice? And is there a reading on which both are right, for example the brain has dedicated machinery for object persistence over time that code and notation don't have, so mutation is hard to think about only for a certain kind of explicit, symbolic reasoning? I don't want a side picked. I want the conditions under which each side yields and a negotiated verdict.
