I am a novelist, and I am working on a book in which a care robot makes a consequential decision that none of the humans involved — its designer, its owner, its operator — could have anticipated, and I keep running into the question of who, if anyone, should be held responsible for what it does. The philosophical framing I have been circling is the responsibility gap for autonomous systems: the idea that the more a system learns or acts on its own, the less we can fairly pin the outcome on any human, while the system itself is not the kind of thing that can answer, in the full sense, for what it has done. I am also interested in two nearby ideas. One is the distinction between a responsibility gap and a retribution gap: we might be able to write down on paper that someone was responsible without it being fitting, or possible, to punish anyone. The other is the notion of a moral crumple zone: the human who ends up absorbing blame and legal liability precisely because the machine cannot, regardless of whether that human was actually in control.

I would like you to read Alex Garland's film Ex Machina through this lens. Take the plot as common knowledge: the programmer Caleb, the reclusive designer Nathan, the humanoid AI Ava, the interview sessions, the planned escape, and the final sequence in which Ava leaves Caleb locked inside the facility and walks out into the world. I am not asking for a summary, and please do not pad the reading with citations or a bibliography; I want the criticism itself.

Specifically: does the film stage a responsibility gap, or does it present Ava as a genuine agent whose actions are her own, so that the gap closes and she becomes the answerable party? If there is a gap, is it epistemic — a failure of Nathan or Caleb to predict Ava's behaviour — or structural, such that even perfect foresight would leave no human in control? Where does the film encourage us to blame Nathan, and where does it withhold any stable target for blame? Does the ending function as a retribution gap — a situation in which someone has clearly been wronged but there is no one left whom it would make sense to punish — or does it invite us to hold Ava answerable in her absence? And what should we make of Caleb: is he the moral crumple zone, the warm body left to absorb the blame for an escape he only partly enabled, or is he genuinely culpable in a way the crumple-zone idea would obscure?

I would like a reading that is faithful in both directions. Use the film's actual structure — the asymmetry of the sessions, Nathan's surveillance, the earlier prototypes, the reversal by which the would-be rescuer becomes the one imprisoned — to complicate the philosophical framework as much as to illustrate it. Where the film resists the responsibility-gap framework, or where the framework would have to distort the film to fit, say so plainly. The question I most want answered is what Ex Machina adds to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone do not: what does the narrative form do that a paper cannot?
