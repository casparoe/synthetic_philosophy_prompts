so a friend and i got into it about hard choices and he walked me into this argument that feels like it proves too much. im not sure whether the flaw is in the setup or the conclusion.

(1) A hard choice is one where neither option is better and the options aren't equal—the relevant values are incommensurable.
(2) If two options are incommensurable, there's no rate of exchange between the values. So adding a little more of one value to an option—a slightly higher salary, one more hour of free time a week—can never make that option better than the other. If a little bit could tip it, you'd have the beginnings of an exchange rate, and the values weren't really incommensurable.
(3) But then adding a lot of one value can't make it better either. A lot is just a bunch of littles, and each little addition leaves the options incommensurable, so the accumulated result should still be incommensurable.
(4) So in any hard choice, no amount of one value can make one option better than the other. That seems false: add a billion dollars to one job offer, or make it come with an extra forty hours a week of free time for the rest of your life, and the choice stops being hard.
(5) Therefore either the original choice was really commensurable all along (it was just close, or equal, and we were missing the exchange rate), or hard choices don't exist.

I don't believe hard choices are an illusion, and I'm suspicious of the slide from "no exact exchange rate" to "no amount can tip it." But I don't know which step to reject or what it costs. I'd most like to keep (1) and the thought that there's no exact rate between, like, money and creative time. Which step goes wrong, and what do I give up by rejecting it?
