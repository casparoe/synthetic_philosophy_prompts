Three months ago I began taking semaglutide, a GLP-1 agonist, and the experience has unsettled me more than I expected. I did not become better at resisting hunger; the hunger largely retreated, and with it the exhausting daily negotiation that had been the center of my attempts at dietary self-discipline for years. I now do with apparent effortlessness what I used to do only briefly and at great cost, and I am unsure whether this should count as self-mastery or as the abolition of the need for it.

Around the same time, I found myself reading Nietzsche's late writings on nutrition, digestion, climate, and the daily regimen of the body. He is the natural reference point for the question because he treats these physiological details as philosophically central. One strand of his thinking is almost dietetic: what one eats, how well one digests, and how lightly the body carries itself determine the health of the intellect and the will, and he counts self-overcoming over appetite among the disciplines of a well-constituted life. Another strand is a ferocious critique of the ascetic ideal, which he diagnoses as a hidden hostility to life, a way of punishing the body rather than strengthening it. An appetite-suppressing drug falls precisely on the seam between these two commitments, because it produces the outcome of bodily discipline without requiring the felt work of resistance.

I would like you to construct the position the mature Nietzsche of the late 1880s would most likely have taken concerning the widespread use of GLP-1 appetite-suppressing drugs, had they existed in his lifetime. Do not settle for an easy conclusion that he would be ambivalent; build the prediction from the commitments above and show how they pull against one another.

Address at least the following four issues. First, the dietetic side: would he regard a reliable way to quiet compulsive appetite as a tool of self-cultivation, on a par with his recommendations of light food and physical rigor? Second, the anti-ascetic side: would he see the drug as asceticism by pharmacological means—especially when it is used mainly to become thinner or to conform—or is it importantly different from the ascetic practices he attacks, because it restores and preserves the organism rather than weakening it? Third, the problem of effort: his language of self-overcoming suggests that value lies in the active overcoming of resistance, whereas a GLP-1 agonist removes a resistance instead of training a capacity to overcome it. Is that felt struggle essential to his conception of self-mastery, or would he care only about the resulting constitution of body and mind? Fourth, the physiological-causal question: he would presumably ask what the drug does to digestion, mood, energy, and instinct. Reason in his own explanatory style here rather than turning him into a modern moralist.

End by stating the verdict you predict, with a realistic estimate of confidence, and identify the points at which you are extrapolating beyond anything he actually wrote. Indicate which facts about these drugs—their side effects, their social context, the reasons people take them—would most affect the prediction.
