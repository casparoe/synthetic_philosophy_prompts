Discussions of the Fermi paradox and long-term civilizational futures tend to treat "more advanced than us" as if it picked out a single ordering. But the two best-known scales in the literature rank advancement along different axes. Kardashev's scale rates civilizations by the power they can command: all the energy available on a home planet (Type I), the full output of a star (Type II), the full output of a galaxy (Type III), with later writers extending the ladder upward. Barrow's microdimensional mastery scale rates a civilization instead by the smallest structures it can deliberately manipulate: objects on its own scale (Type I-minus), genes and cells (Type II-minus), molecules (Type III-minus), atoms (Type IV-minus), nuclei (Type V-minus), elementary particles (Type VI-minus), and finally spacetime itself (Type Ω-minus), the "minus" marking that the scale descends in size rather than ascending in energy.

Compare these two scales as measures of civilizational advancement. What does each treat as the substance of advancement, and what does each quietly select for or leave out? Are there plausible civilizations that rank high on one scale and low on the other, and do those cases show that the two scales are tracking different underlying variables? Which one better captures the notion of advancement that actually does the work in great-filter and Fermi-paradox reasoning, and why? If you had to defend a single measure of advancement for those debates, which would it be — or should the one-dimensional picture be abandoned?
