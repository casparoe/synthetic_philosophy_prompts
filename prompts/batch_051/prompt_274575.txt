I've been rereading Huizinga's Homo Ludens and I keep getting stuck on a line near the top where he writes: "The contrast between play and seriousness is always fluid. The inferiority of play is continually being offset by the corresponding superiority of its seriousness. Play turns to seriousness and seriousness to play. Play may rise to heights of beauty and sublimity that leave seriousness far beneath." I basically want to defend that as a substantive thesis about leisure: play isn't just relief from real life, and the serious/playful distinction isn't a value ranking. But I'm trying to be honest about what could count against it. What would have to be true for Huizinga to be wrong here — not just factually wrong about a particular culture, but wrong in a way that should make me drop the view? I'm especially curious about the worry that any apparent counterexample — say, play that remains subordinate to work, or play that's clearly just instrumentally useful — can be redescribed as a case where play hasn't risen to those heights or where it's "turned into seriousness." Is the fluidity claim actually falsifiable? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and a verdict on whether the position ends up being a real empirical claim or just a way of talking. And if it does turn out to be unfalsifiable, is that bad for a claim of this kind?
