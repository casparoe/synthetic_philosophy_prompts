I've been working on a piece about the origins of modern biblical criticism and have been reading Spinoza's *Theologico-Political Treatise*. In Chapter VI, he writes: "As men are accustomed to call Divine the knowledge which transcends human understanding, so also do they style Divine, or the work of God, anything of which the cause is not generally known: for the masses think that the power and providence of God are most clearly displayed by events that are extraordinary and contrary to the conception they have formed of nature, especially if such events bring them any profit or convenience: they think that the clearest possible proof of God's existence is afforded when nature, as they suppose, breaks her accustomed order, and consequently they believe that those who explain or endeavour to understand phenomena or miracles through their natural causes are doing away with God and His providence."

That passage seems to generate an argument I can't fully defuse. I'd like a diagnosis of where it goes wrong. The argument runs: (1) If calling an event a work of God is just a way of saying its cause is not generally known, then biblical revelation is not a distinct mode of divine action; it is ordinary nature plus human ignorance. (2) The historical-critical method exists precisely to supply the missing natural causes—to read Scripture as a record of human history and natural events. (3) Therefore, to the extent that historical criticism succeeds, it removes the ignorance that made an event look divine, and thereby removes revelation. (4) So a believer committed to historical-critical reading should expect the method to dissolve the object of faith. That conclusion seems to prove too much: it turns explanatory progress into an argument against the very thing being interpreted, and it would make any serious historical study of Scripture a secularizing act. Which premise should a historian of religion reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
