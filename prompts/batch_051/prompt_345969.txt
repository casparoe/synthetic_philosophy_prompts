Below is the standard menu of positions about what thought experiments do, epistemically, in philosophy, as I have collected it.

1. The argument view: a thought experiment is an argument in narrative costume. It can be reconstructed as premises plus an inference, and whatever persuasion it achieves comes from the premises the story supplies or activates. On this view a thought experiment never contributes evidence over and above an ordinary argument.

2. The intuition-pump view: a thought experiment is a device for making a pre-theoretic judgment salient, and the real philosophical work is done by the intuition that surfaces. The central question is then whether that kind of intuition is reliable.

3. The illustration view: thought experiments are pedagogically vivid but epistemically idle. They dramatize a theory or a distinction that has to be defended elsewhere, and a good diagram or concrete example would be doing exactly the same work.

4. The mental-model view: thought experiments are continuous with ordinary reasoning about real situations. We construct a mental model, run it, and read off what happens; the run can be informative because the model encodes tacit empirical, practical, or physical knowledge.

5. The Platonist view: some thought experiments are not reconstructions of old beliefs but a direct a priori route to new beliefs about abstract or lawlike structure. On this view the mind can, in some cases, apprehend the relevant domain directly rather than merely rearranging what it already believed.

6. The ordinary-counterfactual-imagination view: thought experiments are a special case of the counterfactual reasoning we rely on constantly outside philosophy. Their verdicts are justified to the extent that our general capacity for counterfactual imagination is reliable, and no special philosophical method is needed.

I believe this list comes close to exhausting the serious positions on offer. I want a seventh account of what thought experiments do, one whose core claim about the source or status of their philosophical contribution is genuinely distinct from each of the six above, not a rewording, a special case, a combination, or a difference of emphasis. The account may be eccentric or only half-developed, as long as it is authentically different.

Then tell me honestly: (a) what the seventh account says, and (b) whether it is actually a good account, and why.
