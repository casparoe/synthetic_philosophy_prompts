I have been working out a position I call the Reserved Share Principle, which I think dissolves the question of whether taxation is theft. The principle says this: property titles are created by a legal order, and no legal order creates them as unencumbered wholes. Every title, at the moment it comes into existence, carries a standing reservation — a fractional share of the value the title protects, reserved to the political community that makes titled possession possible at all. The tax authority is therefore not an outsider seizing part of what you already owned; it is, in effect, a co-owner from the outset, holding an interest written into the title itself. When you are paid a salary, the gross amount is jointly held from the first instant, and the withheld portion is not taken from you but merely separated out as the state's reserved share. The same would apply to capital gains, inheritances, and rises in property value: the appreciation is never entirely yours, because the reservation is attached to the title rather than to any later transfer.

I know this looks like a word game that redefines property in order to make the problem disappear. But I think it captures something real: the slogan "taxation is theft" presupposes a baseline of full, unencumbered ownership that has never existed in any functioning legal system. Theft is the violation of a rightful claim, and the state's reserved share is not such a violation, because the claim was created with that share already carved out.

I am aware of the neighboring ideas: the bundle-of-rights account of property, social contract justifications of taxation, and the view that pretax income is a creature of the tax code rather than a pre-legal fact. I want to know whether the Reserved Share Principle is a genuine contribution or only a restatement of one of those. Is it coherent? Does it say anything beyond the familiar point that property rights are conventional? Does it already exist under another name? And where does it break — does it, for example, license any tax rate whatsoever, since the size of the reserved share is whatever the law declares it to be, and if the reserved share can be set at 100%, has the distinction between taxation and confiscation simply collapsed?
