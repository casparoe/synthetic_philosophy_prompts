I run anomaly detection at a small chemical plant: we get about a dozen sensor alerts a week (vibration, temperature, pressure) and sort each into same mechanism we've already fixed, a new failure mode, or noise. Wrong calls are costly—either a crew chases a non-problem or a real failure goes unattended—and the plant manager has to sign off on every fix. We get it wrong in both directions: identical traces that turned out to be different failures, and different-looking alerts that turned out to be one mechanism reporting itself through different sensors. Write a reusable procedure, grounded in whatever the philosophy of mechanisms says about what makes two episodes the same mechanism rather than merely similar symptoms, that my engineers (no philosophy background) can run under time pressure. Say what to check and in what order, what counts as evidence that two anomalies share a mechanism, and where it has to hand off to a senior engineer because the call is judgement rather than procedure. Then run it on three cases: (a) two pressure spikes with the same trace, (b) a temperature rise and a vibration rise that appear together but with different lag times, and (c) an anomaly that exactly matches last year's known bearing failure but shows up on a newly installed pump. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer my team won't like and would want to override. Finally tell me what the procedure can't do, and what evidence or consideration would change your mind about the procedure itself.
