I am a retired natural scientist, and I have recently started reading in the philosophy of mathematics. A recurring puzzle in constructive logic has been bothering me, and I would like you to act as a professional logician and diagnose it carefully. It seems to show that the intuitionist's rejection of proof by contradiction is inconsistent with the intuitionist's own account of negation. Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it:

(1) In the constructive reading, a proof of ¬P is a construction that converts any proof of P into a proof of a contradiction. Consequently, to establish ¬P it suffices to derive a contradiction from the assumption P.

(2) Applying the very same rule to the proposition ¬P: to establish ¬¬P it suffices to derive a contradiction from the assumption ¬P.

(3) Classical proof by contradiction is the rule that permits one to infer P once a contradiction has been derived from the assumption ¬P.

(4) Therefore, whenever the condition for the classical rule is met, the intuitionist already has a proof of ¬¬P, by step (2).

(5) But ¬P is just the assertion that P is false, so ¬¬P is the assertion that P is not false. Since every proposition is either true or false, to say that P is not false is to say that P is true. Hence ¬¬P is equivalent to P.

(6) It follows that, whenever a contradiction can be derived from ¬P, the constructive account of negation itself gives us P, by steps (4) and (5).

(7) So proof by contradiction is constructively valid after all, and the intuitionist has no coherent grounds for rejecting it.

I suspect the problem is located around step (5), in the slide from "P is not false" to "P is true," but I want an exact diagnosis rather than a slogan. Please begin by laying out, as precisely as you can, the constructive meaning of negation and of contradiction, and the distinction between a proof of a negation and a proof by contradiction. Then go through the steps one by one and say which is the first to fail, what an intuitionistically acceptable replacement for it would say, and how much of the argument survives once the repair is made. If you can, give a concrete mathematical example where a constructive mathematician would accept ¬¬P but not P, and explain what separates the two. I want to understand whether the rejection of classical proof by contradiction is merely a consequence of dropping the law of excluded middle, or whether there is a deeper point about what it means for a proposition to be proved.
