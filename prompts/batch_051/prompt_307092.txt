Alright, I could use a diagnostic read on a debate I keep getting pulled into. I'm in a philosophy of language reading group and we've been stuck on vagueness for months. We read the standard back-and-forth between the supervaluationists (Fine, Keefe) and the epistemicists (Williamson), and here's where we keep circling: both sides accept the same data. Competent speakers won't draw a sharp line in a sorites series for a term like bald, a one-hair difference never seems to flip the predicate from true to false, and yet the series runs from clearly bald to clearly not bald. The supervaluationist says the predicate is semantically incomplete: on each admissible precisification there is a cutoff, so borderline statements end up neither true nor false, while classical logic stays valid because excluded middle comes out true on every sharpening. The epistemicist says no, bivalence holds and there really is a sharp cutoff somewhere in the series, at a place we're constitutively unable to know. The two theories seem to capture nearly all the same linguistic behavior, and after decades of this nobody appears to have moved.

My working hypothesis is that this is really a standoff about what a semantic theory is supposed to buy you: the epistemicist buys bivalence and a simple two-valued picture at the cost of an unknowable sharp boundary; the supervaluationist buys the intuition that borderline cases lack a fact of the matter at the cost of giving up bivalence. But it might instead be a hidden disagreement about whether meaning is fixed by use, or a verbal dispute about what the word true means in borderline cases. I want a diagnosis rather than a scorecard. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two groups answering different questions under one heading? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a formal result, an empirical fact about speaker behavior, a sharper distinction, or should we stop expecting the debate to end?
