I'm a retired spectroscopist—spent my working life reducing spectra off CCDs—and I've only recently started reading philosophy seriously, mostly legal interpretation and property, which is how I've managed to get myself into this. For years I've been keeping alive one ancient DOS programme for reducing spectra, SPECRED. It was written by a single bloke who ran a one-man software outfit. He died in 2014, intestate, no heirs ever came forward, the company was struck off, and the source code has never been found. What survives is the binary on a wheezing old Windows 98 machine and a stack of installer floppies, with the original licence printed out in the binder.

The licence says, under the heading 'Restrictions':

"The Licensee may not modify, adapt, translate, reverse engineer, or create derivative works based on the Software, and may not distribute, rent, lease, lend, sublicense, or otherwise make the Software or any copy of it available to any third party, in whole or in part, without the prior written consent of the Licensor, which may be given or withheld at the Licensor's sole discretion. This Licence is binding upon the parties and their respective successors and permitted assigns."

The Windows 98 machine is dying, and I'd like to patch the binary so it runs under an emulator on Linux—just enough to make it think it's still on its old OS and to handle dates after 1999, which it can't. A younger colleague at another observatory has also asked for a copy, because after all these years this software is still the least bad tool for one particular instrument. I can't ask the man for written consent: he's dead and there's no successor or assignee to ask. Orphaned software, I believe it's called.

I'm not asking for a legal prediction so much as what the text, read honestly, requires or permits now. Does 'without the prior written consent of the Licensor' mean the restrictions are simply permanent now that consent is impossible? Or does that impossibility cut the other way? Run it through the obvious approaches—the wording, the purpose of the clause, what the drafter would have said if asked, what a reasonable reader takes it to mean—and tell me which reading ought to win, and whether patching the binary and giving away a copy come out seperately. Also say whether the fact that the owner has gone and no successor ever took up the rights changes the moral situation, not just the legal one.

And please answer in British English.
