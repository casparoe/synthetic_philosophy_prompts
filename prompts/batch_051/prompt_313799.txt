I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about whether a person can genuinely have a reason to do something that has no foothold at all in what she already cares about. He kept returning to the case of a smoker who is entirely indifferent to her future health and insisting that, even so, she has a reason to stop; I kept replying that if she has no desire to be healthy, and would not form one even after thinking clearly about what smoking does to her, then the claim that she has a reason seems to lose its grip. From there we went through the amoralist who understands moral language but is left cold by it, the fully informed but purely selfish person, and the person whose only concern is avoiding pain, and we could not agree on which underlying commitment was doing the work in any of these cases.

I help run an undergraduate philosophy club (about twenty students, most with one or two philosophy courses behind them, a few with none), and I would like to turn this dispute into a handout for a session on practical reason: a ten-to-twelve-question sorting quiz called, in effect, "Which view of normative reasons do you actually hold?" The questions should be concrete vignettes — the indifferent smoker, an amoralist, someone with false beliefs about what would actually satisfy their deepest project, someone who would come to care about a stranger only after vivid exposure to that stranger's suffering, and the like — with two or three answer options each, phrased so that a student can answer without ever having heard the labels "internalism" and "externalism." No question should ask which position the student likes or mention the philosophical distinction; the quiz should sort people by what they say about the cases.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least the following leaves: a crude motivational internalism on which a reason requires a present element in the agent's existing desires, projects, or commitments; a broadly Williams-style internalism on which reasons must be derivable from what the agent already cares about by sound practical reasoning, but not necessarily from anything she is currently moved by; an idealized internalism on which reasons may depend on what the agent would want after full information and flawless deliberation; and an externalism on which at least some reasons are grounded in facts independent of the agent's actual or counterfactual motivations. Please build the instrument from the logical space first — the commitments the vignettes pull apart, and the combinations of answers that remain coherent — and only then locate the named positions in that space, noting any combination that seems unoccupied and briefly diagnosing whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. End with a note on which pair of positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate: I suspect the idealized internalist and the externalist will agree on most ordinary vignettes and can only be separated by a case in which the agent's motivational set is impoverished in a way that no amount of sound deliberation repairs, and I would like the facilitator to be told that explicitly.
