I run a reading group on the philosophy of physics — about ten people, philosophy grad students mostly, comfortable with a little probability but no real physics background — and we have a 90-minute session coming up. I want to do Bell's theorem as a game rather than a lecture. The thing I want them to come away with is that two players who can agree on any instructions they like beforehand, and who can carry as much shared randomness as they want into the corners, still hit a hard ceiling that the quantum mechanical predictions of the same situation blow straight through. I want them to run into that ceiling themselves before anyone says "local hidden variables," so no front-loading of the vocabulary; name it only in the debrief.

The game I have in mind is the CHSH game, roughly. Two volunteers, Alice and Bob, go to opposite corners of the room. Each round a referee gives Alice a random bit x and Bob a random bit y, all four pairs equally likely. Alice writes a bit a, Bob writes a bit b, with no communication during the round and neither sees the other's question or answer until scoring. They win the round iff a XOR b = x AND y. They can pre-agree any strategy they like, and between rounds they can revise it. I expect them to find the always-output-zero strategy quickly and sit at 75%; the interesting part is when I offer a prize for beating 75% over a decent number of rounds, because no local strategy can get them there on average.

Then I want a second phase where I show them a setup that wins about 85%, and get them to see why no pair of instruction cards, however clever, could be behind it.

What I need from you is a complete 90-minute design: exact rules, materials (cards, slips, scoreboard; no real money), how to referee so no one can signal, a timeline, and a concrete way to stage the 85% phase without just asserting the result. For the debrief, map the specific moments of the game onto the distinctions that matter — shared randomness vs local hidden variables, determinism, no-signaling, and the exact spot where a local story gets pinned into a contradiction. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guess: someone wins over a short run and thinks they beat the bound, and somebody tries to encode signals in coughs or timing) and how to handle those. And be honest about whatever part of Bell's theorem a classroom can't actually reproduce — I assume there isn't any real entanglement in the room — and what the facilitator should say out loud to cover that gap.
