I'm the content lead for a vocabulary app for adult English learners, and I need to stop my team from making inconsistent calls about what counts as one word. Every time we build a flashcard set we have to decide wether two forms are the same word (so one headword with the other forms folded in as variants) or different words (so two separate headwords). Right now it's vibes. We merge run/ran and walk/walked, but we split act/actor and write/rewrite. We merge happy/happier but split happy/happily. Editors keep fighting about employee vs. employer, use (the noun) vs. use (the verb), married (a married couple) vs. walked, better as a form of good, boring vs. running, and golden vs. gold. The stakes: if we over-split, a learner ends up with thirty cards for go and quits; if we over-merge, they get tested on forms we never actually taught and decide the app is broken. The editors who would use this are smart but have no linguistics training and roughly two minutes per form-pair during a content sprint, and they can't go check corpora or etymologies. Write me a decision procedure, grounded in the inflection/derivation distinction (productivity, obligatoriness, category change, meaning shift, and whatever else actually does the work), that a junior editor can run step by step. Give me a small set of test questions in order, with a worked example of each kind, including at least one case where the procedure tells them to merge two forms they wanted to split or split two they wanted to merge. Flag the cases the procedure can't settle, where someone with linguistics training has to make the call.
