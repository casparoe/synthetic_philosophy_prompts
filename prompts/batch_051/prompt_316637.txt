I'm putting together a one-off graduate seminar called "Do states fail on schedule?" and I want to allocate reading time among four classic cyclical theorists: Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler, and Toynbee. My problem is that I've been reading too much rationalist material on institutional decay lately, and I now find some of ancient political history suspiciously like narrative fallacy — but I don't want to dismiss these authors just because they lacked modern econometric tools or formal models.

Here is a passage I keep chewing on, from Polybius, Histories, Book VI (Shuckburgh translation), where he explains how kingship turns into tyranny:

But when their royal power became hereditary in their family, and they found every necessary for security ready to their hands, as well as more than was necessary for their personal support, then they gave the rein to their appetites; imagined that rulers must needs wear different clothes from those of subjects; have different and elaborate luxuries of the table; and must even seek sensual indulgence, however unlawful the source, without fear of denial. These things having given rise in the one case to jealousy and offence, in the other to outburst of hatred and passionate resentment, the kingship became a tyranny: the first step in disintegration was taken; and plots began to be formed against the government, which did not now proceed from the worst men but from the noblest, most high-minded, and most courageous, because these are the men who can least submit to the tyrannical acts of their rulers.

What I want from you is an evaluative ranking, not just a compare-and-contrast. Please give me: (1) a tier list of the four by how much mechanistic or explanatory content their account of decline has, judged by the standards of a modern social scientist who is allergic to destiny-talk; (2) a set of weights summing to 1 representing what fraction of the seminar's reading time you would allocate to each; and (3) one sentence for each explaining why it lands where it does. In particular, when rating Polybius, take the passage above seriously as his actual mechanism: does "hereditary security loosens appetites, which fuels elite resentment and plot formation" still count as a real causal story, and how does it compare with Ibn Khaldun's asabiyyah, Spengler's organic stage-theory, and Toynbee's challenge-and-response?
