I host a small fortnightly seminar on the history of political economy for retired academics, and this term the session is an interview with a historian of eighteenth-century economic thought who has spent much of her career reconstructing and defending Adam Smith's account of empire and colonies. My own training was in geology, and I still prefer a discussion that proceeds the way a field investigation does: each answer should disclose the next thing to test rather than terminate in a slogan. Could you draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions for this guest? The questions should draw out the internal mechanics of Smith's position and should not invite her to enumerate publications, recount the secondary literature, or supply quotations.

At a minimum, build the sequence around the following six pressure points, and for each main question write the follow-up that anticipates the answer she is most likely to give:

- His claim that the exclusive trade with the American colonies does not make Britain richer but instead pulls capital into less productive employments for the benefit of well-placed merchants. The likely reply is that this is one strand of a broad attack on the mercantile system; the follow-up should force her to say what is specifically bad about the colonial monopoly as against an ordinary tariff.

- His apparently inconsistent defense of the Navigation Acts despite their mercantilist character, on the ground that naval power matters more than opulence. The likely reply is that defense is one of the sovereign's proper duties while commercial gain is not; the follow-up should ask how we are to tell, in a concrete case, whether a restriction is a genuine instrument of defense or merely a captured protection.

- His two proposals for resolving the American crisis: full parliamentary representation in proportion to colonial taxation, or a voluntary separation into a kind of federative union. The likely reply is that he regards the latter as the more realistic and that the empire persists partly from national vanity; the follow-up should press her on whether Smith thought the British Empire was unsustainable on principle or only in its then-current form.

- His treatment of slavery, where he argues that the institution is not the economically efficient arrangement and explains its persistence by the love of domination rather than by the sober calculation of profit. The likely reply is that the masters' pride overrides their material interest; the follow-up should ask whether this same love of domination also does explanatory work in his account of why Britain clings to its colonies.

- His account of the damage done to non-European peoples by European contact and conquest. The likely reply is that Smith saw Europe's expansion as an engine of growing opulence that was twisted by injustice; the follow-up should press on whether his commercial theory can condemn that injustice in its own terms rather than treating it as a regrettable externality.

- His critique of chartered trading companies that also govern, such as the East India Company, on the ground that merchants make poor sovereigns because their interests as traders conflict with their duties as rulers. The likely reply is that the error lies in merging government with a particular commercial interest; the follow-up should ask whether the argument applies only to joint-stock monopolies or to any political order that subordinated rule to trade.

Because she is a practiced exegete, the follow-ups should not be requests for clarification or invitations to expand. Each should identify the weakest inference in the position she has just taken and ask her to shore it up or abandon it.

Please also weave in one or two questions connecting the economic arguments to Smith's moral psychology in the Theory of Moral Sentiments, in particular his critique of the man of system who arranges people as if they were pieces on a chessboard and the impartial spectator's resistance to the intoxicating appeal of great empire. The follow-up to those questions should test whether the moral-psychological diagnosis and the economic diagnosis stand or fall independently of one another.
