Novelist here. I am writing a novel in which the narrator becomes preoccupied with a question about truth and futurity. In Aristotle’s On Interpretation, chapter 9, he argues that for matters about the past and the present, either the affirmation or the negation must already be true or false, but for a future contingent such as whether there will be a sea-battle tomorrow the case is otherwise: if one side were already true now, the event would appear to occur of necessity, so he seems to leave neither side true nor false until the outcome is settled. My variation is this: the narrator knows of a sealed book that simply records, without causing anything, all true future-tensed statements about his world. He may open the book only once, to a randomly chosen sentence, and only after he has formed and announced his intention whether to sail. Does this book change whether the statement “He will sail” is already true or false before he chooses, or does it merely make the original puzzle more vivid? Analyze precisely how the addition of a statement that is guaranteed to match the future but is causally inert affects the argument that bivalence implies necessity. Please take a definite stand on what the modified case shows about truth-value gaps. Finally, say how confident you are and identify the point you are least sure of. End with a one-sentence summary as the final line.
