I've been trying to figure out which things I'm willing to say have beliefs and desires literally, not just "as if," and I keep failing to find the principle behind my own verdicts. A thermostat: no. A chess engine: no, even though we all say it want's to control the center. A honeybee doing its waggle dance: I lean yes. A large language model that passes false-belief tests: no. A cat watching a bird: yes. A corporation that conceals a defect: no—the executives believe it, but not the company. A patient who insists his wife has been replaced by an identical imposter but doesn't call the police and is still warm to her: I want to say he believes it, but not in the full ordinary way. A split-brain patient whose left hand picks one mug while the right hand denies wanting it: I want to say the left hemisphere wants it and the right doesn't, but I flinch at two believers in one skull. What principle gets all or most of these? Make each clause earn its keep, and tell me which of my verdicts I should drop if it can't. Then run the principle on a case I'm stuck on: an octopus that solves novel problems and remembers the solutions for weeks. And tell me whether the principle you end up with pushes toward eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires, or toward keeping them as real kinds.
