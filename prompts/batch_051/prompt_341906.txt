I'm a journalist on the science and ideas beat, and I keep tripping over the same fight in philosophy: are concepts things you analyse, or things you should fix when they're not doing their job. On one side sit the conceptual analysts, who say the job is to tell us what 'woman', 'marriage', 'mental disorder' and so on already mean. On the other sit the ameliorators and conceptual engineers, who say we should build the concepts we need rather than accept the ones we inherited. For a piece I'm writing, I want to imagine what an adverserial collaboration between two good-faith representatives of those camps would produce after a year of arguing — call them the Analyst and the Engineer.

Write me that document. Start with a numbered list of claims both can actually sign (nothing vacuous — no 'concepts are useful'). Then a disagreements table: each row gives the neutral question, then the Analyst's own wording of their answer and the Engineer's own wording of theirs, each phrased so its side would happily put their name to it. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling one would move at least one side — and for each crux, a concrete case or bit of evidence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I specifically want two cases used: the Pluto demotion and the shift in what 'marriage' refers to. Finish with a short section on disagreements that turned out to be merely verbal. My own guess is that one real crux will be the change-of-subject worry — does altering a concept mean you're now talking about something else? — and another is whether ordinary usage carries any normative weight, but don't let my guesses constrain you.
