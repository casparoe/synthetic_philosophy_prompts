A friend has shown me that the familiar defence of the Santa and tooth-fairy deceptions may prove far too much, and I cannot locate the bad step.

(1) The wrong-making feature of lying is exhausted by this: the liar bypasses the hearer's rational agency, substituting his own judgment about what the other shall believe instead of placing the truth before a fellow epistemic agent. There is no further wrong-making feature beyond this.

(2) Small children lack rational epistemic agency in the relevant sense: they cannot yet assess evidence or consent to being given the truth, and their beliefs are properly held in trust by their parents. So when a parent tells a child the Santa or tooth-fairy story, the wrong-making feature identified in (1) is absent.

(3) Where the wrong-making feature identified in (1) is absent, a benevolent falsehood that reliably serves the hearer's interests is not wrong as a lie, and is permissible.

(4) The Santa and tooth-fairy practices are benevolent falsehoods told to children who lack the relevant agency, and they serve real interests: wonder, shared imaginative play, gradual education in the difference between fiction and testimony, and an eventual rite of initiation into the truth.

(5) Therefore the Santa and tooth-fairy practices are permissible.

(6) But rational epistemic agency is scalar and domain-relative, not simply present or absent. Many adults have it only partially or only in some domains: an early-stage dementia patient, a friend in a severe depressive episode, a relative too fragile for a terminal prognosis.

(7) By (3), a benevolent falsehood told to such an adult is not wrong as a lie, and is permissible, whenever the adult's agency in the relevant domain is sufficiently compromised that there is nothing for the lie to bypass.

(8) Yet that is too permissive. A physician who comforts a dementia patient with reassuring falsehoods, a husband who conceals an affair from a severely depressed spouse, a daughter who hides a parent's terminal diagnosis — none of these is rendered permissible merely because the target's agency is diminished and the deceiver is benevolent and trusted. Each remains morally objectionable in a way that citing the target's compromised agency does not answer.

(9) So either (1) is false and lying has some further wrong-making feature; or (3) does not transfer from undeveloped agency to merely diminished agency; or there is a thus-far-unnamed principle separating the child's case from the adult's. If there is such a principle, the appeal to undeveloped agency was never the real justification in the first place.

I would most like to keep both the Santa practice and the prohibition on benevolent paternalistic adult deception, but I suspect any principle that keeps both will look ad hoc. Which premise should I give up, and what does rejecting it cost? Explain why the tempting step looked tempting, and say what the main available positions — autonomy-based, trust-based, virtue-based — each commit me to if I want to keep the Santa practice while blocking the adult cases.
