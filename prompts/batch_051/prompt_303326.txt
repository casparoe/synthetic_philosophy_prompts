I teach a virtue ethics seminar most years, and I've been trying to make sense of why courage in a peaceful society feels so much more diffuse then the courage the old virtue tradition actually talks about. I've arrived at a distinction I'd like checked. Borrowing a word from finance (unfortunately), I've been calling it the counterparty condition on courage. The idea is that courage in its core martial sense is a three-place relation: an agent, a danger, and a counterparty — where the counterparty is an opposing will, or at least something agency-like that could bring about the harm and is in some minimal sense willing it. What the courageous person overcomes is fear of an opposing will, not just fear of a bad event. A soldier holding a line, or someone staring down a man with a knife: the fear has an addressee. But most of what gets called courage in a peaceful society has no counterparty at all — facing a cancer diagnosis, speaking up at a faculty meeting, finishing a manuscript, coming out to one's parents. I want to say those are admirable, but they aren't courage; they're fortitude, or resilience, or nerve — excellences that consist in outlasting a situation rather than standing up to a will. The corollary is that a peaceful society mostly does not exercise courage; it keeps the word warm by stretching it over non-counterparty cases.

The obvious problem is the standard non-martial paradigm: the person who runs into a burning building or a freezing river to save a child. There's danger but no opposing will, and if my test says that isn't courage, I've cut myself off from a case nearly everyone counts as central. I catch myself saying that the fire or the river is a counterparty "in a metaphorical sense," and I know that's the sound of a distinction leaking. Still, I can't shake the thought that the martial virtues survive in peace only in disarmed forms — courage becomes resilience, honor becomes reputation, loyalty becomes fandom — and that we convince ourselves the virtue is preserved because the compliment is. Give me your honest verdict, and make it a definite one rather than a menu of options. Is the counterparty condition doing real work, or am I just relabeling fortitude? Give me the three strongest objections and tell me whether any of them is fatal or whether it can be patched without giving up the entire distinction.
