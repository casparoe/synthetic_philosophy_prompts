I am a PhD student working in philosophical theology and metaphysics, and I recently sat in on an argument between two colleagues that neither would concede. I would like a careful, committed adjudication of it. The dispute was whether the Molinist doctrine of middle knowledge can do the explanatory work it is meant to do without covertly surrendering libertarian freedom. I will reconstruct the exchange as faithfully as I can.

The defender of Molinism began roughly this way. "Suppose God wants to create a world with libertarian-free creatures and also wants to know, prior to deciding which world to actualize, how any possible creature would freely act in any possible set of circumstances. Middle knowledge supplies this. God knows every true counterfactual of creaturely freedom: for any possible creature S and any complete circumstance C, if S were in C, S would freely do A. Since these truths are prior to the creative decree, God can actualize a world by placing particular creatures in circumstances where, He already knows, they will freely do the good. Placing S in C does not cause S to do A; the counterfactual says what S would do, not what S is caused to do, and S retains the power to do otherwise in C."

The objector replied: "The last sentence is exactly where it breaks. In a libertarian framework, what makes it true, before creation, that S would freely do A in C? It cannot be S's nature, motives, character, or the causal features of C, because then S's doing A would be entailed by those conditions, and that is no longer libertarian freedom. But if nothing makes it true, Molinism posits a stock of ungrounded contingent truths. Ungrounded contingencies cannot account for God's choice of the circumstances. So middle knowledge either collapses into covert determinism or into an explanatory vacuum."

The defender answered: "You are presupposing that every contingent truth needs a truthmaker of the sort you demand. That principle is far from obvious. You already accept some truths that are not true in virtue of some positive entity—true negative existentials, truths about what is absent, perhaps certain truths about what would have happened. A counterfactual of creaturely freedom can be a primitive, contingently true fact about an agent's agency. God's knowing it does not determine the act; it registers the act as the one S would perform."

The objector came back: "Negative existentials are a poor analogy, because the problematic cases here are not absences; they are positive truths about what a specific, not-yet-existing creature would do. And if the counterfactual does not flow from anything about S, then the connection between God's planning and S's eventual action is inexplicable. God actualizes C on the strength of 'S would do A in C'; when S is finally in C, S does A. On your primitivist picture, that agreement between the prevolitional counterfactual and the actual free choice is a standing coincidence or a miracle—not providence."

At that point the defender made a final move. "It is neither coincidence nor miracle, because the connection is logical rather than causal. The proposition that S would freely do A in C, together with the proposition that S is in C, entails that S freely does A; God can plan on an entailment without causing the outcome. As for grounding, I deny your demand in this case. A possible creature's counterfactual profile can belong to its individual essence, even if it is not reducible to any categorical property. That unreduced modal profile is what makes the counterfactuals true; accepting such profiles is a cost, but it is the cost of taking libertarian freedom seriously."

The objector's last answer was: "If the counterfactual profile is part of S's individual essence, then it is essential to S that S would do A in C. So in any world in which S exists and is in C—and therefore has S's essence—S's doing A is fixed by what S essentially is. That is still a prior condition that settles the choice, even if the fixing ground is a haecceity rather than a character trait. And if the essence alone does not settle it, the grounding problem returns. You have relocated the problem, not solved it."

They went around once more without progress. I would like a point-by-point verdict: which party is right about which specific claims, where each argument depends on a contestable premise, and whether either side's final position—or some sharpened version of it—survives the exchange. Please be committed in your answer: set out and defend a definite view of the matter, rather than merely cataloguing interpretive options.
