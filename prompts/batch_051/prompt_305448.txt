I've been rereading Descartes's Second Meditation with a study group, and we keep circling around the same problem. The standard summary of the Meditations says Descartes proved "I think, therefore I am," but the actual argument in the text seems more cautious than that, and I want to understand precisely how much it establishes. Here is the passage from John Veitch's translation of Meditations on First Philosophy, Second Meditation, that I keep coming back to:

But I had the persuasion that there was absolutely nothing in the world, that there was no sky and no earth, neither minds nor bodies; was I not, therefore, at the same time, persuaded that I did not exist? Far from it; I assuredly existed, since I was persuaded. But there is I know not what being, who is possessed at once of the highest power and the deepest cunning, who is constantly employing all his ingenuity in deceiving me. Doubtless, then, I exist, since I am deceived; and, let him deceive me as he may, he can never bring it about that I am nothing, so long as I shall be conscious that I am something. So that it must, in fine, be maintained, all things being maturely and carefully considered, that this proposition (pronunciatum) I am, I exist, is necessarily true each time it is expressed by me, or conceived in my mind.

Please make an argument for the following position: that, on the most defensible reading of this passage, what Descartes establishes is not the existence of a persisting self or a thinking substance, but only the truth of the proposition "I am, I exist" as it is actually performed or conceived, so that the proof gives no warrant for anything beyond the present thinking itself. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence of your response, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
