Question:

Explain the following passage from C. S. Peirce's 1878 article "How to Make Our Ideas Clear":

"A clear idea is defined as one which is so apprehended that it will be recognized wherever it is met with, and so that no other will be mistaken for it. If it fails of this clearness, it is said to be obscure."

Then, in your own words, explain whether William James's pragmatic account of truth can be defended against the objection Peirce is raising here. If it cannot, explain why not; if it can, explain the strongest form of the defense and where it succeeds.

Student answer:

Peirce is saying that clearness in the old logical sense is just a subjective feeling of familiarity: you know the idea when you see it and don't confuse it with others. His point is that this feeling can be mistaken, so it is not enough. James thinks a belief is true when it works, meaning it fits our experience and lets us make successful predictions and act successfully. Since James treats practical success as an external check on belief, he is not just relying on a subjective feeling of mastery. So James is not vulnerable to Peirce's objection, because James adds objective consequences: true beliefs work, and when they stop working, experience forces us to revise them. Peirce might reply that "works" is still too vague, but the passage only rejects mere familiarity, not practical consequences. James can be defended by saying that truth is a species of good: true beliefs are profitable to believe in the long run. Peirce's own later view, that truth is fated to be agreed upon by those who inquire long enough, similarly uses a future consensus as the mark of truth, so it is not clear Peirce can press the objection without undermining himself.
