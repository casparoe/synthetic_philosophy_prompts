In a reading group I belong to, we have spent the better part of a term going in circles on the question of whether famines are best understood as natural disasters or as political crimes, and the argument has now reached an impasse that I suspect is structural rather than a matter of missing evidence. The natural-disaster camp keeps returning to the Irish famine, where the potato blight was plainly a natural event, and to the Great Leap Forward, where drought and poor harvests coincided with the catastrophe; they suspect that the language of crime is a way of smuggling retrospective moral blame into a story that is mostly about ecological shock and bad luck. The political-crime camp keeps returning to Bengal in 1943, where the harvest was not dramatically below normal yet perhaps three million people died, and to the fact that food continued to be shipped out of Ireland while the poor starved; they insist the famines were made, not merely suffered, by grain procurement, export policy, rationing failures and administrative neglect. The unsettling thing is that both sides have read the same cases, neither disputes most of the other side's factual claims, and yet nobody has moved an inch in months.

My working hypothesis is that the dispute is really several disagreements stacked on top of one another: a verbal disagreement about where in a causal chain the word cause gets applied, an unresolved counterfactual question about whether mortality would have been comparable under even slightly different policy choices, and a normative disagreement about whether a disaster becomes a crime only through deliberate intent or also through negligence, callousness and structural policy failure. I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on the famines. Is my hypothesis too charitable, too tidy, or roughly right? What kind of disagreement is this, exactly, and what would actually count as progress given that diagnosis? Please write your answer in British English.
