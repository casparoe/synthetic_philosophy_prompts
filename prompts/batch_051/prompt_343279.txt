I am writing up a position on Buridan's-ass cases and would like a consistency audit before I present it to a seminar. My current view is a package of five commitments. (1) In a fully symmetric choice, with two identical bales and no relevant difference between them, a rational agent ought to pick one rather than starve; and picking the left bale is rational even though no reason favours it over the right. (2) Picking is an act-type distinct from choosing: choosing is selection guided by a reason that favours the selected option, whereas picking is deliberate selection among options the agent takes to be indifferent, and no account of rational agency that recognises only choosing can make sense of ties. (3) The boundary from tie to choice is razor-thin: as soon as any genuine asymmetry is introduced, however trivial — the left bale an inch nearer, or a single straw heavier — the agent is no longer picking but choosing, and has a decisive reason for the better option rather than a mere permission. (4) In a pure tie, a rational agent who can pick for free should never pay a positive cost merely to have the tie broken; paying for a tie-breaker is choosing a dominated option, since the outcome is a bale either way and only the paid route costs more. (5) I accept a guise-of-the-good constraint on action: no act is fully rational unless it is done for a good the agent pursues in so acting, though the agent need not articulate that good perfectly. Tell me which of these hang together and which do not: which pairs are in genuine tension, how severe each tension is, which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and what the cheapest revision would be to restore consistency. Finally, say where your audit is least secure — which argument or consideration would be most likely to change it, and why.
