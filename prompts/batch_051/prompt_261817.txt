Lately I've been reading about decision theory on my own, and one argument keeps appearing, sometimes called a money pump. It runs like this. Suppose my preferences cycle: I would take an apple over a banana, a banana over a cherry, and a cherry over an apple. I'm told that I am then like a currency exchange desk posting inconsistent rates between three currencies. An arbitrageur can trade around that desk's own posted rates — each transaction being one the desk itself invites — and drain the desk. Likewise, someone who knew my preferences could offer me a sequence of small trades over time, each of which I accept because I prefer what I'm receiving, until I return to my starting bundle minus the fees. The conclusion drawn is that cyclical preferences are irrational, because they make me exploitable in the way an inconsistent exchange desk is exploitable.

I'd like an audit of this analogy. Can you lay out what corresponds to what, say where the mapping breaks, distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the argument from those that don't, and identify what gets quietly imported from currency arbitrage into talk about preference? I'm especially unsure whether the analogy works given that a desk's rates are posted all at once, while my trades would happen at different moments — so it matters whether I'm being treated as one stable agent across time or as a sequence of preferences that could legitimately change. I'd also like to know whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether a better analogy captures whatever is genuinely wrong with cyclical preferences.
