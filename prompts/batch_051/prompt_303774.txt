A friend and I had a long argument last night about epistemic akrasia. She defended the strict anti-akrasia requirement: an agent who believes P while also believing that her total evidence does not support P is rationally defective, and the defect should be repaired by lowering confidence in P, normally by suspending judgment. I defended a rival idea we kept circling around, which I will call the independence constraint: higher-order evidence about the reliability of an episode of first-order reasoning has defeating force only insofar as it is independent of the first-order evidence it is being used to impugn; when the higher-order doubt merely re-uses or double-counts the very reasoning it indicts, the rational resolution can be to retain the first-order belief and discard the higher-order doubt instead.

I want a thought experiment built to order, for use in a paper on this dispute, in which the independence constraint and the strict anti-akrasia requirement issue opposite verdicts about the same ordinary-life case.

Specifications.

1. The case is ordinary: no demons, drugs, hypnosis, brain lesions, Cartesian scenarios, infallible oracles, or malfunctioning instruments. An ordinary, competent reasoner could find herself in it.
2. The agent forms an outright belief P through first-order reasoning that is in fact reliable and well-supported by her evidence.
3. She then acquires higher-order evidence that her reasoning on this occasion was unreliable. By her own lights, the higher-order evidence genuinely raises the probability that her reasoning went wrong.
4. The higher-order evidence is, in fact, misleading: because of the structural feature in question, it points away from P even though P is true and her first-order reasoning was sound.
5. The two principles come apart cleanly. The strict anti-akrasia requirement instructs her to reduce or abandon P. The independence constraint instructs her to retain P and revise or demote the higher-order doubt. These verdicts must be generated by the structure of the evidence, not stipulated.

Then do the check. Work through the following in prose:

- what P is, and what the first-order reasoning consists in;
- what the higher-order evidence is, and how she comes by it;
- why the higher-order evidence is genuine by her lights, despite being misleading;
- why the independence constraint treats it as double-counting the first-order evidence;
- the exact verdict each principle issues about P;
- which structural feature makes the two principles come apart, and what the case becomes if that feature is removed — does it collapse back into a case the strict requirement handles correctly?;
- the strongest reply a defender of the strict anti-akrasia requirement could give to stop the case from counting, whether that reply succeeds or fails, and why.

After the prose, append a JSON object with these fields, writing each value as a string:

- `scenario`: a one-sentence summary of the case;
- `p`: the first-order proposition the agent believes;
- `first_order_reasoning`: the method and evidence supporting P;
- `higher_order_evidence`: the evidence the agent acquires about her own reasoning;
- `double_counting_feature`: the structural feature the independence constraint exploits;
- `strict_anti_akrasia_verdict`: what the strict requirement tells the agent to do with P;
- `independence_constraint_verdict`: what the independence constraint tells the agent to do;
- `defender_reply`: the strongest reply available to the defender of the strict requirement;
- `case_without_feature`: what the case looks like once the structural feature is removed.
