I sit on my university's animal research ethics committee. Our policy contains the following clause: "Any research involving live vertebrate animals, or any other animal reasonably considered capable of experiencing pain or distress, requires prospective approval from this committee before the work may begin." The clause was drafted decades ago by a committee thinking in terms of mice, birds, and frogs, and its second phrase has never been tested here.

Two applications have arrived for the same meeting.

The first is from a behavioral ecologist who wants to present bumblebees with two types of artificial flowers, one of which delivers a brief, non-injurious heat pulse, in order to study avoidance learning. She does not claim bees cannot suffer; she asks the committee to reach a view about whether the clause's second phrase covers them.

The second is from a roboticist who has built a hexapod robot with no living tissue, whose control architecture reproduces several features of insect nociceptive processing: a damage-detection layer, a drive state that suppresses other behavior as damage signals rise, graded withdrawal and limb-favoring after injury, and recovery after repair. He wants to run a sequence of destructive manipulations — overheating actuators, cutting synthetic tendons, progressively damaging legs — and measure how the robot's task priorities change. He argues that since a robot is not an animal, the clause cannot reach him at all.

The drafters plainly did not have soft robotics or invertebrate sentience research in view. Walk me through what the clause requires of the committee for each application, taking each of the standard interpretive approaches in turn: the plain words of the text; the evident purpose of the clause; what the drafters would have said if asked; and how a reasonable researcher reading the policy today would understand it. For each approach, say whether it pushes toward approval, review, or rejection for the bee and for the robot, and note where the approaches diverge.

Then step away from the clause wording. Give me a principled way of ordering the three cases — bee, robot, and an ordinary thermostat — that does not assume a single sharp cut-off where sentience begins, and use that ordering to justify your actual recommendation for each application. Which of the two applications is the harder call for this committee, and what kind of evidence would most shift that verdict?
