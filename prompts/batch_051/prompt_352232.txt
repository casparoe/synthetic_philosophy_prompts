You are a philosopher working in bioethics, especially the ethics of reproduction and enhancement, and you are comfortable doing reflective equilibrium on concrete cases. Think slowly, explicitly consider several candidate principles and where each one fails, and then give your best answer.

I have been collecting my own verdicts about parents selecting their children's traits, and I want to know whether any single principle captures them. You may assume, as I do, that being brought into existence with a condition is not itself a harm to the child as long as the child's life is worth living.

My verdicts:

1. A couple both carry a mutation causing a disease that kills by age five after progressive pain and cognitive decline. They use IVF and preimplantation genetic diagnosis and implant only an embryo without the mutation. I think this is required, not merely permitted.

2. A couple screens out an embryo carrying a mutation that raises lifetime risk of a serious cancer from background to around 80%. I think this is permissible but not obligatory.

3. A culturally Deaf couple deliberately selects an embryo carrying a gene variant for congenital deafness, because they want a child who will be a native member of their signing community. I think this is wrong, even though I accept that many deaf people lead fully flourishing lives and I am not treating deafness as making a life bad.

4. A fertile couple uses IVF with genetic screening and selects the embryo predicted to have the highest educational attainment, at considerable expense. I think this is permissible but not obligatory, and I find it somewhat unsettling.

5. The same couple instead selects for a trivial cosmetic trait, blue eyes, other things equal. I think this is permissible but frivolous, not exactly wrong.

6. A couple with two sons uses sex selection to have a daughter because they want a balanced family. I think this is fine.

I am especially bothered by the contrast between 3 and 4: I judge selecting for a valued minority identity to be wrong while judging selection for a predictive advantage to be allowed, though both shape the child's future according to the parents' idea of a good life. I also cannot say why 1 is required while 2 is merely optional.

Is there a coherent principle that captures all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn? State the principle precisely, show how it handles each case, and be honest about whether it is just a disjunction dressed up as a principle. Then apply it to the case I cannot settle: parents of a child with a fatal blood disease select an embryo that is both disease-free and a tissue match for the sick older sibling, planning to bank the newborn's cord blood for transplant.
