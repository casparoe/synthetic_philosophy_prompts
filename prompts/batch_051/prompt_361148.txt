Consider this case. A midsize bank systematically launders money for a criminal organization. Marina is a compliance officer. Her job is to sign a weekly report certifying that flagged transactions have been reviewed; she knows the review is a sham and that her signed reports are what allow the laundering to continue smoothly. She never interacts with the criminal organization, and she has no power to change the bank's client list. If she refused to sign, she would be fired and replaced within the week by someone willing to sign, and the laundering would continue at exactly the same scale, through the same channels, on the same schedule. Assume these facts are not in dispute.

Now consider the following argument:

(1) A person is complicit in wrongdoing only if, had the person refused to perform the act in question, the wrongdoing would have gone differently in some respect: less likely, less severe, slower, easier to detect, and so on.
(2) Had Marina refused to sign, the wrongdoing would not have gone differently in any such respect: she would have been replaced, and the laundering would have continued unchanged.
(3) So Marina's signing does not satisfy the condition for complicity stated in (1).
(4) Therefore Marina is not complicit in the money laundering.

Is this argument valid? If it is not valid, exactly which step fails and why? If it is valid, then say which premise or premises you would reject, and give the strongest example you can in which someone is complicit in wrongdoing without making the kind of difference described in (1). It is tempting to notice that the argument would clear every functionary in the bank in the same way; is that a reason to reject the argument, or a conclusion we should learn to accept?
