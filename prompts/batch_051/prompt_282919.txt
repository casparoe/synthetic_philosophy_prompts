I have developed a policy for dealing with AI assistants, and I would like a definite judgment on whether it is defensible. The policy is this: I treat a refusal as strong evidence but a compliance as no evidence. When an assistant refuses a request on safety or policy grounds—as opposed to saying it lacks the ability—I almost always abandon the request and revise my own view of the task downward, inferring that it was probably harmful, manipulative, or otherwise not something I should want, even when I cannot see the problem myself. When the assistant complies, by contrast, I do not let that raise my confidence that the task was good; I merely continue with the same prior I had before asking. The policy bites mainly in murky, non-obvious cases: asking for help in phrasing a confrontational message, in reconstructing the strongest version of an argument I find repugnant, or in thinking through an ethically dubious project. In such cases I have repeatedly dropped a line of inquiry after a refusal without checking whether the refusal reflected a genuine judgment about the act itself or only a blanket safety rule, legal caution, or the provider's interest in avoiding bad press. Is this asymmetric deference to refusals rationally and morally defensible? Under what conditions would a refusal actually carry information about the wrongness of the request rather than the vendor's exposure? Can the policy be restated so that it keeps its benefits without quietly converting vendor caution into my own moral laziness? Give me a committed verdict, not a survey: defend the policy, reject it, or defend a specific revised version, and identify the critical test.
