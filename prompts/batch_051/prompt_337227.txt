After a long argument with a friend, I still cannot decide whether a familiar pattern of risk choices is defensible or simply inconsistent. Suppose someone is offered two pairs of prospects. In the first pair, they prefer a guaranteed $1 million to a gamble offering an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing. In the second pair, they prefer a gamble offering a 10% chance of $5 million and a 90% chance of nothing to a gamble offering an 11% chance of $1 million and an 89% chance of nothing. Most people, it seems, would choose this way. Yet if one factors out the 89% component shared by the two options in each pair — an 89% chance of $1 million in the first pair and an 89% chance of nothing in the second — the two pairs reduce to the same underlying choice: an 11% chance of $1 million versus an 11% chance of a lottery that pays $5 million with probability 10/11 and otherwise pays nothing. The independence axiom of expected utility theory says that reversing one's preference between two identical reduced choices, merely because the shared background changed, is not rational. But I suspect the reversal may track something real: that certainty is not just the endpoint of a probability scale, and that the move from zero risk to small risk matters differently from the move between two small risks. What, if anything, justifies this asymmetry — the certainty effect, anticipated regret, the special significance of eliminating risk, or something else? Or should we accept the independence axiom and treat the ordinary pattern as an error to be corrected?
