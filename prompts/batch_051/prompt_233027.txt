Then he said this is why Sidgwick helped found the Society for Psychical Research in 1882: he needed an afterlife to patch the hole in his ethics, so he spent years as a kind of undercover theologian running experiments on mediums and telepathy, and he let his need for the answer make him more credulous than he would ever have allowed his students to be.

Then I read a chunk of Sidgwick's inaugural address to the SPR, and he seemed to be saying something more deliberate. I don't have the exact words here, but roughly he says the question whether there is any spiritual world behind the physical order is the most important question we can ask, not because we already believe in it, but because our civilization has never settled it by evidence rather than tradition. That seems less desperate than my professor made him sound: someone who thought the stakes were very high but the methods had to be scientific. My professor also kept quoting the part where Sidgwick says the Society doesn't start by assuming the phenomena are supernatural, it just wants to find out what actually happens. That sounds cautious, not credulous.
