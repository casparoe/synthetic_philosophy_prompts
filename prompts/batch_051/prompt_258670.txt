A friend who has been reading an introduction to classical Chinese philosophy recently gave me the following summary of Gongsun Long's White Horse Dialogue. According to my friend, Gongsun Long argues that a white horse is not a horse. The argument begins from the observation that the word "horse" is the name for a shape, whereas the word "white" is the name for a color; since naming a color is not naming a shape, the expression "white horse" does not name what the expression "horse" names. Gongsun Long then points out that someone sent to look for a horse could bring back a bay or black horse, but someone sent to look for a white horse could not. My friend concluded that Gongsun Long is therefore denying that white horses fall under the kind horse — that white horses are not horses in the ordinary sense — and added that he must also hold that there are no white horses, since for a thing to be a white horse it would first have to be a horse. My friend said this is why the School of Names was accused of sophistry.

I am not sure the final step is fair. The key passage, as I have seen it rendered, is roughly this: someone asks whether a white horse can be not a horse; the answer is that it can. "Horse" is that by which the shape is named, and "white" is that by which the color is named; naming the color is not naming the shape; therefore a white horse is not a horse. Is my friend's summary an accurate account of what the dialogue is doing? Please separate what the text actually supports from common simplifications, say what a careful defender of Gongsun Long would object to, and assess whether my friend's conclusions about falling under a kind and about existence follow from the argument as presented.
