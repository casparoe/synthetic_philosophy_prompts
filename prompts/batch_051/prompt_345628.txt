I make my living playing poker professionally, and I have never studied philosophy or political theory; my whole vocabulary for uncertain outcomes comes from the tables: expected value, variance, bankroll management, sample size, game selection, runout, edge, tilt, and the difference between a good decision and a bad result. I recently read Machiavelli's The Prince, and the concepts that stayed with me were fortuna and virtù. As I understand them, fortuna is the element of human affairs that no one fully controls — the unpredictable river that floods, the change of circumstance that elevates one ruler and destroys another — while virtù is the quality that lets a person meet that uncertainty: foresight, adaptability, decisiveness, boldness, the willingness to build defences before the flood arrives and to act swiftly while conditions favour you. I found myself compulsively translating this into poker terms, and a lot of it seemed to land: fortuna as variance, virtù as the set of skills that keeps your edge positive and your decisions sound even when the runout punishes you. Restate Machiavelli's position on the relationship between fortuna and virtù in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report. I want to know which claims translate cleanly, which ones you had to bend or distort to make them fit, and which ones have no counterpart at all in how I think. In particular, is my shorthand — fortuna is variance, virtù is maintaining your edge — right, or does it quietly drop something Machiavelli is actually claiming? As you go, please keep the settled part of the record separate from the genuinely disputed part: where scholars or careful readers disagree about how to read him, do not present one side as if it were the consensus.
