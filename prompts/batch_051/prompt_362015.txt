I am a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy. I have been reading Russell's theory of descriptions because I am building a small natural-language front end that must translate definite descriptions—phrases like "the current user" or "the primary key of this table"—into first-order logic. As I understand the theory, a sentence of the form "The F is G" does not simply pick out an object and attribute G to it. Instead, it abbreviates three claims: something is F, nothing else is F, and that thing is G. Formally, "The F is G" expands to ∃x(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y = x) ∧ Gx).

This worked for the simple cases I tried. But when I added negation, I produced the following argument, and it seems to show that the analysis collapses as soon as a definite description meets a single negation operator.

(1) By Russell's rule, "The F is G" expands to ∃x(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y = x) ∧ Gx).

(2) By the ordinary behavior of negation, if a declarative sentence is false, then its fronted negation "It is not the case that the F is G" is true.

(3) When there is no F, the expansion in (1) is false, because its first conjunct—that some F exists—is false. So, on Russell's rule, the original sentence "The F is G" is false in that case.

(4) Therefore, by (2) and (3), "It is not the case that the F is G" is true whenever there is no F.

(5) But "The F is not G" is just the same assertion as "It is not the case that the F is G," with the negation written after the verb instead of in front. Since Russell's rule is supposed to be a translation rule for English, it should apply to this sentence in the ordinary way.

(6) Applying Russell's rule to "The F is not G," treating "not G" as the predicate, gives ∃x(Fx ∧ ∀y(Fy → y = x) ∧ ¬Gx).

(7) When there is no F, the conjunction in (6) is false, again because the first conjunct—that some F exists—is false.

(8) So the same assertion comes out true by (4) and false by (7).

(9) A single declarative claim cannot be both true and false.

(10) Therefore Russell's rule cannot handle negated descriptions, and the theory is broken.

I cannot accept the conclusion: the theory is supposed to do far too much work to fall apart over something this simple, so the argument must go wrong somewhere. I would most like to keep the ordinary semantics of negation in (2), but I am not sure whether holding onto it forces me to give up Russell's analysis of descriptions entirely, or only some smaller assumption I have made along the way. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
