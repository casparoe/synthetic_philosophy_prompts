A colleague has been pressing the following line on me, and I am not sure how to answer. He argues that the distinction between legitimate persuasion and manipulation cannot be drawn where most philosophers want to draw it.

(1) To manipulate someone is not to lie to them or to bypass their rational faculties outright; it is to move them by selecting and shaping the presentation of considerations rather than by the evidential weight of those considerations alone.
(2) The persuasive force a consideration has for a given audience is never presentation-independent: the same data can carry or lose force depending on order, framing, salience, and whether it is made vivid or abstract.
(3) Therefore any speaker, simply by deciding which considerations to mention and how concretely to put them, inevitably influences which of them will move the audience.
(4) What happens through such presentational choices happens through the shaping of presentation rather than through evidential weight alone.
(5) So every instance of persuading another person is an instance of manipulation in the sense of (1); the only thing "legitimate persuasion" names is manipulation whose presentational slant we endorse.

I resist (5), but I cannot tell whether the trouble is already in (1), in the slide from (2) to (3), or in (4). Is the argument valid? If so, is the conclusion merely an unwelcome truth? If not, say exactly which step fails, and give the corrected version of the claim that a defender of legitimate persuasion should hold.
