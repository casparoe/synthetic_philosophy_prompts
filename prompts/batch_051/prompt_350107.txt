Suppose that, beginning in 2040, a cheap, safe, and widely available drug reliably removes the felt aversiveness of effort. A person who takes it can exert themselves just as long and just as skillfully as before, but no longer experiences trying as draining, aversive, or something to be put off. The drug does not create motivation, interest, or talent; it does not remove physical pain, the need for sleep, or the objective time a task takes; and it does not make boring tasks interesting. It simply eliminates the experiential cost of exertion once a person has decided to act.

Please give me your best argued prediction of how this intervention plays out over the following three decades in each of these domains:

(a) education and the signaling value of credentials;
(b) competitive sport, music, and other performance domains;
(c) career sorting, workplace organization, and who ends up in demanding positions;
(d) everyday practices of praise, blame, admiration, and judgments of desert.

I am less interested in a yes-or-no verdict on whether effort is "really" valuable than in the chain of reasoning. For each predicted change, identify the mechanism by which the change occurs, the actors whose incentives shift, and the constraints that slow or dampen the change. Be explicit about which predictions you hold with high confidence, which are guesses, and what empirical assumption about human motivation each guess depends on. Conclude by saying what this counterfactual suggests about how much of the value we currently attach to striving is intrinsic to effort itself and how much is a response to its costliness. Answer in American English.
