My philosophy department finally got my two advisors to do an adversarial collaboration on moral testimony, and I got stuck drafting the document. One of them holds that you can gain moral knowledge from what other people tell you in more or less the same way you can learn ordinary empirical facts by testimony; the other holds that there's something epistemically defective about forming your moral beliefs on someone else's say-so — that it doesn't yield moral understanding even when you end up with the right view. They've agreed that the joint statement should have three parts. First, a numbered section of claims both can sign: about how moral testimony actually operates, about cases they share, about moral error and disagreement, whatever substantive common ground there really is, without being vacuous. Second, a disagreements table where each position is stated precisely, in wording its own side would accept. Third, a cruxes section: the two or three sub-questions such that, if settled, at least one of them would have to move, and for each crux the case, argument, or experiment both agree bears on it, with each person's prediction recorded in advance. I suspect one crux is whether a stipulatively reliable moral advisor makes a difference, and another is whether the pessimist's complaint is really epistemic or just about autonomy. Flag anything you think is purely verbal. American English, please.
