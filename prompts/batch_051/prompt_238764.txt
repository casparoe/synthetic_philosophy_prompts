I have no formal background in philosophy or history, but I have worked out, mostly through arguments with a friend, a rule for what I will count as a real explanation of a past event. My rule is this: an account counts as an explanation only if it gives me a general pattern, the kind of claim that says when conditions like these hold, events like that tend to follow. If an account only traces a particular chain of decisions, coincidences, and mistakes, I conclude that it is a story rather than an explanation.

I put the rule to work recently when a friend explained the start of the First World War as the product of diplomatic miscommunication, misread signals, and rigid mobilization timetables running into one another. He walked through the sequence carefully, but I told him that, however accurate his reconstruction was, he had not explained the outbreak until he supplied the general pattern connecting miscommunication or rigid mobilization to war. He answered that some historical events are singular, and that a narrative can make them intelligible without making them predictable.

I have noticed, though, that I only enforce this rule for large-scale historical events. If someone asks me why my cousin left her job, the particular slights, stresses, and opportunities are enough for me; I do not demand the general law of career changes. I am not sure this asymmetry is honest.

What I would like to know is whether my rule, especially with that asymmetry, is defensible or whether it is just a bias dressed up as a principle. When should a narrative that cites no general pattern be accepted as genuinely explanatory, and would that exception also apply to history? Could my rule be restated so that it keeps whatever it is good at without excluding singular historical explanations that genuinely explain? Please treat this as a policy I use under real constraints, not as an abstract logical test, and please answer in American English.
