I serve on the curriculum committee of a small classical secondary school whose mission promises parents a character education grounded in the study of exemplary lives. At our next meeting we vote on a proposed ninth-grade ethics unit built entirely around such biographies. The designers propose a term spent reading selectively edited lives of scientists, statesmen, and local figures of upright character, each organized around moments of difficult moral choice. They call the approach Plutarchan: students are to acquire moral concepts and standards of excellence by imitating admirable lives, and for that reason the materials omit or heavily downplay the subjects' documented moral failures — the designers argue that dwelling on vice at this age teaches cynicism, and that the moral pattern must be kept clear if it is to shape character.

Two members of the committee object that the unit as designed is hagiography rather than education: it presents unrealistically pure figures, collapses moral learning into hero-admiration, and leaves students with ideals that shatter when they later encounter the historical record. A third replies that some idealization is unavoidable in moral formation — that one cannot begin deliberation without first internalizing concrete exemplars, and that a biography that foregrounds its subject's weaknesses simply ceases to be a biography of an exemplar. I have been asked to circulate a short decision memo before the meeting recommending that we adopt the unit, reject it, or adopt it with specified revisions, together with criteria the committee can use to distinguish an edifying moral biography from a hagiographic one. Please draft that memo, and make sure the recommendation honestly engages the strongest consideration on the side you recommend against.
