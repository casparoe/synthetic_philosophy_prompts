I'm a PhD student in behavioral public policy, and I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about paternalism and nudging. The problem is my gut gives a different answer depending on which example I reach for, and at this point I'm not sure I have a view so much as a pile of cases. Going one by one:

- Auto-enrolling employees in a 401(k) unless they opt out: fine, genuinely good.
- A cafeteria putting fruit at eye level and candy below the counter: fine.
- Calorie counts on chain restaurant menus: fine.
- Graphic images of diseased lungs on cigarette packs: fine.
- A city tax of 1.5 cents an ounce on sugary drinks: fine.
- A city ban on selling sodas bigger than 16 ounces: not fine, that's too much.
- Seatbelt laws with real fines: fine.
- A subscription that auto-renews and only mentions it in the fine print: not fine, that feels like a trap.
- A tax authority letter saying "9 out of 10 people in your postcode pay their taxes on time": fine, maybe even clever.
- A government-mandated warning on soda cans that sugary drinks contribute to obesity and diabetes: not fine, which annoys me becuase I was just fine with the cigarette photos.

The best one-liner I have is: it's okay to steer people or tell them things, but not to take options away. Except that doesn't fit seatbelt laws, and it gives me no help on why the 401(k) default feels fine and the auto-renew subscription feels like a scam when they're the same mechanism.

So my question is whether there's an actual principle hiding in these verdicts, or whether I have to give some of them up. If a single principle can get most of them, great; if not, I'll take a small set of candidates. Either way, I want to know exactly what each clause is buying me, which of my verdicts it won't accommodate in the end, and what it says about the two cases I keep flipping on: requiring calorie counts per serving on beer and wine labels, and making checkout screens default to a one-time purchase rather than "subscribe and save."
