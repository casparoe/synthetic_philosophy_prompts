I'm a sourcing director at a mid-size apparel company. Every quarter my team and I run a supplier review on about thirty cut-and-sew factories across Bangladesh, Vietnam, and Honduras. For each one I have to sign off on whether we renew, keep buying but demand changes, or pull out. Right now the rule of thumb is basically: if the factory meets local law and our code of conduct, keep it; if not, put it on a corrective action plan. Its starting to feel thin, because I keep hitting the same kind of case: the factory is legal, the workers consent (nobody's chained to the floor), and the job is genuinely better than what they'd have otherwise — better than subsistence farming or a worse factory — but the deal still feels wrong because the gains are split so unevenly and they're choosing between bad options. I've heard this called mutually beneficial exploitation: a transaction can be voluntary and make somebody better off, and still be exploitative. A lot of people in my industry push back that if the worker is better off than her next-best option and agreed to it, we aren't harming her by dealing with her, and refusing to buy would just take that better option away.

Can you write me a decision procedure my team can actually run in about four hours per factory, grounded in whatever the philosophical debate on mutually beneficial exploitation actually supports, that tells us whether to keep buying, exit, or keep buying while remediating specific things? I want the questions in the order to ask them, what counts as a good enough answer to move on, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand off to a person because it's a value call rather than a fact call. Constraints: my compliance officer is smart but has no philosophy background, worker interviews are sometimes unavailable or unreliable, we can't unilaterally raise a supplier's wages without a price negotiation, and the CFO and legal counsel both need to be able to see exactly why we chose what we chose.

Then run the procedure on three factories. (1) Factory A pays 20% above the local garment average but gets workers to accept 72-hour weeks by paying overtime in arrears every three months, so quitting means forfeiting pay; other nearby factories are hiring, so exit wouldn't be catastrophic. (2) Factory B pays exactly the legal minimum, well below a living wage, conditions are safe and not coercive, and workers say they prefer it to the alternatives. If we exit, many of them move to informal work that's worse. (3) Factory C is close to a model: living wage, real consent, safe — but the price we pay is set by a market where we and four other big buyers have most of the leverage, so we capture nearly all the surplus our orders create. Make sure at least one case comes out with an answer we won't like, and say honestly what the procedure can't settle.
