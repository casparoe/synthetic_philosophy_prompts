I am a retired natural scientist—most of my working life was spent in physics—and I have recently been reading Spinoza’s Ethics carefully for the first time. In Part I, Spinoza defines substance as follows: “By substance, I mean that which is in itself, and is conceived through itself: in other words, that of which a conception can be formed independently of any other conception.” A few propositions later he argues: “There cannot exist in the universe two or more substances having the same nature or attribute.”

I find myself taking this argument seriously, and I am tempted by the conclusion that there is exactly one substance and that ordinary finite things—particles, organisms, minds—are modes of that single substance, not independent beings in their own right. I have also encountered contemporary priority monism, which seems to preserve some of this picture without all of Spinoza’s apparatus.

I want an honest map of what would have to be true for the Spinozist position, or its priority-monist descendant, to be false. Which of the argument’s assumptions could fail? What would the world have to be like for each failure to be actual, and which of these escape routes are live in present-day metaphysics rather than merely logically possible? What would a pluralist have to show: a decisive formal result, an explanatory failure, an unacceptable consequence, a case the monist cannot describe, or something else? I would also like you to distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be awkward, and to say what defenders of the position would themselves concede counts against it. Do not end on a merely non-committal survey; if you think the position is well insulated, say so and say whether that insulation is a problem. Please end your answer with a single sentence beginning “Summary:” that states your overall assessment in one sentence.
