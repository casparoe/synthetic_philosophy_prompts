At a recent seminar on the philosophy of history, I listened to two colleagues dispute whether the familiar proverb for want of a nail licenses genuine causal claims about history. I reconstructed their exchange from my notes; the wording is mine, but I have tried to keep the arguments faithful.

The first colleague maintained that the proverb is a sound piece of causal reasoning. Holding fixed the campaign—the horse, the rider, the message, the reserves—the missing nail can be a necessary condition for the horse going lame, the message failing to arrive, and the battle being lost. Each link in the chain can be a genuine cause in the circumstances. That we would not ordinarily single out a nail shows only that explanatory salience is interest-relative; it does not show that the causal contribution is unreal.

The second replied that the proverb is a retrospective narrative illusion. In any such sequence there will be hundreds of similarly small events that led nowhere, and only after the outcome is known do we trace one chain back and call it decisive. The first's claim rests on a counterfactual—had the nail held, the kingdom would have stood—but in a chaotic historical process that counterfactual is wildly divergent, and documentary evidence cannot tell us what would have happened. The claim is therefore unfalsifiable.

The first answered that unpredictability is not acausality. Chaotic systems are deterministic, and sensitive dependence on initial conditions is part of their causal structure, not evidence against causal structure. To be unable to predict the outcome from the state of the nail does not show that the nail made no difference. Moreover, the local links—shoe, horse, rider—can be tested by controlled variation in the ordinary way, which is more than bare narrative.

The second answered that controlled variation tests the local links but not the global claim that the kingdom fell because of the nail, and it is the global claim that carries the proverb's interest. There is also a selection effect: we notice small causes followed by large effects and forget equally small causes followed by none, so the inference from the chain's having occurred to the nail's having been decisive is an artefact of hindsight.

The first rejoined that the selection effect vitiates only a frequency estimate—how often small causes make large differences—not the judgement that in this particular case the chain ran through the nail to the defeat. To deny that judgement, one would have to show that the later links were overdetermined, or that the message would certainly have been replaced.

The second rejoined that the onus lies the other way. The first needs a criterion for distinguishing the nail from the innumerable background conditions without which the defeat would likewise not have occurred—without presupposing the very narrative the proverb recounts.

Neither would concede, and I did not press them. I would be grateful for a careful adjudication. Who is right about which specific claims? Where does each misstate the other's position, and what are both missing? In particular, can an interest-relative or counterfactual account distinguish the nail from the mere background without becoming vacuous?
