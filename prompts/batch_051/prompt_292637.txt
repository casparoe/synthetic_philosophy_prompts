So I'm taking a course on philosophical method right now, and we've been doing reflective equilibrium and what role intuitions are supposed to play in arguments. There's an asymmetry in how the seminar treats objections that's been bugging me, and I'd like an essay that takes it seriously.

Here's the asymmetry. If someone proposes a substantive moral principle—say, an act is wrong exactly when it fails to maximize total well-being—then one counterexample case (the transplant surgeon who could kill one healthy person to save five) is treated as close to fatal. Nobody says, 'well, it's just one intuition; the principle is still the simplest, best theory.' We expect the principle to be revised or abandoned. But if someone reports a set of case judgments that violate transitivity—say, across three possible lives they judge A better than B, B better than C, and yet C better than A—noone concludes that transitivity has been refuted by the cases. Everyone assumes that one of the pairwise judgments is mistaken and starts looking for a confound or a slip. So case intuitions are allowed to overturn substantive principles, but formal constraints like transitivity are not allowed to be overturned by case intuitions, even though both are general claims that earn their keep partly by fitting what we find intuitive.

Write an essay on whether that asymmetry is justified. Give the strongest candidate justification you can, and test it against nearby cases—for instance, a simple but substantive principle like 'only pleasure is intrinsically good,' or a set of intransitive aesthetic judgments. In the end, say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up, and justify the verdict.
