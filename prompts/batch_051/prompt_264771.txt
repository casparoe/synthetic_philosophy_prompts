Here's one I keep meaning to use in my metaphysics-of-science seminar. Take the mid-century fight over whether cigarette smoking causes lung cancer. The quick story: Doll and Hill (and others) found a strong, consistent association in observational studies; Fisher kept insisting this wasn't enough, since the correlation could in principle be explained by a constitutional confound—say a gene that predisposes you both to smoke and to get lung cancer—so without randomization you haven't shown causation; and the 1964 Surgeon General's report ended up defending the causal conclusion by stacking a whole body of epidemiological and biological considerations rather than pointing at one decisive experiment. Dont relitigate the medicine; I know it and I'm not asking for that. What I want is to run the episode through two theories of causation. On the counterfactual approach (roughly: c causes e when e would not have happened if c hadn't, or would at least have been much less likely), and on the interventionist approach (roughly: X causes Y when there is some possible intervention on X that would change Y, with the rest of the causal network held fixed). First direction, theory to case: what are the variables, what would an intervention on 'smoking' even have to be, and where exactly do the two theories locate Fisher's objection—is it a problem about which counterfactuals are true, or about whether the relevant counterfactual is testable at all? What would each theory have told Fisher and Doll and Hill they needed to establish? Second direction, case to theory: does Fisher's challenge show that counterfactual views are hostage to a similarity metric across worlds (how close is the world where this smoker's genes come apart from this smoker's cancer?), or just that observational evidence underdetermines the counterfactual? And does interventionism's talk of 'possible interventions' handle the fact that we can't randomize people to a pack a day, or does it quietly build in more metaphysics than it admits? Finally, I want a verdict on whether the whole fight partly rested on sliding between evidence-for-causation and what-causation-is—or whether a real case like this shows those two can't be kept apart.
