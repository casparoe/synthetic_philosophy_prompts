Here's something I've been turning over. There's a whole set of everyday rules about who we owe thanks to, and they pull in different directions. If a friend gives me a birthday present I'll never use, I'm expected to write a thank-you note; skipping it reads as rude, even though I'm no better off than if the gift had never arrived. But if I get the same benefit from pure luck — I find the money on the street, or a gust of wind blows a paper I needed back into my hand — nobody thinks I owe thanks to anyone. A stranger who accidentally does me a favor gets a quick 'thanks' and nothing owed; a friend who tries hard to help but fails gets real gratitude, because its the thought that counts. Effort even seems to outweigh size: a ten-dollar loan from someone who has almost nothing earns more gratitude than a car from someone who wouldn't miss it. And a debt of thanks can outlive the benefactor, while a lucky break creates no debt at all. So what does this practice presuppose gratitude actually is — a response to someone's intentions or sacrifice rather than to benefits themselves? Is that picture coherent, or is 'gratitude' just sloppily covering two or three different things, the way 'luck' does? And what would our thanking norms have to change to if we thought gratitude was about the benefits you recieve, wherever they come from — would we owe it to the weather, to machines, to the dead? Please write the answer in British English.
