I work as a software engineer and tend to think about legal rules the way I think about system specifications, so I would like a mechanism analyzed rather than a general debate. Suppose a jurisdiction introduces the following rule for whole-brain emulation. Before undergoing a destructive scan, a person may file a directive stating that, at the moment of the scan, their legal personhood and assets are to pass to at most one emulation produced by that scan. A court-appointed fidelity auditor then compares each candidate emulation against the scanned person's pre-scan baseline on a large battery of memory probes, interviews, and reasoning tasks, producing a numerical fidelity score. The directive assigns the personhood and assets to the highest-scoring candidate above a fixed threshold; if no candidate clears the threshold, the estate and legal rights are treated as if the person died with no qualifying successor and pass to the ordinary heirs. Please analyze how this institution would actually behave. In particular, what incentives does it create for people deciding whether and when to be scanned, for emulation providers who control the scan and the candidate set, for heirs, and for the emulations themselves; how would each party game the rule; does the auditor's fidelity score track personal identity or merely something correlated with it; and are the predictable failure modes the kind that small amendments could fix, or do they point to a deeper problem with routing identity through such a mechanism?
