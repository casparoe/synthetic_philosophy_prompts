I've been trying to make sense of the 2024 decision where the geologists declined to make the Anthropocene an official epoch, and I keep coming back to Kuhn's idea of paradigm shifts, though I'm not sure it actually fits. My rough understanding of what happened: a working group spent well over a decade doing what sure looks like normal-scientific work in Kuhn's sense — assembling evidence, choosing a type section, making the technical case — and settled on a lake in Ontario where a thin layer of fallout plutonium from mid-century nuclear tests would serve as the golden spike, with the epoch beginning around 1950. Then the stratigraphic bodies that actually govern the geologic time scale voted the proposal down. The opposition was partly that an interval only a few decades old, made by one species's activity, doesn't belong in the same classification scheme as the Cambrian or the Pleistocene, and partly a fight about whether the voting process itself followed the rules.

I'd like a careful Kuhnian analysis of this episode, not a general essay about paradigms. Who is the paradigm-holder here — the stratigraphers defending the time scale, the working group, the broader Anthropocene research community outside geology? Was the rejection an instance of normal science refusing an anomaly, a failed revolution, or something the framework doesn't have language for? And where does the machinery strain: is there real incommensurability between the stratigraphic way of dividing time (material breaks in rock, deep time) and the Anthropocene as an event-scale, human-historical concept, or is that overreading a mundane bureacratic dispute? Then run it the other direction and say what the episode shows about Kuhn — whether it confirms his story about how disciplines resist novelty, or exposes that his model was built for cases that don't look like this one.
