I've been going back and forth on a twist on the Ellsberg paradox and I want to see how the standard analyses actually cash out when you change one detail.

The setup I'm starting from is the two-urn version: urn A has 50 red balls and 50 black balls. Urn B has 100 balls in an unknown mix—could be anything from all red to all black. You pick a color and an urn, a ball gets drawn, and you win $100 if the ball matches your color. The usual finding is that people prefer A over B when betting on red, and also prefer A over B when betting on black, which an single-prior subjective expected utility maximizer can't do, while maximin expected utility with a set of priors over B's composition can do it.

Now vary the setup this way. Instead of B being some opaque urn with no story, B gets filled by someone flipping a fair coin 100 times: heads, add a red ball; tails, add a black ball; then shake the urn. You don't see the flips, but you know the whole procedure and you know the coin is fair. Does the ambiguity aversion survive? On one hand you still don't know how many red balls are in B. On the other, a Bayesian who reduces compound lotteries should now be indifferent between A and B for either color, because drawing from B is a Binomial(100, 0.5) mixture over urn compositions and the expected fraction of red is 0.5. Would a maximin expected utility agent, or just the person with the usual Ellsberg preferences, still avoid B once they know the generating process? I keep getting confused about whether there's a principled difference between unknown because nobody told you and unknown because the flips weren't shown to you but came from a known fair process. If there is a difference, what exactly makes the original urn B ambiguous in a way the fair-coin urn isn't? If there isn't, why did we ever treat unknown composition as different from known chance?

Then as a smaller follow-up: suppose the coin isn't known to be fair, and the person flipping can pick any bias you don't know. Does the paradox come right back, and do the standard decision rules part ways again about A versus B?
