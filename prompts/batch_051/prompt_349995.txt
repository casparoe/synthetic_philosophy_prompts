I'm a philosophy professor (ethics and social epistemology) and I've been kicking around a concept I call epistemic training wheels. The claim is that Santa, the tooth fairy, and similar coordinated childhood falsehoods aren't just tolerated lies; they're a scaffold. Adults don't just tell a child something false—they converge on a whole false world, maintain it for years, and then permit or gently engineer its discovery. So the real content of Santa isn't the proposition that a man in a red suit comes down the chimney. It's the structured experience of finding out that the people you trust most, all of them together, presented a false world to you, and that this discovery doesn't make love or the rest of your beliefs collapse. I want to say that's a distinct epistemic skill: revising on your own evidence without sliding into global distrust. It can't be taught by just telling a child that adults sometimes lie; you have to let them fall, but with padding down—hence training wheels. This also seems to explain why a practice everyone knows is false keeps getting rebuilt: the point was never credulity but the orchestrated discovery, and each generation recreates it because the discovery worked on them.
