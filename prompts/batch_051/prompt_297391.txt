I'm teaching a smallish grad seminar that's half philosophy-of-action people and half computer-science types, and I want to run a session that makes a pretty slippery idea feel inevitable: program equilibrium. The basic move, as I understand it, is this. In an ordinary one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, defect strictly dominates, so textbook game theory says mutual cooperation can't be a stable outcome. But suppose the "moves" are decision rules or programs that can refer to the other side's program before they're run. Then mutual cooperation can be an equilibrium, because a rule that says "cooperate iff the other rule cooperates" punishes defecting programs by defecting back at them, and a would-be defector can't collect the sucker payoff by hiding its rule. I want the students to run into this through what happens in the room, not through me explaining it up front, so the name shouldn't be mentioned until later in the debrief.

The class is about 18 people, we have one 90-minute session, and it's just a seminar room with no special equipment. Please design a runnable game or activity that starts from an ordinary PD round — some students will defect and the usual justifications will come out ("can't affect what they do," "one-shot, no reputation") — then moves to a round where participants submit decision rules that may condition on the other side's rule, and the rules are actually read, matched, and executed. Payoffs should be real enough to matter: rankings, bragging rights, some small token, but no actual money. Give me the exact rules, materials, grouping, and timing, plus a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the formal idea: what changes between choosing an act and choosing a procedure, why "defect" stops being obviously best, where the equilibrium sits, and what the "you had to commit to a readable rule" assumption is doing. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm especially worried about rules that don't terminate or go in circles because two rules keep referencing each other, and about students writing vague rules that can't be unambiguously executed. And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: I suspect the real friction is that actual agents don't hand each other their source code, and the activity won't touch the harder issues about self-modification or opaque agents. I'd like people to leave seeing program equilibrium as a live idea rather than a weird formal trick.
