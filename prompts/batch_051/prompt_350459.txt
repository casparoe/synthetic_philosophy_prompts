The standard list I was taught for what makes something a state has four items: a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states.

Now take the East India Company around 1800. It began as a trading corporation chartered by the English crown, owned by shareholders and run by a board of directors they elected, and its whole point was to make money for them. But by 1800 it governed enormous territories in India. It collected taxes, ran civil and criminal courts, minted coin, kept an army larger than most European states had, signed treaties, fought wars, and did diplomacy with Indian rulers and other empires. It ruled millions of people who had no vote for its board and who were sort of its subjects and sort of its market at the same time. Yet it also wasnt fully sovereign: Parliament could revise or revoke its charter, London regulated it more and more, and its original title in Bengal came from the Mughal emperor granting it the right to collect revenue, not from outright ownership of the land. Eventually Parliament just took its governmental functions away and ran them directly.

So, does the East India Company around 1800 count as a state? Work through the four criteria honestly. Where the answer is no, say whether the criteria are doing their job or whether they're missing something (a ban on being organized for profit, a requirement of recognition by other states, a requirement of not being subordinate to another body, or something else). And if the answer is yes, then what exactly is the difference between a state and a corporation — is it just scale, or purpose, or the fact that we've agreed not to call one of them a state?
