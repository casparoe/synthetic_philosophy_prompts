I have been trying to reconstruct a line of thought that came up in connection with lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and the more carefully I reconstruct it the more it seems to show that the view collapses into the unmodified negative utilitarianism it was meant to replace. Since I find that conclusion hard to accept, I assume the argument has gone wrong somewhere, but I cannot locate the failure.

(1) If two kinds of value are commensurable, they can be placed on a single scale with finite units, and on such a scale any finite burden can be made up by a sufficiently large finite benefit. So if pain and pleasure are commensurable, then for any finite episode of pain there is some finite quantity of pleasure such that the combined state is at least as good as a neutral alternative containing neither.

(2) But there are pains no finite quantity of mild pleasure could offset. Consider an hour of continuous, high-intensity pain of the kind the threshold is supposed to mark. Any state consisting of that pain together with a duration of mild, pleasant background enjoyment still seems clearly worse than a state with neither the pain nor the pleasure.

(3) By (1) and (2), such pain is not commensurable with mild pleasure. It has lexical priority over it: no amount of below-threshold pleasure can outweigh it. Call the intensities at which this priority begins the threshold.

(4) The trouble is that the reasoning for (2) does not appear to use any feature particular to the threshold. Take a pain just below any proposed threshold and run the same test: no duration of mild enjoyment seems enough to make undergoing it worth choosing, either. If (1) and (2) suffice to establish lexical priority for the first pain, the same premises establish it for this one.

(5) Repeating the move walks the threshold down the intensity scale until every pain, no matter how slight, turns out to be lexically prior to all mild pleasure. The threshold then either has no rationale or must be set at zero.

(6) If the threshold is set at zero, no amount of pleasure can ever compensate for even the mildest pain. On the resulting lexicographic ordering, any world containing a pinprick is worse than a world without it, however much pleasure the first world also contains. That is exactly the kind of unqualified verdict — up to and including the conclusion that a world with no felt pain at all is preferable to one containing any — that the threshold was introduced to avoid.

(7) So the view either rests on an arbitrary line or collapses into the position it was designed to replace. I most want to keep (2): the refusal to trade agony for mild pleasures seems to be the entire intuitive basis of the view. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to give up, and what sort of view is left standing?
