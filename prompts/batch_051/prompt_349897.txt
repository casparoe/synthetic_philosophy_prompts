I've agreed to lead a two-hour discussion for a group of working scientists — no formal philosophy, but professionally committed to truth as an ideal — on one claim: a value can present itself to the person who holds it as intrinsic, as needing no further justification, while in fact functioning instrumentally, its grip tracking the goods it delivers rather than any terminus of justification. I want to build the session around three cases — truth-seeking, tradition, and purity — with truth-seeking doing most of the work. Plan the session for me, not as a script but as an explanatory architecture: what distinction has to be in place before the claim is even intelligible to this group; what order the three cases should go in, what each case is doing in the sequence, and what depends on what; which single example should carry the session, and what features it must have — and must lack — if the claim is to survive contact with this audience's instincts. Map the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they are likely to arise, and say at which point in the sequence each should be headed off rather than answered on the spot. Tell me what should be deliberately left out at this level, and what would count at the end of the session as evidence that they have grasped the distinction rather than merely learned to call things 'instrumental' when they stop liking them.
