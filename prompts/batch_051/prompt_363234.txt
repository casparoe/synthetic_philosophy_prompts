Sort of weird one, but I keep noticing it and I dont know whether it has a name.

When people do moral arithmetic—comparing how bad two things are, or whether some amount of one thing is worth some amount of another—they switch between operations without signalling which one they're using. My sister and I argued about whether it's worse for one person to have a mild headache for a hundred days or a hundred people to have that same headache for one day; we both just multiplied severity by duration by number of people and treated equal products as equal. Minutes later at the same table, someone said they'd never let one person be tortured for a year, no matter how many mild headaches it prevented. That's not multiplying; it's a barrier being put down before the arithmetic starts. And separately, a friend who works on animal minds told me the value of a conscious moment is something like the amount of consciousness in it multiplied by how good it feels, so a small pain in a highly conscious creature counts more than the same pain in a barely conscious one, and zero consciousness gives zero value even if the outward behaviour looks identical. Yet we also happily add across people—total welfare is just everyone's added up—but we'd never add across kinds of harm, as if one humiliation plus one broken leg made two of a single currency.

So the thing I'm after is this patchwork of operations: sometimes you multiply, sometimes you sum, sometimes you refuse arithmetic altogether. Does it have a name? Is it just that some values can't be put on the same scale, or is that only one corner of it? What are the nearest concepts, and where exactly are the edges—for instance, how would you tell a genuine barrier from a multiplication with an enormous but finite number hidden inside it? A bare label won't settle it for me; I care about the carving.

Bottom line first, please: in the first sentence tell me whether this thing has a name, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating my question.
