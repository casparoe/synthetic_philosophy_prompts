I am preparing a seventy-five-minute seminar for advanced undergraduates in philosophy who have read the *Mencius* on the four sprouts but know nothing about Korean Neo-Confucianism. The topic is the Four–Seven debate: the dispute between Yi Hwang (Toegye) and Ki Tae-sŭng (Kobong) over whether the moral emotions—the Four Beginnings—and the ordinary emotions—the Seven Emotions—have different sources. I want you to produce a teaching plan, not a lecture text.

The central idea I want them to take away is that the debate is not a quarrel about two lists of emotions, but about how to hold two claims together. First, morally good responses are not simply one more kind of psychological event. Second, human feeling is nevertheless a continuous, mixed, and often unruly life. Toegye secures the first claim by giving the Four Beginnings a different metaphysical source, *li*, while Ki Tae-sŭng presses the second by asking what then happens to actual, embodied moral emotions, which always seem to involve *ki*. Plan the explanatory arc: what conceptual machinery—*li*, *ki*, the notion of a source, and the difference between classifying emotions and explaining their genesis—must be in place before the disagreement can even be formulated. Say in what order the pieces should be introduced so that both positions look motivated rather than arbitrary, and give the single carrying example you would use to keep the debate concrete throughout.

Also anticipate the misconceptions this audience will bring: that *li* and *ki* are simply good and bad forces, that Toegye is a dualist separating reason from emotion, that the debate is really about which list of emotions is more complete, and any others you think likely. For each misconception, state where in the sequence it should be headed off. Finally, describe the check for understanding that would show me they have grasped why the debate matters, rather than merely being able to restate the two positions. Set out explicitly what you would leave out or openly refuse to cover at this level, and why leaving it out does not falsify the picture. Use British English throughout.
