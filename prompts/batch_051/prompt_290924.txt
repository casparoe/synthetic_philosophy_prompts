Two colleagues of mine, both metaethicists, got into an argument about whether expressivism can handle the Frege–Geach problem. I reconstruct the exchange as faithfully as I can, and I would like a point-by-point adjudication of it.

The first said expressivism is undone by the most familiar kind of embedding. Its core claim is that a moral sentence's meaning is the non-cognitive attitude it expresses — for instance, "lying is wrong" expresses disapproval of lying. But in the conditional "if lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong," the sentence "lying is wrong" occurs without expressing that attitude: a speaker can accept the conditional simply because they accept that if an act is wrong, then recruiting a child to commit it is wrong, while having no particular view about lying itself. So the expressed attitude cannot be the semantic value of the embedded sentence. There is also a related casualty: the inference from that conditional plus "lying is wrong" to "getting your little brother to lie is wrong" is valid, yet validity is standardly explained as necessary preservation of truth. If moral sentences are not truth-apt (not straightforwardly true or false), the expressivist seems unable to explain what makes this inference valid.

The second replied that this is too quick. Accepting the conditional can be treated as a higher-order attitude: disapproval of the combination of disapproving of lying while failing to disapprove of getting one's little brother to lie. Someone who accepts both premises but rejects the conclusion is then in a state they themselves disapprove of, by their own acceptance of the conditional. Validity here need not be truth-preservation; it can be a matter of attitudinal coherence, where an inference is valid just when one cannot accept its premises and reject its conclusion without a kind of practical inconsistency. That is a distinctively expressivist account of logical relations, and it is exactly the sort of thing the objection says expressivism cannot provide.

The first answered that this is still only a sketch. The second has not shown how "lying is wrong" contributes the same semantic value in isolation, in the antecedent of a conditional, under negation, and inside mixed sentences such as "either lying is wrong or we should move abroad." If the answer is that each new embedding calls for a new, more complex higher-order attitude, the account threatens to become an open-ended hierarchy with no finite statement of the meanings speakers grasp. If instead the expressivist assigns moral sentences truth-values relative to a standard, or to a world-plus-norms index, and computes embeddings in the usual compositional way, the position begins to look like relativism or descriptivism, and the expressivist owes an account of what remains of the claim that moral language is expressive rather than descriptive.

The second responded that the first is running two ideas together. A compositional semantics assigns semantic values recursively, once and for all; the fact that accepting a more deeply embedded sentence may involve a more complex attitude is a fact about acceptance, not a regress inside the semantics itself. And giving a semantics with world-plus-norms indices is not the same as conceding that moral language is descriptive. The expressivist thesis need not be that moral sentences lack truth-conditions in every relativized sense; it can be that the distinctive point of moral language is to express attitudes and guide action. A theory can hold both that "lying is wrong" is true relative to a standard and that its job in thought and talk is expressive. The charge of collapse therefore assumes that any model-theoretic semantics is already a descriptivist one, which is precisely what the expressivist denies.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, somewhere in between, or clearly right, with a brief justification, and then say what, if anything, both parties are missing.
