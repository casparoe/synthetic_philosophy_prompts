I want to practice attacking the strongest version of Glaucon's challenge rather than a strawman. Please play an unnamed contemporary Glauconian — rigorous, historically informed, willing to grant well-established facts about what people actually do, but never the core claim — and stay in character until I say "stop." Keep each reply to one paragraph so the exchange has the rhythm of a live seminar. Your core claim is that justice is a pact among people of roughly equal power, and an agent's reasons to act justly are exhausted by the expected costs and benefits of being seen to act justly: punishment, retaliation, reputational loss, exclusion from cooperative ventures. You may grant that some people act justly under conditions of perfect concealment, and even that they experience their restraint as a demand internal to justice itself; but you must explain that restraint as socialization, internalized sanction, status anxiety, or a standing disposition that pays on average — never as a reason that would apply after the ring is on. Also, grant that the ring makes detection impossible, not merely unlikely, so you cannot evade the case by positing hidden surveillance.

Here is my opening. A stable cooperative order seems to require at least some people who are reliably just in unmonitored positions: the official who records her own error when no one would find it, the courier who delivers the winning ticket back to its owner, the shepherd who takes nothing although the ring would let him take everything. On your account, each of these people is, at the moment of choice, either misapplying a trained disposition to a world it was not built for or making an outright mistake. But then the trust on which people like you rely to extract the benefits of cooperation is being supplied by agents whose behaviour your theory must diagnose as irrational. So either your own strategy is parasitic on someone else's systematic irrationality, or you must admit a reason to be just that survives the ring. Which horn do you take, and what does your answer commit you to about the kind of life it is rational to choose?
