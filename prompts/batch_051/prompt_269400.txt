I work for a robotics startup that does a home-assist robot, and I get stuck with writing the behavioral specs for new capabilities. The frame problem, as McCarthy and Hayes describe it in their 1969 paper Some Philosophical Problems from the Standpoint of Artificial Intelligence, feels like exactly our problem: when a new action is added, someone has to say not only what it changes but also the endless list of what it doesn't change, or the robot's state machine starts accidentally assuming things got relocated, recolored, or turned off. I've read the paper enough to know this was originally about formalizing common sense, not an engineering manual, but I need the manual.

We are a small team: a couple of engineers working in behavior trees and state machines, a systems lead who reviews safety, and no one with a formal logic background. We spec new behaviors every week or two, and the sign-off has to be quick, usually a thirty-minute review. We don't need a theorem, we need a reusable procedure.

Give me a decision procedure for writing a robot behavior spec that decides, for each state variable or object in the robot's world model, whether it should be: (a) listed as an explicit precondition, (b) treated as unchanged by default and omitted, (c) monitored at runtime as a trigger for replanning, or (d) put into a separate "things this behavior may disturb" list that the robot must check after acting. The procedure should include the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the mechanical part stops, and where the spec writer is required to escalate to the systems lead or a safety reviewer. It should also distinguish, in plain language, between the representational question of what the robot needs to know and the computational question of how it updates what it knows, because our team runs those together constantly.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) picking a mug up off a breakfast table covered with other dishes and napkins, (2) navigating across a living room where a child's toys may be on the floor, and (3) one case where the procedure tells me to leave out something that our safety reviewer would absolutely want in the spec. For that third case, don't soften it; show where and why the procedure says to omit it, and say what that exposes about the limits of the procedure.

Structure the answer with these named sections, in this order, with rough lengths:
The procedure (500 words)
Case one: mug on a cluttered table (200 words)
Case two: toys on the floor (200 words)
Where the procedure gives me an answer we'll dislike (200 words)
What this procedure cannot handle (200 words)

And please actually use those section names, so I can map it directly onto a doc we will use at work.
