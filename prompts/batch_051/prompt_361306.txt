I'm a philosophy professor putting together a course on testimony and the epistemology of religious claims, and I'm bothered by an analogy that keeps showing up in apologetics and in my students' papers. The analogy is that the gospels are like four independent witnesses in a trial. The argument goes: if witnesses agree too exactly, that's collusion; genuine witnesses contradict each other in small details without ruining their credibility; so the gospel discrepancies are just what you'd expect from honest independent reports. I've been rereading Augustine's Harmony of the Gospels, and he runs this pattern on the difference between Matthew's Sermon on the Mount and Luke's Sermon on the Plain: as I understand him, he says they were simply two different sermons, so there is no conflict. That looks like a legal "two separate events" explanation, and it makes me want a full audit of the courtroom analogy.

What I'd like from you is this. Give the explicit mapping—what plays the role of the witnesses, the event, the court, the exhibits, cross-examination. Then say where the analogy breaks. I especially want the disanalogies that matter for the "differences are a sign of honesty" conclusion, not decoration. For example, in a court we have some independent causal route from each witness to the event; with the gospels you have literary interdependence, maybe one or more writers using another. In a court, the prior probability of a report being fabricated or garbled is not fixed by a theological commitment; in inerrancy debates it often seems to be. And in a court, a "two separate events" explanation is a last resort when a single-event explanation can't cover the data, not the first move. Use Augustine's mountain/plain case as the test: what would a court need before accepting two separate events, and does the analogy give the inerrantist that, or is the analogy doing the work? Finally, tell me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether a better analogy is available. Keep the whole answer under 900 words, with a correspondence table and then a bottom-line paragraph.
