I’m in an epistemology class where we’ve spent two weeks on Clifford and James, and I keep geting stuck on a particular paragraph from James’s “The Will to Believe.” Here is the passage I’m looking at:

“Next, let us call the decision between two hypotheses an option. Options may be of several kinds. They may be (a) living or dead; (b) forced or avoidable; (c) momentous or trivial; and for our purposes we may call an option a genuine option when it is of the forced, living and momentous kind.”

What I can’t figure out is what kind of claim this is. Is James just stipulating a term, or is he smuggling in a normative standard? The definitions of forced, living, and momentous all look like they describe facts about a person’s situation and psychology, but then he says “for our purposes” a genuine option is the combination of all three, and later he seems to think genuine options are the ones where it is okay to let our passional nature decide. My proffesor presented this as a direct answer to Clifford’s evidentialism, but if James has just defined a special class of options, I don’t see how a definition alone can defeat the claim that we should proportion belief to evidence. Is the actual anti-Clifford work being done by his later claim about passional nature, with “genuine option” just setting up the kind of case where letting passion decide matters? Or is there a sense in which the definition itself already has epistemic force? Also, is “genuine” here doing something more than just “psychologically live, forced, and momentous for the agent”? That seems like it could be true of lots of choices where we still shouldn’t believe without evidence.
