I've been reporting on debates in moral psychology, and I keep running into an asymmetry that seems unstable as soon as you look at it closely. In much ordinary conversation, people draw a fairly firm line between preferences and biases. Preferences are treated as legitimate things for a person to have, even when they are arbitrary; pluralism about preferences is close to the default. Biases, by contrast, are treated as defects to be corrected — something closer to an epistemic or moral failing. Yet it is not obvious that the two are different in kind. A preference can be groundless, resistant to counter-evidence, and partial; a bias can be stable, sincerely experienced as one's preference, and organized around features the agent takes to be relevant. And the words seem to chase the context: much the same disposition gets called a preference when it shapes intimate life and a bias when it shapes an institutional decision. Take a hiring committee member who favors candidates from her own university. If she describes this as a preference — she trusts that training — colleagues treat it as defensible or at least understandable. If a colleague calls the same disposition in-group bias in the committee's formal review, she faces censure. Same disposition, same evidence, different label, different verdict.

The question I want to put to specialists, and then explain to readers, is this: is there a defensible principle that separates a preference from a bias, or are we mostly just sorting by whether we happen to endorse the evaluation? Where would you draw the line, and can your line survive hard cases: an aesthetic aversion that ignores all evidence; an accurate but morally objectionable generalization about a group; a self-directed pessimism that gets classified as a cognitive bias; a partial, other-regarding preference that harms third parties and is nonetheless respected; and a workplace preference for culture fit that works as a proxy for a protected category? If your principle would force us to revise some ordinary verdicts in those cases, say which verdicts you would revise and why.
