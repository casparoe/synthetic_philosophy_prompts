Pretend you're an epistemologist who works on intellectual virtue and cognitive outsourcing, and think hard before answering. I want a real diagnosis, not a pep talk. Here's a policy I've adopted, and I want to know whether it's defensible or just elaborate self-congratulation.

I don't ask an LLM to explain anything until I've first written out, in my own words, what I think is going on, and ended with at least one sentence staring exactly where I'm stuck. Then I let the model respond only to that stuck point, not summarize the whole topic. My reasoning is that explanation is the main thing I don't want to outsource: if the model hands me a clear map too early, I'll mistake fluency for understanding, and I'll never develop the ability to grope through an idea myself. Forcing myself to locate a confusion also seems good because a question you can't pose is a question you can't own.

But here's my worry. Lately my pre-model explanations are turning into tidy paraphrases of whatever I just read, and I sometimes feel like I understand it before I actually do — the writing itself produces the feeling. And the confusions I bring to the model are suspiciously well-shaped: they're usually the kind of thing a language model answers cleanly, in numbered points. So maybe I'm not protecting my understanding; maybe I'm just building a nicer-looking stall in the same marketplace. Is the policy defensible as stated? Can it be fixed so it keeps the benefits without generating false confidence? And is there any way from the inside to tell whether I'm learning to think or just learning to write the prelude to a good prompt?
