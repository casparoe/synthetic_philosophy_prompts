I recently had a long argument with a friend about whether modern Stoic popularizers, with their emphasis on the “dichotomy of control,” are actually faithful to Epictetus. The argument kept returning to a passage I want to use as the centerpiece of a short essay. It is from the Enchiridion, chapter 6, in George Long’s translation:

“Be not elated at any advantage which is not your own. If a horse should be elated and say, ‘I am handsome,’ it might be endurable. But when you are elated and say, ‘I have a handsome horse,’ know that you are elated at an advantage which belongs to a horse. What, then, is your own? The use of appearances. So that when you act conformably to nature in the use of appearances, then be elated; for you will be elated at an advantage which is your own.”

My draft essay argues that this passage is harder to fit into the familiar “what is in my control versus what is not” reading than people usually assume. The obvious move is to say that the handsome horse is an external, while the correct use of appearances is internal. But I want to push further: even an appearance like “I have a handsome horse” is, in one sense, internal, yet Epictetus treats being elated at it as a mistake. That suggests the relevant distinction is not simply between the external world and my inner states, but between the representational content of an impression and the present activity of making use of it. If that is right, then the popular advice to “focus only on what is inside your head” may still miss what Epictetus means by “your own.”

I would welcome comments on this thesis. In particular: Does my reading of the passage overstate the difference between “appearance” and “use of appearances” given how compressed the Enchiridion is? Is there a simpler reading of the horse example that my essay is ignoring, and would I actually be better off acknowledging that simplicity rather than resisting it? And finally, does the claim hold up once one remembers that the text is meant as a practical training manual rather than a piece of formal epistemology?
