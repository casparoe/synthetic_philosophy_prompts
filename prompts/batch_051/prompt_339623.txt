Three years ago my brother was declared dead by neurological criteria after an accident, even though his heart was still beating and the ventilator was still moving air through his chest. Since then I have read what I could find and talked with as many doctors as would talk to me, and I have reached a view I now have to defend in front of people who will push back hard. In two weeks I am presenting to our hospital's clinical ethics committee as a patient-and-family representative. My thesis is this: brain death and circulatory death are not two tests for the same underlying fact. They pick out different things — roughly, the loss of the person and the loss of the circulating organism — and the hospital quietly slides between these two meanings whenever it calls them both death. I will argue that the hospital should keep the two standards apart in what it says to families. It may still withdraw life support, and it may still recover organs where consent is clear, but it should stop telling a family that a warm body with a pulse and working organs is dead in the same sense as a body whose heart has stopped and will not restart.

My argument, as I have it now, runs like this. First, the reason usually given for brain death is that the brain is what holds the body's many systems together as one living whole, so once the whole brain is gone the organism has come apart. That does not match what I have seen and read: bodies declared brain-dead can heal wounds, throw fevers, and in rare cases carry a pregnancy for months, which is a strange thing for a corpse to do. Second, the actual work being done by calling brain death death is practical rather than biological — it lets us stop machines without having to say we are letting someone die, and it lets us take organs under the rule that organs come only from the dead. I think those practical goals can be met by honest consent instead. Third, this sliding between meanings has a cost: families are told the patient is already dead and therefore are made to feel there is no real decision left, or they are told the same word in two situations that feel nothing alike and they come away distrusting the hospital.

The committee I face is not hostile, but it is not a philosophy seminar either. It includes the neurologist who runs the hospital's brain-death protocol, a transplant coordinator, a critical care nurse, the hospital attorney, and a chaplain. The neurologist and the transplant coordinator have built their careers around the current standard and will defend it as settled. The attorney will care first about what the state statute and the hospital's liability require. The nurse and the chaplain have watched many families struggle with this language, and they will care most about whether changing it would make families suffer more.

Give me the objections I should expect, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased in the way these particular committee members would phrase them. For each objection, give me what I should concede, what I should hold, and a reply I can deliver in under two minutes. Be honest about the weakest point in my position — the objection I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody raises, the one I am probably hoping nobody asks — and tell me how to handle it if it comes up.
