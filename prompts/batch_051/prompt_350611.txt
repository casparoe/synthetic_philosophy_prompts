I've been arguing with a colleague about dispositions, and we keep returning to fragility as our test case. I'd like a working definition of fragility — understood here as the disposition to break when given a sharp blow with a hammer — that I can use to settle the dispute. The obvious candidate is a simple conditional: an object is fragile just in case, if it were given that blow, it would break. I suspect this is too crude, but I don't want a survey of the literature or any citations; I want the definition itself to do the classification.

Here are the cases. (1) An ordinary wine glass standing on a stone bench. (2) A solid steel anvil. (3) A wine glass of the same type as (1), packed inside a block of stiff foam so that a sharp hammer blow is absorbed and the glass does not break. (4) A wine glass of the same type as (1), fitted with a small device that instantly alters its molecular structure to a rubbery state at the first sign of an incoming blow, so that when struck it bends rather than breaks. (5) A solid steel block fitted with a small explosive device designed to shatter the block the moment it is struck; when struck, it flies apart. (6) A gem-quality diamond, which is famously hard to scratch and yet can be shattered by a sharp hammer blow.

My own judgments are these. (1) is fragile and (2) is not. (3) is fragile even though the simple conditional says otherwise. (5) is not fragile even though the simple conditional says it is. I have some pull toward saying (4) is fragile before the blow, but that seems to commit me to saying the glass can lose its fragility between the time I attribute it and the time the blow lands, and I don't know whether to accept that. About (6) I have no settled view. I gather cases like (3), (4), and (5) have technical names, but I don't need the labels. Propose a definition, run it through all six cases, and tell me where it gives a verdict you would revise the definition to avoid, and where you would instead keep the definition and defend a surprising verdict.
