A friend and I recently spent an evening arguing about a practice that has become common in AI safety research and model auditing, and we ended without agreeing on what the practice commits one to. The practice, sometimes called automated interpretability, is this: when researchers want to know what part of a model is doing — which features a particular internal component responds to, or why a model produces a certain pattern of failures — they do not trust the model's own explanation, on the grounds that a model asked to report on itself may confabulate. Instead, they bring in a second model, an interpreter, to inspect the first model's activations, weights, or outputs and produce a verbal explanation of what is going on; this explanation is then scored largely by how well it predicts the target model's behavior or activations, and the best-scoring explanation gets written into an audit or used in a safety judgment.

The puzzle my friend and I kept circling is this: if a model's self-explanation cannot be trusted because the model may lack a reliable grasp of its own reasons, why is another model's explanation treated as evidence? That asymmetry seems stable only if interpretation demands less than understanding — if an interpreter need not occupy the target's point of view, but only track and summarize its dispositions. But then the practice appears to presuppose that interpretation is, at bottom, predictive description rather than the recovery of meaning or reasons. Alternatively, the practice may be neutral here: perhaps it strictly presupposes only that an interpreter's sentences can be true of the target without the interpreter's knowing why they are true, and the deeper theory is left open.

I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of interpretation — or of the relation between prediction, description, and understanding — that this practice presupposes. Distinguish what is strictly required from what it merely fits well with; say whether the practice is coherent given its own suspicion of self-reports; and consider what would have to change if one instead held that interpretation requires the interpreter to occupy the target's normative standpoint, and not merely to predict it. In doing so, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
