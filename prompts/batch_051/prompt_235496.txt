In my environmental science class we read the classic tragedy of the commons argument: when a shared, finite resource such as a pasture is open to everyone, each user gets the full benefit of adding one more animal while the harm from overgrazing is spread across all users, so each person rationally keeps adding animals until the resource collapses. Two assumptions seem to be doing the work: the resource is rivalrous (one cow's grass is unavailable to another cow) and depletable. I have been wondering whether the argument still applies to digital commons, where neither assumption obviously holds: a thousand extra people reading an encyclopedia article, downloading an open-source program, or entering an online discussion space do not use up the article, the code, or the space the way cows use up grass, because digital goods can be copied at near-zero cost. Some people I have talked to conclude from this that the tragedy simply dissolves online and that the pasture story was really only about grazing rights, not cooperation in general. I am not sure.

Please respond using the following sections, in this order:

1. The original argument's moving parts (about 120 words): identify the premises that do the work in the tragedy of the commons and flag the one most threatened by the move to digital goods.
2. What the digital shift changes (about 150 words): explain how non-rivalry and near-zero marginal cost alter the situation, using a concrete example such as open-source software or a large wiki.
3. Does the argument survive? (about 200 words): argue whether the tragedy is dissolved, only delayed, or transformed into a different failure mode such as under-provision, quality erosion, or governance problems, and say which premise must be replaced for the argument to remain useful.
4. My verdict (about 80 words): state your overall judgment about how much force the original argument retains online, and name one condition under which that judgment would flip.
