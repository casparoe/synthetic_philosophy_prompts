Take the 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora on the island of Sumbawa, in present-day Indonesia, and the 'Year Without a Summer' of 1816 as a case study for the idea that non-human events can be historical actors. The eruption injected sulfur into the stratosphere; in 1816 parts of North America and Europe saw summer frosts, ruined harvests, food shortages, and social distress. I want a careful analysis that runs in both directions.

First, clarify the conceptual terrain. Distinguish three things: (1) an eruption as a cause in a physical and biological chain; (2) the attribution of agency to a non-human event, understood as figurative; and (3) environmental determinism, the thesis that such physical events largely explain, or even fix, the course of human affairs. Say precisely what would have to be added to the causal story to make talk of the volcano as a 'historical actor' more than a metaphor, and what would have to be added beyond that to make the account deterministic.

Second, apply these distinctions to the episode. Assess how much of the 1816 outcomes was plausibly done by the volcano itself and how much was done by mediating human arrangements—agrarian structure, poor relief, trade restrictions, price controls, and the responses of governments. Be explicit about which effects look like direct, near-unavoidable consequences of the eruption and which look like products of specific institutions and choices.

Third, use the episode to test the theory. Appeals to germs, climate, and volcanoes as historical actors are often accused of being environmental determinism in new clothes. Does Tambora support that charge, or does it show that non-human agency can be taken seriously without being deterministic? Conclude by saying what a non-deterministic account of non-human historical agency would have to claim about an event of this kind, and where you would draw the line between causal power and agency.
