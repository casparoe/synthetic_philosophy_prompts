I write historical novels, and a large part of my research reading is the kind of big-picture history that tries to explain the most basic contours of human societies: why food production spread quickly along some axes and slowly along others, why state-like political forms displaced looser forms of life in some regions, why some parts of the world accumulated wealth and military power while others did not. I read the well-known grand narratives in this vein and I also read their academic critics, and the disputes between them often turn on questions of evidence that I cannot settle from my desk.

Over time I have settled into a fixed policy as a reader. Whenever such a work attributes a large historical outcome to a small set of deep causes — a continent's orientation, a package of domesticable crops, a religious or ethical outlook, a mode of social organization — I accept the causal story only if I can picture, in concrete terms, the kind of local, datable evidence that would show the proposed mechanism actually operating on the ground in a particular place and period. If I cannot picture that evidence, I regard the claim as narrative rather than history and refuse to let it change what I believe about how the past works. Recently I applied this to the suggestion that the east-west axis of a landmass explains how quickly agricultural practices spread across it, and I discarded the claim because I could not say what archaeological findings would overturn it. My friends say I am throwing away the most instructive part of such books and that demanding a scene I could film is an aesthetic preference dressed up as a standard of evidence. I genuinely cannot tell whether I am defending myself against unfalsifiable storytelling or indulging a novelist's custom of mistrusting any explanation I cannot dramatize. Is the policy defensible as it stands, and is there a version of it that keeps the useful pressure on grand narratives without collapsing into a general refusal to learn from macro-history?
