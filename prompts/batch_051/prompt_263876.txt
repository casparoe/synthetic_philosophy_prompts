I am a novelist and I teach a graduate fiction workshop, and I have been circling a question that I now need to answer in a form I can actually work with. Next term my syllabus includes at least two writers whose biographies are morally indefensible, and I find myself wanting to tell my students to read the book, not the life, without being sure I believe it. At the same time, I am finishing a novel whose protagonist is an artist whose work is better than his character, and I cannot settle whether the book I am writing is allowed to insist on that distinction.

I think almost entirely in the vocabulary of craft, and I want you to restate the philosophical debate about whether immoral art can be good art in that vocabulary, rather than in the vocabulary of aesthetics or ethics, which I do not have fluent control of. The terms I work with daily include the implied author as distinct from the real author, narrative distance, voice, focalization (who sees and who speaks), free indirect style, and the ways a book rewards, ironizes, or quietly endorses a character's perspective; I also think in terms of where a novel invites the reader's complicity and where it withholds it. For each major line of position in the debate — roughly, the view that the work is judged on internal criteria alone, and the view that moral defect in the work or the artist counts against the work as art, along with any important position between them — tell me what translates cleanly, what must be distorted to fit, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all.

Then give me the residue report. I want to know which parts of the debate failed to survive the translation, and whether that residue signals a real distinction my craft vocabulary cannot draw — perhaps about the actual living artist's deeds, or about the ethics of teaching and consuming the work — or whether it signals that the philosophical disagreement is partly an artifact of the vocabulary in which it is normally conducted. Assume I know narrative theory well but not the philosophical literature: for the positions themselves, give me enough plain-language specification of their commitments to make the translation possible, without treating the analogy as the argument.
