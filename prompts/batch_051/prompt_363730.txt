I run the plain-language communications team at a large public-health agency, and I review patient-facing materials before they go out — fact sheets, screening letters, vaccine explainers. Our writers and clinicians lean on metaphors constantly: cancer is a battle, depression is a chemical imbalance, the immune system is an army. Every week I have to decide whether to let one through, rewrite it literally, or swap in a different metaphor, and there is no shared rule for doing it. Half the time the metaphor does genuine explanatory work and half the time it sneaks in assumptions that nobody would have signed off on if they had been stated literally (calling treatment a fight against cancer quietly implies the patient can win or lose, and that not fighting hard enough is some kind of failure). I know there is a real philosophical dispute underneath this. As I understand it, Davidson held that metaphors don't have a special meaning over and above their literal meaning — they make us notice things, but they don't assert anything extra — while the Lakoff and Johnson conceptual metaphor tradition treats metaphors as the surface of systematic conceptual mappings that structure how we think, so that talk of war against disease is not just decorative, it is a way of reasoning. I want you to turn one or both of those views into a decision procedure my team can actually run, not an essay. It should be a checklist or a flowchart in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with "rewrite," where it stops with "keep," and where it hands the decision to me because its a judgment call rather than a mechanical rule. The people using it will have clinical training but no philosophy, and about twenty minutes for each material.

Then apply the procedure to three cases that come up constantly: (1) a cancer screening leaflet that says early detection is your best weapon, (2) a mental-health leaflet that says depression is a chemical imbalance, and (3) a vaccine explainer that describes the immune system as an army that remembers its enemies. I want at least one of those to be a case where the procedure tells me something I probably won't want to hear, since we have used that framing for years. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire, which philosophical distinctions can't survive being turned into a checklist, and where the checklist can't settle the question. Before you give the procedure, take a paragraph to lay out the two positions in your own words and say which distinctions you are keeping and why. Think carefully about how each step will actually play out on deadline with a nurse and a graphic designer in the room. Please answer as a professional philosopher of language who has spent real time in health communication, and write the whole thing in American English.
