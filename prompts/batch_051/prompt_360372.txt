Last week I got cornered at a party by two friends, Maya and Jonas, who were relitigating a thought experiment one of their professors had floated in a seminar. A demon tells you he'll make your life miserable unless you admire him. Maya said obviously that gives you a reason — maybe even a decisive one — to admire the demon. Jonas said it gives you a reason to want to admire him, or to get yourself to admire him, but it is not itself a reason to admire him; the only things that can be reasons to admire a person are facts about what that person is like. Neither would give an inch, and the exchange went roughly like this.

(1) Maya: If a reason is just a consideration that counts in favor of an attitude, the threat plainly counts in favor of admiring him. You'd be silly not to try to bring about that attitude in yourself, so a reason is clearly in play.

(2) Jonas: The threat counts in favor of *being in the admiring state*, not in favor of the attitude's object. Admiration is by nature taking someone to be admirable. The threat doesn't make him admirable; it makes admiring him useful. So you have a reason for wanting to admire him, not a reason to admire him.

(3) Maya: That's just the wrong-kind-of-reasons distinction, and it doesn't settle anything by itself. Consider belief. If someone offers you a pile of money to believe the butler did it, you do have a reason to believe it — a corrupting, pragmatic reason, but a reason all the same. Saying it's the 'wrong kind' labels the difference; it doesn't show the pragmatic consideration isn't a reason.

(4) Jonas: The belief case actually cuts my way. A cash payout is no epistemic reason to believe the butler did it; it's a reason to make yourself believe it. We distinguish 'believing in response to evidence' from 'being caused to believe.' The demon case is the same structure: the threat bears on the state, not on the object.

(5) Maya: You keep using the phrase 'bears on the object' as if it weren't the very thing in dispute. Why must a reason to φ be limited to what bears on φ's correctness? In ordinary talk, a reason to do something is just what makes it worth doing. If the demon's threat makes admiring him worth doing, then it's a reason. Fine, you can call it a wrong-kind reason — but then you owe an account of what makes that kind defective, rather than just defining it away.

(6) Jonas: Here's the account. Attitudes are individuated by their correctness conditions. To admire someone just is to take them to be admirable. So a consideration that doesn't bear on admirability isn't a consideration for that very attitude; it's a consideration for inducing a state in yourself. That's why 'he saved the drowning child' and 'the demon will hurt you otherwise' aren't two species of the same genus. One is a reason for admiration; the other is a reason for wanting admiration, or for self-manipulation.

(7) Maya: Then what about a parent who deliberately cultivates genuine affection for an unlovable stepchild because the child needs it? This isn't just wanting to feel affection, and it isn't responding to the child's being lovable. The affection itself seems called for by the child's need. On your view that's either a wrong-kind reason for the affection itself, or it has to be redescribed as mere self-manipulation, which seems like a distortion of ordinary moral life.

At that point they both looked at me and I honestly couldn't say who was right. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, say who is right about what, and say what, if anything, both parties are missing.
