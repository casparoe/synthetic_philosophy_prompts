I've been trying to work out what I actually think about Leibniz's claim that God chose the best of all possible worlds, not by arguing the theology but by checking my gut on a bunch of cases. I keep getting pulled in different directions. Here are my verdicts—see if there's a principle behind them, or tell me which ones have to go.

- A world with a stubbed toe and nothing else wrong, where the toe's owner becomes braver because of it: I can just about call that best if the alternatives were odd enough. It doesn't bother me much.
- A world where a child dies slowly of an illness, nobody learns anything from it, and the death has no further effects whatsoever: I can't accept that as part of a best possible world. Full stop.
- Take any world of moderately happy people and add one extra happy person, changing nothing else. I can't help judging the second world strictly better. But that seems to imply the first wasn't best, and if you can always add another happy person, then no world can be best. That conclusion bothers me a lot.
- A world where everyone is blissful but nobody is free: I call it worse than a world with free will and a fair amount of suffering, though I can't state the principle that does that work.
- A world with no evil but also no courage, compassion or forgiveness: I lean that it's somehow poorer than a world with a little evil and those virtues. But I won't use that to justify the dying child.
- A world with the same total happiness as ours but distributed more equally: better, no hesitation.
- A world with slightly less total happiness than ours but nobody in abject misery: also better, so I'm clearly not a pure totalist.

Is there one coherent notion of 'better world' that gets most of these? What does it say about the one-extra-happy-person case, and about cases I haven't thought of—does it force me to admit there is no best possible world, or can it keep a best possible world? I'd rather have a simple principle that overturns a couple of my verdicts than a complicated one that saves them all. And if the whole exercise is confused—say because 'best possible world' only makes sense if the set of possible worlds already has an unsurpassable member, or because comparing whole worlds against each other is a category mistake—then say so plainly rather than playing along. Please answer in British English.
