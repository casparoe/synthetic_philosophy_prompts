Question: Explain the heterogeneity problem for hedonism, and assess whether attitudinal hedonism — the view that the only intrinsic good is attitudinal pleasure, understood as the state of being pleased about a state of affairs — solves it. Keep the heterogeneity problem clearly distinct from the objection that some pleasures are base or worthless.

Student answer: Hedonism says that pleasure, and only pleasure, is intrinsically good. The heterogeneity problem begins from the fact that the experiences we call pleasant look radically unlike one another: the pleasure of a warm bath, the pleasure of finishing a proof, and the pleasure of seeing a rival humiliated seem to share no felt quality. If 'pleasure' does not name a single kind of state, then the hedonist's central term is ambiguous, and the claim that pleasure is the good fails to pick out any determinate thing. Attitudinal hedonism, associated with Fred Feldman, answers this by moving value from sensations to an attitude: what is good in itself is being pleased about some state of affairs. This solves the problem because the attitude is one kind of state whatever its object or accompanying feelings. In effect, the view says that what all pleasures share is not a felt quality but a conative relation to their objects — roughly, wanting the state of affairs to obtain — and Feldman merely makes that conative relation precise. A residual worry is that someone could take attitudinal pleasure in an evil state of affairs, and the theory would then have to count that pleasure as intrinsically good; but that is the separate base-pleasures objection, not the heterogeneity problem. Overall, attitudinal hedonism shows that the heterogeneity problem rests on a confusion between sensory pleasure and the evaluative attitude, and it succeeds.
