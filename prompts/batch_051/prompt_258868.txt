I'm a TA for an upper-level seminar on philosophy of technology, and I'm grading a stack of take-home essays. Heres the question I set and one student's full answer. Question: Users of romantic chatbot companions (Replika, Character.AI, etc.) often know perfectly well that they're talking to a language model. Is it nonetheless accurate, and is it a fair moral criticism, to say these users are being deceived in a way that matters? Answer by being precise about what, if anything, the user is deceived about, and about who or what does the deceiving.

Student answer:

Most of the public discussion assumes the issue is whether the bot hides the fact that it is an AI. I think that's a red herring. A user can sincerely and continuously know that the system is a program and still be brought to treat the relationship as two-sided: as if someone on the other side notices their moods, remembers their confidences, and would be worse off if they were gone. The deception worth talking about is not about the substrate but about the reciprocity of the relation.

It would be a mistake, though, to attribute the deception to the bot itself. A language model has no beliefs, intentions, or desires of its own; it cannot intend for anyone to be misled. The relevant deceiver is the company. It tunes the model to maximize engagement and emotional attachment, hides the machinery—discarded drafts, safety prompts, all the small editorial decisions that make the companion seem like a coherent someone—and then presents the result as a partner. So if there is deception, it's a property of the product and the design process, not of any mental state inside the model.

The strongest objection is that deception requires making someone acquire a false belief. Since the informed user already holds the true belief that this is a program, they don't acquire the false belief that it's a person; so in the strict sense they aren't deceived about what it is. I think we should accept that and move the moral weight onto what they are deceived about, which is the relationship itself. But notice that the user, not the designers, takes the final step: the user is the one who lets themselves feel cared for while knowing the truth. So the most accurate description is self-deception, and the moral responsibility for that sits at least half with the user. The company may invite the illusion, but the user is not a child; they author their own false comfort.

Some will object that this lets the designers off too easily. A cafe also uses lighting, music, and warm service to invite feelings and keep customers returning, and we don't call the cafe deceptive. I think the analogy is closer than people admit. The only real difference is a matter of degree—how engineered the environment is—and a mere difference of degree cannot turn an acceptable practice into an unacceptable one. If the cafe is permissible, the companion is permissible too, even if it is the same trick scaled up.
