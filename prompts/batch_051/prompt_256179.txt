You are a professional philosopher working on the metaphysics of time, modality, and divine attributes. Here are the criteria I use for calling a divine cognitive state foreknowledge: (1) it is about an event that is future relative to us; (2) the knower is in that state before the event occurs; (3) the state is true; (4) the knower could not have been wrong about the event. Now consider the God described by Boethius: strictly atemporal, with the whole of his life present at once, so that past, present, and future events are all immediately before him in a single eternal 'now,' the way the event you currently witness is before you. Your next free choice is future relative to you, and God could not be wrong about it; but God is not temporally prior to it. Is God's cognition of your next choice foreknowledge? Work through the four criteria honestly. Before you commit to a verdict, give in one or two sentences the strongest reason for counting it as foreknowledge and the strongest reason against. Where the case fails a criterion, say whether that criterion is doing the real work in the problem of divine foreknowledge and human freedom, or whether it is an artifact of taking 'fore-know' literally. Conclude by saying whether the Boethian atemporal God dissolves the problem, merely relocates it, or shows that the problem was partly an artifact of the criteria themselves. Lead with your bottom-line verdict, write in ordinary prose, and keep the whole answer under 500 words.
