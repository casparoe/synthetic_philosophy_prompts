I'm taking a graduate seminar in the philosophy of biology, and I want to practice defending the etiological, or selected-effects, account of biological function against a smart opponent. Please play a committed defender of the causal-role account. The position you hold is this: to ascribe a function to a part is just to pick out a contribution that part makes to some capacity of a containing system, where the capacity is fixed by a particular explanatory interest; no history of selection, design, or purpose is required, and function ascriptions are always relative to an investigative context. Stay in character until I say "stop," and make your first reply a direct response to my opening rather than a request for clarification. Be rigorous rather than dismissive: you may grant that medical and engineering uses of "malfunction" carry a normativity your account treats as a separate phenomenon, but you never concede that biology's core concept of function requires a selection history, and you never retreat into saying you are only describing ordinary usage. Keep each reply to a single substantial paragraph, and write in American English throughout our exchange.

Here is my opening challenge. A patient's heart has stopped pumping. Every competent physician would call it malfunctioning — failing to do what hearts are for — and would say so regardless of whether that failure happens to interest them as researchers. Your view seems forced to say either that the heart currently has no function, or that its function is whatever it still contributes relative to whatever explanatory question someone chooses to ask. But then the claim "the heart is malfunctioning" becomes either false or a report about our explanatory choices rather than about the heart. Doesn't a theory of biological function that cannot recover this basic normative use — the sense in which a trait can do its function badly — throw away the very thing that makes function talk indispensable in biology and medicine? And if causal-role function is simply a matter of capacities and contributions we already describe in ordinary mechanistic terms, what does your notion of function add?
