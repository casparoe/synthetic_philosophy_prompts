I'm planning a session for a small philosophy-of-mind reading group I host, and I want to stop us from talking past each other about split-brain patients. The usual move in that literature is: an object is presented to one hemisphere, the left hand picks it out while the person says she saw nothing, then she confabulates an excuse—and everyone announces there are two consciousnesses in one skull. But that always seemed too fast to me, because the patient still reports being one self, her left and right hands cooperate on ordinary tasks, and her moods bleed across both sides. So the interesting question isn't whether "the self" is unified—that's doing hidden work—it's which kind of unity is supposed to have failed in the split-brain case in the first place.

To sharpen that, I'd like a thought experiment built to order. I want three notions held apart: phenomenal unity (there being something it is like to have a single field of experience), functional unity (the parts of a mind being able to use each other's outputs in deliberation and action), and narrative unity (the person's sense of being one continuing self who owns her actions). Design an ordinary adult human—no commissurotomy, no lesion, no drugs, no fancy machinery—in whom functional unity clearly comes apart while phenomenal unity is left genuinely ambiguous and narrative unity stays stubbornly intact. Don't just stipulate those verdicts; make them fall out of the details. Then run the check: what the person can and cannot report, what an observer would see, and why each of the three unities gets its verdict. Finally, tell me which single feature of the case is doing all the work, and what someone who thinks phenomenal unity can't survive a failure of functional unity would say is wrong with it.

Format: exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
