I have been working through Nozick's truth-tracking account and I think I have located a serious problem, but I want to know whether the problem is real or whether I am smuggling something in. The sensitivity condition says that a subject knows that p only if the subject would not believe that p if p were false, holding fixed the method by which the belief was actually formed. This gives the familiar pair of verdicts: I know that I have hands, but I do not know that I am not a handless brain in a vat, since in the nearest world where I am a handless brain in a vat I would still believe that I am not one. So far, so good.

But the proposition that I have hands entails the proposition that I am not a handless brain in a vat, and I know that it entails it. So I have a known premise and a known entailment whose conclusion I am apparently not entitled to count as knowledge. That seems to mean deductive inference no longer transmits knowledge: I can know p, know that p entails q, competently deduce q, and still fail to know q. And once that transmission is broken, I do not see what role knowledge is supposed to play in reasoning. Closure under known entailment looks less like one principle among several and more like the very thing that makes knowledge usable — the rule that lets knowledge extend through inference. So my worry is that Nozick has not answered the skeptic but changed the subject: he keeps the word 'knowledge' but gives us a state too weak to license inference, and then claims victory over skepticism. Is this a fair description of his view, or does it rest on a mistake? And if the description is fair, why should we accept that the state he saves is the knowledge we were worried about losing?
