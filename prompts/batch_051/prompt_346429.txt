In Plato’s *Phaedrus*, Socrates describes two complementary dialectical procedures. The first is “that of perceiving and bringing together in one idea the scattered particulars, that one may make clear by definition the particular thing which he wishes to explain.” The second is “that of dividing things again by classes, where the natural joints are, and not trying to break any part, after the manner of a bad carver.” People in philosophy of science sometimes treat this second passage as the founding statement of the idea that scientific classification aims to carve nature at its joints: a good taxonomy, like a good butcher, cuts along pre-existing divisions rather than imposing arbitrary ones.

But Socrates introduces both procedures as elements of a single craft of speech and thought — collection and division — not as a general theory of the special sciences. My question is whether this passage can bear the weight that philosophers of science place on it. What exactly does the carver image get right about successful scientific classification, and what does the analogy quietly import that may not hold? In particular, the image invites us to think of natural kinds as boundaries that exist in advance of inquiry and simply await a careful instrument. Is that picture defensible for kinds such as biological species, chemical elements, psychiatric categories, or types of fundamental particle? Which disanalogies between jointed animals and kinds in the sciences matter if we are trying to decide whether there is a single standard of “naturalness” across all sciences, and which disanalogies are merely decorative? Finally, tell me what evidence or argument would most likely change your verdict about how much of the joint-carving picture survives once the disanalogies are made explicit, and where your own reasoning is least secure.
