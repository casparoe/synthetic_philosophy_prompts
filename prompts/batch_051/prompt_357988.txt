I'm a bio major taking a philosophy of biology elective to satisfy my science-and-society requirement, and one of the TAs gave me this argument and told me to come back with the bad step or a cost I'm willing to pay. I've been turning it over for a week, and I still can't see the exit.

(1) The function of a trait is the effect for which it was selected — that's what makes pumping blood the function of the heart while making heart sounds is a mere side effect.
(2) So whether something has a function at all, and which one it has, is settled entirely by its selection history.
(3) Now take a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person — heart and all — that forms by sheer cosmic coincidence in a swamp yesterday. By (1) and (2), its heart has no function, even though it pumps blood exactly like yours.
(4) But if you handed that heart to any competent physiologist without telling her where it came from, she wouldn't hestitate to say its function is to pump blood. Her evidence would be the current organization of the thing; no evolutionary backstory needed. If a view says she's wrong, so much the worse for that view.
(5) So function must be a matter of what a part currently does for a larger system, not where it came from — the causal-role picture.
(6) But the causal-role picture can't stop: my nose's function is holding up my glasses, a tumor's function is recruiting blood vessels, a heartbeat's function is making the lub-dub sound. It erases the difference between functioning normally and being a side effect, which is half the point of function talk in the first place.
(7) So the selected-effects view is false (from 3 and 4), and the causal-role view is false (from 6). Function talk in biology is unsalvageable.

I don't buy (7) — that seems to prove way too much, since working biologists clearly use function ascriptions to do real work in physiology and ecology. But every fix I try either makes me a revisionist about lab-built organs or opens the floodgates to tumors and heart sounds. Which step or inference would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? In particular, I'd like to know what your answer commits you to saying about the first heart that ever evolved, and about whether the physiologist in (4) is literally wrong.
