Ok so here's where I've ended up after a couple years of reading philosophy of science, and I want to know whether it actually hangs together. Im a retired biologist — spent my career in a wet lab — so the Darwin and Wallace case is the one I care about most, but I think the same points apply to Newton and Leibniz.

(1) I think the big discoveries are mostly inevitable once a field is ripe. The calculus showing up twice, natural selection showing up twice, within a few years of each other — it convinces me the ideas were in the air and the timeline wouldnt have changed much if either named figure had never lived.
(2) But I still think priority is a legitimate thing to care about. If Newton actually got there first, that's a fact about the actual history, and we shouldn't pretend it doesn't matter just because the discovery was overdetermined.
(3) I also lean toward a counterfactual criterion of credit: a scientist deserves more credit to the extent that the actual history of science is different because of their work. If someone else would have published essentially the same result within a year anyway, I think that does reduce the first person's claim to special credit.
(4) At the same time, I don't think "both equally" is the right verdict for Darwin and Wallace. Darwin's version was worked out in far more detail and backed by a lot more evidence, so I'd give him more credit — not for being first, but for the quality and depth of the contribution.
(5) And I'm allergic to the Great Man picture of science. Most of the real work is incremental, and the community deserves most of the credit rather than the named geniuses.

Can I hold all five? Go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension, which only look that way, and rank the tensions by severity. Then tell me what the cheapest fix is and specifically which of (1)–(5) I'd have to give up or weaken. I'd rather be told one of them has to go than hear that everything is fine.
