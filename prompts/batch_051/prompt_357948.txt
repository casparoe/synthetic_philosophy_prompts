An argument has been circulating in my research group, and I want a precise diagnosis rather than a verdict from above. The conclusion strikes me as far too strong: it would make even ordinary talk of side effects depend on a finished theory of value. But I cannot find the step at which the argument cheats.

Consider an AI assistant given a bounded task — say, ordering a replacement part — and instructed to otherwise avoid side effects. The argument is presented as follows:

(1) Every action changes the world along indefinitely many dimensions, and any change outside the narrow envelope designated as "the task" is a side effect.
(2) An agent operating under a low-impact constraint must be able to tell, for each candidate action, whether its total side effects fall below the acceptable threshold.
(3) The only way to make that determination is to compare the world as it would be after the action with the world as it would have been had the agent done nothing, and to measure how large the value-relevant difference between those two worlds would be.
(4) Measuring value-relevant difference between whole worlds is just applying a value function: a ranking or scoring of world-histories by how good they are.
(5) Such a value function must cover every feature the action could change; if any feature is left out, the two worlds might diverge catastrophically along exactly that dimension while the comparison remains blind to it.
(6) So a low-impact constraint must be defined with respect to a complete value function over world-histories.
(7) A complete value function over world-histories is precisely what a full specification of value is.
(8) Therefore, short of a full specification of value, the instruction "avoid side effects" has no determinate content; the notion of a side effect is empty.

The only dodge I can construct is to define impact in terms of capability rather than value: an action is low-impact just in case, relative to doing nothing, it does not much reduce the range of future goals the agent could still achieve. But the argument's author replies that this merely relocates the problem, since choosing which goals to include and how to aggregate them is itself a value-laden specification.

My questions: (i) Is the argument valid as written, and if not, which numbered step first fails? (ii) What is the strongest conclusion the argument actually supports? (iii) What is the cost of giving up the step you identify — in particular, does the capability-based dodge survive, or does the argument's author win after all?
