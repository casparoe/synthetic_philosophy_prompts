In my physics class this year we derived the standard conservation laws and then went through Noether's theorem: roughly, each continuous symmetry of a physical system corresponds to a conserved quantity, so time-translation symmetry is paired with energy conservation, spatial-translation symmetry with momentum conservation, and rotational symmetry with angular momentum conservation. My teacher summed this up by saying that the symmetry is the reason the quantity is conserved — that the symmetry explains the conservation law. I could follow the mathematics, but I kept wondering whether explains is doing real work there. The derivation seems to show that a symmetry and a conservation law are two faces of a single formalism, not that one of them does the explaining and the other is merely the thing being explained. It feels a little like noticing that any equation relating force, mass, and acceleration contains all three quantities and then claiming that the equation explains mass.

I tried to settle this with a friend, and he proposed the following test: if one could start from a newly recognized symmetry, derive a conservation law that had never before been measured, and later confirm that law experimentally, then the symmetry would have genuinely explained the law. I am not sure this would test explanation rather than merely testing the internal consistency of a mathematical framework. I would like a careful answer to this: what is the strongest concrete test of the claim that symmetries genuinely explain conservation laws rather than merely redescribe them, what kind of result would count in favor of the explanatory claim and what kind would count against it, and what would such a test inevitably leave unsettled? I am especially interested in where the line falls between evidence for the mathematics and evidence that the symmetry story is the right explanation.
