Recently my work has forced me to think carefully about what we mean when we say one thing caused another. I work on models that estimate the probability that a particular action contributed to an adverse event, such as whether a driver's behaviour caused a collision. The modelling itself is tractable, but the word cause keeps reappearing in the requirements and in how the results are interpreted, and I have been reading Bertrand Russell's essay On the Notion of Cause to understand why the term feels so difficult to pin down. In that essay, Russell argues that the notion of cause is a survival from an earlier, pre-scientific way of thinking, modelled on one thing pushing or willing another; that it has no functional place in the physical sciences; and that the law of causation survives only because it is mistakenly thought to do no harm. He develops this point by discussing gravitational astronomy and the law of gravitation, which he says states a functional relation between configurations at different times rather than a relation of cause to effect.

I would like you to interpret that passage and then test Russell's argument against the later development of formal causal inference in statistics and computer science: structural causal models, do-calculus, and counterfactual reasoning. Does this body of work refute Russell's charge by giving cause a mathematically precise meaning that does genuine scientific work? Or does it vindicate his charge, because what these formalisms actually model is something else — invariance under intervention, probabilistic dependence, or information flow — rather than the old anthropomorphic notion of cause? Or is the correct verdict that the new formalisms let us ask well-defined causal questions precisely because they replace the notion with something Russell would have found unobjectionable? I want to know whether Russell's eliminativist conclusion survives this development, needs repair, or is dead, and which specific premise the development bears on.
