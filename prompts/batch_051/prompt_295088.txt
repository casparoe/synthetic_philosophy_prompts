I'm a novelist, not a philosophy person, so I'll probably mangle the theory. I'm drafting a historical novel set during a national awakening, and I've been reading around two ideas: invented tradition—all this stuff we think of as ancient, tartans and kilts and coronation ritual and folk costume, turns out to have been stitched together fairly recently, usually to make a group feel old and solid—and imagined communities, the idea that a nation is a bunch of strangers who will never meet all picturing themselves as the same "us" moving through one history together. Rewatching Braveheart recently I couldn't switch the lens off, because the film is about a nationalist uprising and also just is a machine for inventing tradition.

Read Braveheart through this lens for me, but skip the usual anachronism-pedantry—yes, the kilts are centuries off and the battle is missing its bridge, I know. What I want is a reading of the film as a portrait of nation-making, including the parts where it doesn't know it's doing the portrait. Pick two or three scenes and show what each one does: how Wallace's private vengeance gets widened into a war for a people, how the commoners get lit as a people while the nobles squabble over land and titles, how costume and ceremony make something recent look primordial. Where does the film show tradition being invented, and where does it simply pass off its own inventions as ancient fact? Is there any scene where it seems aware of what it's doing, or is it entirely unselfconscious? Then tell me where the reading stops paying rent: what in the film resists the invented-tradition/imagined-community account—the concrete grievance of occupation, the revenge plot, whatever the theory has to wave away.

Finish with what Braveheart does that the theory doesn't: what a working novelist can learn from its craft about making a nation feel real to a reader, beyond what Hobsbawm or Anderson would hand me. Hard cap: 700 words.
