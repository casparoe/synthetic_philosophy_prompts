Act as a philosopher working at the intersection of early modern metaphysics and contemporary moral psychology. I’m preparing a discussion section on gratitude, benefactors, luck, and the past, and I want you to work through a passage from Leibniz that I think gets at a deep question about whether gratitude requires a certain kind of freedom in the benefactor.

In Leibniz’s *Discourse on Metaphysics*, section II, he attacks the view that things are good merely because God wills them. The passage I have in mind runs:

"In saying, therefore, that things are not good according to any standard of goodness, but simply by the will of God, it seems to me that one destroys, without realizing it, all the love of God and all his glory; for why praise him for what he has done, if he would be equally praiseworthy in doing the contrary?"

Now apply this to a case about gratitude that Leibniz did not anticipate. Suppose Maia is pulled from a burning car by a stranger, Nico. Nico later tells her that he did not deliberate about whether to help; he simply acted from a long-trained emergency disposition. Maia then learns that Nico’s action was fully causally determined by his heredity, upbringing, and neural state, and that in any nearby possible world with the same laws and past he does exactly the same thing. Is Maia’s gratitude to Nico less fitting than it would be if Nico had faced genuine alternative possibilities? Does Leibniz’s argument—that praiseworthiness requires goodness grounded in a standard rather than in arbitrary will—imply anything about whether a benefactor must be non-arbitrary, or must have been able to do otherwise, for gratitude to be owed? I want you to separate carefully what Leibniz needs for love and praise of God from what gratitude to a human benefactor requires, and to mark the places where the analogy breaks. Also weigh whether gratitude partly tracks luck and the past in a way that sits badly with Leibniz’s later claim that everything true of an individual is already contained in their complete concept.

Please think carefully, start with a brief reconstruction of the passage before applying it, and do not just assert conclusions; earn each step. At the end, say how confident you are in the claims you make, and single out the point you are least sure of.
