Take Denis Villeneuve's film *Arrival* (adapted from Ted Chiang's "Story of Your Life") and read it through Borges's treatment of time. The obvious point of contact is "The Garden of Forking Paths," in which the sinologist Stephen Albert explains that Ts'ui Pên's novel and labyrinth are one and the same: a maze not of space but of time, a book that contains every possible future simultaneously. That sounds strikingly like the heptapod language through which Louise Banks comes to experience her own life as a simultaneous whole rather than a line, remembering a future she has already lived. But I suspect the analogy smooths over real differences. Borges's forking paths emphasise vertiginous alternatives — every choice producing every outcome — whereas *Arrival* seems to give Louise access to a single, determinate future, including her daughter's life and death, rather than a plurality of branches. Moreover, Borges moves between several models of time across his essays and stories, including circular time and an idealist refusal to take time as basic, so the reading has to specify which Borges is doing the work. Where does a Borgesian lens genuinely illuminate the film — its handling of apparent flashback and flashforward, its treatment of grief and foreknowledge, its suggestion that a writing system can reorganise perception — and where does the film pull away from Borges toward a different metaphysics of time? Does the film, by showing a character who knowingly lives a future she cannot alter, add something to the philosophical debate about time and choice that Borges's more abstract paradoxes do not, or does it quietly reframe those paradoxes as a problem about personal acceptance?
