Bayesian conditionalization looks to me like a non-negotiable commitment of practical reasoning: once you have learned something, you should weigh only the outcomes that remain live, and choices should not be driven by possibilities your evidence has ruled out. Yet this commitment seems to come apart in the counterfactual mugging. A perfect predictor tosses a fair coin; if it lands tails, it asks you for $100, and if it lands heads, it gives you $10,000 just in case it predicted that you would have paid had it landed tails. You are asked, so you know the coin landed tails and that paying now cannot influence the head-branch gift. Conditionalizing on that evidence tells you to refuse. Still, before the toss I would certainly have bound myself to pay if asked, since the policy is worth $4,950 in expectation, and I cannot shake the sense that the post-toss choice should not abandon the very policy that made the prior gamble rational. Give me the strongest case for debunking this intuition as an artifact of imagining a branch I know is non-actual, the strongest case for amending the theory so that an act may be evaluated by the policy it implements across ruled-out branches, and a negotiated verdict on which should yield, or the precise reading under which the two are compatible instead of merely relabeled.
