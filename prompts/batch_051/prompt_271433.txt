I teach a short adult-education course on photography and philosophy, and our first session ended on the transparency debate: the claim that looking at a photograph is, in some literal sense, a way of seeing the photographed object itself, because the camera works as a prosthetic eye and the print as a window, opposed to the view that a photograph is an image we inspect rather than a scene we see through. I would like to close the second session with a poem that lets the students feel the pull of both positions without my having to lecture at them.

Please write a sonnet of fourteen lines, in any rhyme scheme you think fits, and tell me which scheme you chose. The first eight lines should be spoken by someone genuinely seeing through a photograph: an adult looking at a snapshot of their mother as a child, describing the child and the place as if the speaker were there in the scene. The volta should come when the photograph's material features intrude — grain, curling edge, fading, the photographer's accidental framing — and the speaker realizes they are looking at an object, not through a window. The closing couplet should not settle the debate. It should leave open whether this intrusion is the loss of a felt presence or the start of seeing the photograph for what it actually is. A philosopher of photography should recognize both sides of the dispute and the structure of the turn, but it should also work for someone who has simply held an old family photograph and found themselves caught between those two ways of looking.
