I am a philosopher whose research is in normative ethics, but an administrative shuffle has put me in charge of a graduate seminar on free will and divine providence. While preparing the unit on Molinism, I asked a colleague in philosophy of religion for a concise introductory paragraph I could hand to students. He sent me the following, with the assurance that it is a fair, teachable overview of the position: "Molinism holds that God enjoys three kinds of knowledge. Natural knowledge covers necessary truths and possibilities; free knowledge covers what God has decided to create; and between them lies middle knowledge: God's pre-volitional knowledge of what every possible free creature would freely choose in any possible situation. Because God possesses this knowledge before deciding which world to actualize, he can survey all feasible worlds and actualize one in which each free agent's choices fit exactly with God's plan, without compromising libertarian freedom. This gives God comprehensive providential control: although every choice is made freely, nothing happens that God did not positively choose. The view's chief doctrinal cost is its commitment to conditionals about creatures who never exist; critics complain that nothing could make such conditionals true."

I am not trained in this literature, so I need an accuracy audit before I put this in front of students. My untutored worry is that the phrases "fit exactly with God's plan" and "positively choose" are doing more work than a careful Molinist would allow, but I do not know whether that is a real objection or just my training in ethics tugging at ordinary language. Please go through the summary claim by claim: indicate what a careful Molinist would accept, what is subtly overstated or conflated, and whether the conclusion about comprehensive providential control actually follows from the accurate material. I would like a corrected, teachable version to give the class, not a list of sources.
