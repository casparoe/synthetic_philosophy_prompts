I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about effort, cause everyone always says "the struggle is part of the reward" but I definitely don't buy it in every case. Going by my gut:

- My neighbor built a bookshelf by hand over like three weekends. It came out fine but basically the same as one you'd get at ikea, and the materials cost more. I still thought it was cooler, like the effort made it worth something extra even though the thing itself is the same.
- A kid in my class studied a ridiculous amount for a history essay and got an 83 with a note saying his argument was interesting but messy. Another kid wrote it the period before and got a 96. I'd rather have the 96, obviously, but I feel like the 83 kid's grade means more.
- My friend refuses to use a calculator and did a whole stats worksheet by hand. Same answers, took him hours. I think that's just pointless and kind of annoying.
- My cousin learned this hard guitar solo by ear over a whole summer. It honestly sounds the same as if he'd just watched a tutorial, but he says he "knows it in his bones." I believe him, and I think it's more his than if he'd been shown.
- My uncle dug out a big patch of his yard and then filled it back in because he wanted to see if he could. Same yard before and after, and he was proud of it. I don't get that at all, it seems like wasted effort to me.
- A runner who trained for years vs a runner with the same natural talent who didn't train but has the same 5k time. I think the trained one's time means more, but I can't say why.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? My best guess is something like: effort adds value when it changes you or when the thing becomes yours in some way, but not when you could've gotten the same result more easily. But then the B vs A case is wierd, because the 83 kid's effort didn't change the grade he got, unless having tried counts as a change. Please tell me what principle I should have, given these gut calls, and whether I'd have to give up any of the verdicts. Then tell me what the principle says about a case I keep going back and forth on: a kid who spends all year making a video game for a class project, and the game is honestly worse than a $10 game you could buy, but he learned to code doing it.
