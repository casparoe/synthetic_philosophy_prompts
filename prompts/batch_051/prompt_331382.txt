I'm taking a course on the philosophy of well-being, and the hedonism module has left me stuck on a question I expected to be straightforward. It is easy to state the hedonist's claim: pleasure is the thing all good lives have in common. But as soon as I try to say what makes an experience a pleasure, my ordinary confidence disappears. The cases we unreflectively call pleasures do not have much in common on their surface: a brief sensory delight, relief after a scare, the satisfaction of untangling a problem, being moved to tears by a piece of music. I want a working definition of "pleasure" (or "pleasant experience") precise enough to do real work in a theory of well-being, and I want to see it tested rather than merely stated. If "pleasure" has no common core, then hedonism may be treating a loose family of unlike things as a single foundational unit, so the definition matters.

Here are the cases I have been using to pressure any candidate definition. (1) The taste of cold water when you are extremely thirsty. (2) The wave of relief when you learn that a feared medical test has come back negative. (3) The low, steady nausea in the hour before a migraine. (4) Sitting in a bare room with nothing to attend to, feeling neither good nor bad. (5) Being brought to tears by a piece of music you love and would choose to hear again. (6) Scratching a mosquito bite hard enough to leave a mark, when the scratching stings but you cannot make yourself stop. (7) The quiet, tired satisfaction of finally solving a puzzle that had been irritating you. (8) The glee you feel when a rival is publicly humiliated, even though you suspect it reflects badly on you. Case (1) strikes me as an obvious pleasure, and (3) and (4) as obvious non-pleasures. I lean toward saying that (2) is relief rather than pleasure, and I find (7) a persuasive candidate for pleasure despite being so unlike (1); about (5), (6), and (8) I keep changing my mind.

Please propose a definition, run it through all eight cases, flag the verdicts it gets wrong or cannot deliver, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. Tell me which of your classifications is least secure. As you do this, please separate what is genuinely settled from what is contested among philosophers who work on pleasure, and do not present a contested account as if it were the consensus.
