I co-teach a summer ethics program for high-school juniors, about 18 students, 90-minute block. I want to run an activity that makes them feel, rather than just hear, the difference between valuing a place for what it gives us (instrumental), for the histories and relationships built around it (relational), and for its own sake (intrinsic). Design a structured game around a single local decision—I'm thinking a small urban creek that a developer wants to bury in a culvert—where the same proposal is argued and voted on three times under different ground rules. Round one: only reasons about human costs and benefits may be used. Round two: only reasons about relationships, memory, identity, and community history. Round three: no reasons may mention human interests at all, and anyone who slips loses their next vote. I want the rules to create a specific frustration: round one feels efficient but thin, round two feels personal but maybe sentimental, and round three feels both the most principled and the hardest to sustain. No lecture up front, and don't name intrinsic/relational/instrumental until the debrief. I need exact scenario details, role/stake cards, a token system, and a debrief that maps each round onto that distinction and then pushes on whether the three kinds of value are really separate. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—I'm especially worried the third round collapses into metaphor, like the creek "wanting" to be free, which is just hidden human feeling—and how to steer that without lecturing. And be explicit about which part of the philosophical debate the game can't reproduce.
