I am a professor of philosophy working at the intersection of decision theory, game theory, and the philosophy of agency. In three weeks I am presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on rationality under imperfect symmetry, hosted by a university research center whose regulars are microeconomic theorists, formal epistemologists, philosophers of mind, and a few computer scientists working on multi-agent learning. My paper defends a version of superrationality that I call deliberative superrationality, and I expect the question period to be punishing.

The audience, as precisely as I can characterize it: roughly half are economic theorists who treat Nash equilibrium as the default normative solution concept and are instinctively hostile to evidentialist reasoning; a quarter are formal epistemologists who accept that evidential decision theory gets Newcomb cases right but do not want to concede that it is decision theory full stop; the rest are split between philosophers of agency, who will care most about the distinction between act-level and policy-level reasons, and machine-learning researchers, who will ask what algorithm could implement my principle and whether it is learnable in decentralized training. None of them are superrationality partisans; several will find the very project faintly mystical.

The thesis I intend to defend up front is this. In a one-shot strategic interaction between agents who know they run the same deliberately adopted policy and know neither can causally influence the other, a policy is rationally admissible only if it satisfies a fixed-point test: for each candidate policy p, evaluate the payoff profile that would result if all agents in the symmetry class conformed to p, and ask whether a single reflective deliberator choosing p for all of them at once would strictly prefer p to every alternative policy thus evaluated. In the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma under common knowledge of a shared policy, this test selects mutual cooperation, because joint cooperation Pareto-dominates joint defection; defecting survives only as an act-level choice, not as a policy one can coherently select for all similarly situated agents. I also extend the view to approximate symmetry: when the other is merely similar rather than identical, the admissible policy maximizes a convex combination of the solo best-response payoff and the symmetric joint-adoption payoff, with the weight on the symmetric term encoding how confident the agent is that the other runs the same procedure. This yields a smooth transition from orthodox best-response reasoning to full superrationality, and it has the right edge behavior: if the other is known to run a different policy, the symmetric weight goes to zero and the view recommends defection against a known defector.

The argumentative path of the paper, compressed:

1. Causal decision theory forbids cooperation in the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma because it models the other's action as causally fixed; but that modeling choice throws away the information carried by common knowledge of a shared policy.

2. Evidential decision theory recovers cooperation in the perfect-twin case, but only by treating my act as evidence about the other's act. That leaves it hostage to the familiar non-deliberative common-cause counterexamples, and in merely similar cases it makes the degree of cooperation a function of a correlation coefficient rather than of any normative feature of the shared deliberative situation.

3. Deliberative superrationality needs no causal channel and no evidential channel; it works directly at the level of policy selection under joint adoption. Its normative force lies in the inconsistency of using a shared policy to predict the other's act while selecting one's own act as though no such policy were in force.

4. The approximate-symmetry extension shows the view is not hostage to the impossible twin assumption. It reproduces payoff-dominance reasoning in coordination games, makes cooperation proportional to the symmetry actually warranted, and collapses to orthodox best-response reasoning against agents known to run different policies.

I want a question-and-answer prep sheet, not a further defense. Please act as an expert philosophical interlocutor: someone who knows the formal decision theory cold and who also knows how each group in that room argues, what it considers decisive, and what it considers a nonstarter. Think carefully before writing. Begin by brainstorming the likely objections separately for the economists, the formal epistemologists, the philosophers of agency, and the machine-learning researchers; then merge, deduplicate, and order the result by expected damage, that is, roughly how likely the question is times how much trouble it causes.

The prep sheet should contain:
