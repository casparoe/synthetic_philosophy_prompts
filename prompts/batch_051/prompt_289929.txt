ok so I keep swinging between a Dworkin-ish luck egalitarianism and the worry that the whole enterprise is kind of disrespectful, and I'd like an audit of whether the package I've ended up with actually hangs together. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) inequalities that trace to brute luck—genes, birthplace, congenital conditions—are unjust and society should compensate for them;
(2) inequalities that trace to genuinely voluntary gambles are not unjust: if you knowingly take a fair risk and it goes south, you don't have a claim to be made whole;
(3) if you could have insured against a foreseeable risk and chose not to, you've turned the downside into option luck, and you can't claim compensation for it;
(4) in practice the state shouldnt be in the business of sorting people into 'chose badly' and 'unlucky'; its policies should be choice-insensitive (universal healthcare, no blame-based means testing, that sort of thing);
(5) the deeper point of equality is equal respect, so a state that makes help conditional on showing you didnt choose your situation treats people in a way that's already objectionable, and not merely inefficient;
(6) still, my gut says that if you get paralyzed in a motorbike crash you courted and I get paralyzed by a congenital spinal condition, your claim on social support is weaker than mine;
(7) and the 'everything is brute luck in the end' slide doesn't move me: even if your risk tolerance was shaped by your upbringing, your gamble is still yours.

Go through these and tell me which of them are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, how bad each real tension is, what the cheapest fix for each would be, and which single revision would make the whole set most coherent.
