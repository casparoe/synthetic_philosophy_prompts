Next week I present a paper at my department's philosophy of science workshop. My thesis is that al-Ghazālī's occasionalism is not the caricature that says causation is an illusion and that anything may follow upon anything for no reason at all; properly read, it is the view that created things possess no causal powers of their own, and that the regular sequences we observe are directly created by God on the occasion of earlier events according to a customary course (ʿāda) that God freely sustains and could in principle interrupt. I argue that this is a coherent account of lawful regularity and induction, that it does not make scientific explanation impossible, and that it handles miracles better than views that posit independently efficacious natural powers.

The workshop audience is philosophically sharp but likely unsympathetic in a specific way: two Humean regularity theorists who take causation to be nothing over and above constant conjunction, perhaps with best-system laws doing the organizing; one powers-based causal realist who holds that natural kinds have dispositional essences and that laws are grounded in those dispositions; one philosopher of religion sympathetic to concurrentism (the view that both God and creatures are genuine causes) who finds occasionalism too eager to deny secondary causation; and several sharp graduate students with no settled stake in any of these camps.
