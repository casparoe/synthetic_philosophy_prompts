I've been chewing on an asymetry in U.S. antidiscrimination law and ordinary moral judgment, and I can't tell whether there's a principled line behind it or just a historical accident.

Take two junior designers at the same ad agency. One is fired after the boss finds out she's Muslim; the other is fired after the boss finds out she's a Communist. Both firings are for creed-like commitments central to the person's identity, both are irrelevant to designing banner ads, and both groups have long histories of being persecuted. Yet in most of the U.S. the first firing is illegal—religion is a protected class under federal law—and nearly everyone I know treats it as obviously wrongful. The second firing is legal in most states, and many of those same people would defend it or shrug. I find I have the same split in my own gut and I can't extract the principle that generates it.

I want the strongest principled justification you can find for treating religion as a suspect ground of discrimination while leaving political belief mostly unprotected — or, if you think no such justification works, an explanation of why the asymmetry persists anyway. Run through the obvious candidates and test them against nearby cases: immutability (religion and party membership are both chosen-ish, so this looks weak), history of persecution (political dissidents have been persecuted plenty), centrality to identity and conscience (seems to cover both), and any other candidate you can construct. Then give me an honest verdict: should we keep the religion/politics asymmetry, collapse it in one direction (extend something like protected-class status to political belief), or collapse it in the other (stop treating religion as specially suspect)? If a narrower principle survives—say, only some political commitments count, or the two grounds are suspect for different reasons—state the principle and say where it draws the line.
