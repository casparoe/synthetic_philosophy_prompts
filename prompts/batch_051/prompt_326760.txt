I serve on the amnesty committee of a truth commission in a country emerging from a generation of military rule. Under our enabling statute, the commission may grant individual amnesty for politically motivated crimes committed during the conflict, extinguishing both criminal and civil liability, provided the applicant gives full and truthful disclosure of every relevant act. The case before us involves a former army intelligence captain, now in preventive detention, who has confessed in detail to his part in the disappearance and killing of four student activists in 1989. During his testimony he disclosed information that led investigators to a previously unknown mass grave containing nine additional victims. His account has been corroborated by contemporaneous military records and by two fellow officers, and our investigative unit believes the disclosure is now complete. He remains, however, entirely unrepentant: he describes the killings as an unfortunate but necessary measure of counterinsurgency and, when asked whether he would apologize to the families, said he would not perform a regret he does not feel. The families of the four named victims have petitioned us to refuse amnesty so that he can be prosecuted for murder; they argue that shielding an unrepentant killer would mock the very idea of reconciliation. Several commissioners disagree, maintaining that our mandate is an exchange of truth for amnesty, that the statute deliberately says nothing about remorse, and that denying amnesty here would teach future applicants that candor buys them nothing and thereby dry up the disclosures the commission was created to obtain. There is a further complication: the captain did not volunteer the mass grave unprompted. He initially denied any role in the nine additional deaths and admitted to them only after being confronted with flight logs and a subordinate's testimony. His lawyers argue that this is simply how full disclosure works in practice and that our statute imposes no requirement of spontaneity; the victims' families reply that evidence extracted by confrontation is not the kind of truth the commission was meant to purchase.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's hearing. It should recommend a concrete decision — grant amnesty, deny it, or grant it subject to conditions if you judge the statute can be read to permit them — and it should engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than merely restating the statutory language. Aim for roughly 900 words, written in American English.
