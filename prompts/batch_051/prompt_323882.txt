I'm a retired chemist, so be patient if I get the political theory wrong. I've been reading about this idea of performance legitimacy — the claim that a government can be legitimate not because of elections or consent but because it delivers prosperity, order, and stability. People usually point to China, and to Singapore under the PAP.

I'd like to actually test the idea against Singapore, which I know a bit about. The rough story, as I understand it: Singapore is expelled from Malaysia in 1965 as a poor, vulnerable, multiethnic island; Lee Kuan Yew's PAP then delivers decades of growth, public housing, low crime, and tight political control — weak opposition, restrictions on speech and assembly. Most people don't revolt, and plenty seem to accept it.

Heres what I want: take Singapore from 1965 to the present as a case study for performance legitimacy, and run the analysis both ways. First, put the theory to work: what does it say the government was facing, what options did it have, and what should count as evidence that legitimacy has actually been earned? Where does consent show up, if it shows up at all? Then turn it around: what does the Singapore case do to the theory? Does it confirm it, or does it expose that the theory is just relabeling 'people put up with it because they're fed and safe' as 'legitimacy'? Is Singapore a case the theory handles well, handles badly, or was never designed for?

Im not asking whether Singapore ended up a good place. I want to know if the theory does real explanatory work or just dresses up outcomes.
