I've been going in circles on the Born-rule problem in Many-Worlds for a while—the usual rationalist-blog back-and-forth about Deutsch, Wallace, all that—and I've ended up with a list of the objections to the decision-theoretic derivation that I already know. Don't give me any of these, or a rewording of them:

1. It's circular. The axioms (measurement-neutrality, "games that differ only by a unitary are the same game," that sort of thing) only look plausible because they encode the idea that squared amplitudes are the weights that matter. Unpack them and the conclusion is already sitting there.

2. It gives the wrong kind of probability. Even if the theorem is right, it tells a rational agent how to *act*—how to bet across branches—but it doesn't tell her what to *believe*. In a deterministic multiverse there's still no story about what a self-locating credence could even mean, so the epistemic version of the Born rule is left untouched.

3. The result is about an idealized agent who knows the full pure state and maximizes expected utility without computational limits. Real reasoners don't know the state, can't compute branch weights, and have messy non-Savage preferences, so it's unclear why the theorem should bind us.

4. It assumes branches are sharp. Real decoherence is only approximate and interference terms never vanish exactly. If the branch structure is fuzzy and emergent, the "games" and "payoffs" the proof trades in may not be well-defined.

5. It can't rule out branch-counters without begging the question. Someone who genuinely prefers to weight branches equally, or by amplitude-to-the-fourth, has preferences that are perfectly coherent by ordinary standards; the decision-theoretic setup only declares them irrational after assuming a norm that priviledges amplitude-squared.

6. It can look like a restatement. The Born probabilities come out of the inner-product/L2 structure that's already in the formalism, so there's a worry that the proof is a complicated way of saying "the math already uses amplitude-squared."

I want one more objection. It has to be genuinely different from all six—not a special case, not a combination, not the same point with different jargon. It can be aimed at the decision-theoretic project in general or at some specific step. Then tell me honestly whether the new objection is actually any good, or whether I should drop it.
