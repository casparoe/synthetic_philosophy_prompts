I'm a software engineer, and I've been following the argument about whether large language models can actually report their own reasoning when they produce a chain of thought, and its been stuck in the same place for what feels like a while. One camp says the CoT a model prints out is basically a press release issued after the fact: the model was trained to predict plausible text, so when you ask it to explain an answer it pattern-matches onto explanations rather than introspecting over what its own activations did. Another camp pushes back that this is too cynical, because in practice the intermediate steps constrain the final answer a lot — if CoT were pure rationalization, changing the CoT shouldn't change the final answer nearly as often as it does in experiments where people steer or edit the steps. And then there's a third group that says the whole question is confused: once the weights are fixed there's just a forward pass, and anything you say about the model's reasoning is a post-hoc interpretation, not a thing you can read off like a log file.

My working theory is that the real fight isn't about any particular experimental result but about what would even count as evidence. The behavioral people want the model to pass some kind of honesty test, the mechanistic people want the tokens and the activations to line up in an inspectable way, and the third group thinks both are chasing an object that doesn't exist. Is that diagnosis right, or is there an actual empirical question underneath that could settle things? What kind of disagreement is this, and what would progress look like given your diagnosis? If the honest answer is that no progress should be expected, I'd rather hear that and why.
