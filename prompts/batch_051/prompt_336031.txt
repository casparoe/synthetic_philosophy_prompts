I'm a novelist, and I'm working on a scene where a campus speaker gets disinvited after student protests. The characters keep reaching for Mill, and at a dinner party last week someone actually summarized him as: the only legitimate reason to restrict someone's liberty is to prevent harm to others. But what struck me is how this one-liner gets stretched to cover wildly different positions. One person used it to argue that hate speech must be basically unrestricted because words can't directly harm you, while another used it to argue that hate speech should be banned because psychological harm is still harm, and a third said it applies only to the law but not to social pressure like disinviting someone. I want to go back to the actual essay, On Liberty, rather than the slogan. In the introductory chapter Mill says the subject is civil or social liberty, not the so-called liberty of the will, and that society's own mandates can be a worse tyranny than political oppression. Then in the chapter on thought and discussion there's the passage where he claims silencing an opinion robs the human race, posterity as well as the living, and those who disagree with the opinion even more than those who hold it. What are the different precise principles that one-liner could be claiming — especially around what counts as "harm," whose harm, whether it's a claim about law or also social pressure, and whether offence or distress counts? Which of those readings are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are actually false, and which reading would actually settle the disinvitation in my novel? I want a menu of readings, not a single interpretation.
