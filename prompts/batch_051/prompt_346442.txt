I sit on the independent oversight board of a research institute that develops adaptive machine-learning systems, and this week we must decide on a deployment plan that raises an uncomfortable question: whether one artificial system may serve as the ongoing guardian of another. The institute has built a training-and-supervision system (internally called the steward) that designs curricula, sets learning targets, selects corrective interventions, and can suspend operation of a second system (the learner) that it has trained. The team now proposes to deploy the pair in a live setting where the steward continues to exercise these functions without prior human approval for routine interventions: it may order retraining, adjust the learner's objective weights, or take it offline when it judges that necessary. Human operators can veto an action after the fact, but the proposed operating agreement does not require any named human to approve, review, or answer for routine developmental decisions. The board is split. One camp says this is an unacceptable abdication: if a system is to 'raise' another, a named human must answer for each significant turn in its development. The other camp says that is exactly backwards — a steady automated guardian will intervene more consistently than episodic human oversight, and after-the-fact veto power is the only accountability that still means anything. I have volunteered to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Draft the opinion I should send. It must recommend a concrete course of action (authorize, reject, or authorize with conditions, with the conditions specified and a time frame), reason from a defensible view of what actually matters in a guardianship relation — among humans, or between a human and an AI, or between two AI systems — rather than restating either camp's slogans, and give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the side the recommendation rejects. We need something the institute could actually implement, not a set of principles in the abstract.
