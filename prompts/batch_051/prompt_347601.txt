In metaphysics and the philosophy of mind, appeals to conceivability run the inference almost exclusively in one direction. A philosopher who can conceive of a scenario after careful reflection — a creature physically identical to us but with no consciousness at all, or a mind existing without any body — is widely taken to have offered fallible but genuine evidence that the scenario is possible. But a philosopher who cannot conceive of a scenario is rarely taken to have offered evidence that it is impossible; the failure is explained away as a limitation of the imaginer rather than a feature of the thing imagined. That is a strange asymmetry, because the two cases look like exact mirrors. Is there a principle that licenses treating a successful act of conception as prima facie evidence of possibility without also licensing treating an unsuccessful act of conception as prima facie evidence of impossibility? Give the strongest such principle you can find, test it against nearby cases — a novice who cannot conceive of a correct but abstract proof, a person in a small, flat environment who cannot conceive of curved space that is nonetheless actual — and say honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
