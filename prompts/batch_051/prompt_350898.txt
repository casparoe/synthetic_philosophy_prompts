Write a villanelle about Scheffler's afterlife conjecture: the idea that a great deal of what we value in our own lives already tacitly presupposes that human beings will go on after we ourselves are gone. Let one refrain carry the obvious harm of human extinction—the deaths themselves—and let the other carry the subtler harm the conjecture names: the way the loss of a shared human future would quietly hollow out the projects, attachments, and meanings that seem to depend only on the present. The refrains should do philosophical work rather than merely repeat: their returns should enact how this dependence on a collective future keeps resurfacing once one begins to look for it. Keep the "afterlife" strictly secular and collective, with no suggestion of supernatural survival. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Scheffler's argument would recognize it, and coherent enough that a reader encountering the idea for the first time could follow it as a poem.
