I am a retired physical scientist, recently drawn into philosophy, and I have become puzzled by how much work philosophers make the phrase "following a rule" do. I would like a working definition of that phrase—or, if it really names two different things, two definitions—that handles the following cases. A child who has been taught the standard column algorithm answers 68 + 57 = 125. The tide goes out on a schedule a physical oceanographer could predict to the minute. A first-time cook follows a written recipe for a sauce they have never made. A chess engine, running a fixed evaluation function, chooses its next move. A fluent speaker produces the sentence "the dog the man fed barked" without being able to state the syntactic rule at work. A person who has never before added numbers above 56 answers 125, and I ask what makes this a continuation of the same rule rather than a different rule that agrees with their past on every small sum checked so far. I am confident the first case is rule-following and the tide is not; for the others I want a reasoned verdict. Propose the definition, run it through each case, and where it yields a surprising or no answer, either revise it or defend that answer.
