I am a PhD student working on learning and self-regulation, and I keep running into an asymmetry in how we judge cognition directed at one's own cognition.

In education, metacognition is treated as close to an unqualified good. Students are taught to monitor their comprehension while reading, to predict which material they have not yet mastered, and to plan their study around those predictions; a student who notices that she does not yet understand a passage is praised for having exactly the capacity instruction is trying to build. In skilled performance and in parts of clinical psychology, the same reflective stance is treated as a liability. Coaches warn experienced athletes away from attending to the mechanics of their own stroke or footwork, on the grounds that component-level attention makes the movement disintegrate. Therapists treating rumination try to reduce an activity that sounds, at a coarse grain, like the one educators cultivate: sustained attention to one's own thoughts and feelings.

At a coarse grain the object is the same in all these cases, so thinking about your thinking cannot be good full stop. But I do not want to settle for the suspicion that we simply call the exercise metacognition when it helps and overthinking when it hurts. What is the strongest single principle that sorts the cases, and does it survive contact with the hard ones?

Give a committed formulation of whatever distinction is being tracked. Candidate axes you may test include whether the monitored process is already automatized; whether monitoring runs concurrently with the task or is applied when the task is over; whether the object is an uncertain representation one can revise, such as one's understanding or memory, versus a fluent procedure one should leave alone; whether the goal is to calibrate for later or to control now. I want the best version, even if it uses more than one axis, but I do not want a menu of disconnected options.

Then test your principle against these cases before endorsing it: a novice learning a serve by verbalizing its steps; an expert whose serve deteriorates when she verbalizes the same steps; a student who checks her comprehension after each paragraph; an anxious person lying awake mentally rehearsing the day's conversation; and a meditation instruction to notice thoughts arise without engaging them. Does one principle handle all five, and does it explain why the first and third get prescribed while the second and fourth get proscribed? If no principle survives, say which side of the original asymmetry should be revised: the educational enthusiasm for metacognition, the clinical and athletic suspicion of self-focused attention, or both.
