In his essay "Of Tragedy," David Hume writes: "We find that common liars always magnify, in their narrations, all kinds of danger, pain, distress, sickness, deaths, murders, and cruelties; as well as joy, beauty, mirth, and magnificence. It is an absurd secret, which they have for pleasing their company, fixing their attention, and attaching them to such marvellous relations, by the passions and emotions, which they excite." I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I want to test whether Hume is entitled to use this observation as evidence about tragic pleasure. Construct a thought experiment in which a normal adult takes net pleasure in a true, artlessly narrated account of another person's suffering, where the pleasure is best explained by relief from ennui—the account Hume borrows from the Abbé Dubos and illustrates with this passage—but cannot be explained by the conversion of painful passions through eloquence or aesthetic form. Constraints: one ordinary-life setting, no fiction, no theatre, no sadism, no morally unusual characters, and the narration must be flat and unadorned. After the scenario, show the reasoning of the conversion account, the fictionality-awareness account associated with Fontenelle, and the relief-from-ennui account, each applied to the case. Then identify the structural feature that makes the case favor relief over conversion, and finish by stating whether this shows that the conversion account is wrong about tragedy itself or only that Hume's "common liars" observation and his positive theory explain different kinds of case.
