So I've been running a small online marketplace for art supplies with my sister, and we just added seller karma scores because our buyers kept saying they were scared of getting scammed. A week in, a friend who does game theory sends me this argument and now I can't tell whether the score is doing anything at all:

(1) The point of a karma score is to let a buyer rationally trust a seller they've never dealt with.
(2) The score just aggregates ratings from earlier buyers, who are strangers to the current buyer too.
(3) A rational buyer can treat those ratings as evidence about the seller only if she has reason to think the raters are honest and competent.
(4) Since she doesn't know the raters individually, her only possible reason to think they're honest and competent would have to come from the raters' own reputations on the platform.
(5) But those reputations are built the same way, from ratings left by still earlier users, so justifying trust in the raters requires justifying trust in those users, and so on.
(6) The platform has finitely many users, so the chain bottoms out in users whose word is accepted with no reputation behind it.
(7) Accepting someone's word with no reputation behind it is exactly the leap of faith the karma score was supposed to replace.
(8) So all the score ever does is push that leap of faith further back; it never gives anyone a rational basis for trusting a stranger.

He says the same move applies to any reputation-like system: credit scores, restaurant reviews, academic citations, all of it. I want to say hes wrong, because our buyers visibly do trust the score and it seems to work, but I can't locate the exact step where he slides. Which premise is weakest, and what do I give up by rejecting it? Or if hes right, what does that mean for the score we just built?
