I am teaching Kazuo Ishiguro's novel The Remains of the Day in a course on philosophy and literature, and I would like you to read it through the ancient dispute over whether freedom from emotional disturbance is a good and at what cost: the Stoic ideal of apatheia, the Epicurean ideal of ataraxia, and the broader idea of equanimity. Take the butler-narrator Stevens as the test case. His ideal of dignity appears built on precisely the composure those traditions praise: he keeps serving composedly while his father is dying upstairs, he refuses for decades to acknowledge his feeling for the housekeeper Miss Kenton, and he narrates the ruin of what he might have had in a tone so even that the reader is not always sure he has fully felt the loss. Yet the novel quietly suggests the reverse: that his calm is not an achieved indifference to what cannot be controlled, but a deep self-deception; that it made him a passive instrument of Lord Darlington's moral failures precisely because he refused to let anything disturb him; and that it cost him whatever was left of love, honesty, and an examined life.

I want a rigorous interpretation rather than a plot summary. In particular: by Stoic lights, would Stevens's composure even qualify as apatheia, or is it a counterfeit tranquility that rests on exactly the kind of false value judgments the Stoics meant to remove — treating professional standing, a conception of dignity, and the prestige of Darlington Hall as goods rather than indifferents? Where does the novel stand on whether such freedom from disturbance is desirable, and where does it resist or complicate the philosophical framework? And what does Ishiguro's treatment of Stevens add to the philosophical debate that propositional argument alone cannot supply? Aim for an interpretation I could defend in a classroom discussion of the novel.
