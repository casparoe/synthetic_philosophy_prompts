As someone working through the literature on dispositions in metaphysics, I have been struck by how recognizable, and how intractable, the conditional-analysis debate has become. The simple analysis says an object is disposed to manifest M in response to stimulus S just in case it would M if it underwent S. The counterexample literature has by now settled into a taxonomy. Finks remove the disposition or its basis when the stimulus occurs: a fragile glass watched over by a mechanism that would make it shatterproof in the instant before impact would not break if struck, yet it seems fragile. Masks block the manifestation without removing the disposition: a lethal poison administered together with an antidote that would prevent death would not kill if ingested, yet it seems lethal—and adding an intrinsic causal basis appears not to help, since the basis is present while the manifestation is blocked. Mimics produce the manifestation without the disposition: an object that would break if struck only because an independent device would shatter it at the same moment satisfies the conditional, yet seems not to be fragile. The literature has responded with refinements—intrinsic bases, ceteris paribus clauses, quantifiers over suitable conditions, patterns across nearby cases—and each refinement has been met with a further family of cases. I cannot detect convergence: the same cases are read by one side as refuting semantic claims and by the other as artifacts of an overstrict analysis or as evidence that the analysis must hold only under normal conditions.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because it is not one debate. One project concerns the truth-conditions of ordinary disposition ascriptions; another concerns what dispositionality is in the world; a third concerns how a correct counterfactual analysis should handle interfering conditions. If that is right, the counterexample method keeps generating cases that are decisive for one project while being merely distracting, or evidentially ambiguous, for the others.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the disagreement substantive, verbal, or a disagreement about which possible cases count as admissible data? Is a hidden disagreement about counterfactuals, or about what a manifestation is, doing the work? Be concrete about what each side must be committed to for your diagnosis to hold. Then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis—a distinction, a formal result, empirical work on ordinary judgment, a change of question—or explain why convergence should not be expected at all.
