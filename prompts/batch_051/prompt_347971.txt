For a colleague's retirement dinner, I have agreed to give the opening toast, and I mean it to be a real toast rather than a paper: about five minutes, warm, and listenable by a room of mostly non-philosophers. The colleague has spent four decades on Plato's later thought, particularly the Laws, and there will be a modest circle of ancient philosophy specialists present among family, staff, and administrators. I want Plato's writings on drinking to do genuine structural work in the speech. The Laws treats carefully arranged drunkenness as a kind of controlled test of character and self-knowledge, a safe way to learn what a person is like when his usual defenses are down, and the Symposium depicts a party in which the wine is deliberately moderated so that conversation, rather than intoxication, sets the evening's course. The hard and interesting part is that I will be delivering this while proposing the first drink of the evening, in praise of a man who thought harder than almost anyone about exactly that act. Please write the toast so that the Plato specialist in the room sees the Laws and the Symposium working under nearly every passage, while someone who has never opened Plato hears only a graceful, affectionate tribute from one colleague to another.
