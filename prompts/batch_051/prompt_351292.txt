I'm taking an undergraduate course on Kant this term, and in lecture the professor gave what he called a "working summary" of the distinction between intuitions and concepts. My notes, cleaned up, read:

"Kant's basic move is to distinguish two faculties. Intuition is the passive faculty by which objects are given to us; it delivers raw sensory material — colors, sounds, pains — and is in that sense singular and immediate. The understanding, by contrast, is active and applies concepts, which are general: they are the words and categories we use to sort the manifold of intuition. On Kant's view, neither faculty gets us knowledge on its own; as he put it, a concept with no corresponding intuition has no content, and an intuition with no concept is 'blind.' So perception is never a passive copying of the world. Every intuition we consciously experience is already brought under concepts by the understanding; the mind contributes the organization, not just the filing. That is why Kant concludes that we cannot know things as they are in themselves: all knowledge involves our own conceptual contribution."

I suspect some of this is right and some of it subtly off, and my study group keeps going around in circles about it. In particular, I'm not sure intuitions are best described as raw sensory material, and I don't know whether it's right that perception is "already conceptualized" in the way the summary suggests. Could you give an accuracy audit of this summary? I'd like to know what should be corrected, what a careful Kant scholar would accept as a fair simplification for an intro course, and what is mistaken in a way that matters for the Transcendental Deduction. Does the final step to things in themselves actually follow from the accurate parts, or is that an overstatement?
