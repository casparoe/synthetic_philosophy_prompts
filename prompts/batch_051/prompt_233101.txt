I am a PhD student in moral philosophy working on what we owe to benefactors—and on whether gratitude is even the right frame for what we owe to luck and to the past. Before going deeper into the debates, I want to fix in advance what a theory of gratitude is answerable to. Please write the desiderata for an adequate theory of gratitude: the phenomena it must capture, the cases that serve as tiebreakers between accounts, the distinctions it must respect, and the puzzles it must dissolve or explain away. By a theory of gratitude I mean an account of what gratitude is, when it is fitting or owed, and how it relates to what the benefactor intended, what the beneficiary actually received, and the historical good fortune of having been benefited at all. The target is a theory adequate for reflective agents deciding how to respond to their own benefactors and good fortune, not merely a third-person taxonomy of cases.

Please make the list exacting. Whatever its final shape, the list should force an account to take positions on at least the following:

- The attitude-versus-duty split: whether gratitude is fundamentally a feeling, a virtue, a debt, or an action-guiding requirement, and how the account handles states in which these come apart—someone who feels warmly but never acts, and someone who acts helpfully out of cold obligation.
- The benefactor's intention: whether gratitude requires the benefactor to have acted for the beneficiary's sake, and how the account treats the dutiful benefactor who does not particularly care about you, the unwitting benefactor who helps without knowing it, the mixed-motive benefactor, and the malevolent act that backfires into a benefit.
- Cost and outcome: whether the benefactor's sacrifice matters independently of the benefit conferred, and what to say about the costly failed attempt, the trivial effort that yields a large windfall, and the benefit that is squandered or transformed by the beneficiary after it is received.
- Unwanted and paternalistic benefits: when a benefit is imposed on a recipient who did not ask for it, or rests on a judgment that the recipient is incompetent, the theory must say how gratitude and resentment are related and whether one can properly cancel the other.
- Impersonal, indeterminate, and historical beneficiaries: gratitude toward ancestors, institutions, nature, or blind luck. An account must either make literal sense of these or justify treating them as metaphor, and it must explain what, if anything, is fitting or owed when there is no identifiable benefactor with whom one could reciprocate.
- Ingratitude: what counts as ingratitude, whether it is a defect of feeling, judgment, behavior, or some combination, and whether gratitude or its expression can be legitimately demanded or enforced by benefactors or third parties.

For each desideratum, give the case or structural consideration that motivates it rather than just a label. Mark each desideratum as load-bearing or negotiable, and say briefly what makes it so. Flag any pair or larger set of desiderata that looks difficult to satisfy jointly, and explain where the tension comes from.

In a short closing section, briefly score three ideal-typical accounts against your list—a debt/reciprocity account, a benefactor-intention account, and a fitting-emotion account—but keep the weight on the desiderata and their motivating cases rather than on exegesis. Write the response in American English, and do not include citations, references, or a reading list.
