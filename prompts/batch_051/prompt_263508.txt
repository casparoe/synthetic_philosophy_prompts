I am a machine learning engineer, and most of the philosophy of consciousness I try to read either collapses into mystery-talk or sounds suspiciously like things I already build: agents with world models, latent state estimates, learned auxiliary prediction heads, a self-model that tracks the agent's own hidden state, and a language head trained to report on internal variables. I am fluent in this vocabulary but not in philosophy, and I have been trying to determine whether higher-order theories of consciousness are making a claim my vocabulary can express, or whether the interesting part of the claim is exactly what my vocabulary drops on the floor.

The target is the family of higher-order theories. In the actualist higher-order-thought version, a first-order mental state — say, a visual representation of red — is conscious just when there occurs a distinct, assertoric, non-inferential higher-order representation to the effect that one is in that state. The higher-order representation itself is not conscious unless it is in turn targeted by a still higher representation; the regress is typically halted by allowing these higher-order thoughts to be unconscious. The dispositionalist version says the relevant higher-order representation need not actually occur: it must merely be poised, or readily available, to occur. The self-representational version denies that two states are needed: a state is conscious when it represents itself in the right way. Two objections matter to me. First, misrepresentation: if the higher-order state can represent "I am seeing red" while the first-order state is green or absent, does the theory imply a conscious experience with no matching first-order content, and is that a reductio or a feature? Second, the explanatory gap: why should adding a higher-order representation of a state make there be something it is like for the system, rather than merely adding another unlit computational layer?

Restate each of these three positions in the vocabulary I actually use — latent states, world model rollouts, policy inputs, auxiliary self-prediction heads, a language-report head, privilege at training time versus runtime, gradient-blocked monitoring, and hallucinated reports — being as faithful as you can rather than using the translation as disguised criticism. Then give me the residue report: which parts map cleanly onto self-monitoring and meta-representation, which parts you had to fudge, and which parts have no counterpart at all. In particular, does consciousness on these theories just become "being flagged by a self-monitoring head," and if so, has the hard problem disappeared or been relocated? Does the actualist–dispositionalist dispute amount to the question whether the monitor must actually run forward on the current state, or is its availability in the weights enough? Does self-representation correspond to a self-referential embedding, and does that do explanatory work at runtime? Conclude by telling me whether what failed to translate is evidence that my vocabulary is missing something, or that the higher-order debate was less precise than it looked.
