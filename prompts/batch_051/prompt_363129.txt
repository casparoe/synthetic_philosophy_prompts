I returned recently from an interdisciplinary workshop on collective behavior, and I have been turning over an argument I heard there, made by a biologist who studies social insects. His claim was that an ant colony is not merely analogous to an organism; it is, in the strictest sense, a single minded individual — the colony as a whole perceives, decides, remembers, and has interests, in much the way a human being does. His favored image was the familiar one: the colony is a brain, each ant is a neuron, pheromone trails and antennal contacts play the role of action potentials and synaptic connections, and the colony's nest-site choice is a decision in the same sense in which my deciding to raise my hand is a decision. From this he drew two conclusions: first, that colony-level behavior is genuinely cognitive and the colony is an individual thinker; second, that it is therefore not a category mistake to speak of the welfare, perhaps even the moral considerability, of the colony as such, over and above the welfare of any particular ant.

I would like to be prepared to audit this analogy properly in my seminar rather than merely gesture at its problems. Please give me the full treatment. Set out the mapping explicitly — ant, queen, pheromone trail, colony-level foraging decision, nest-site choice, and whatever plays the role of memory — against their brain counterparts, and mark where each correspondence is strained. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the two conclusions — the colony as an individual thinker, and the colony as a bearer of welfare — from the ones that are merely superficial. In particular, I want you to identify which features of a brain the analogy quietly imports into the colony picture: centralized control, a single integrating mechanism, a clean distinction between the goals of the whole and the goals of the parts, a unified point of view, perhaps top-down regulation of the units. Ask whether the colony actually has them. Finally, tell me whether either conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if the brain analogy has been doing more work than it can carry, say what a better analogy would be and what it could legitimately do.
