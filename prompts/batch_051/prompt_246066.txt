I want a fair and precise second assessment of one student's answer, so I am going to show it to you without telling you my own reading of it.

Exam question:

Do we have special obligations to our compatriots — that is, to people who share our nationality — or is compatriot partiality simply an arbitrary preference for those who are in some way similar to us? Present the strongest argument you can for each side, and state which you judge more convincing.

Student answer:

"The strongest argument for compatriot partiality begins from the fact that not all special concern is arbitrary. The obligations of parents to children, and the permissions friends have to help one another before helping strangers, arise from the relationships themselves rather than from any calculation about overall welfare. National membership is plausibly another relationship of the same kind. Compatriots share a language, a public culture, a collective memory, and — at least in the standard case — the same political institutions; these ties give them a claim on one another that outsiders lack. National membership is, for most people, unchosen. But this does not damage the argument: our families are also unchosen, yet no one denies that family membership generates special duties. Indeed, involuntariness brings nationality closer to the family than to friendship, and so strengthens the associative analogy rather than weakening it. A second consideration points in the same direction. A country's institutions supply security, infrastructure, education, and other public goods that its members benefit from; those who accept these goods while denying that they owe anything special to their compatriots would be free-riding on a cooperative scheme. The strongest cosmopolitan objection is that drawing a moral line at national boundaries is as arbitrary as drawing one at race or sex. But nationality differs from race or sex: it is a collective project that its members can endorse, revise, and make their own, not a merely ascribed category. And equal moral worth does not require treating everyone identically; it requires only that we not discount anyone's interests without a justifying reason. The special relationship among compatriots supplies exactly such a reason. The associative and fair-play arguments therefore jointly establish that compatriots have special claims on us, and I judge that conclusion more convincing than the cosmopolitan alternative."
