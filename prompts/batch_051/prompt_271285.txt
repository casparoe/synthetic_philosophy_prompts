Here's a thing I've been turning over that ends in a conclusion I can't accept, and I want someone to tell me which premise is doing the damage. The setup is the absent-minded driver puzzle, but with my own argument strapped onto it. A driver on a one-way highway has to take the second of two exits. If he takes the first exit he gets 0; if he takes the second he gets 4; if he drives past both he gets 1. The catch is that at each exit he cant tell whether it's the first or the second, and he can't remember whether he already passed one, so his behavior has to be the same at both. Let p be the probability he exits at an intersection.

(1) Before the trip, the optimal strategy is p = 1/3. Expected payoff is p·0 + p(1−p)·4 + (1−p)²·1 = 1 + 2p − 3p², which maxes at p = 1/3 and gives 4/3.

(2) Mid-trip, at an intersection, the driver has gained no information that distinguishes first from second. So if exiting with prob 1/3 was the best thing to plan, it should still be the best thing to do now.

(3) But conditional on being at an intersection, he can update. If he follows some exit probability q at each intersection, then the first intersection is reached for sure and the second one only if he doesn't exit at the first, so P(this is the first) = 1/(2−q) and P(this is the second) = (1−q)/(2−q). Given those credences, the expected payoff of playing q is W(q) = 2(1+3q)(1−q)/(2−q).

(4) W(q) is maximized at q = 2 − √21/3 ≈ 0.47, not at q = 1/3. So at the intersection, the driver should prefer to exit more often than his own best ex ante plan says.

(5) Nothing about his situation changed between the planning stage and the intersection except that he's now inside the trip, and that self-locating fact was fully predictable from the start.

(6) So one and the same expected-utility calculation says: plan p = 1/3, but when the moment comes, prefer q ≈ 0.47. Expected-utility theory would then be internally inconsistent, and the very idea of a plan you can rationally stick to would be in trouble.

I'd really like to keep (1) and (2): (1) is just arithmetic, and (2) has to be right unless rational planning is a joke. I'm most suspicious of (3), but if I reject the updating there, I don't know what I'm allowed to believe about self-locating credences, and that feels like a big bill to pay. Where does the argument go wrong, and what does it cost?
