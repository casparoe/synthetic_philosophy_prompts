I keep having strong, apparently inconsistent, reactions to free-riding cases, and I want to know whether they can be brought under one principle. I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so please define any technical terms you introduce and don't presuppose familiarity with particular authors or debates.

Here are my verdicts on ten cases.

1. Bus fare evasion. A person twice a week rides a city bus that is rarely more than half full. He stops paying and sneaks on. His riding displaces no paying passenger and the bus would run anyway. I think this is wrong.

2. Public radio. Someone has listened to a listener-supported radio station for years and never donated, even though he could easily afford it. I don't think he is doing anything wrong.

3. A startup builds on a library. A well-funded startup builds its product on a permissively licensed open-source library. It contributes no code, no money, no bug reports, and never mentions its use. The project has a healthy group of contributors and the startup succeeds. I don't think the startup does anything morally wrong — ungenerous, maybe, but not wrong.

4. A company resells the open-source project. A company takes a permissively licensed open-source project, makes no meaningful changes, sells a hosted version as a paid service in direct competition with the maintainers' own paid offering, and contributes nothing back. I think this is wrong, or at least different in kind from case 3, even though the license permits it and no condition of use is violated.

5. Group-project slacker. In a three-person project at work, one member does almost none of the work but shares the credit and the bonus. I think this is clearly wrong.

6. Snow-shoveling rota. In a small neighborhood, households take turns shoveling snow and checking on the block's elderly residents. One household never takes a turn but is happy to have neighbors clear its own walk when its members are away. I think this is wrong.

7. Vaccine refusal. During an outbreak, a healthy adult with no medical reason skips vaccination, figuring that everyone else's immunity protects him. I think this is wrong, even though I cannot point to any particular person he harms.

8. Not voting. A citizen never votes but benefits from, and often complains about, what the government does. I don't think this is morally wrong, though it is a bit shabby.

9. Tax evasion. A person evades taxes while continuing to use roads, courts, schools, and the police. I think this is wrong.

10. One burned-out maintainer. A company's product depends on a widely used open-source package maintained by a single volunteer who is visibly overwhelmed. The company has profited handsomely and has never donated money or helped triage issues. I want to say this is not wrong, but it bothers me more than case 3, and I can't tell whether that is a real moral difference or just a difference in how vividly the maintainer's situation is presented.

The tensions are obvious to me. Cases 1 and 2 look structurally similar: one person benefits from a service that is mostly paid for by others, and his own non-payment changes almost nothing. Yet I call one wrong and the other fine. Cases 7 and 8 also look similar in structure — contribution to a big collective practice where any one person's involvement is negligible — but I judge them differently. And I can't say what makes case 4 worse than case 3, or whether case 10 is really different from case 3 at all.

My best attempt at a principle is this: free riding is wrong when either (a) I take a benefit from a scheme whose terms require contribution and I dodge the requirement, or (b) the scheme would be seriously damaged if a substantial fraction of people copied me. That principle handles several cases: 1 via (a), 5 and 6 via implied terms of fairness, and 7 and 9 via (b). It lets case 3 off the hook when the project is healthy. But it also seems to make cases 2 and 8 wrong, since a listener-supported station collapses if nobody donates and a democracy does not function if nobody votes — and I don't want to say either is wrong. I'm also unsure what it says about cases 4 and 10, which is part of why I'm asking.

My request: give me a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. State each principle precisely enough that I can apply it to a new case, and say which clause of the principle is doing the work in which case. Then apply it to two further cases I haven't settled: (i) a person who joins a community garden mainly to harvest from the shared beds but never contributes labor or dues, and (ii) a company that trains a profitable model on a volunteer-maintained public dataset and gives nothing back, while the dataset's maintainers struggle to pay hosting costs. Finally, tell me honestly which of my ten verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and whether the cheaper repair is to refine the principle or to abandon one of those verdicts. Wherever you give a verdict or principle, also tell me what would change your mind: which argument or counterexample would most likely make you revise the principle or flip one of your answers, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
