I went back and forth for hours last night with a friend about inheritance taxes, and I woke up still turning the argument over. His side was the standard equal-opportunity argument for taxing inheritances: inherited wealth is unearned, nobody deserves to be born rich, and if you care about equal opportunity — about kids starting from something like the same line — then you should want inheritances taxed heavily, maybe nearly to the point of eliminating them. I kept pushing on the empirical picture underneath it. That argument, at least in the form I know it, seems to imagine dynastic privilege as a big pile of money handed over at death, with the estate tax as the tool that stops the pile from becoming a head start. But most of the advantage rich parents now pass down doesn't arrive at death at all. It's the house in the good school district, the tutoring, the networks, the unpaid internships, the SAT prep, the easy confidence, the safety net that lets a kid take risks. By the time the will is read, a huge share of the damage to equal opportunity has arguably already happened.

So here's what I can't settle. Please act as a philosopher working on distributive justice and equality of opportunity — I want actual argument reconstruction, not the usual talking points. Does the standard equal-opportunity argument for inheritance taxes survive this development? Or does the fact that the family, not the estate, has become the main transmission belt for advantage after a couple generations mean the argument is dead, needs repair, or ends up targeting something else entirely? Start by reconstructing the argument premise by premise so it's clear exactly which premise is under pressure, then brainstorm two or three possible repairs before you commit to the best one. Dont worry about what's politically realistic — I care about whether the argument itself still goes through.
