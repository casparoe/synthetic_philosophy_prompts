A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about the ethics of credibility, and by the end I found I was committed to the following five claims. I would like a consistency audit before I write them up.

(1) Testimonial injustice is a wrong done to a speaker in their capacity as a knower: the hearer gives them less credibility than they are owed because of identity prejudice.

(2) Credibility excess is not an injustice in the same sense. The over-believed person is given more credibility than they are owed and typically benefits, so the distinctive harm of testimonial injustice is absent.

(3) The remedy for testimonial injustice is primarily a corrective virtue in individual hearers: each hearer should scrutinise their own credibility judgements for prejudice and adjust them.

(4) Hermeneutical injustice is structural rather than individual: it consists in gaps in a community's shared interpretive resources, and no amount of good will by individual hearers can supply the missing concepts.

(5) In principle, the credibility a speaker is owed is settled entirely by the evidence: an ideal hearer who possessed all the relevant evidence and no prejudice would converge on the same credibility assignment.

Does this set hang together? I would like an audit rather than a survey. Which pairs are in genuine tension, and how severe is each? Which pairs only appear to be in tension, and why do they dissolve? For each genuine tension, say what would have to give, and identify the single least costly revision that would make the set coherent. Please answer in British English.
