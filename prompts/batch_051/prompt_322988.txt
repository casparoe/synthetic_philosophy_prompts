I have spent the past year on the clinical AI oversight committee of a large teaching hospital, where we review every patient-facing AI system before it can be piloted in our network. We are now considering a proposal from a vendor for a "therapeutic companion" voice assistant to be placed in the memory-care unit. The system is designed primarily to reduce agitation in patients with moderate-to-severe dementia, and much of its behavior involves short, soothing exchanges. Some of its scripts are straightforwardly factual, but a meaningful subset is less clear. For example, when a patient asks after a spouse who died several years ago, the system is scripted to respond with something like "He's not here right now, but you're safe and I'm staying with you." On other occasions it tells patients that a nurse will be by "in just a moment" when no nurse has been dispatched and the timing is unknown, on the grounds that this reduces repeated call-button use. The vendor's central defense is that the system cannot deceive anyone: it has no beliefs, no communicative intentions, and no interest in whether the patient is misled, and in any case these utterances serve a therapeutic function similar to a clinician's compassionate redirect or a family member's gentle untruth. Several committee members find this persuasive; others think it would license exactly the kind of manipulation our charter exists to prevent, and that whether a system deceives should be judged by the impression it reasonably creates in a vulnerable user rather than by its inner states. The committee is genuinely divided, and the chair has asked me, as the member with the most background in ethics, to draft the memo we will vote on.

The memo needs a bottom-line recommendation: approve the pilot as proposed, reject it, or approve it subject to specific restrictions or redesigns. It should also give a defensible account of what it means for a system of this kind to be honest in its dealings with patients given that the system cannot intend or believe anything, and it should apply that account to the scripted examples above. I would like the memo to propose general criteria the committee can use in future cases where patient-facing AI produces socially fluent but non-veridical communication — criteria that do not collapse into a bare rule against false assertions, but that also do not treat therapeutic benefit as a blank check. Finally, the memo should do full justice to the strongest consideration on the losing side before explaining why it does not carry the day, since several members will need to be able to see their position reflected in the document even if they are outvoted.
