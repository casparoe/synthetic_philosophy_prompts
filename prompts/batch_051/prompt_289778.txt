I am an undergraduate taking a seminar on the philosophy of animal minds, and a discussion from our unit on invertebrate welfare has stayed with me. The claim at issue was roughly this: even if you assign quite a low probability to any individual insect's being sentient — capable of felt pain or other morally significant states — the number of insects affected by ordinary human activity, in agriculture, in food storage, in routine pest control, in landscaping, is so vast that the expected amount of suffering at stake might still be enormous. On this view, the reply "but we are not sure insects can suffer" is too quick: under expected-value reasoning, uncertainty lowers the probability, but it does not make the arithmetic of enormous numbers go away.

Our instructor pressed us on what this argument is actually supposed to license. A classmate objected that the structure seems to prove too much. If a small probability of a morally relevant state, multiplied by an astronomically large number of affected individuals, is enough to generate a serious obligation, then the same style of reasoning would also seem to generate obligations elsewhere whenever there is a minuscule chance of an enormous good or harm — including speculative long-shots that we would ordinarily dismiss without a second thought. Without some principled way of bounding or discounting such calculations, accepting the scale argument seems to risk decision paralysis or a policy of taking every remote possibility with enormous stakes seriously.

I would like to write an essay on the following question: does the scale argument justify a significant shift in how we treat insects, given our uncertainty about insect sentience? The essay should weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, including the objection that the reasoning proves too much, and should arrive at and justify a definite verdict, even if the verdict is hedged or conditional rather than a flat yes or no.
