Okay here's something that's bugged me about hedonism for a while. Bentham-style hedonist talk as if pleasure is one kind of mental stuff, but when I introspect, the pleasure of scratching an itch, the pleasure of finally getting a proof to click, a little malicious glee at someone's downfall, the foggy warmth of a hot bath after a long day, and the relief when a pain stops don't seem to share any felt quality except that I'm disposed to call them all pleasant. That's the heterogeneity problem. A freind of mine thinks it's an empirical question: pleasantness could be a real univocal dimension, like pitch, implemented in some common reward signal, and all those surface differences are just the contents. I think 'pleasant' is more like 'game', a family resemblance word, or just a label we learn to slap on whatever we want more of. How would one actually test that? I want a concrete design (behavioral, psychophysical, neural, whatever), a clear statement of what results would favour the common-currency view versus the family resemblance view, and then an honest account of what the test would leave open. You might think about whether forced choices among wildly different pleasures should be transitive if there's one underlying scale, or whether finding a shared reward signal in the brain would actually settle anything about felt quality. And here's the part I care about: could the common-currency view pass every test you design and still be false about there being a simgle felt quality? Is the philosophical question just not the empirical one, or does hedonism only need the empirical half to go well?
