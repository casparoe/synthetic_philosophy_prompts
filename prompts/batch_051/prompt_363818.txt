I find that discussions of reflective equilibrium keep stalling at exactly the point where I want precision: everyone grants that a crisp theory can push against a firm intuition, but almost nobody will commit to how much weight each should carry in a particular collision. I want to force a committed ranking out of that vagueness.

Here are five theory–intuition conflicts, described so that each one stands on its own without dependency on a specific literature.

1. Transitivity of betterness. The theory says the relation better-than is transitive: if A is better than B and B better than C, then A is better than C. A person judges a quiet life better than an ambitious one because of its peace, the ambitious life better than a wandering one because of its impact, and the wandering one better than the quiet one because of its intensity, and reports this as a genuine cycle of value rather than as proof that one of the three judgments should be revised.

2. Anonymity of distributions. The theory says that if two allocations of a fixed stock of goods yield the same list of individual bundles—differing only in which named individual receives which bundle—then the two allocations are equally good. A school planner prefers the timetable that keeps two close friends in the same classes over one with the same individual course schedules, and insists the friendship itself is part of what is allocated, not a free-floating label attached after the bundles are fixed.

3. Irrelevance of stopping rules. The theory says that how much a body of data favors one hypothesis over another depends only on how much more probable the data are under one hypothesis than under the other; the experimenter's decision rule for when to stop collecting is irrelevant. One researcher peeks and stops as soon as the headline effect crosses a conventional threshold; another pre-registers a fixed sample and happens to collect identical numbers. The intuition says the first result is weaker evidence.

4. Exchangeability of reasons. The theory says a fact can count as a reason for a choice only if it is expressible as a property of the situation that anyone could be moved by, so a reason that essentially involves the agent's own position in the world—such as the fact that the child in question is one's own—can at most excuse a choice, never justify it. A coach with one roster spot must choose between their own child and a slightly stronger player. The intuition says resolving a near-tie in one's own child's favor is permissible as a reason, not merely as a forgivable bias.

5. Constant discounting versus improving sequences. The theory says a rational agent discounts each unit of future well-being at the same fixed rate per period, so for a fixed total quantity of happiness spread over a life, moving some happiness from later decades to earlier decades always makes the life at least as good. A person who must arrange the same total happiness over forty years strongly prefers the arrangement whose best decade comes last. The intuition says the improving life-story has value that the discounting model cannot represent.

Please sort these five into a single tier list with two or three tiers, from the cases where the theory should receive nearly all the weight to the cases where the intuition should. You may place more than one case in a tier, but do not leave any case unplaced and do not collapse all five into one tier. For each case, give one or two sentences identifying the structural feature of the conflict that does the largest work in your placement. Then end with a general rule, stated as a definite commitment: which two or three properties of a theory–intuition conflict should most increase our deference to the theory, which should most increase our deference to the intuition, and apply the rule to the case in your ranking that fits it least comfortably.
