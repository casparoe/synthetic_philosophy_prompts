I've been rereading Spinoza's Ethics, especially Part V on the intellectual love of God, and I keep coming back to a distinction I started making in my notes. I call it "horizontal necessitation" vs. "vertical necessitation." Horizontal necessitation is the kind where a finite thing follows from an earlier finite cause in the temporal chain — the thing is pushed along by what came before it in duration. Vertical necessitation is the kind where something follows immediately from the eternal essence of God, the way a property follows from a definition, with no timeline involved. My thought is that Spinoza's intellectual love of God is vertically necessitated, not horizontally necessitated. That's why it doesn't feel like a constraint: its not something that happens to you from outside, it's the mind's adequate idea of God unfolding as part of God's own self-knowledge. So the old worry that necessitarianism makes love pointless or coerced is aimed at the wrong kind of necessitation. I'm sure something like this distinction already exists in the literature — maybe under the duration-versus-eternity distinction or the sub specie aeternitatis stuff. But I haven't seen it put exactly this way. So: first, is this distinction coherent or am I just rebranding the temporal/eternal distinction? Second, does it actually dissolve the problem, or does it just relocate it — because a critic could say "fine, but vertical necessitation is still necessitation, so the love is still not up to you"? Third, where does it break? Give it to me straight — lead with the bottom line in your first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question.
