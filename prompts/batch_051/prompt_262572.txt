Please give me your credence that a broadly rational human agent can ever be right to pay a positive amount not to receive costless, decision-relevant, non-manipulative information. I know Good's theorem says that, for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer who updates by conditionalization, the expected value of costless information is never negative, so please treat that theorem as the null position and force yourself to say which of its assumptions fails in any case you count as a genuine exception. I'm arguing about exactly this with a friend: she puts her credence near 0.05 because she thinks the theorem settles it, while I'm near 0.4 because the assumptions look load-bearing. Give your own number, but decompose it into at least these sub-questions, with a rough probability for each: first, that strategic or self-signaling settings create exceptions, because receiving the information changes what you can honestly report, promise, or signal; second, that Newcomb-like cases create exceptions, because the information concerns your own future choice or a predictor's reaction to it; third, that information can be harmful by changing your preferences, values, or motivations rather than merely your beliefs; and fourth, that allegedly costless information is never costless once you count attention, emotional impact, and the inability to un-know or decide as if you hadn't learned it. Say how these combine into your headline credence, identify which sub-question your own number is most sensitive to and which sub-question my friend and I most likely disagree about, and say what evidence or argument would move your number the most.
