Some psychologists and philosophers claim that sadness and grief are not merely hedonic costs but have a cognitive function: sadness is said to reduce overconfidence, improve memory for detail, and calibrate estimates of how much control one has, while grief after a real loss is said to force a painful updating of one's model of the world. I want to know how much of this can be tested rather than merely asserted.

Please design the strongest concrete experiment, or small set of experiments, that would test whether sadness or the aftermath of loss improves at least some kinds of epistemic performance rather than merely making people feel worse. Specify the manipulation or comparison, the control condition, the dependent measures, and the results that would count in favour of the claim and the results that would count against it. Be explicit about the difference between a transient induced sad mood and genuine grief: say which parts of the claim a laboratory mood induction can reach, which parts require naturalistic or longitudinal data, and whether a positive result about induced sad mood licenses any conclusion about grief. Finally, suppose the empirical results come out strongly in favour of sad moods improving certain judgements. What exactly would that establish about the evaluative claim that a life without sadness or grief would be worse — not merely less pleasant, but less in touch with how things are? And what would even the best possible test leave unsettled?

Please write your answer in British English.
