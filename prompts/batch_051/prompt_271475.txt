I am drafting a paper for a workshop on the question of whether “spirituality without religion” can be a serious object of study rather than just a popular buzzphrase. My thesis is that William James’s method in The Varieties of Religious Experience gives us a better starting point for evaluating non-institutional, non-theistic spirituality than either theological critics or neuroscientific debunkers allow. I lean especially on the methodological remark in the preface where James says: “In my belief that a large acquaintance with particulars often makes us wiser than the possession of abstract formulas, however deep, I have loaded the lectures with concrete examples, and I have chosen these among the extremer expressions of the religious temperament.” My argument is that we should evaluate spiritually-inflected practices outside religious institutions—meditation, fasting, pilgrimage, ecstatic movement, contemplative silence—by their fruits for the individual life, rather than by their origin, institutional credentials, or fit with a general definition of religion. I expect the audience to include a sociologist of religion who studies the “spiritual but not religious” demographic, two philosophers of mind who think religious experience is just a misleading label for non-doxastic affective states, a theologian sympathetic to mystical traditions but worried about losing communal discipline, and a historian who thinks James’s examples are skewed toward dramatic Protestant conversion narratives. Prepare me for the question period. What objections will they raise, in roughly what order, in their own terms? Which objections have real force? What should I concede up front, what should I hold, and where is the weakest point in my paper that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss? Please clearly separate what is settled in James scholarship from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
