I am a novelist, and I am drafting a book whose climax turns on a dispute between two characters in an online game: one destroys a rare item in another player's inventory, and the item is later restored, bit for bit, from a server-side backup taken an hour before the deletion. I do not want the argument between the characters—or, later, between readers—to reduce to a legal question about terms of service, property, or damages, and I do not want it dismissed as a matter of it only being a game. Instead, I want the disagreement to be generated by two philosophical positions about virtual objects. Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. One position holds that virtual objects are real digital artifacts whose persistence conditions are settled by the underlying data and its causal role in the simulation, so that a bit-for-bit restored object just is the original object. The other position holds that virtual-object talk is a governed make-believe: the objects themselves are not the place to locate real harm, but the make-believe is woven into real human practices, expectations, and attachments, and those can be genuinely harmed. Build a case in which the two positions deliver opposite verdicts about a virtual harm. The digital-artifact realist should conclude that no genuine harm occurred, because the object has been restored and no offline interest has been set back. The make-believe theorist should conclude that a real harm did occur, in the player's actual, non-fictional practices or attachments, even though the object is back.

Constraints: the setting must be an otherwise ordinary multiplayer game with routine server-side backups; no exotic technology, no memory tampering, no secretly conscious simulation, and no perfect predictors; and the harmed player must suffer no bodily injury, no financial loss, and no exposure of private data. The separating device must be a feature of the scenario, not an explicit stipulation of the verdicts. After presenting the scenario, do the check: state the object, the mechanism of deletion and restoration, and the verdict each position gives, with the reasoning that actually generates that verdict. Then say which structural feature of the case does the work, explain what happens if that feature is removed and whether the two positions collapse into agreement, and finish with how a defender of the digital-artifact realist view would attack the case in order to avoid the no-harm verdict.
