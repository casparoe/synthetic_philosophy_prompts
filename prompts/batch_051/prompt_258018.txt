I've spent much of the past year reporting for a general-interest magazine on the science of emotion regulation — the reappraisal techniques at the center of cognitive-behavioral therapy, the design of mental-health apps built around reframing one's thoughts, and the broader cultural assumption that the way to change a feeling is to change the thought beneath it. That reporting has landed me in a philosophical dispute I cannot settle on my own. The view I keep being drawn to is a broadly neo-Stoic cognitivism about emotion: an emotion just is, or is constituted by, an evaluative judgment about something I take to matter to my flourishing. Fear is the judgment of impending harm; grief is the judgment that someone irreplaceable has been lost; anger is the judgment that I have been wronged. The view has genuine systematic virtues. It explains why emotions have aboutness — I do not merely fear, I fear the dog; I miss the person; I resent the remark. It explains why emotions can be assessed as warranted or unwarranted, proportionate or disproportionate, in something like the way beliefs can. It explains why correcting an appraisal can loosen an emotion's grip, and it dissolves the awkward picture of a "cold" judgment somehow causing a separate "hot" feeling by treating the evaluative thought as already charged with affect.

But my reporting keeps surfacing an equally stubborn intuition on the other side. People with specific phobias can sincerely and explicitly judge — as their own settled conviction, not a therapist's script — that no harm is coming, while their heart races, their hands sweat, and their attention is seized anyway. A man in mourning can fully accept that his wife is gone and still be ambushed by grief at the sight of an empty chair. In such cases emotion presents itself as something undergone rather than concluded: a felt, embodied response that persists against one's own best judgment, leaving a residue that no judgment seems to exhaust. If the emotion just is the judgment, it is difficult to see how it could remain while the judgment is absent or explicitly denied. I am not looking for a verdict that simply anoints one side. I want a negotiated settlement. What would it take for the recalcitrant-emotion intuition to be debunked — shown to be an artifact of conflicting or partial judgments, of appraisal at one level and something else at another, or of the body lagging behind the belief? What would it take for cognitivism to be amended or abandoned in its face? And is there a reading of "judgment," or a division of labor among judgment, perception, and feeling, on which the theory's systematic virtues and the phobic person's sweating palms both survive intact? Cost out each move rather than declaring a winner.
