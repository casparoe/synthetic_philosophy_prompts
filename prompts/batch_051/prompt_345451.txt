Lately I have been reading in global history, and a question keeps following me from my background in physics. The Eurocentrism debate is often framed as a problem about the narrator's location: universal histories claim to be written from nowhere, but they are actually written from somewhere—a European center that quietly treats the rest of the world as periphery, as cases, or as latecomers. The critical alternatives all seem to struggle with the same difficulty: either you multiply local perspectives and lose any integrated story, or you try to write from everywhere at once and fall back into pretending you have no location.

I cannot shake the resemblance to something from relativity. In special relativity no inertial frame is privileged; in general relativity there is often no single global frame at all, so one covers spacetime with local coordinate charts and patches them together according to transformation rules. The historical analogue would be: there is no God's-eye view of the past, only many situated histories that can be connected but never summed into one privileged account. But I cannot tell whether this is a real structural analogy or just a pun on "frame" and "perspective." Coordinate charts are arbitrary and interchangeable, and the transformations between them are exact; historical perspectives seem to carry normative commitments, distortions, and unequal authority, and I see no analogue of a well-defined transformation between, say, a labor history of Bengal and a diplomatic history of Vienna. Also, in physics the manifold exists independently of the charts, whereas it is not obvious that "the past" exists independently of its tellings.

Is this parallel deep or superficial? If it is deep, what would need to be true of historical inquiry for something like coordinate transformations or patchwise compatibility to make sense across perspectives? And what is one thing the analogy would predict about history-writing that is probably false?
