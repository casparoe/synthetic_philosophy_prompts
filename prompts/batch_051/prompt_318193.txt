I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long-form essay about the long argument over whether the U.S. Constitution was a pro-slavery or an anti-slavery instrument—and, more abstractly, about where a text's meaning is supposed to live: in its words, in its drafters' private intentions, in compromise, or in later practice. Frederick Douglass's Glasgow speech "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?" (delivered March 26, 1860) keeps drawing me back, and I keep getting stuck on this passage:

> It would be the wildest of absurdities, and lead to endless confusion and mischiefs, if, instead of looking to the written paper itself, for its meaning, it were attempted to make us search it out, in the secret motives, and dishonest intentions, of some of the men who took part in writing it. It was what they said that was adopted by the people, not what they were ashamed or afraid to say, and really omitted to say.

I need help understanding what exactly Douglass is arguing here. I can see that he is trying to block an objection—namely, that many Framers privately intended to protect slavery—but the move he makes looks stronger than just "read the text." Could you first lay out the precise rule of interpretation he is proposing: what counts as "the Constitution" for him, and what kinds of evidence he is ruling out as guides to its meaning? Second, stress-test that rule: if I grant it, does it actually deliver his conclusion that the Constitution is not a slaveholding instrument, or does it simply relocate the dispute to which of the document's clauses and phrases count as plainly stating a guarantee? Third, help me apply the passage to a current debate: when present-day originalists say we should consult text and original public meaning rather than the private wishes of the drafters, are they making Douglass's move, or is there a real difference in what evidence about the past each approach would admit?
