I follow a rule in deciding whom to believe, and I suspect it is a cultural prejudice wearing an epistemic costume. I was raised with something like the Jantelagen and tall-poppy ethos, where people are expected to play down their achievements and visible self-confidence is treated as a defect; I now live among people shaped by the American Dream, where success is displayed and celebrated. The rule: when two people offer conflicting empirical claims I cannot check myself, I discount the confident, polished presenter who narrates successes and states clean conclusions, and I trust the self-deprecating one who stresses caveats and limitations—recently that meant siding with a hesitant hedger over a fluent speaker about whether a supplement improves sleep. My American friend objects that confidence is at best weak evidence of accuracy and that my rule punishes the very style that gets good work noticed. Is this policy defensible as an epistemic shortcut? If not, what would a corrected version look like—one that attends to how confidence and accuracy are calibrated, what self-deprecation signals under Jantelagen norms versus under American-style norms, and when over- or understating should move my credence? Please write your answer in American English.
