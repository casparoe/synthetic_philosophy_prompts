I keep bumping into this pattern in arguments about whether some ad, platform, or political line is racist. Someone points out that the literal content is innocuous — it mentions crime, or community values, or who counts as a real American, but nothing explicitly racial — and the reply always comes back: "right, but its a dog whistle." The charge sometimes feels true to me, and sometimes feels like a way to skip the argument, but the more I hear it the more I suspect both sides are leaning on the acoustics of the analogy instead of on any shared account of how the speech is supposed to work. As far as I can reconstruct it, the analogy is being used to carry three claims at once: the statement is racist despite its innocent surface because it transmits a second message that the in-group hears and outsiders can't; the speaker must have intended to transmit it, because after all someone blows a dog whistle on purpose; and an outsider's inability to hear the message is neither evidence against its existence nor a valid defense. I want an actual audit of how far the analogy holds, not a verdict on some particular politician.

So do the correspondence table properly. What maps to the whistle, the frequency, the sound, the dog, the human, the trainer, the training? Then separate which disanalogies matter from which don't. A few candidate failures I've been turning over: a real dog whistle isn't a proposition or a coded message, the dog is not interpreting it, and a nearby human does hear something even if they can't assign a meaning to it — whereas the statement's non-racist content is fully audible to everyone and it's only the racist uptake that's audience-relative. Also the in-group here are reasoners making an inference, not conditioned responders, and the speaker's intent might be doing more work than the acoustic story can support. Tell me which of those failures actually undercut the three claims above, whether the conclusion about intent survives, and whether there's a better analogy — an in-joke, a shibboleth, steganography, a lawyer's nudge — that carries the same conclusions more honestly.
