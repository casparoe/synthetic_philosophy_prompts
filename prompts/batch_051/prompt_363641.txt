Having spent my career in the natural sciences and only recently turned to philosophy, I have been reading around self-knowledge and introspection, and a phenomenon from the study of large language models keeps striking me as in need of precise characterization. Suppose a model is asked to solve a problem and, in the same prompt, to set out the steps by which it arrived at its answer. The resulting derivation is fluent and often persuasive, but under manipulation it behaves oddly: one can delete a step that the derivation presents as load-bearing, substitute a different but equally plausible step, or reorder the steps, and the final answer generally remains what it was. The text reads less like a record of the route to the conclusion than like a commentary composed around a conclusion already reached. I know that in human psychology there are the notions of confabulation and of post hoc rationalization, and the case resembles both; but it also seems different, because the system's only channel for reporting its own operation is the very channel through which it produces the answer. Is there an established concept for this gap between the reasons a system states and the factors that actually determine its output, and is it even coherent to demand that such a stated derivation be a truthful report of a real process in the system? I would not be satisfied with a bare label. What I am after is the carving: exactly what the phenomenon is, how it differs from the two psychological notions above, where its boundaries lie, and the strongest argument for treating the gap as genuine rather than as an artifact of the way the system is prompted or read.
