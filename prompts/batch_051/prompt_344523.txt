I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine. At a conference on technology and governance I sat through a panel on performance legitimacy: the claim that sustained prosperity, public order, and technological capacity can make the rule of a non-democratic system legitimate. One panelist, defending the record of Singapore and, more cautiously, China, brushed aside procedural objections with the old line that it does not matter whether a cat is black or white as long as it catches mice. The audience treated this as decisive, but on reflection the sentence seemed to pack several different claims into one image, and I would like to unpack them for a column. I am not asking for a verdict on any particular regime; I want the logic of the saying itself. Please give me exactly five distinct precise claims the line might be taken to assert, ordered from the most innocuous to the most ambitious. For each claim, in one or two sentences: state it precisely; classify it as trivial, plausible, contested, or false; and say whether it actually licenses the inference the panelist needed — the move from the system's delivering prosperity, order, and modern capacity to the system's being legitimate. Number the claims one through five. No introduction and no conclusion.
