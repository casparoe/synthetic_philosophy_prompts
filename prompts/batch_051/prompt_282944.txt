I have been trying to pin down what people mean when they say the alignment problem is "solvable in principle," and I keep reaching for the Hilbert program as a test case. By "alignment" I mean the problem of making advanced AI systems reliably do what their operators intend, rather than pursuing catastrophic but unintended proxies. My rough understanding of the Hilbert episode is this: Hilbert was not asking for an efficient procedure but for a finitary consistency proof for formalized mathematics, or at least for arithmetic, and the program failed in a precise way when Gödel showed that the kind of system Hilbert had in mind could not prove its own consistency by the finitary methods Hilbert allowed, with Church and Turing later making the notion of an algorithm precise and showing that some well-posed problems have no mechanical solution at all. This looks to me like a rare case in which an "in principle" solvability question was made precise and answered negatively, without stopping ordinary mathematical practice.

I would like an analysis of that episode through the lens of the in-principle/in-practice distinction that recurs in alignment debates. What exactly did the negative results rule out, and what did they leave open for working mathematicians? If a person claims that alignment is solvable in principle, what would the corresponding claim have to be, and what would count as establishing it: an algorithm for some formalized version of the problem, an existence proof, a constructive strategy, or something weaker? And where does the analogy come apart, given that alignment has no accepted formal success criterion, concerns an empirical and possibly shifting target, and involves systems whose behavior is mediated by training rather than by a fixed axiom system? I want to know whether the Hilbert precedent supports treating "solvable in principle" as a rigorous and useful category, or whether transplanting it to alignment mostly smuggles in assumptions from formal mathematics that do not hold.
