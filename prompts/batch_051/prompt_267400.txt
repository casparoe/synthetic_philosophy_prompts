Looking for a favor. Over the years teaching math-adjacent courses in a philosophy department, I've found myself circling around the same observation: the moment a proof feels oppressive isn't when it's too rigorous, but when the rigor is doing all the work and the motivation hasn't shown up yet. I've been calling this motivational debt. The idea is that when I hand you a step in a proof and don't tell you why, I'm lending you the step: you can accept it provisionally, but the loan needs to be payed off later. A good proof is a chain of small loans with a repayment plan. A rigorously bad proof is a pile of unsecured loans — every step is valid, but nothing makes the sequence feel inevitable. The standard epsilon-delta proof that a sum of continuous functions is continuous often has this flavor: each inequality checks out, but nothing tells you why those bounds were the right ones. I want to claim something stronger than the metaphor. Rigor and motivation aren't competing demands on a proof; they're more like a ledger and a cash-flow. Rigor tells you exactly how much debt each step carries, and motivation is the arrangement by which that debt gets discharged. I also suspect every proof has a lower bound on how much debt it can carry before it stops enlightening anyone; some of our most rigorous proofs charge more than most undergrads can ever repay, which is why a student can verify each line and still be unable to extend the arguement two weeks later. I keep going back and forth on whether this is a real distinction or just a dressed-up word for something boring. Here's what I actually want checked: is the construct coherent, does it already exist under a better name, where does it break (maybe the debt is in the student's background rather than the proof? maybe some steps create no debt at all?), and does anything survive?
