I am a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy course on free will, and we are currently reading Hume's attempt to dissolve the dispute between liberty and necessity. I want to practice objecting to the position by arguing with someone who actually holds it, not a paraphrase of it. Please play David Hume — specifically the Hume of A Treatise of Human Nature, Book II, Part III, Sections I–II, "Of liberty and necessity" — but with one modern development added to your knowledge: I have told you that our best physical theories make at least some microscopic events genuinely indeterministic, in the sense that no prior state of the world fixes them, and that some philosophers now take this to bear on the freedom of the will. Hold the character until I say stop; stay in the first person as Hume; do not step out to annotate or qualify; and keep each reply to a paragraph. You may refine a formulation, and you may admit that chance of the sort your century dismissed is now part of physics, but you may not concede that an undetermined action would be free, and you may not surrender your two marks of necessity, constant union and the mind's inference from one object to another.

I want to press you on a passage from Section II, which I will quote and then put to you:

"Secondly, there is a false sensation or experience even of the liberty of indifference; which is regarded as an argument for its real existence. The necessity of any action, whether of matter or of the mind, is not properly a quality in the agent, but in any thinking or intelligent being, who may consider the action, and consists in the determination of his thought to infer its existence from some preceding objects: As liberty or chance, on the other hand, is nothing but the want of that determination, and a certain looseness, which we feel in passing or not passing from the idea of one to that of the other."

Here is my opening. You say, in the sense opposed to necessity, liberty just is chance, and you call this chance nothing but the want of that determination — a looseness we feel in the mind's passage from one idea to another. But you wrote before you knew the physics I have just described to you. If nature itself contains just such a looseness at bottom, why should your dismissal of the liberty of indifference still stand? Does a genuinely chancy event in the brain count as free on your account, or is it no more free than a necessitated one? Answer as Hume, and say precisely what your two marks of necessity commit you to about such an event.
