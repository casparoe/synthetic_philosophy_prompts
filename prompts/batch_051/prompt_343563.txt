I'm in a reading group on capacity and consent, and one member has been defending the binding force of advance directives by leaning again and again on the story of Ulysses and the Sirens. In the story, Ulysses knows in advance that the Sirens' song will destroy his judgement, so he has his crew lash him to the mast and orders them not to untie him however much he begs; when the moment arrives, he begs and struggles, and the crew refuse. His version runs something like this: just as Ulysses before the enchantment is entitled to bind his later, temporarily undone self, so a person drafting an advance directive is entitled to bind a later self that has lost the capacities that made the earlier choice authoritative. The later refusal to go along with the treatment plan is the Sirens' song, not a genuine change of mind, and it should not count against the directive.

I want an audit of this analogy, because I suspect it is carrying more weight than he concedes. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the mast, the ropes, the crew, the decision to sail past, the Sirens, the later begging, and the return of ordinary judgement. Then identify the disanalogies, separating the ones that are load-bearing for the conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative. He has pre-empted one of them: he admits that the later self with dementia usually never regains the earlier capacities, so there is no Ulysses-after-the-voyage to thank the crew — but he says that only makes the earlier authorisation more decisive, since the earlier self is the last fully competent person to have spoken. Test whether that move actually helps or whether it cuts against the analogy. In particular, consider whether the later self is better modelled as the same person whose judgement is temporarily distorted, or as someone whose values and concerns have been replaced on an ongoing basis; whether the Sirens episode presumes that the later Ulysses would ratify the restraint once restored to himself; and what the story quietly imports about the unity of the self across the episode. Then say whether his conclusion survives once these points are on the table, and whether some better analogue ought to replace the Sirens story in this debate. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question.
