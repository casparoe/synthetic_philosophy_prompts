A friend and I were arguing about whether people in places without reliable, affordable plant foods have any obligation around animal products, and I ended up with an argument that now seems to let everyone off the hook. I can't tell where it breaks:

(1) Whether buying animal products is wrong depends solely on whether a reasonable plant-based alternative was actually available to the buyer.
(2) In lots of places such alternatives are not available or affordable for most people—income, local markets, food deserts, refrigeration, food culture, and so on.
(3) But whether an alternative is available is itself the result of many individual choices: stores stock what sells, prices track aggregate demand, and policies respond to what consumers and voters reward.
(4) So the same facts that make animal products the only realistic option for each individual are facts that individuals like them collectively produced.
(5) By this logic a person could also excuse buying goods made with exploited labor whenever fair-trade alternatives aren't stocked where they shop, because that absence is likewise produced by processes they didn't individually get to decide.
(6) But then participation in nearly any widespread harmful practice is excusable, as long as the practice is common enough that opting out is costly or unavailable to any one person.
(7) That seems too permissive to be right.

So which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it force me to say about the original case—the herder or fishing community that genuinely has no plant-based options? I'd like to keep something in the spirit of (1), but I don't see how to stop it from sliding into (6).
