I'm a clerk for a state appeals-court judge, and next week the panel has a desuetude case — the doctrine that a criminal law can stop being law through very long non-enforcement. My judge has a small pile of past opinions in exactly this area, and I have to write the bench memo predicting how she'll vote and which rule she'd be committing to. I'd rather someone who actually thinks about the rule of law wrestle with it. Think carefully: before you commit, lay out the candidate principles that could each make sense of her whole record, then check each against the new facts.

Her past votes, compressed:

1. Ice-cream truck permits. A 1949 ordinance required a nuisance-inspection permit for ice-cream trucks. The city stopped issuing those permits in 1980 and the health department did equivalent inspections after that. No trucks operated in the city from 1985 to 2005. In 2006 a new owner is prosecuted under the 1949 ordinance. Judge held the law enforceable: a law nobody's been breaking isn't dead, just asleep.

2. Sunday medicine blue law. Passed in 1927; made it a misdemeanor to sell non-prescription medicines on Sunday. Not enforced since 1955. Pharmacies sold openly on Sundays for decades, the pharmacy board knew, and in 1972 the board sent a memo saying it would not enforce the Sunday rules. In 2010 the city prosecuted a pharmacist who had relied on that memo. Judge barred the prosecution: dead-letter statute.

3. Summer dog ban. A 2008 ordinance banned dogs in public parks on summer weekends; it was enforced until 2015, then went unenforced. A dog owner was prosecuted in 2019. Judge let it stand: eight years of disuse isn't desuetude, especially with no reliance.

4. Roadside fruit-stand law from 1932. Never enforced even once. Fruit stands operated openly in one rural county for fifty years, but the county attorney's office genuinely didn't know the law existed. A stand got charged recently. Judge allowed the prosecution to go forward: no knowing acquisecence by the state, so the statute hadn't died.

5. Photography-studio permit rule from 1955, passed for tintype peddlers. Never enforced against studios. Studios operated openly for decades, paid business taxes, and the city sent them letters saying no photography-specific permit was needed. Judge barred the 2018 prosecution and leaned hard on the city's letters.

New case: a 1940 city ordinance requires a permit to sell fruit from a cart. The city stopped enforcing it by 1960. Fruit carts have operated openly and continuously since, city licensing staff know, and each year the health department issues every cart vendor a metal badge under a different program. The defendant, Dana, is a local reporter who sold fruit from a cart for one afternoon as an article stunt; she knew about the 1940 ordinance and wanted to see whether the city would still use it. The regular vendors are not charged.

So: how should the judge vote if she wants to be consistent with her own prior opinions? Which of her past cases is the closest analog? If her record underdetermines the new case — I suspect it does, but show me — what principle is she adopting under each available ruling? And then name the further case (the fact you'd vary) that would cleanly split the remaining candidate principles apart. No citations or reading lists; I need the rule-work, not a bibliography.
