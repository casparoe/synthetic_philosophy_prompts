I am taking a seminar in moral psychology in which the word "bias" does a great deal of evaluative work. We say without hesitation that a judge's ruling was biased, that a hiring committee's decision was biased, and that a person's assessment of an argument was biased. Yet we are equally confident that a person who prefers tea to coffee merely has a preference, not a bias, and that some unmistakable partialities — toward one's child or one's oldest friend — are not biases at all even though they are plainly preferences. When I pressed the instructor on the principle behind this distinction, I received three different answers: a bias is a departure from a statistical norm; a bias is a judgment influenced by the wrong kind of factor; a bias is a preference that resists adjustment in light of reasons. Each of these handles some cases but none handles all of them, and each locates the defect in a different place. I would like a working definition of "bias" — in the evaluative sense that licenses an accusation that someone's judgment or decision is defective — precise enough to get the following cases right and to explain why the clear non-cases are not biases.

Cases I would confidently call biases:
(a) A judge hearing a contract dispute in which she owns a substantial share of one of the companies involved; on a genuinely close question of interpretation, she rules for that company.
(b) A journal referee who, because a submission comes from a known rival, reads it far more aggressively for flaws than they would read the same paper from a friend.

Cases I would confidently call mere preferences:
(c) Preferring tea to coffee because one finds coffee bitter.
(d) Consistently choosing to spend one's free time hiking in the mountains rather than at the beach because hiking feels more restoring.

Cases I cannot sort:
(e) A parent who gives their own child the benefit of the doubt in any childhood dispute, though they would not extend the same charity to an unfamiliar child on identical evidence.
(f) A physician who sees two patients with identical ambiguous symptoms; because the disease is far more prevalent in one patient's demographic group, she orders a biopsy for that patient and not for the other.
(g) A person raised in a culture organized around filial piety who believes adult children owe their parents weightier duties than most outsiders would accept; they can give reasons on demand — reciprocity, the parents' vulnerability — but they held the view before they had the reasons.
(h) A voter who judges the electoral system fair when her party wins and unfair when her party loses, while sincerely reporting that she applies a single neutral standard of fairness throughout.
(i) A person who supports a tax policy partly because it would benefit them financially, but who also finds the best arguments for the policy genuinely convincing.

Please propose a working definition of "bias" rather than a dictionary gloss, apply it to each case in a sentence or two, and say whether the definition counts the case as bias. Where the definition yields a verdict you think is wrong, or where it declines to answer, say so explicitly, then revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Finally, identify which of your final classifications you are least confident about and why.
