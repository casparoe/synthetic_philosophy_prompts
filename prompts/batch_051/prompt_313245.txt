I need to pin down what "common knowledge" amounts to — the formal notion Aumann's agreement theorem needs, not just the ordinary phrase — and I've got six cases I want the definition to sort:

1. At a wedding, the DJ announces over the mic that the cake is served, and everyone on the dance floor turns and sees everyone else turning toward the cake table.
2. The same announcement is instead emailed to the 200 guests as a blind carbon copy, so nobody sees who else got it.
3. A professor writes a short argument on the blackboard during a seminar. Each of the five students individually follows it and accepts the conclusion, but each privately thinks they're the only one who did, and hides the fact.
4. In a poker game, a card falls face-up while being dealt. Both players see the card, and each sees the other looking at it, but neither says a word and they play on as if it never happened.
5. A memo goes out in a company's Slack channel to all forty people on a team. Read reciepts are on, everyone has opened the memo, and everyone can see that everyone has opened it. But the memo is written in such dense legalese that half the team skims it and forms no idea what it actually says.
6. A notice is pinned to a corridor board at 2 a.m. and taken down at 6 a.m.; exactly one person besides the person who put it up sees it.

Here's where I stand: I'm confident that 1 and 4 count as common knowledge and that 2, 3, and 6 don't. Case 5 is where I go back and forth: I can't tell whether the *contents* of the memo are common knowledge, or only the fact that everyone opened it. I also suspect the ordinary phrase and the formal notion come apart — people call all sorts of things common knowledge when they're merely not secret, while the formal thing seems to require the whole infinite ladder of "everyone knows that everyone knows that..." Propose the definition, run it through all six, tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or tell me why my verdict on one of them should change. At the end, say which of these cases would let Aumann's theorem get a grip on the people involved.
