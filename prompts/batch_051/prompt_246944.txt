I work in reinforcement learning and am fluent in its standard vocabulary: agents, environments, policies, returns, reward functions, and failure modes such as reward hacking, wireheading, specification gaming, distributional shift, and the sim-to-real gap. I keep hearing philosophers discuss Nozick's experience machine as an objection to hedonism about well-being, and the debate sounds to me like a philosophical version of problems my field already names. I want a systematic translation, not an introductory explanation. Restate the experience machine thought experiment and the three theories of well-being it is standardly used to test — hedonism, desire-satisfaction theory, and objective-list theory — in my vocabulary, theory by theory, as faithfully as you can. For each, say which claims translate cleanly, which you had to distort, and which have no counterpart at all. Then give me an accounting of what did not survive the translation. The standard reasons for refusing the machine should be translated and audited as well: that we want actually to do things rather than merely have the experience of doing them, that we want to be a certain kind of person, and that we want contact with a reality deeper than the one the machine simulates. In particular, I want to know whether the refusal to plug in is best rendered as a claim about reward mis-specification, a claim about distributional shift between the machine and the real environment, or a claim my framework cannot state at all. If the last, tell me whether that gap is a defect of the RL vocabulary or evidence that the philosophical notion in question is less clear than it looks. Keep the translation honest: do not smuggle RL assumptions in and call the result a translation.
