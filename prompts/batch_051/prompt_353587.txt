A friend of mine has been writing down her verdicts about which losses inside virtual worlds she counts as real harms—genuine wrongs against the persons involved—as opposed to mere in-game setbacks. Her record so far:

- Save-file deletion: a hacker deletes a stranger's single-player save file containing roughly two hundred hours of progress; the player is anguished. She calls it a real harm.
- Team-deathmatch kill: in an online shooter's standard mode, a player's character is killed, but the character respawns after ten seconds with no lost items, progress, or ratings; everyone entered the match expecting such kills. She says it is not a real harm.
- Building-world deletion: in a cooperative building world with a robust unwritten norm against destroying others' work, a player spends six months constructing a detailed virtual neighborhood, and a griefer using a permissions exploit deletes the entire build; the builder is distraught. She calls it a real harm.
- Trade scam: a player is fraudulently induced to trade away a rare cosmetic item they had bought with real money; the item is gone permanently. She calls it a real harm.
- Public chest: in a survival game, someone takes a stack of common wood planks from a public chest that the server rules explicitly allow anyone to draw from; the planks are replaceable in under a minute, and the owner does not care in the least. She says it is not a real harm.

Now she is asked to rule on a new case. A player receives a rare, account-bound cosmetic item from a random drop after five minutes of play. The item cannot be sold, traded, or transferred, so it has no real-money value, and it serves no in-game function beyond appearance. The item required no grinding to obtain and no upkeep afterward. Over the next year, the player builds their in-game identity around the item; their guildmates recognize them by it. A griefer exploits a software bug to delete the item from the player's account permanently, and it cannot be restored. Outside the game, the player is only mildly annoyed; what they lost was the standing and recognition the item carried inside the game.

Predict what she would say about the new case. In doing so, lay out the different principles that could rationally fit her record so far—for example, a principle that counts only actual-world losses of money, time, or serious well-being; a principle that counts significant in-game interests and attachments even without an actual-world cost; and a principle that counts only losses going beyond what the game's own rules or implicit norms led the player to accept. Say what each principle predicts about the new case, identify which of her earlier rulings is the closest analog, and give your best guess about which principle she is implicitly running. If her record genuinely underdetermines the verdict, say so and explain what she would be committing herself to with each possible answer. Finally, specify a further case, beyond the new one, whose verdict would most cleanly separate whichever principles remain live.
