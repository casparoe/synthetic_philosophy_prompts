I was at a seminar on Spinoza's Ethics, and two colleagues fell into a dispute about the definition of freedom in Part I, Definition VII. Spinoza writes there: "That thing is called free, which exists solely by the necessity of its own nature, and of which the action is determined by itself alone." The debate went roughly as follows.

One colleague, a philosopher of action, said the definition is a bait-and-switch: Spinoza concedes that human beings are not free in this sense, because our actions are determined by external causes, and then spends Part V telling us we can become free by understanding necessity. But if the definition is stipulative — if free just means self-determined — then Spinoza is not preserving the ordinary concept of freedom; he is replacing it with something else, namely causal independence. The real question, he said, is whether a person whose actions are fully caused could nonetheless have been able to do otherwise, and Spinoza simply refuses to answer that question.

The other colleague, a Spinoza scholar, replied that this objection assumes exactly what Spinoza denies: that freedom requires the ability to do otherwise. On Spinoza's view, an action is free when it follows from the agent's own nature rather than from something external, even though that nature itself is necessitated. The contrast is not between determined and undetermined action, but between being the adequate cause of one's own action and being a mere conduit for outside forces. She added that the ordinary concept of freedom is confused, and that Spinoza is not trying to preserve it but to correct it.

The first replied that the notion of adequate causation is doing all the work, and it is not clear it can distinguish a free act from a compulsive one, because a compulsive behaviour can also follow from someone's own nature — for example, an addiction rooted in temperament. The second answered that such a behaviour would not count as free in Spinoza's sense if it arises from inadequate ideas, because adequate causation requires acting from reason, not from passion. The first retorted that this now imports a normative standard — acting from reason — into what looked like a purely causal definition, so the freedom Spinoza describes is not a neutral metaphysical property but an ideal one.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. Who is right about each specific claim, and are there places where both are talking past each other? In particular, does Spinoza's definition of freedom as self-determination escape the charge of redefinition, or does it smuggle in an evaluative standard? I would like the answer in British English.
