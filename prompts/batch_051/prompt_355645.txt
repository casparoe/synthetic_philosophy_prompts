In my courses on the ethics of cooperation and collective action, I have been collecting cases that seem to sit uneasily between free riding and social parasitism, and I would like to know whether this exact configuration has an established name or instead needs to be carved off from its neighbors. The pattern, put abstractly, is this: a person takes the benefits of a cooperative practice and contributes nothing to maintaining it, yet their participation as a taker—or the distinctive manner in which they take—is itself part of what gives the practice its value to those who do contribute. If the person were excluded, or made to contribute in the ordinary way, the practice would lose something that the contributors themselves say they value.

Two instances, kept short. (1) A chamber music society at a small college is sustained by membership dues and a rotation of volunteer stewards. One member has never paid and never volunteers. The officers quietly waive the rules for him because, on the evenings he attends, he asks the kind of unprepared, slightly scandalized questions the society was founded to hear, and several paying members say they attend partly in the hope of seeing it happen. The officers are unanimous that if he were billed or assigned a shift, he would stop coming, and that the society would be diminished in their own estimation. (2) A rural carpool operates under a rule that riders take turns driving. One rider never drives and never pays for gas. Each driver nonetheless prefers a week with her in the car to a week without: she keeps the conversation going and keeps the driver alert on a long stretch of unlit road. The drivers know about the missed turns, could enforce the rule if any of them asked, and separately hope no one does.

In both cases there is no hidden cost waiting to be discovered: the contributors are aware of the situation, accurately judge that they benefit from the non-contributor's presence, and decline to enforce the rules. The person is receiving benefits without paying or taking the assigned turns, but is also, on the contributors' own preferences, making the scheme better rather than worse.

My question is what to call this. Does the philosophical literature on collective action, exploitation, or fair play contain a name for the configuration in which free taking is itself part of the shared good? I am not looking for a bare label; I want the concept carved at its edges. The standard diagnosis of free riding as a matter of unfairness—accepting benefits without bearing a fair share of the costs—and the picture of a social parasite as a net drain on a host both seem to fit imperfectly here, because the non-contributor is improving the practice rather than diminishing it. Is this still free riding? If so, what exactly is wrong with it, if anything? If it is not free riding, what is it, and under what conditions does the configuration turn into ordinary free riding or ordinary parasitism? Please answer in American English.
