I'm teaching a third-year seminar on the social world, 16 students, one 80-minute slot. I want a classroom game that makes them feel Lewis on convention — specifically the idea that a convention is a regularity that solves a recurring coordination problem, that it's arbitrary (another regularity could have done the job just as well), and that it holds together mostly through mutual expectation rather than through being best or formally agreed. I also want a second stage that gets them to feel how a bare convention starts shading into a norm once people treat deviations as not just unexpected but wrong, and how a convention can shift or die when two groups with different conventions get mixed together. I don't want a lecture-with-a-vote or a debate; I want them to run into a coordination problem and realize afterwards that they've built a rule nobody actually decided on.

Give me rules, materials (no real money; points toward something silly is fine), a timeline for the 80 minutes, and a debrief that maps concrete moments in the exercise onto the moving parts of Lewis's account — coordination equilibrium, salience, precedent, common knowledge, and the claim that a different regularity would have been just as good. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Be honest about what the game cant get across — I suspect the full common-knowledge structure, and the difference between "arbitrary convention" and "rightness that would survive everyone else stopping," are hard to simulate. Also, in the facilitator notes for the debrief, separate the parts of Lewis's picture that are basically settled among people who work on this from the parts that are genuinely contested (for instance whether common knowledge is actually needed, whether conventions are better understood through rational choice or cultural evolution, and whether his notion of "arbitrary" really covers norms like promise-keeping). Don't present a contested reading as the consensus. And don't say the word "convention" to the students until after the game has done its work.
