I have recently taken over as the item-bank lead for the algebra courses at an online mathematics platform, and I have inherited a queue of grade disputes whose shape is always the same: a student is marked wrong for entering a mathematically correct answer in a different order, or the system’s own displayed answer changes when the backend returns roots in a different order. I need a reusable procedure my item writers can follow, because we cannot rebuild our grading system into a full computer-algebra system, most of the writers have an undergraduate mathematics background at most, each item has to be signed off by a curriculum editor before it goes live to students, and we can spend roughly fifteen minutes per item.

The phenomenon I keep returning to is this. The roots of a polynomial arrive as an unordered set: if I label them r1 through rn, any permutation of the labels leaves every symmetric statement about the roots untouched. Some quantities, like the sum of all the roots or the product of all the roots, do not depend on the labels at all. Some writings of a root are canonical because they select it by a definite property, such as the unique real root of x^3 − 2 or the larger of the two real roots of x^2 − 5x + 6. Others are not: one frequently sees items that refer to “the complex root” of a cubic with a conjugate pair, or that ask for the roots “in the order your calculator displays them,” as if an arbitrary naming convention were part of the mathematics. I want the procedure built on that distinction, but I do not trust myself to have anticipated every failure mode.

Please write the procedure as a checklist in words, with the checks in the order an item writer should run them. For each check give: the question to ask about a proposed item; what counts as passing; and the one-sentence principle that motivates it. The procedure should conclude in exactly one of three verdicts: approve as written, approve only after the item adds an explicit canonical convention, or return to a mathematician for review. It should also flag the points where a non-specialist might feel certain while actually making a mathematical judgment call.

Then run the procedure on these four items:
(1) an item that asks the student to enter the roots of x^2 − 5x + 6, with the grader currently checking against the ordered list “2, 3”;
(2) an item that asks the student to enter the imaginary part of “the complex root” of x^3 − 1, with the system accepting only the value its backend happens to compute;
(3) an item that says “let b be the positive root of x^2 − 2 = 0” and asks the student to enter b^2; and
(4) an item that asks for the roots of x^2 + 1 = 0 in the order displayed by the student’s graphing calculator, where the team wants a single canonical answer string so that reports do not vary between students.

In your worked applications, include at least one case where the procedure returns a verdict the item writers will find unreasonable, and explain exactly what they are being told to give up. End by saying what the checklist cannot do: which cases will still require a mathematician’s judgment, and which items will pass the checklist while remaining misleading.
