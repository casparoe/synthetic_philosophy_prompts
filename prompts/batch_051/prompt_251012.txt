My doctoral supervisor died six weeks ago, and I have been asked to deliver the eulogy at her memorial. She was a scholar of the critique of grand narratives: she argued, in her teaching as much as in her writing, that the impulse to turn a life or an era into a single coherent story is a way of suppressing whatever does not fit, and that the people left out of the story are the ones who pay for its coherence. She used to distinguish between a life rendered as a story and a life lived as an unordered pile of days; the first, she said, is what biographers sell, and the second is what we actually get.

The audience will be a difficult mixture. About half will be colleagues and former students who will recognize every theoretical move and will be troubled by a eulogy that quietly commits the very move she spent her career diagnosing. The other half will be her children, siblings, and old friends, who want to be told that her life amounted to something and will experience a purely deconstructive speech as a refusal to love her. The speech should run about eight minutes.

The hard constraint is this: the eulogy must not impose a grand narrative on her life — no claim that everything in her life was leading to some one thing, no single through-line, no early episode offered as the key that explains the rest. But it must still do what a eulogy is for: it must make the fact of her life weigh on the room as a whole, and it must let the family feel that her life has been gathered up rather than scattered. I want the speech itself, followed by a short set of notes in which you explain your structural decisions — where you used sequence without plot, repetition, or juxtaposition to do the work that narrative would normally do, and where you think you came closest to sliding back into the very mode she criticized.
