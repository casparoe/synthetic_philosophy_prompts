After four decades in the life sciences, I have recently turned to moral philosophy, chiefly the classic discussions of the fact/value distinction — Hume's remarks on 'is' and 'ought' and Moore's naturalistic fallacy — and they have left me suspicious of every inference from facts to values. Yet I can construct an argument that appears to derive an 'ought' from a series of 'is' claims, and I cannot, with any precision, locate the defective step. I would like you to press on it.

Here is the argument as I would set it out.

(1) Natural selection is a purely causal process, and it is established empirical fact that it has produced in human beings a characteristic set of ends they reliably pursue: avoiding injury, forming durable attachments, rearing offspring, and cooperating in groups.
(2) On the etiological account of function, the function of a trait is simply the effect for which it was selected; to say that the attachment system is 'for' securing care for the young is therefore a factual, historical claim, with no evaluative content smuggled in.
(3) Consequently there are plain facts about what human behavioral and affective systems are for, of exactly the same kind as the fact that the heart is for pumping blood.
(4) And where there is a function, there is a corresponding standard of proper operation: a heart that pumps well is a good heart, and one that does not is defective. This is the familiar functional sense of 'good,' not yet a moral one.
(5) Therefore the evolutionary facts in (1), together with the functional facts in (2) through (4), license the conclusion that certain ways of living are good for human beings, and that we ought to live in those ways. The 'ought' appears to have been extracted from a heap of 'is' statements — which is exactly the move I am now told cannot be made.

I suspect the conclusion is too strong, but I cannot say whether the difficulty lies in premise (2), in premise (4), or in the final step's slide from functional goodness to an 'ought.' Is the argument valid as written? Which step, if any, fails, and on what reading of it?

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Items one through four should treat steps (1) through (4) in order, stating whether each premise is true on its most defensible reading and whether it does the work the argument requires of it. Item five should state the overall verdict on step (5): whether the argument goes through, and if it does not, where the first illegitimate move occurs.
