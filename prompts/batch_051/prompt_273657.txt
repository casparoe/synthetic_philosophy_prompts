A friend walked me into this and I cant find the exit. His argument is that the line between a moral preference and a moral bias is basically a story we tell ourselves.

(1) To call someone's moral judgment "biased" is to say it deviates from the judgment they'd reach if they were responding properly to the relevant considerations.
(2) But in morality there's no independent benchmark of "responding properly" — all anyone has is the set of judgments they'd keep after good reflection.
(3) So whenever you call a rival moral view biased, you're using your own reflective judgments as the baseline for correctness.
(4) The other person can run the exact same move in reverse: from their side, your reflective judgments are the deviant ones, so your view is the biased one.
(5) Therefore neither side has a non-question-begging reason to call the other biased rather than just different in preference.
(6) So the distinction between a moral preference and a moral bias does no real work beyond marking whose side you're on.
(7) That can't be right. Take someone whose moral condemnation of a group dissolves once you correct her false factual beliefs about that group's behavior. We'd say she was biased, not merely that she had a different moral taste. But the argument seems to force us to say there's no principled difference.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd rather give up (2) than (7), but I dont know what that costs. Tell me exactly which step fails, or if it's valid, which premise I should reject and what I'm paying for keeping the obvious fact in (7).
