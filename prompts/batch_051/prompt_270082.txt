I've been toying with a way of thinking about metacognition that I've been calling "metacognitive shorting," and I want someone to tell me whether it's a real idea or just a metaphor with extra steps. Epistemic status: half-baked, probably reinventing something, but it keeps paying rent in my head.

Here's the setup. Take a first-order judgment like "this argument is sound" or "I should message this person." Normally you're long your own cognition: you act on the judgment by default. Then you do the rationalist thing and ask "wait, am I just pattern-matching? Am I confused?" That move—deliberately suspending your default trust in your own output to check whether it's biased, sloppy, or self-serving—is what I'm calling shorting your own cognition. You're betting against your own first-order process, so you profit (in accuracy) exactly when it was about to be wrong.

So far so obvious. The part I actually want stress-tested is this: holding a metacognitive short has a carry cost that people systematically undercount. The cost isn't just the time; its that sustained self-suspicion changes your priors about future self-trust, and it compounds when you do it recursively. When you ask "am I biased?", you now have a second-order judgment ("no," or "maybe," or "I genuinely can't tell about myself") that you also have to decide whether to trust, and each level eats attention and returns less. Meanwhile the potential payoff of the short is capped by how wrong the first-order process could plausibly have been, which depends on the domain. In a familiar, high-feedback domain (say, debugging code), first-order errors are usually caught quickly and fixed cheaply, so shorting yourself has almost no edge and you're mostly just paying carry. In an unfamiliar, high-stakes, low-feedback domain (say, "is my model of my own motivations self-serving?"), the short can pay off a lot but also has near-unbounded carry, which is why people end up in those loops where more introspection keeps not settling anything.

My proposed stopping rule is roughly: close the short when the expected accuracy gain per additional unit of reflection drops below the carry cost—not when you feel certain, because the feeling of certainty is itself a first-order output, and the whole reason you're shorting is that you've stopped treating those as reliable by default. I've been calling this the "close-the-trade rule." I have a nagging suspicion it quietly collapses into either (a) banal optimal-stopping / value-of-information reasoning that already exists, or (b) a justification for endless rumination disguised as prudence, because you can always tell yourself the tail cost of being wrong is enormous.

I know this sounds like the sort of thing someone posts after reading too many decision-theory papers. Here's what I want from you. Act as someone who knows both the metacognition literature in psychology and the more philosophical debates about reflection, epistemic akrasia, and when thinking about thinking helps or hurts. Think it through carefully; start by brainstorming and steelmanning the strongest version of my idea before you attack it. Then give me the three strongest objections. Tell me where it's genuinely new (if anywhere), where it breaks, and whether the finance metaphor is doing real work or just restating "think about whether continued thinking is worth it." And please separate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this; don't present one camp's view as if it were the consensus.

One more thing: I'm less interested in the object-level question "should I introspect more or less" and more interested in whether this model gives a usable criterion for when the answer changes. If the answer is just "it depends," I want to know what it depends on in a form I could actually operationalize.
