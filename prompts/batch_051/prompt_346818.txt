Some debates in the philosophy of logic persist not because anyone misunderstands the proof, but because the opposing camps never settle what would even count as a resolution. I would like a diagnosis of one such impasse, not a verdict on which side is right.

Consider Yablo's paradox. For each natural number n, let S_n be the sentence that says that every later sentence in the sequence — S_{n+1}, S_{n+2}, and so on — is untrue. Under classical logic together with the transparent truth principle that a sentence is true just in case what it says is the case, this sequence is inconsistent. If some S_n is true, then every later sentence is untrue, so S_{n+1} is untrue; but since S_{n+1} says that every sentence after it is untrue, S_{n+1}'s being untrue means that some sentence after it is true, contradicting S_n. If instead no sentence is true, then every sentence is untrue, which is exactly what each sentence says of the later ones — so each sentence is true after all. The derivation itself is not in dispute.

What has stalled is the question of what the paradox shows. One side reads it as demonstrating that paradox does not require self-reference, since no sentence in the sequence refers to itself: each refers only to strictly later sentences. The other side answers that the self-reference has been spread out rather than eliminated. On this view, each sentence quantifies over a totality of sentences whose truth statuses are defined globally, each sentence's truth value depends on the status of the sequence as a whole, and the circularity is present at the semantic or structural level even if no local syntactic self-reference is present. Curry's paradox sharpens the stakes: it is standardly read as showing that negation is not needed for paradox either, since an arbitrary conclusion can be derived from a self-referential sentence using the conditional and the truth schema alone. If Yablo's sequence is genuinely non-circular, the familiar 'self-reference plus negation' diagnosis of the liar collapses from two directions at once.

My working hypothesis is that the dispute is largely about how to individuate the phenomenon: the two sides are using 'self-reference' and 'circularity' for different things — one a local, syntactic relation; the other a global, semantic-structural property — and each side is correct under its own criterion, while nothing in the disputed proof forces a choice between them. But I am not confident. There may be a substantive formal question underneath, for instance whether the paradox can be reconstructed in a setting where the reference relation is provably well-founded and no sentence lies in the transitive closure of its own reference, or whether the global characterization is merely a proof artefact rather than part of the semantics. Diagnose this disagreement: is it verbal, is it substantive, or are the two sides answering different questions under a single heading? And on your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a shared formal criterion of circularity, a theorem settling the well-foundedness question, a redescription of the original question, or an argument that no progress should be expected?
