I'm writing an essay for a seminar on the ethics of the future, and I keep circling around something I cannot settle. Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture holds that much of what we now value depends on an unacknowledged background assumption: not that we ourselves will go on living, and not that we already care about the well-being of not-yet-born people, but that there will be a human future after us, roughly continuous with the one we know. Remove that assumption, he argues, and many of our activities would lose their hold on us even if our own lives went exactly as they otherwise would have. His infertility scenario isolates the point: the existing generation lives out normal lives, but no further children are born, so the human line quietly ends. Alfonso Cuarón's film Children of Men, adapted from P. D. James's novel, looks at first like that scenario made cinematic: a world that has mysteriously become sterile, in which public life has decayed into a militarized security state, the youngest living person is a celebrity whose death is publicly mourned, and a single pregnancy takes on what can only be called messianic significance.

What I want to know is whether the film actually shows Scheffler's conjecture at work, or whether it shows a series of adjacent but different phenomena that are easy to confuse with it. The film's breakdown is immediate, social, and violent, whereas Scheffler's thought experiment is deliberately quiet: people simply live out their natural spans. Is the despair on screen a loss of the background conditions of value, or is it better explained as a collective last-period problem, in which self-interested maintenance of institutions collapses once no future person will ever benefit from it, plus the ordinary grief and panic of mass sterility, neither of which requires the afterlife conjecture? Where in the film do characters actually seem to undergo the specifically Schefflerian loss, that is, the discovery that a practice they had kept up, maintaining a building, teaching, playing music, no longer is what it was, even though their own survival is not at stake? And where does the film depart from Scheffler, for example by pinning hope to a single miraculous birth when the conjecture concerns the ordinary continuation of generations? I would like the reading to be faithful in both directions: not reducing the film to an illustration of a thesis, and not stretching Scheffler's argument to cover everything the film depicts.
