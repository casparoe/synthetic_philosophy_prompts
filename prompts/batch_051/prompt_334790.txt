I am working through a tension in how to think about letterforms, and I want you to arbitrate it rather than survey it.

On one side, I am drawn to the view that a letter category is a family-resemblance concept with no essence. There is no single stroke, counter, or curve that every 'a' has and that no other letter has: a single-storey handwriting 'a', a double-storey book typeface 'a', and a blackletter 'a' can differ from one another as much as some 'a's differ from 'd's. On this view, being an 'a' is a matter of graded similarity to a cloud of stored exemplars plus a learned mapping to a grapheme slot. The category has no sharp boundary and no shared core, and talk of "the letter a itself" is a reification of a statistically useful grouping. This position is attractive because it is parsimonious, fits prototype- and exemplar-based accounts of categorization, and predicts that a logotype can be stretched until recognition fails without any single moment at which the letter's essence departs.

But I also have an intuition I cannot shake: that there is something all these shapes have in common, an invariant a competent letterer grasps when they draw an 'a' in a style they have never seen, and that makes a small change flip otherwise similar marks from 'a' to 'd' or 'q'. It seems as if the real work is done by a structural or relational fact about how the parts are arranged—a topological skeleton or a set of internal contrasts—not merely proximity to past exemplars. When a three-year-old's wobbly 'a' succeeds at being an 'a' while a beautifully drawn 'd' does not, it is hard for me to believe the difference is only statistical. The theory tells me this intuition is a projection by a cognitive system that reifies categories it has learned, and that it should be debunked. The intuition tells me the theory has mistaken the recognition mechanism for the nature of the category itself.

Present both positions at their strongest. Then give me your negotiated verdict: should the intuition be debunked and dropped, should the family-resemblance/prototype view be amended or abandoned in favor of a structural-relational account of letter identity, or is there a reading of "what makes every 'a' an 'a'" on which both survive? I want to know what would justify each of those moves—what should lead me to treat the invariance-intuition as a cognitive artefact, and what should lead me to conclude that the "no shared feature" claim was an artefact of too meager a feature vocabulary.

End your answer with a single line in the form \answer{...}, containing a short phrase for your verdict (for example, that the intuition yields to the theory, that the theory yields to the intuition, or that the two are compatible under a structural reading of letter identity).
