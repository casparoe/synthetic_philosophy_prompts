I have spent the past few months as a newly elected member of a small municipal council, and one episode has unsettled me. To keep a developer from suing the town into insolvency, I voted to approve a settlement in which the town publicly stated that a rejected permit had been incomplete on procedural grounds, even though everyone involved knew the real reason was political pressure from an influential neighborhood group. In private life I would not accept that a person may knowingly sign a false official statement to escape a costly conflict. Yet I found, and my fellow councillors found, that the public role changed the way we reasoned about the act: the falsehood appeared required by the office, and at the same time it left me feeling that I had done something that could not be fully washed away, which is not how I would have described the act if a private person had committed it under comparable pressure and been plainly excused.

That tension led me back to Chapter XV of The Prince, where Machiavelli argues that a ruler who makes it his profession to be good in every matter will come to ruin among many who are not good, and that a prince who wishes to keep his state must therefore learn how not to be good, and to use or not use that capacity as necessity dictates. I take the passage to be making a claim not only about effectiveness but about the standing of the act itself: the ruler is not simply permitted to do what private persons may not do; he is in part constituted by that permission, and the permission does not make the act clean. The ordinary view Machiavelli is rejecting seems to be that the moral law applies to rulers exactly as it applies to anyone else, and that a good end cannot cleanse an unclean instrument.

The asymmetry I want you to examine is this. When a private citizen writes the same kind of knowingly false official statement to avoid a comparable disaster, we tend to say either that it was wrong but excusable, or that it was right all things considered; we rarely insist on both. But when an officeholder does it under color of office, many people say both that the office required it and that the officeholder is nonetheless rightly guilty for having done it. Is there a principle that generates that difference? The strongest candidate I can formulate runs as follows: public office is a role with constitutive norms that may require otherwise forbidden acts, and the officeholder voluntarily takes on a burden of wrongdoing on behalf of the community, so the wrongdoing remains real even though the act may be required by the role. That is one version of the dirty hands thesis. But I am unsure whether it is anything more than a redescription of the asymmetry. Test it against nearby cases: a union negotiator who lies about a strike threat, a parent who falsifies a school district document to get a child into a safer school, a surgeon who coerces a detained patient into a necessary operation. Does the office-role principle mark a genuine difference between any of these and my settlement vote, or does it collapse into the ordinary excuses of necessity and duress? I want an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry between the officeholder and the private agent is defensible, or whether it should be given up in favor of treating both with the same moral categories.
