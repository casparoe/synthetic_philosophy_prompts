For a long time I have followed the debate over whether quantum indeterminacy can do any real work for free will, and it seems to me to be stuck in a pattern I can describe with some precision. The libertarian side needs an open future: if determinism is true, there is no alternative possibility and, they say, no ultimate sourcehood. Quantum mechanics looks like the natural ally, because it shows that the world is not deterministic at the microlevel. If decision-relevant neural events are sensitive to quantum events, then there are genuine forks in the agent's own history — points at which more than one continuation is physically possible. The other side, mostly compatibilists and hard incompatibilists, replies that this is precisely the wrong kind of openness. Quantum randomness, they say, is noise: a chancy event is no more within the agent's control than a determined event, and it is not something the agent can claim as their own doing. Making my choice probabilistic does not make it mine.

The libertarian response, as far as I can tell, takes two main forms. The event-causal form concedes that indeterminism entering arbitrarily into a decision would be a problem, but argues that indeterminism need only appear at special junctures — moments of inner conflict in which an agent is genuinely torn between incompatible motives. There, an effort of will is indeterminate in outcome, and whichever outcome occurs is one the agent endorses from within that tension, so the choice is not random with respect to the agent's reasons, while the agent genuinely could have chosen otherwise. The agent-causal form says that the agent as a substance, not any event, causes the decision; indeterminism is merely the condition under which the agent has more than one alternative, not a cause that competes with the agent.

The critics answer that the event-causal version merely relocates the luck problem. Suppose two worlds share the identical past, the identical laws, and the identical reasons, motives, and effort of will, yet in one the agent chooses A and in the other chooses B. Then the difference between those worlds is not attributable to anything about the agent — not to reasons, character, or effort — and there is no answer to the question of why A rather than B occurred. Calling the choice indeterministic names the problem rather than solving it. The critics often add that quantum mechanics makes this worse: the probabilities of quantum outcomes are law-governed and insensitive to the agent's reasons, so hitching free will to quantum events adds a veneer of physics without adding control. The agent-causal version, they say, is either unintelligible or collapses back into event-causation once one asks how the agent's causing relates to the agent's reasons.

At that point the libertarian makes the move I have heard many times, and this is where the debate stops: the critics, they say, are demanding that a free choice be fully intelligible in terms of the agent's reasons and character — that it be, in effect, determined by the agent's own states. That demand already assumes that freedom is a form of determination, and an indeterministic theory is guaranteed to look defective by that standard; but the standard itself is what is contested. The critics reply that they are not demanding determination, only that the agent's contribution make a difference, and an irreducibly chancy difference is no contribution at all. Each side now regards the other as begging the question, and the argument cycles without movement.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really an empirical dispute — neither side is waiting on better neuroscience about, say, quantum effects in the brain — and not merely a verbal one, but a disagreement about the standard of explanation appropriate to a free action: what it means for an agent to control or own an outcome, and whether control requires the outcome to be made intelligible by the agent's reasons. But I am not confident that diagnosis is complete, and I can imagine alternatives: a hidden difference about whether indeterminism can ever be attributed to the agent rather than to the world, or two different senses of "could have done otherwise" running under a single phrase.

What I want is a diagnosis of the structure of this impasse, not a verdict for one side. Is there a distinction — for example, between control as determination by reasons and control as authorization of an outcome among open alternatives — that would let the two sides see what they are actually disagreeing about? Would any experiment about quantum effects in decision-making matter even in principle, and if so what would it have to show? Or is the question "does quantum randomness help free will?" really several questions sharing one name, such that the only available progress is to split them apart?
