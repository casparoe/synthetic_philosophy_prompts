Here is a puzzle I keep circling around, and I want it translated into terms I can work with. I make my living building forecasting models, so I am at home with priors and posteriors, likelihoods, calibration, out-of-sample error, data-generating processes, and the difference between estimating a parameter and acting on the estimate. I have no training in action theory, and that is exactly the problem. The philosophical claim I cannot shake is roughly this: while a person is still making up her mind, she cannot coherently assign a probability to her own eventual choice, because prediction and decision are two different stances toward the same event. A prediction treats the choice as something to be observed, already settled in the world from the model's point of view, waiting to be read off; a decision treats the choice as open, its outcome up to the very deliberation that is now in progress. Attempting to do both at once seems either to collapse the decision into a forecast about a person the decider no longer fully is, or to collapse the forecast into a way of making the decision rather than predicting it. I want you to state this claim, and the strongest form of the argument behind it, in my vocabulary: as a claim about what a forecaster can and cannot model. Then I want an honest residue report. Which parts of the claim survive translation intact? Which parts can be forced into the language of priors, likelihoods, and data-generating processes only with distortion, and what gets bent in the forcing? Which parts have no expression in that vocabulary at all? Finally, tell me which way the residue cuts: does it show that the forecasting framework is missing a real feature of the situation, and if so what feature would a model need to represent in order to capture it, or does it show that the philosophical claim was trading on an imprecision that dissolves once it is made fully explicit?
