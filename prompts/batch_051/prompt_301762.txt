Consider a jurisdiction that leaves mandatory arbitration clauses in consumer and employment contracts enforceable, but subjects them to the following new conditions. Every arbitration award must be published in a public, searchable register, with the parties identified only by redacted category, such as a national retailer or a delivery driver. Arbitrators must state written reasons, and in any later arbitration, by any parties, a published award deciding the same recurring contractual question is binding on the arbitrator; an arbitrator who wants to depart must first certify the question to a trial court, whose published ruling then joins the register and binds future arbitrations as well. Write an analysis of how this arrangement would behave in practice over a decade or so. Who stands to control which disputes end in a published award rather than a confidential settlement, and how would repeat-playing companies try to shape the emerging body of private precedent? What happens to the substantive content of this private common law over time, to courts' dockets, and to the relative power of repeat players and one-shot individuals? Identify the first two or three ways the system would be gamed, and then assess whether adding a public ombudsman with standing to force a declined or settled case to an award would repair the worst failure mode without turning the system back into ordinary litigation.
