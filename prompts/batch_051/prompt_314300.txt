I've been turning over a question that I think usually gets asked the wrong way around. People who work with word embeddings often treat the geometry as a discovery — they point to analogies like king − man + woman ≈ queen and to cosine similarity as evidence — and then build a semantics on top of that geometry. I want to reverse the direction of explanation. What would a geometry of meaning have to get right before we grant that meaning has, or can be modeled by, that kind of structure? The deliverable is a desiderata list for a theory of lexical and phrasal semantics that represents meanings as points or regions in a geometric space (vector spaces, manifolds, or whatever turns out to be needed). For each requirement, give the concrete case or contrast that forces it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or, say, a convenient extra. The theory should be adequate to a competent speaker's lexical and phrasal semantic competence: graded similarity judgments, composition, polysemy and context shift, antonymy, entailment and contradiction, and whatever else belongs on the list. I am not asking for citations or a survey of models; I am asking for the conceptual spadework — so every requirement should be justified by a case, not by an authority.

Some starting irritations, to fix the level of detail I have in mind. (1) Similarity in a fixed metric space is symmetric: the cosine between "dog" and "poodle" is the same as the cosine between "poodle" and "dog". But people reliably judge "a poodle is like a dog" as stronger than "a dog is like a poodle." Does the geometry owe us an account of that asymmetry, or can it be legitimately pushed off onto pragmatics? (2) "Hot" and "cold" are near-neighbors in many embedding spaces precisely because they share contexts, and yet they are as close to opposites as words get. Is nearness the right geometric correlate of semantic relatedness at all, or is nearness being made to do two incompatible jobs? (3) "Red dress" and "red herring" seem to require different composition rules, while static word vectors compose the same way everywhere. So the task: compile the full spec; say which of these and which further items are genuine desiderata rather than habits of one research camp; tell me whether any subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable in a single flat vector space; and identify the one requirement that does the most to exclude naive fixed-metric accounts of meaning, with a sentence on why that one.
