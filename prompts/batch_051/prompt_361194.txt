The phrase "the Church-Turing thesis" has started to follow me around, and I get the uneasy feeling that it means something different each time it is invoked. In a philosophy of mind seminar, someone rejected the idea that a machine could do mathematics as competently as a human by saying that this would violate the Church-Turing thesis. In a conversation about physics, someone insisted that the thesis shows no physical process can compute anything beyond what a Turing machine computes, so hypercomputation is impossible in the actual universe. A computer scientist I asked about it shrugged and called it the claim that the informal idea of an effectively calculable function coincides with the precise class of Turing-computable functions — or equivalently the recursive functions, or the lambda-definable functions — which she regarded as more of a definitional remark than a discovery about the world. And a friend in cognitive science used it to argue that cognition must be computational, on the grounds that anything a finite physical system can do is Turing-computable anyway.

I cannot see how one thesis could be all of these things: a definition, an equivalence between formalisms, an empirical constraint on physics, and a claim about minds. I would like you to disambiguate the phrase for me. What are the distinct precise theses that travel under the name "the Church-Turing thesis," what is the logical status of each — definition, mathematical result, empirical conjecture, or something else — and how plausible or well-supported is each? Most importantly, which of those readings actually licenses each of the four moves I described, and which of the four moves is licensed by no defensible version of the thesis at all?
