I keep saying one theory is "closer to the truth" than another, and I've realized I don't actually have a definition under the hood. Not truth itself — I'm fine taking that as correspondence for now. I mean the comparative notion: Newton over Aristotle, a mostly-right weather model over one that never commits, a legend that gets the river right but the battle wrong. I want a working definition of truthlikeness (verisimilitude, same thing for my purposes) that handles these cases:

1. A theory whose only claim is "something exists" is true, while a detailed theory of planetary motion that's wrong in a few predictions is false. I want the false one to count as closer to the truth. So "true beats false, end of story" won't work.

2. A false theory that nails the central mechanism but flubs a handful of details, versus a false theory that gets a hundred peripheral facts right but has the central mechanism wrong. I want the first to count as closer, so the definition has to say something about why some truths weigh more than others.

3. "The Moon is made of rock or made of cheese" is true but empty, while "the Moon is made of basalt plus a bit of green cheese" is false because of the cheese but gets the rock part right. I have a strong intuition the second is closer to the truth, and I don't see how the first could beat it.

4. Take a false theory and add a true claim about something unrelated. The new theory is still false, but it seems like it should be at least a little closer — it got everything the old one got, plus one more thing right. But if closeness is a matter of counting true and false consequences, the new theory also adds false consequences (all the conjunctions involving the old false claim), so its not obvious the count improves. Your definition should say what happens here. I genuinely don't know what I want the answer to be on this one.

5. A forecaster who always says "50% chance of rain" and one who says "it will rain tomorrow" and is right four days out of five. Is "closer to the truth" the same thing as getting a better score under the usual rules for scoring forecasts, and if not, where do they come apart?

And a standing background worry: if you define closeness as "entails more truths and fewer falsehoods," any false theory entails infinitely many truths and infinitely many falsehoods, so the arithmetic has to be finessed somehow — maybe by restricting which consequences count, or by comparing something other than consequences. Dont just wave at that.

Propose the definition, run it through all five, and tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer and you'd have to either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. If you have to lean on a possible-world similarity metric, fine, but say what the metric is and why that one, because that always strikes me as shoving the problem into the metric.

And please answer in American English.
