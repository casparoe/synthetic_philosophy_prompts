I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas beat and I'm working on a piece about whether the future is already settled, so I've been interviewing a philosopher of logic and time by reading him sentences one at a time and asking whether, as of right now, he calls each one true, false, or neither. So far his scorecard is:

- "The sun will rise tomorrow" → true.
- "Either the sun will rise tomorrow or the admiral's fleet will be wrecked by the storm" → true.
- "There will be a sea battle tomorrow" — with the future genuinely open, no orders issued, weather could turn either way, either side could still avoid the fight — → neither.
- "There will not be a sea battle tomorrow" → neither.
- "Its possible there will be a sea battle tomorrow, and it's possible there won't be" → true.
- "There was a sea battle yesterday" → true, since the engagement did happen.

Before I file I have one more sentence to put to him: "Either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there won't be." I honestly don't know what he'll say. His earlier answers look to me like they fit at least two rules, though I could be missing one. On one rule, each unsettled future sentence is itself neither, and "or" works the way you'd expect in a three-valued logic: a true disjunct makes the disjunction true, but two neither disjuncts leave the disjunction neither. On another rule, a sentence is true if every still-open way the future could go makes it true, false if every still-open way makes it false, and neither otherwise. The first rule says the disjunction is neither; the second says it's true, because no matter whether the battle happens or not, one side of the disjunction comes out right. I don't think he's a plain bivalentist, since he flinched on both the sea-battle sentence and its negation.

What should I expect him to say about the disjunction, which rule is he most likely runing, and what's the best single follow-up sentence I should have ready to pin down which rule he's actually using?
