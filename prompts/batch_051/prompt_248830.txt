I'm leading a one-off ninety-minute session for a philosophy club of advanced high-school students on the Darwinian–Skinnerian–Popperian–Gregorian ladder of kinds of minds. Don't summarize the four kinds; plan how I should teach them. What has to be established first about natural selection as a designer of fixed competences before the Skinnerian creature is even a contrast worth drawing, and what about Skinnerian trial-and-error learning must be in place before the Popperian creature's internalized testing of candidate acts is intelligible, and what about that private preselection must be in place before Gregorian creatures can be introduced as more than tool-users? Choose the single example that should carry all four rungs — a forager sampling a novel food, or something better — and say what it must have (one task solvable by all four mechanisms) and what it must lack (no concealed appeal to language, explicit reasoning, or social transmission before those are introduced). Then lay out the misconceptions this audience will form — that the ladder ranks creatures by intelligence or worth, that each rung replaces the earlier ones, that Popperian creatures literally run conscious thought experiments, that Gregorian just means using artifacts — and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. End with one question that would show me whether they have understood that each rung is a new way of purchasing good design with less death and less time, not a ranking of minds.
