I keep coming back to the Kyoto School idea that the fundamental thing is something like pure experience: the pre-reflective given before the split into "me over here" and "the world over there." When a sound grabs you, or when you're fully absorbed in something, the story goes, there is just the experience; only afterwards does reflection carve it into a subject who heard and an object that was heard. What attracts me is how much work the move does. If subject and object are both abstractions from a single more basic place (the basho), then the whole problem of how a mind gets access to a world dissolves instead of being solved — there was never a gap to bridge. It's one elegant posit, and it has the nice implication that the most absorbed, unselfconscious moments of life are closer to how things actually are than the reflective ones.

But I have a stubborn intuition that experience is never actually like that. Even in the most absorbed state, the experience is still mine and still of something. Consciousness seems to be consciousness-of — directed — right down to the bottom, and always from a first-person point of view. The subject–object structure doesn't feel like something I add afterwards by reflecting; it feels like it's already there in the seeing itself. And the language fights back too: "experience" is a two-place word, something of something by someone, and if I strip away the of and the by I'm not sure I'm left with an experience rather than a bare event. Worse, when I try to think my way down to the pure experience, I catch myself doing exactly what the theory says is derivative: standing back and turning the experience into an object for thought. It smells like trying to see the frame by looking harder at the picture.

So I'm genuinely torn. Should I trust the theory's elegance and debunk the intuition as a symptom of doing philosophy, i.e., of reflecting on experience instead of living in it? Or should the intuition push me to amend the theory, maybe treating pure experience as an ideal limit that ordinary intentional experience approaches but never reaches? Or is there a compatibilist reading where directedness and mineness are real features of experience but unfold inside a place that itself never shows up as an object? Give me the strongest version of each of these three moves and say what would justify each, not just which one you'd pick.
