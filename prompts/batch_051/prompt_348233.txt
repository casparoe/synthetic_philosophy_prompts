I'm a PhD student working in moral psychology and ancient philosophy, and I've been trying to construct a variant of the ring of Gyges from Republic II that isolates something I find under-theorized in Glaucon's challenge. In the familiar story, a shepherd acquires a ring that makes him invisible, uses it to enter the palace, seduce the queen, kill the king, and seize power. Glaucon's gloss is that a just person with such a ring would behave no differently from an unjust person, because what restrains people is not justice itself but the fear of punishment and the desire for a good reputation. The original example works by giving one person total impunity inside a city where everyone else remains visible, detectable, and predictable.

Consider this change instead: every adult in the city receives an identical ring at the same moment, and each ring works only for its assigned bearer. The wearer becomes invisible at will; any act performed while invisible is unobservable and leaves no identifying trace, though the person remains tangible and can be killed or wounded by ordinary physical force. Everyone therefore has exactly the impunity Gyges had, but no one occupies Gyges' position of being the single invisible agent surrounded by visible ones.

I want to know how far Glaucon's argument survives this universalization. Does his claim—that the just and the unjust would act the same, and that the just person who refused to exploit the ring would be regarded as a fool—depend on the asymmetry built into the original? In a city where everyone can defect invisibly, can anyone secure the goods that were supposed to make injustice worth choosing: stable power, trusted confederates, enforceable promises, a reputation for justice that earns trust? If those goods presuppose that most other people remain visible and broadly just, what becomes of the thesis that justice is chosen only for its consequences? More precisely, is the universal-ring city a case where self-interest continues to recommend injustice but the net result is mutually ruinous, or does the disappearance of reputational goods make justice more rather than less attractive to a prudent egoist? And how must Socrates' reply shift once the social benefits of justice can no longer be assumed to exist: does his case have to rest entirely on the internal condition of the soul, and is that enough?
