I'm a science and ideas journalist, and my current work keeps bringing me back to the doctrine of double effect — the principle that, roughly, it can be permissible to bring about a bad result as a foreseen side effect of pursuing a good end, even though it would be impermissible to intend that same result as a means to that end. It comes up everywhere I look: end-of-life sedation, separating conjoined twins, military operations that knowingly kill civilians, and the difference between diverting the trolley and pushing a bystander onto the track. I'm building a working file of the objections serious critics raise against the doctrine, and I don't want the same four or five on repeat. So far my list has these. The closeness problem: the pairs of cases the doctrine wants to separate — a hysterectomy that removes a cancerous uterus and thereby ends a pregnancy, versus a craniotomy performed to deliver through an obstructed birth — seem too similar in what is done and in what is lost for the intended/foreseen distinction to carry that weight. The moral-inertness objection: whether a death is intended or merely foreseen looks like the wrong sort of fact on which to ground permissibility, since it says nothing about the harm done, the interests or rights in play, or what anyone has reason to prefer. The proportionality objection: once the doctrine requires the foreseen harm to be proportionate to the good achieved, the proportionality test is doing all the real work and the intended/foreseen distinction is a dispensable wrapper. The gaming objection: because the doctrine is keyed to a private mental state, an agent can reframe the very same act so that the harm comes out as merely foreseen, which makes the doctrine both too permissive and unreliable. And the restrictiveness objection: it appears to forbid intended harms that are plainly permissible, killing a lethal attacker being the stock case.

I suspect I'm missing at least one distinct line of attack — one that is not a rewording, special case, or combination of anything on that list, and that doesn't just say the distinction is vague, gameable, or morally inert. Write that objection for me in a form I could actually use: state the argument with enough structure to follow, give the kind of case or consideration that gives it force, and say why the doctrine's standard defensive moves wouldn't simply absorb it. Then give me an honest assessment: is it any good, or does it collapse under pressure once stated precisely? I'd also like you to say how confident you are in what you claim, and to single out the point you are least sure of.
