For a philosophy of perception seminar, I am presenting a paper arguing that Molyneux's question—whether a person born blind, upon first gaining sight, could visually distinguish a cube from a sphere before touching them—is not a clean test of cross-modal transfer but is entangled with the same acquired perceptual judgment that Locke takes to operate in ordinary adult vision. I want your credence that the newly sighted person in Molyneux's case would fail to reliably distinguish the two by sight alone. Give me a number and decompose it. Locke writes in An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter IX, Section 8:

“When we set before our eyes a round globe of any uniform colour, v.g. gold, alabaster, or jet, it is certain that the idea thereby imprinted on our mind is of a flat circle, variously shadowed, with several degrees of light and brightness coming to our eyes. But we having, by use, been accustomed to perceive what kind of appearance convex bodies are wont to make in us; what alterations are made in the reflections of light by the difference of the sensible figures of bodies; – the judgment presently, by an habitual custom, alters the appearances into their causes. So that from that which is truly variety of shadow or colour, collecting the figure, it makes it pass for a mark of figure.”

Against that background, your decomposition should assign rough probabilities to at least these sub-questions: that the newly sighted person's initial visual idea of a globe is of a flat, variously shaded circle rather than a three-dimensional shape; that the habitual judgment Locke describes is necessary for seeing the globe as convex; and that tactile training with cubes and spheres transfers to visual shape recognition without that visual habit. How do those probabilities combine, and which sub-question would most shift your final credence if you changed it?
