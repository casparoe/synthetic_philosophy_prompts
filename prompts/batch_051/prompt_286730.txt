I recently finished an introductory essay on the philosophy of biology that reconstructs the debate over whether the principle of natural selection is a tautology, and I want to know how much of that reconstruction I can trust. The essay describes the debate as follows.

The classic objection, it says, is that the slogan that the fittest survive is empirically empty: if fitness just means actual survival and reproductive success, then the slogan says only that those organisms that survive and leave the most offspring are the ones that survive and leave the most offspring. The standard reply, the essay continues, is the propensity interpretation of fitness. On this view, fitness is not an organism's actual reproductive output but a probabilistic disposition—rather like a coin's bias, present even when a particular toss comes up tails—to produce a certain expected number of offspring in a specified environment; a fit organism could be killed by lightning before reproducing and still be fit. The essay then argues that this reply merely relocates the problem. First, an expected number of offspring is just a statistical average computed over actual outcomes, so the propensity account still defines fitness in terms of actual survival and reproduction, now hidden under the language of probability. Second, since a disposition that is never manifested—such as high fitness in an organism struck by lightning—is never observed, ascriptions of fitness on this account are unfalsifiable and therefore scientifically idle. Third, natural selection works on actual differences in reproductive success, not on hypothetical tendencies; a disposition to reproduce contributes nothing to evolutionary change unless it is realized, so mentioning it does no explanatory work. The essay concludes that the propensity interpretation therefore trades a tautology for an equally serious problem of unobservability.

I am not equipped to assess this because the literature is unfamiliar to me, and my suspicion that the conclusion is too strong may be baseless. Will you audit the essay's reconstruction? I want to know which parts accurately report the standard positions, which are simplifications, and which are misstatements or conflations, and I want to know where a defender of the propensity interpretation would object and what they would offer instead. In particular: does the propensity view actually define fitness in terms of actual outcomes, or does it define fitness in terms of a causal disposition that can diverge from actual outcomes? Is the unfalsifiability charge a fair statement of the strongest objection to the view, or an overstatement? And does the final conclusion, that the tautology is replaced by unobservability, survive once the accurate parts are separated from the rest?
