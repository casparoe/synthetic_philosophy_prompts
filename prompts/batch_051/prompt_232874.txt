I'm in a grad seminar on immortality and wellbeing, and we spent two weeks on Williams's Makropulos case: Elina Makropulos has lived for several centuries on an elixir, and by the time the play catches up with her she's so bored and detached that she lets the elixir lapse and dies. Williams's line, as my professor reads it, is that this isn't just Elina's personal quirk. The thought is that a life only matters to the person living it when they have categorical desires—desires that give them reasons to go on at all, not just "if I happen to be alive I'd like..."—and that any normal human life would eventually run through every categorical desire it can sustain, until continued existence becomes tedious. At that point "eternal bliss" is supposed to be a contradiction, because bliss has to be wanted from the inside. I don't buy that this is a conceptual point: it sounds like an empirical claim about hedonic adaptation and habituation, which would in principle be testable, and if it's empirical it might just be a fact about our current psychology rather than about endless life as such.

So I want to know how you'd actually put pressure on this. Design the best test you can of the claim that the tedium of immortality is a conceptual truth about the structure of desire rather than an empirical generalization about human beings. Say what result would favor each reading, and what the test could never settle even in principle. Format: exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
