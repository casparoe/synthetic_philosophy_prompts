my friend keeps citing Good's theorem to say that you should never refuse free information, even if it might ruin your day. i tried to reconstruct the argument and now i'm not sure where the overreach is, if there is one. here's what i have:

(1) you're about to make a decision, and someone offers you a piece of information for free before you choose.
(2) let A be the act you would have chosen without seeing the information, with expected utility u given your current beliefs.
(3) after seeing the information, one available strategy is to ignore it and just do A no matter which piece of information arrives; from your current perspective this strategy has expected utility u.
(4) a rational agent picks, from the available strategies, the one that maximizes expected utility from their current perspective.
(5) so the best strategy after agreeing to see the information has expected utility at least u from your current perspective.
(6) so seeing the information cannot be expected to make you worse off.
(7) therefore a rational agent should never refuse costless information, and paying to avoid information is always irrational.

i accept (1)–(6), but (7) feels like a leap. is the whole thing valid? if not, which step fails, and what's the strongest version of the conclusion that actually follows?
