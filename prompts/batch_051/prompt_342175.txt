Here is an asymmetry in how many of us engage with fiction. We accept without a murmur that a novel can stipulate descriptive impossibilities — a talking snake, a woman who walks through walls, a star that burns cold — and we immediately adjust our imaginative frame to suit them. But when the same narrative voice stipulates that a character acted rightly in framing an innocent person, many readers report a sudden refusal: imagining the action itself is easy, but entering the fiction's evaluation of it seems not to be on offer. This is puzzling because the same readers follow a murderous king or an unrepentant thief through a story without resistance, imagining the deeds vividly and even sympathetically. So the recalcitrance is not an unwillingness to imagine wrongdoing; it is something about the fiction's asserting that the wrongdoing is right. Is there a principle that draws the line where our practice draws it — permitting almost any expressly stipulated descriptive deviance while refusing expressly stipulated moral deviance — and that makes the refusal a warranted response rather than mere squeamishness? I want the strongest candidate principle you can construct. Test it against at least two nearby cases: a stipulation of aesthetic value (a novel declaring a plainly banal sonnet sublime) and a stipulation of epistemic status (a novel declaring a character's obviously false belief to be knowledge). Then give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept as it stands, given up, or kept in modified form. Finally, say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
