For my wellbeing and public policy seminar I need a handout dialogue. Write a dialogue between two equally competent policy advisors — an economist at the treasury and a philosopher seconded to the prime minister's office — about whether the country should formally commit to moving up, say, five places in the World Happiness Report over the next decade. Neither should be a strawman; each should actually respond to what the other said.

The economist defends the target: the ranking tracks real goods (income, health, social support, freedom, low corruption), its a better north star than GDP, and a life-evaluation measure is the right object for policy even if it isn't moment-to-moment affect — people care how their life rates overall, not just whether they feel cheerful.

The philosopher pushes on three fronts. First, the ladder question asks people to rate their life against the best life they can imagine, so the number is a comparison against a moving reference point, not a raw readout of how well life is going; a country can climb just by lowering expectations or changing who gets surveyed. Second, once the ranking becomes a target it stops being a clean measure (the usual Goodhart problem), and several of the report's six factors are already things governments target, so the ranking is partly a repackaged index. Third, even if the measure were accurate, there's a question whether happiness is the sort of thing you can aim at directly, or whether it's a byproduct that disappears when you chase it.

Have the economist answer those three points specifically, and the philosopher respond in turn, so by the end they've located the real disagreement — probably less about the data than about whether a measure of reflective life satisfaction can survive being made a policy target. Around 800-900 words, and it should end at a sharper point than it began.
