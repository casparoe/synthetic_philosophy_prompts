I've gotten myself into a debate with a friend about the Mīmāṃsā view of scripture and testimony, and I want to pressure-test it from a direction I don't usualy see in intro surveys. The position, as I've understood it, is that the Veda has no author — not a god, not a person, nobody — so its authority cannot be a matter of trusting a competent speaker. Instead validity is intrinsic (svataḥ prāmāṇya): a Vedic sentence, like a cognition in general, counts as knowledge unless and until some specific defeater knocks it out. You don't first establish the text's reliability and then believe it; the presumption runs the other way, and the burden is always on the objector to show why a particular passage shouldn't be accepted. Apparent internal conflicts get settled by interpretation rather than by rejecting a passage, and the Veda's jurisdiction is dharma — what to do — rather than empirical fact.

What I want is a map of what would count against this view, including by the lights of somebody who actually holds it and not just by mine. What would have to turn out to be true — historically, linguistically, epistemically, morally — for the authorless-Veda-plus-intrinsic-validity package to be in real trouble? I can vaguely imagine candidates: credible evidence that the text was composed by particular ritual communities or editors over time; a rival tradition's equally self-certifying revelation making incompatible demands; the text commanding something that still looks vicious after the interpretive principles have done their work; or the whole apparatus being so protected by its burden-of-proof rules that no evidence could ever get in. I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd like you to say which ones a committed Mīmāṃsaka would themselves concede as counting against the view, and how they'd absorb or deflect the others. It would also help to know where the view sticks its neck out — what it predicts about language, the history of the text, or the structure of authority that its rivals don't — and whether the position ends up so insulated that this counts as a problem rather than the view working as designed.
