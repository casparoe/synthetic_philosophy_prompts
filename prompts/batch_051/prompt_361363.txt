I teach an upper-level undergrad philosophy of mind course, and a good third of the students are the sort who came up through LessWrong and have read GEB two or three times, so if I set a straight question on the Aunt Hillary ant fugue I'll get forty essays that all do the "whole is more than the sum of the parts" dance and then handwave that Aunt Hillary is just a story the observer tells. I want a final essay question that makes that move not work. The crux I'd like it to force is this: the ants in the fugue are already little agents with goals, so the colony looks less like a brain and more like a Chinese-nation or homunculi-headed system, which makes the standard functionalist "in principle, sure, multiple realizability" response feel too cheap; but if a student says the colony can't have a mind because the pheromone signals are meaningful only to an outside interpreter, they need to explain why that same objection doesn't apply to neurons, whose firings are also "meaningful" only under some higher-level description. One trap I'd like the question to lure them into is the motte-and-bailey between "the colony literally has a mind" and "the colony is a useful level of description"; an A answer would have to name an extra property a system needs in order to be the user of its own colony-level information rather than just a system that looks smart to us. Maybe seed a concrete weird variant in the question so it can't be answered from the standard summary, like a colony where each pheromone readout is mediated by a small dumb robot that copies the signal perfectly but also forwards a copy to a datacenter, and ask whether that changes anything and where the mind would be located under that arrangement.

I'm imagining a 75-minute in-class essay worth 25 marks, for a class that has already covered functionalism, the homunculus regress, and absent-qualia-style objections to functionalism. Please write the exam question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme that seperates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain; also add one-line notes on the two or three mistakes wich should cost marks (for example, conflating "we can interpret the colony as representing X" with "the colony represents X").
