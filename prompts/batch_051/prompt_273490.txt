I'm auditing a philosophy of social science seminar and I've been keeping a list of my own verdicts about which explanations of social phenomena are individualist and which are holist, because I keep feeling like I'm drawing the line inconsistently. I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, is actually doing the work behind my intuitions.

Here are the cases, roughly as I'd describe them.

1. A bank run is explained by the fact that each depositor, having heard rumors about the bank's liquidity, reasonably decides to withdraw early to protect their savings; the collapse is a consequence, but no depositor wanted the bank to fail. I call this individualist without hesitation.

2. A persistent gap in homeownership rates between two racial groups is explained by the fact that, generation after generation, individual families in one group have had less inherited wealth on average, so fewer can scrape together a down payment even though lenders treat them the same. I call this individualist too, though it feels less clean.

3. The same gap is explained instead by a 'racialized housing regime': a set of interacting laws, lender practices, appraisal assumptions, and informal norms that keeps reproducing the gap even when the particular people implementing them change. I call this holist.

4. A tribe's taboo on eating a certain animal is explained by the shared belief that the animal is sacred, together with each member's fear of supernatural punishment. I want to call this individualist, because the taboo just is the pattern of individual beliefs and choices. But a friend called it holist, on the grounds that the content of the belief is irreducibly social. I can't decide.

5. Wage stagnation in an industry is explained by 'competition among firms for profit' rather than by any particular manager's choices. I call this holist, though it bugs me, since firms are ultimately just people.

6. A rural-urban split in an election is explained by the fact that rural voters felt their status was declining, by the opinion leaders they trust, and by the way district lines were drawn. I call this indivualist, since it's all facts about individuals and their physical surroundings, even though 'status' is a social thing.

You're a philosopher of social science. Please think carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming several candidate principles before committing, even if you end up rejecting most of them. Then tell me: is there a single principle that captures all these verdicts, or a small set of candidates? State the principle precisely, say how each clause is motivated by one of my cases, and tell me what it says about a case I haven't listed — say, explaining a housing price bubble by 'contagion' or 'animal spirits.' Be honest about which of my verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to give up or revise. Also, since I know this area has some settled parts and some genuinely contested parts, please separate those in your answer and don't present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
