I have been asked to circulate a short statement of my commitments about group decision-making for a workshop on institutional design, and I would like a consistency audit of the draft before I send it. I have been working through the judgment-aggregation literature and the discursive dilemma: the fact that majority votes on a conclusion and majority votes on its premises can come apart, as when three judges vote so that there is a majority for p, a majority for q, and a majority against p&q. The following is roughly where I have landed, and I would like to know whether the package is tenable.

(1) The collective stance of a multi-member body should be fixed question by question by majority vote: for any proposition on the table, the position of the body is the position of the majority of its members, full stop. No subgroup should get to dictate the group's answer to a question it lost.

(2) A body should not be left adopting an inconsistent set of positions. If a group ends up committed to p, committed to if p then q, and committed to not-q, that is not an interesting diversity of opinion; it is an institutional failure.

(3) For an expert or advisory committee whose task is to track the truth, the right response to the discursive dilemma is premise-based aggregation: the committee should accept the conclusion that follows from whatever premise-majorities it has endorsed, even when a majority of members would reject that conclusion if it were put to a direct vote.

(4) For a criminal court, the opposite holds: the verdict should track the majority on the verdict itself, not an element-by-element majority. A conviction that most jurors reject as a verdict cannot be rescued by the observation that most accept each element of the offense taken separately.

(5) Majority procedure draws its point from the equal standing of the members. This justification does not require that the members in the majority on a question share the same reasons, and it does not require that the same people constitute the majority across different questions.

(6) I am inclined to think there is no neutral way to decompose a decision into "the premises" and "the conclusion." How the issues are carved up can change what the majority favours, so a majority-based procedure is determinate only once some non-majoritarian choice of agenda or framing has already been made.

I suspect this hangs together better than it might look at first, but I do not trust my own eye here. Go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only seem to be, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and say for each what would have to give and what the cheapest fix would cost. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the overall diagnosis and single out the point you are least sure of.
