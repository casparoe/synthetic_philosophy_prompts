Last evening I had a long argument with a friend about Gongsun Long's "White Horse Discourse," and I came away genuinely unsure what the text is trying to do. My friend's position is that the whole piece is a kind of verbal trick: it demonstrates only that the compound term "white horse" is not interchangeable with the bare term "horse," and that once you distinguish an expression from the thing it picks out, the paradox evaporates. I kept resisting this and saying that Gongsun Long seems to be making a stronger claim about horses and their properties, not merely about words, but I could not state the difference in a way that survived my friend's objections. The passage we circled back to was this one, from Donald Sturgeon's translation of the Gongsun Longzi:

    White does not fix what is white, this can be put aside. "White horse" speaks of white fixing what is white. That which fixes what is white is not white. "Horse" does not pick or exclude color, thus a brown or black horse can be brought. "White horse" does pick or exclude color; brown and black horses are excluded by color, thus only a white horse can be brought. That which does not exclude is not that which does exclude. Thus I say: "a white horse is not a horse."

Please explain, as charitably as you can, what argument Gongsun Long is actually making in that passage and how it fits the dialogue as a whole. Specifically, I want to know whether my friend is right that the position is only about words and what may be substituted for them, or whether the argument is better read as a claim about things themselves. How are the distinctions the passage draws — between white as such and white as fixed to a thing, and between not excluding color and excluding color — supposed to support the conclusion? A good answer would help me see why a serious reader might treat this as philosophy rather than a stunt with words.
