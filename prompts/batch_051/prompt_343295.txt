Two people in my building got into an argument that never resolved, and I keep turning it over. Maybe you can sort it out.

Maya was saying that if a company builds a profile of you — where you go at 2 a.m., what you buy, guesses about your health — and then sells it, they've taken something that belongs to you. Her reasoning was basically: it's about you, it comes from your life, so it's yours. If you don't control it, that's a kind of theft, even if you never knew it was collected.

Dre pushed back. He said "about you" doesn't make a thing yours. Information isn't like a bike. If ten people learn you had eggs for breakfast, your information isn't reduced — nothing is taken in the ordinary sense. Ownership is the wrong category, because information isn't rival and it isn't naturally excludable. You have a privacy interest, he said, not a property interest; privacy is about who may access something in what context, not about who owns it.

Maya came back: lots of owned things are non-rival. A patent, a song — you don't lose the song when I copy it, but it's still yours. She said personal data is like intellectual property, except the "creation" is your own life, so the claim is even stronger.

Dre said that's the problem: patent and copyright are legal artifacts designed to incentivize making things. The rights attach because the creator produced something. But a fact about you can be independently discovered. If someone watches you in public and jots down that you smoke, that's their observation, their record. Does your ownership reach into their notebook? Into their memory? If two people watch you do the same thing, are they each holding a copy of your property without having deprived you? It gets weird fast.

Maya said fine, maybe "property" is just a handle, but the underlying point stands: you should have a say in, and a share of, the value extracted from data about you. A big ad-targeting model is valuable because its built out of millions of people's behavioral traces. Those people are the cause of its value. Causation plus being about you is enough.

Dre said causation can't ground ownership, otherwise you'd own part of the census, since your existence caused one of the entries. And a shopkeeper's ledger of what you bought is their record of their own side of the transaction, not yours; they need accounts anyway.

Maya said a transaction isn't just theirs: it involved you, so the record is jointly authored. Dre said no — a record of an interaction is each side's memory of it. You can't veto someone writing down what happened to them.

They kept going until someone changed the subject. I honestly don't know who's right about what here. Can you go through the actual moves — the non-rivalry point, the independently-discovered-fact point, the causation point, and the joint-record point — and say who's right about which, and what both of them are missing?
