I've been trying to make Frege's sense/reference distinction do some real predictive work, and I keep ending up in a mess. The passage that started it, from the Max Black translation of Frege's "On Sense and Reference", is this:

"It may perhaps be granted that every grammatically well-formed expression representing a proper name always has a sense. But this is not to say that to the sense there also corresponds a reference. The words 'the celestial body most distant from the Earth' have a sense, but it is very doubtful if they also have a reference. The expression 'the least rapidly convergent series' has a sense; but it is known to have no reference, since for every given convergent series, another convergent, but less rapidly convergent, series can be found. In grasping a sense, one is not certainly assured of a reference."

My tentative rule was: an identity sentence is informative for someone exactly when the two sides present the same object under different modes of presentation, and it shouldn't matter whether those sides actually refer. That rule fit my opening examples and then slowly came apart. Here are my verdicts on a stack of cases. Tell me what principle actually captures them, test it against a case I haven't listed, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't keep. If the choice is between a baroque principle that saves all of them and a simpler one that overturns one or two, I'd lean simpler, but make the case.

1. Before I knew any astronomy, "Hesperus is Phosphorus" was genuinely informative; now it just reads as a triviality. So whether the sentence informs depends on the person and time, not on the sentence alone.
2. "The fortieth president of the United States is Ronald Reagan" is informative to someone who knows the office by description but has never connected it to the name.
3. "Clark Kent is Superman" is informative in much the same way, even though I don't think there is any flesh-and-blood referent behind either term; it's a spoiler about a story, and it feels like it tells you something.
4. "Neptune is the planet whose gravitational pull explains the anomaly in Uranus's orbit" is informative, and true.
5. "Vulcan is the planet whose gravitational pull explains the anomaly in Mercury's orbit" feels just as informative as (4), even though I'm convinced there is no such planet and both terms are empty. The Le Verrier hypothesis is doing the same cognitive work for me as the true Neptune case.
6. But "the planet whose gravitational pull explains the anomaly in Mercury's orbit is the planet whose gravitational pull explains the anomaly in Mercury's orbit" isn't informative to me; it also doesn't feel comfortably trivial the way "Hesperus is Hesperus" does. It feels gappy, almost as if the sentence is trying and failing to pick something out.

So the pair of (4) and (5) is what I most want explained by one principle: two sentences of the same shape, one with referents and one with empty terms, that I nevertheless treat as equally informative. And (6) makes me doubt the simple version of Frege where same sense equals trivial and different sense equals informative.
