I'm a PhD student in decision theory, and I've been drafting a short essay on the Allais paradox for a seminar on the foundations of expected utility theory. I'd appreciate your comments on the notes below; I'm less interested in whether you agree than in where the argument is weak, where it merely relabels a known problem, and where it might actually advance things.

Here is the idea. In the usual common-consequence version of the paradox, the first pair presents a choice between a sure $1 million and a gamble offering a 0.10 chance of $5 million, a 0.89 chance of $1 million, and a 0.01 chance of nothing; the second pair presents a choice between a 0.11 chance of $1 million (with a 0.89 chance of nothing) and a 0.10 chance of $5 million (with a 0.90 chance of nothing). The modal pattern is to prefer the sure million in the first pair and the $5 million gamble in the second, which violates independence because the second pair becomes the first pair when the shared 0.89 chance of nothing is replaced by a shared 0.89 chance of $1 million in both options. I want to argue that this common-consequence operation does two things at once: it changes the common outcome, and it changes whether one of the options is certain. The modal reversal might therefore be driven by a certainty effect—an overweighting of probability-one outcomes—rather than by a general failure of independence across risky prospects. I then want to preserve a version of independence for the purely probabilistic domain and add a separate certainty premium parameter, so the model still predicts the Allais pattern but no longer claims that people trade off overlapping probability mass inconsistently.

I'm most unsure about the following: whether the certainty-premium model makes predictions that differ from simply denying independence in the risky domain; whether keeping independence everywhere except certainty earns its keep or just relocates the mystery; and whether a defender of the axiom should instead explain the pattern as an artifact of how the problems are framed rather than as a genuine preference reversal. Please give me exactly five comments on these notes, each one or two sentences, formatted as a numbered list with no introduction or conclusion. At least two of the comments should address the strongest objections to my argument.
