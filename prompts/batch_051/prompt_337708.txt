Write a final-exam question for an upper-level undergraduate course in metaphysics and the philosophy of mathematics, on supertasks and Thomson's lamp. The exam is ninety minutes long, the question should be answerable in roughly 600–800 words, and it is worth 25 marks.

The question must be one that a student who has only memorized the standard Thomson's lamp discussion will answer poorly. Build the question around a modified or comparative case that the student cannot have seen verbatim—for instance, a lamp with more than two states, or a supertask whose successive states have a well-defined limit set beside Thomson's lamp—and make the case force students to work through these distinctions:

(i) a sequence of states lacking a mathematical limit versus a physical state left unspecified when a supertask is completed;

(ii) an instruction that leaves an outcome unspecified versus an instruction that makes the outcome impossible or inconsistent; and

(iii) what Thomson's lamp does and does not show about whether supertasks are possible in general.

Your deliverable has three parts. First, present the exam question itself, including all setup or data the student needs within the question and clear instructions about what the student must do. Second, give a model answer showing the reasoning a strong upper-level student should produce, including how the modified case should be analyzed and how it compares with the original lamp. Third, give a marking scheme that distributes all 25 marks across the specific moves a top answer must make, and that names the specific mistakes that should cost marks—for example, conflating "the limit does not exist" with "a contradiction follows," treating Thomson's lamp as a standalone refutation of all supertasks, or treating the final state as a physical magnitude that the laws of nature must determine rather than something the set-up may simply fail to specify. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
