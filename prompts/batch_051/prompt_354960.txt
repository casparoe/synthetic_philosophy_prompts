Recently I returned to serious piano study after twenty years away, and the daily difficulty of it has forced me to confront what I actually think about the value of effort. I have arrived at the following five commitments, and I would like to know whether they hang together. (1) Effort can be valuable for reasons that are not purely instrumental: a skill or result achieved through sustained struggle is more admirable, and in some respect better, than the identical skill obtained without effort, even when the two agents end up indistinguishable. (2) At the same time, I do not think hardship that is unchosen — poverty, illness, drudgery — is valuable, and I think we have strong reasons to eliminate or reduce it. (3) I suspect that what is valuable in striving is what it does to the agent: it builds discipline, self-knowledge, and the ability to fail and recover. (4) I also hold that how much effort went into producing an outcome adds nothing to the outcome's value for the person who experiences it; a benefit is no better for having been costly to obtain. (5) Finally, I believe that institutions and tools should be designed to make demanding activities easier and more widely accessible, rather than to preserve struggle for its own sake. Are these five views jointly tenable? I can feel that some of them pull against one another, but I cannot tell which conflicts are genuine, which dissolve once the terms are clarified, and what I would have to give up — and at what cost — to make the rest coherent. I would prefer an audit of my commitments, ordered by the severity of the tensions, over a general lecture on effort.
