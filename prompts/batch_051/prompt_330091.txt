I am preparing a session for a graduate course on the history of biblical interpretation, organized around the shift from Origen’s allegorical reading to twentieth-century fundamentalism’s insistence on the plain sense. I want to put that opposition to work on a single test case, and I would like you to reason through it with me.

The text is Exodus 23:19 in the NRSVUE: “You shall not boil a kid in its mother’s milk.”

The case is this. A small food company produces a stew it calls “guiltless kid.” The meat component is grown in vitro from cells taken from a living kid goat; the cooking liquid contains milk from that same kid’s mother. No animal is killed or butchered, and no whole goat enters the pot. The dish is still, quite literally, the cells of a kid boiled in its own mother’s milk, and the producers advertise it as a way of eating goat-in-mother’s-milk without the death that the rule seems to recoil from.

How does the verse bear on this dish under the approaches the course is contrasting? Walk through these: the plain words, read as a modern fundamentalist reads them; the purpose or point of the rule, whether we reconstruct that purpose as humanitarian, symbolic, or a boundary against a foreign cultic practice; a conjecture about what the text’s authors or community would have said if asked about cultured meat; what a reasonable reader of the sentence today would take it to mean; and an Origen-style allegorical reading that suspects the letter of a food law points beyond itself. Then say where these readings genuinely diverge and where they converge. If they diverge, which should govern practice, and does that choice depend on where one thinks the text’s authority lies — in the words, in the moral point of the command, or in the spiritual sense the words may figure?

I am not principally after a verdict; I want to know whether this case separates the methods or whether the methods end up agreeing. If the case is badly set up, or if it trades on a caricature of Origen or of fundamentalism, say so plainly and correct the setup before answering — I would rather have the exercise criticized than have it force a result.
