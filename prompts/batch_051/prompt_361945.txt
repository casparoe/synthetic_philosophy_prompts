I'm a software engineer on a platform team at a mid-sized software company. My team maintains the internal developer platform: the monorepo, CI checks, lint rules, code-review policies, runbooks, and a few customer-facing service commitments. Over time we have accumulated a large set of dormant rules: rules that are still written down somewhere, often still present in configuration files and policy documents, but that have not actually been enforced for months or years. We expect to run a cleanup over roughly a hundred of these rules in a two-week window, and then reuse the same procedure quarterly, so the deliverable needs to be a repeatable procedure, not a one-off judgment.

Management has given us a constraint: for each dormant rule we must either re-enforce it and wire it back into our automated checks, or formally deprecate and delete it. Leaving a rule half-enforced is the one outcome we are not allowed to keep.

I recently came across the legal doctrine of desuetude: roughly, a law can lose its binding force through long non-enforcement together with settled practices that contradict it. The standard rule-of-law objection is that if disuse can quietly repeal a rule, people cannot know in advance which rules actually bind them, and enforcement becomes arbitrary. I have no formal background in law or philosophy, so I need this tension translated into something an engineer can apply.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure for classifying any dormant rule in our system as one of three outcomes: re-enforce, formally deprecate, or escalate to a human owner because the decision is really a values judgment. The procedure should be runnable in about thirty minutes per rule by an engineer without legal training, and each decision should be recorded in a way that is defensible to an engineering manager and, for security- or customer-facing rules, to our compliance or legal team.

The procedure should include: the questions to ask in order; what counts as sufficient evidence at each step, including how long counts as long, how visible the non-enforcement was, whether people noticed and relied on it, and what the written record shows; where the procedure stops with each of the three outcomes; what to do when the evidence is missing or ambiguous; and the places where the procedure must hand the decision to a person because no checklist can settle it. It should also make explicit the difference between a rule that has merely been ignored and a rule whose non-enforcement has created a reasonable expectation that it will not be enforced, and say why that difference matters and where the procedure is likely to misfire.

Then apply the procedure to three dormant rules we actually have:

1. A lint rule requiring every database migration to include an up/down migration pair. It has been disabled in CI for four years and ignored ever since, and most recent migrations lack the rollback side, but the rule text still calls itself mandatory.
2. A security policy requiring the security team to review every new dependency before it is added. It has gone unenforced for about 18 months, the audit system is now flagging hundreds of unreviewed dependencies, and management is being asked to clean up the backlog.
3. A customer-facing API stability commitment stating that error response shapes will not change without a major version bump. In practice we have changed those shapes repeatedly for two years, and clients have built tooling around the current behavior, so restoring the written commitment would break them.

Make sure at least one of the worked examples arrives at an outcome the team would rather not accept, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot decide on its own.
