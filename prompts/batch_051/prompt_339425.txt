I am an undergraduate in a course on animal minds and moral status, and I also serve as the student member of my university's animal care and use committee. The committee meets every two weeks, reviews one or two new protocols per meeting, and each member is expected to arrive with a written preliminary judgment formed in no more than about half an hour of unsupervised review. The chair, a veterinarian, and the community representative both sign off on the final justification for any differential treatment of animals. I have been asked to draft a reusable screening procedure for a question that keeps dividing us: when a protocol justifies different treatment of research animals—housing, enrichment, analgesia, permissible invasiveness, endpoints—by appeal to brain size or a brain-size-derived measure such as encephalization quotient or total cortical neuron number, when should that justification be accepted, and when should it be rejected or sent back for further evidence?

I know enough of the philosophy to be sure that the two flat answers are wrong. Brain size cannot be automatically relevant, since much of brain mass tracks body size and serves allostatic and sensorimotor regulation rather than whatever capacities matter morally. But it cannot be automatically irrelevant either, because neuron number, neuron density, and forebrain organization are among the limited proxies we have for what an animal can feel, anticipate, and want. What I need is the intermediate structure: a procedure that forces the committee to say whether brain size is being invoked as morally relevant in itself, as a proxy for a specific morally relevant capacity, or as a bias disguised as a fact.

Please write the procedure as:

1. An ordered checklist or decision tree, with explicit test questions at each step, that classifies each brain-based justification as cleared, rejected, or returned for further evidence.

2. A short version that a non-philosopher member could use, keeping the distinctions that must survive but no more.

3. Three worked calibration cases: (a) a case in which brain size is used to deny protections where nervous-system complexity suggests a false negative—I have in mind an invertebrate such as an octopus; (b) a case in which a large absolute brain is used to demand protections that the animal's demonstrated capacities do not clearly support; and (c) a case in which a brain-based distinction does track a genuine morally relevant difference. Show in each example what the brain measure is doing and why.

4. At least one worked case where the procedure gives an answer the committee will likely dislike—for instance, requiring more protection for a small-brained animal than we currently grant, or blocking a distinction we find comfortable.

5. A closing statement of what the procedure cannot settle: where it must defer to comparative cognition and welfare science, and where a residual ethical judgment remains that no test can replace.

This is to be used in a real committee under time pressure, so clarity matters more than theoretical coverage; flag honestly where simplification risks misfiring.
