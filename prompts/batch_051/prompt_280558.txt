I'm the systems designer on a mid-size RPG and I've been put in charge of the karma/reputation system. The creative director is adamant that karma should not just be a reputation score with NPCs; she wants it to track what characters morally deserve—proper karmic desert, with actions and consequences attached—and she keeps citing actual karma-as-desert as the model. The problem is that every time I sit down to spec this I hit a philosophical wall. Do intentions count, or just outcomes? If someone tries to poison a well and fails, did they accrue bad karma? Does a good act done for selfish reasons earn less? Does undoing the harm later (returning what you stole, compensating the victim) cancel the karmic debt, or is the earlier bad karma already occured and fixed? Should there be a ceiling on karmic debt, or can one act make a character permanently unrecoverable? Can karma be transferred—can one person's good deed cover someone else's bad one, or a character take on someone else's karmic debt? Does karma decay over time if nothing further happens? The system has to be implementable in code: every distinction has to be a rule with a number, a flag, or a dice roll, and the quest designers need to be able to run it without me. I've read enough philosophy and rationality blogs over the years to know that the honest answer half the time is it depends, but a shipped game can't say it depends.

Please write a reusable decision procedure—a flowchart in words—that my team can actually run for any in-game act to assign karmic consequences, grounded in the most defensible version of karma-as-moral-desert you can give, simplified for implementation. It should say what questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with no karmic charge, and where it hands the decision to a human writer because it is a value judgement rather than a fact. Flag which distinctions must survive simplification and which can be safely dropped. Then run the procedure on three acts: (1) a character steals scarce medicine to save their dying sibling, but the medicine was already promised to a dying stranger who needed it just as much; (2) a character sincerely tries to poison someone's water, fails entirely, and no one ever finds out; (3) a character kills a group of people while genuinely believing, on misleading but not absurd evidence, that those people were about to blow up a city. I especially want to see a case where the procedure gives an answer the narrative lead will hate, and what the procedure still can't decide. Please write the answer in British English.
