I've been arguing with a friend who does linguistics about whether "preposition" (or "case", or "part of speech" more generally) names a real grouping out there in the world's languages, or is just a convenient label grammarians inherited because Latin and Greek happened to have certain slots to fill. He claims prepositions are a natural class. I keep thinking of English "of", which behaves more like a genitive marker than like "under" or "behind", and of languages where the same relational work is done by case endings, postpositions, or verbs, and it seams to me the category is just family resemblance plus a bit of grammatical tradition. We'd like to get past trading examples.

Treat the claim "prepositions (or some other grammatical category, if you'd rather reframe it) form a natural kind" as something that could be tested, at least partly. Design the best test you can: what data you would gather and why (a sample of unrelated languages? distributional behaviour in corpora? something else), what pattern of results would count strongly for the natural-kind view, and what pattern would count against it. Be honest about the residue: if the test comes out in favour, is that showing more than "there's a statistically reliable cluster here", and does the phrase "natural kind" earn its keep or just relabel the cluster? And if you think the question is badly posed from the start because "natural kind" is the wrong frame for grammatical categories, say so plainly and tell me what you'd ask instead.
