I'm a novelist, a few chapters into a book set in the French Revolution, and I've hit the problem that every historian I read seems to be writing about a different event. I don't want a reading list. I want a character. Take Maximilien Robespierre as my protagonist's old friend and occasional antagonist, and tell me what he'd make of the way later generations have explained him.

There are three readings of the Revolution that you still run into. One: the whole thing was class struggle wearing a costume — the bourgeoisie rode the sans-culottes into power, smashed feudalism, then cut them loose once the street started asking for what it'd been half-promised. Two: the Revolution was an ideology doing what ideologies do — the Terror wasn't mainly a reaction to invasion and civil war, it was the logic of the ideas themselves, general will, virtue, the nation that has to be regenerated and then purged of whatever refuses to be. Three: the real motor was contingency — war panic, the king's flight, faction fights, bad harvests, the personalities of a handful of men; the ideas and the classes were scenery, and accidents drove the sequence.

And there's the line people always reach for, about revolutions devouring their children, which I've never seen attached to a source I trust. Would Robespierre think that line is fair to Danton, to Camille Desmoulins, to the Hébertists, and to himself in the end? Or would he think it's girondin sentimentality, which mistakes the whole character of what he thought he was doing?

I want a prediction, not a hedge: which of the three readings (or what mixture, if you really can't pick one) would he recognize himself in, and which would he take as a libel; and how would he take the devouring-their-children idea. Then do the self-critical bit for me, because I need to know how much to trust this when I write the scene. Tell me where your prediction is shakiest and what argument, passage, or event in the record would most likely change your mind.
