A friend and I were arguing about a passage in Wittgenstein’s *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*. The passage — at 4.1212 in the standard numbering, if you want to check — runs as follows:

> What can be shown, cannot be said.

My friend, who is basically a hard-nosed programmer like me, thinks this is either false or empty. His view is that if a fact cannot be expressed in a proposition, then we have no way of grasping it well enough to call it knowledge; if we can grasp it, then we can in principle state it, even if we have to extend our language to do so. I pushed back along the lines of the later Wittgenstein: there are things we master in practice without being able to articulate them — like what it is to follow a rule, or how a word projects onto new cases. He replied that this just means I have not yet found the right representation; it does not show that some content is in principle unsayable. He then argued that the whole idea of unsayable truths is a mystification: it sounds deep, but it collapses into either (a) something that is perfectly expressible, or (b) something about which there is nothing to be said at all. I answered that (b) is exactly the point — that some things are shown in the structure of what we do or in the structure of language, and that trying to state them produces nonsense of a special kind, not ordinary falsehood.

I want to know which of us is closer to the text, and which of us is closer to being right. What does *shown, not said* actually mean in the context of the *Tractatus*? Is my friend right that it is either expressible or empty, or is there a coherent third option? Be plain if one of us — or both of us — is misunderstanding the passage. Adjudicate the exchange point by point, and when the dust settles, say which of us is more right overall.

If the question rests on a confused premise, or if you think our argument is too loose to be settled as stated, say so directly instead of politely playing along.
