I am a novelist, and I have been asked to write down my settled views about fiction and the emotions so that a small seminar can test whether they hang together. I would be grateful for an audit before I present it. I can see that some of these beliefs look as though they may be in tension, but I cannot tell which tensions are real, which are merely apparent, or which belief is doing the damage; I hold them all with roughly equal conviction. The list:

1. The pity or grief I feel for a character I am writing, or for a character in a novel I am reading, is a genuine emotion of the ordinary kind. It is not a weaker substitute for pity or grief, not an imagined emotion, and not a state of make-believe.

2. At no point do I believe the character exists or that the depicted events really happened. I never lose my grip on the fact that the scene is invented, and the feeling does not require any suspension of disbelief.

3. The feeling is directed towards the character and towards what befalls her in the story, not towards a proposition, a description, or a mental image of my own.

4. An emotion is rationally fitting only if its object really has the relevant features: pity is fitting only in the face of actual undeserved suffering, and fear only in the face of actual danger.

5. All the same, I think my pity and fear about fictional scenes are, in central cases, rationally fitting. The novel earns them, and a reader who felt nothing would be failing to see something that is there on the page.

6. I also recognise the obvious metaphysical point: no mind-independent particular in one of my novels ever suffered. The suffering is internal to the representation and is not a property of some real thing.

7. My fictional pity and fear bring with them almost no readiness to act. I have no impulse to warn the character or to intervene, and I would regard a strong impulse to intervene as a misfire rather than as the natural expression of the emotion.

8. Yet I also hold that what characterises a state as the emotion it is, pity rather than aesthetic admiration or sentimental self-indulgence, is partly its characteristic pattern of appraisal and action-readiness. Once those are subtracted, I do not know what would be left to constitute the emotion.

9. As a craft matter, I can reliably summon these fictional emotions when I need them. I decide that a scene should be pitiful, settle into the point of view, and the pity arrives; it is not performed, and it does not feel forced.

10. But I also believe that emotions in general are not under direct voluntary control. I cannot simply decide to pity an actual person or to fear an oncoming lorry, however much the situation might call for it.

Please work through the list as a package. Tell me which pairs or triples are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. Order the real tensions by severity, say what would have to give in each case and what the cheapest repair would be, and then identify the single revision that would restore the most coherence overall, explaining what that revision costs. I would rather you press on the internal consistency of the list than lecture me on the options; please do not include citations or recommendations for further reading. Please write your answer in American English.
