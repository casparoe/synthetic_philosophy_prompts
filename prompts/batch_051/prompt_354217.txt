I've been arguing with a friend about where to give money, and I keep coming back to a proverb neither of us seems able to pin down. I currently make a small monthly donation to a food bank and to an organization that distributes mosquito nets, mostly because the need there is concrete and I can see what the money buys. My friend says this is a mistake: he insists I'm treating symptoms while leaving causes untouched, and he kept returning to the saying that if you give a man a fish you feed him for a day, but if you teach a man to fish you feed him for a lifetime. His conclusion is that I should redirect my giving toward education, job training, or advocacy that changes the rules, because direct material aid is just a temporary patch and systemic change is the only thing that lasts.

I'm not convinced the proverb actually gets him to that conclusion, but I can't articulate why. I'd like you to lay out the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be making: about the difference between relieving a need and building a capacity, about whether feeding and teaching are even alternatives, about who is actually in a position to be taught, about how long "a lifetime" is and who gets to count it, about whether the fisherman's problem is individual or structural in the first place. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive claim that needs defending, or simply false as a generalization. Then tell me which of those readings would actually license his conclusion that I should prefer systemic or capacity-building interventions over direct material aid, and which readings would not license it even though people tend to cite the proverb for exactly that purpose. I'm less interested in a single correct interpretation than in a menu of readings with verdicts, so I can see precisely where our disagreement sits.
