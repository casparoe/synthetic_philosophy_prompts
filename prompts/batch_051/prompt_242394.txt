You are a historian of religions with a side interest in monetary theory. I want you to help me decide whether a parallel I have noticed is real or merely verbal.

In R. B. Anderson’s introduction to The Younger Edda (1880), he writes: “With a view of giving a pretty complete outline of the founder of the Teutonic race we have in our notes given all the Heimskringla sketch of the Black Sea Odin.” That “Black Sea Odin” is the euhemerist Odin of Snorri’s Heimskringla and the Prologue to the Prose Edda: a human chieftain from the east, not a god by birth, whom later generations come to worship as a god. I keep wanting to say fiat money has the same shape. A currency unit starts as a mark of a promise, a token, or a ledger entry; people accept it, and that acceptance congeals into “being money,” so that a social relation is reified into an attribute of the object. Euhemerism also looks like the reification of a social relation: veneration turns a ruler into a god, and the cult then takes the divine status to have been there all along.

Before you give a final judgement, please canvass the mapping carefully: what exactly in the euhemerist account corresponds to what in monetary theory, and which features of each side are doing the real work? Then say whether this is a deep structural analogy, a loose metaphor, or a confusion, and where it breaks. Begin by brainstorming two respects in which the parallel holds and two respects in which it may mislead. Please answer in British English.
