Consider two colleagues who read the same ambiguous message from a supervisor. One becomes anxious and concludes that the supervisor is unhappy with her work; the other becomes hopeful and concludes that the supervisor is pleased. In ordinary life, the first is treated as having detected signals the second missed, while the second is treated as seeing what she wants to see. Both formed their conclusions from identical evidence, and both states are emotions: one negative, one positive. Is there a principle that justifies crediting unpleasant emotions with more access to reality than pleasant ones, or is this only a psychological habit of distrusting good feelings? The strongest answer will need to say something about what emotions are—judgments about value, perceptions of value, or bodily feelings that carry no evaluative content on their own—because each theory predicts different conditions under which an emotion tracks how things stand. The principle should then be tested against cases in which negative emotions plainly fail as instruments of detection: paranoid fear without an object, grief-stricken certainty that nothing will ever be well again, and anger that manufactures an offense. Say, in the end, whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
