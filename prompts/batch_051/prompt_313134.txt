I am a novelist drafting a character—a former logic instructor who now writes amateur philosophy—and I need to make sure the philosophy I have given him is coherent enough to survive a dinner-table argument. I have been rereading W. V. O. Quine’s “Two Dogmas of Empiricism,” and one passage has become the hinge for the character’s view. Quine writes: “Meaning is what essence becomes when it is divorced from the object of reference and wedded to the word.” I take that to be a rejection of meanings as hidden mental furniture. The character will defend the following set of commitments, presented as his mature view: (1) Quine is right that no non-circular criterion has been produced for drawing the analytic–synthetic boundary, so “true in virtue of meaning alone” cannot do foundational epistemological work. (2) Ordinary synonymy is still real enough: that “bachelor” means the same as “unmarried man” is a stable fact about English, not merely a philosopher’s convenience. (3) The sentence is the primary unit of semantic evaluation, but word-level synonymy remains a useful shorthand in ordinary speech. (4) Rejecting the boundary does not force me to say that every statement is just as revisable as any other, or that all disagreement is verbal. (5) The difference between a substantive scientific dispute and a verbal dispute is always a matter of degree and practical purpose, never a difference in kind. Before I write the scenes in which he defends this, I want a consistency check: which of these commitments are actually in tension with one another, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and explain for each where the pressure comes from. Include the objection you think would make the character squirm the most.
