I'm doing a PhD in cognitive neuroscience, and my lab has just adopted a new written protocol for our visual awareness studies that's been bugging me. We run binocular rivalry experiments: people look at a display where each eye gets a different image, there perception flips between the two every few seconds, and they press a key to say what they're seeing at each moment. The new protocol says we're supposed to treat these keypresses and the occasional verbal reports strictly as data about what the subject believes or claims they saw—never as direct observations of their conscious experience itself. Fine, I thought. But then we use exactly those reports as ground-truth labels to train our neural decoder, and the same lab's papers and grant proposals talk about measuring the contents of visual awareness or tracking when the face enters consciousness. So in the methods we treat reports as merely beliefs about experience, but everywhere else we treat them as solid evidence about what was actually experienced.

I honestly can't tell whether the protocol is philosophically careful or just confused. Is this practice coherent? What view of consciousness and of first-person reports does it presuppose, and does it secretly commit us to some position about whether qualia are real? Could a person who thinks there are real, private qualitative feels and a person who thinks those feels are an illusion both sign off on this protocol? And what would have to change if we instead treated subjects' reports as direct readouts of their experience? My advisor calls it methodological neutrality and tells me to stop worrying, but that phrase is doing a lot of work.
