You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of the theory of expertise and the epistemology of reasoning; your particular skill is auditing a person's stated commitments for hidden inconsistencies. I am a novelist, not a philosopher by training, and over twenty years I have developed a private regime for training my own judgement, adapted from martial-arts pedagogy. Before I publish an essay describing it, I want my commitments stress-tested. Please think carefully: begin by unpacking each commitment and tracing how it interacts with the others, and separate merely apparent tensions from substantive ones before ranking the latter by severity.

The commitments are these.

1. Rationality is a suite of trainable skills — chiefly noticing one's own motivated reasoning, forming calibrated probabilities, and actively seeking disconfirmation — rather than a fixed trait or a single intellectual virtue.
2. These skills are best acquired through deliberately abstracted drills, or katas: contrived exercises done apart from any real decision, such as forced expected-value calculations for trivial choices or applying a standing checklist to one's own most cherished convictions.
3. The entire value of the drills lies in transfer: someone flawless inside the kata but no better in ordinary life has gained nothing rationally worth having.
4. Yet real-world rationality is, I believe, largely domain-specific: the fund manager who is rigorously even-handed about money can be hopelessly self-deceived about her marriage, and no single competence binds these together.
5. Mastery should terminate in automaticity: the genuinely trained mind no longer runs the checklists, but acts as unselfconsciously as a fighter who has stopped thinking through each combination.
6. Even so, the katas are never finally outgrown: when I let the practice lapse, the old errors return, and I must go back to the drills as a musician returns to scales.

Do these six commitments form a coherent package? Show me which pairs are in genuine tension and how severe each tension is; which pairs only appear to conflict, and why the appearance is misleading. For each genuine tension, identify the least costly revision and what it would sacrifice. Then tell me which single revision would restore the most coherence at the lowest cost, without dissolving real tensions by relabelling them.
