I'm a philosophy professor, and I've started to notice that the phrase "the sciences carve nature at its joints" has become a bit of a conversation-stopper around my department—used to settle arguments it probably shouldn't be settling. Last week in our philosophy of science seminar, a colleague dismissed psychiatric categories like depression by saying they don't carve nature at its joints, unlike the periodic table, so they're less real. A grad student then pushed this further and said physics is in the joint-carving business while sociology is not, as if that by itself showed social kinds are second-class. A third person used the same slogan to argue there must be exactly one correct taxonomy for any domain, and science is just trying to find it.

I know the phrase has some ancient pedigree and has been picked up in debates about natural kinds, but I suspect its actually bundling together very different claims—about whether the world has a built-in structure, whether there's a unique best classification scheme, whether some sciences are more "fundamental" than others, whether good kinds are the ones that support induction and explanation, and whether all of this is mind-independent. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing, from the fairly trivial to the quite contentious, and give a verdict on each? And importantly: which of those readings would actually license the moves my colleague and grad student made, and which wouldn't? Please also say how confident you are in the assessments and single out the point you are least sure about.
