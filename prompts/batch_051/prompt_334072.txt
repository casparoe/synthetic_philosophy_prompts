A city proposes a "liberty audit" scheme: every year, each resident gets a card with three scores—one for negative liberty (how many rules restrict them), one for positive liberty (how much they can actually act on their endorsed goals), and one for republican liberty (how much of their life depends on someone else's discretion). Residents can spend one "reclassification" per year: they can petition the audit board to move a specific burden from one category to another, say a licensing requirement currently scored only as a negative-liberty restriction gets reargued as domination, or a noise ordinance gets reargued as a positive-liberty cost because it kills a local music scene. The two categories that gain the burden count get priority in the next year's deregulation agenda; the category that loses it drops. Walk through how this would actually play out. Who gets good at "liberty laundering," and along what fault lines? Where does the scheme force the board into hard conceptual decisions—for instance, is dependence on an algorithm arbitrary power, or is a restriction that expands one person's positive liberty still a negative-liberty cost to someone else? What second-order effects on how residents talk about freedom should we expect, and can the scheme be fixed without abandoning the three-way distinction?
