so i'm on the board of a small housing co-op and over the past year i've been the one stuck writing up how we make decisions, and i've ended up with a set of views i'd like checked for whether they actually hang together. We run things by splitting a big decision into the little yes/no questions it depends on, take a majority on each one seperately, and whatever the majorities say becomes our official line. Here's roughly where i've landed:

1. That method is basically right. Majority on each piece, take those answers as our position, done. I don't see why we'd need fancier machinery than a vote per question.

2. But if a majority says "we can afford it" and also says "it will actually get used," and then the official decision is "don't do it," that's a contradiction, and a group that puts out a contradiction like that has made a bad decision, not just a filing error. Our official reasons and our official call have to fit together.

3. I'd push back hard against making everyone's personal ballot public, or nudging people to vote in a way that's internally consistent. That feels like thought-policing. It's the group's coherence that matters, not any one person's. A friend keeps telling me this conflicts with 2, but I don't see the conflict.

4. It also shouldn't matter how we chop up the ballot. Whether we ask "should we sign the lease?" as one up-or-down question, or split it into "can we afford it?" and "will it get used?" and then also vote on the main question, we should end up in the same place. If chopping the question differently changes where we land, that's ballot tricks, not a real change of mind.

5. Last one: as long as every individual member's own ballot is internally consistent — each person votes yes on the main thing when their own sub-answers point that way — then majority rule on each piece can't produce an inconsistent official position. A group-level inconsistency would have to come from somebody's messy individual ballot, and I don't think it's fair to blame the method for that.

So before I write this up for the rest of the board: do these five hang together? Go through the pairs and tell me which ones are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and rank the real tensions by how bad they are. Then tell me the cheapest fix — which commitment has to go — and what I'd lose by dropping it. And tell me what toy example or argument would make you change your mind about that fix, or where your own reasoning is most likely to break.
