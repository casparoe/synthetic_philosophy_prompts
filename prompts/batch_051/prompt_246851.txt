I've been trying to get a handle on George Eliot's actual positive view, not the standard notion that she was just an atheist who liked the aesthetics of religion. She translated Feuerbach, clearly rejects Christianity's factual claims, but also seems convinced that Christianity's moral and emotional core has to be kept or transposed into something like a religion of humanity built on sympathy. The part that keeps tripping me is this: if her view of persons is as deterministic and socially saturated as it looks—everyone is the product of circumstances, inheritance, and the people around them—then what gives the ought of sympathy any bite? Why isn't sympathy just a disposition some people have and others don't, with no more normative force than eye color? And if it is just a caused trait, why does she write as if a failure to extend sympathetic understanding to someone like Bulstrode is a moral failure rather than simply another caused outcome? Can you give me a gears-level reconstruction of what, if anything, remains of her religion-of-humanity ethics once you refuse to smuggle Christian moral realism back in? I don't want the standard humanist gloss; I want to see exactly what bears weight and what doesn't.
