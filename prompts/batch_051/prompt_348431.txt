I am writing a short statement of my position on how evidence relates to scientific theory for my high school's philosophy seminar, and I would like it checked for consistency before I present it. I currently hold the following five commitments:

(1) Any finite body of observations can be accommodated by indefinitely many mutually incompatible theories about unobservable things, all of which fit the observations equally well.
(2) Theory choice in science should not be limited to fit with the data; explanatory power, simplicity, and unification are legitimate reasons for believing one theory over another.
(3) Simplicity and unification themselves are not guides to truth. They are pragmatic virtues — they make theories easier to grasp, use, and build on — and a simpler theory is not, simply in virtue of being simpler, more likely to be correct.
(4) If two theories have exactly the same observable consequences, then the evidence cannot justify believing one over the other, and a rational person should suspend judgment between them.
(5) I am also a scientific realist in the following sense: our best current theories in fundamental physics are at least approximately true in what they say about unobservables such as electrons and fields.

Please treat this as an audit of my position. I would like to know which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, which pairs only appear to be in tension and dissolve under closer examination, and how severe each genuine tension is. For each genuine tension, tell me what would have to be revised and at what cost. Finally, tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set hang together.
