My roommate and I went around and around last night on whether a person's data belongs to them, and I honestly ended up more confused than before. Then at dinner two friends started the same argument and got further than we did. I mostly just listened and tried to keep score, but I couldn't, so I need someone to give a point-by-point verdict on how it went.

Sofia opened with what she called the obvious view: your data—the places you go, the things you buy, the people you message—is produced by your own life, so in some deep sense it's yours. A company vacuuming up your location history is collecting something you made, and doing it without your say-so is a taking.

Marcus said no. He said facts are not the kind of thing a person can own. Nobody owns the fact that it rained or that bread costs three dollars. If you walk into a shop and buy bread, the fact that you bought bread ends up in the shopkeeper's ledger and his memory too; it isn't exclusively in your head anymore. Raw information about you is non-rival and non-excludable, so treating it as property is confused.

Sofia shot back that property doesn't reduce to physical scarcity. Intellectual property is property in non-rival things, and we give it to people so they control what their labor produces. Your behavioral data is the fruit of your life, so she said it belongs to you.

Marcus said intellectual property is a legal creation with a limited purpose—to encourage creation—and it applies only to specific works and inventions, not to raw facts about the world. And anyway, he said, your "activity" is usually an interaction: when you bought the bread, the shop generated the transaction record just as much as you did, so Sofia's argument would give the shop an equal claim to the same data.

Sofia replied that what does the work is that the data is about a person. It's bound up with privacy, and privacy is a protected interest in your own life. Marcus said that's exactly his point—privary isn't property. Privacy is a right against certain uses, not ownership of the information. Sofia said maybe that's the same thing once you remember property is just a bundle of rights. Marcus said it's not the same, because property can be sold. If she's prepared to say a person should be free to sell every datapoint about themselves to a broker, then "data is property" means something; if she's not, then she was speaking in metaphor.

Sofia said there are inalienable property rights too—you own your body and your organs even in places where selling them is illegal—so alienability can't be the test. Marcus said that by the time "property" gets stretched to cover a non-transferable moral claim over facts, it just means "a strong interest," which is the question they were arguing about, not an answer.

I care less about the policy bottom line then about the argument itself. Can you go through and say who was right at each step—on the scarcity point, on the intellectual property analogy, on the interaction objection, on the conversion to privacy, and on the bundle-of-rights and inalienability moves? And at the end, tell me who had the better overall position, and whether either of them ever actually addressed whether info about you belongs to you.
