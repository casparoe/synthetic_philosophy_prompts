A colleague in the law school and I have been going back and forth about a naming regime, and we're long past arguing about whether it's a good idea; I want someone to actually run the mechanism and tell us how it would behave. Suppose a small country simply abolishes family names. At birth, each child gets exactly one legal name, chosen by the parents from a national registry of names not currently in use, and no two living citizens may carry the same name. The name is yours until you turn 18, at which point you may change it once, for free, to any unused name; your old name goes back into the pool, and any further changes cost a steep fee. When someone dies, their name rejoins the pool after a fixed delay, say ten years. The state maintains a public lineage registry so parentage can still be looked up, but parentage appears nowhere in anyone's name, and marriage confers no naming rights. For legal purposes the name alone is supposed to be sufficient, since its unique.

I teach philosophy of language, so I can see this touches the given-name/family-name distinction, reference, social kinds, all that. But don't give me the abstract version; I want the in-the-weeds walkthrough. What does the scheme incentivize among parents, eighteen-year-olds, employers, bureaucrats, scammers? Where does it get gamed first—people sitting on desirable names, all the normal names being exhausted, informal surnames reappearing under the table, rich parents somehow buying up names through middlemen? Does a de facto family name re-emerge even though the law refuses to recognize one, and if so, through what channels? What happens to the way names refer and sort people when every citizen is, semantically, a one-off tag rather than someone who inherits a category? And can the failure modes be fixed without reintroducing family names in all but name or building a naming bureaucracy so heavy it defeats the point?

I'm not after a yes or a no on the proposal; I want you to actually game it out and tell me where it buckles first.
