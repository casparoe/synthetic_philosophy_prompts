I am a software engineer who works mostly on distributed systems, so I spend a lot of time thinking about high availability: hot standbys, automatic failover, leader election, and the principle that what matters is that some healthy replica is serving requests, not which replica it is. Lately I have been reading about continuity-of-government arrangements in the United States, and one practice keeps nagging at me because it seems to apply exactly this engineering logic to political authority, and I cannot tell whether that is coherent.

The practice is as follows. During the annual State of the Union address, nearly everyone in the constitutional line of presidential succession is assembled in one room: the Vice President, the Speaker of the House, the President pro tempore of the Senate, and most of the Cabinet. To protect against an attack that killed everyone present at once, one Cabinet secretary who is constitutionally eligible for the presidency is selected in advance to stay away, kept under protection at an undisclosed secure location for the duration of the address, and not publicly identified beforehand; if the assembled leadership were killed, this designated survivor would assume the presidency. The public generally does not know in advance who this person is, and the office itself—a Cabinet secretary—is an appointed position rather than an elected one, though the appointment is confirmed by the Senate. The broader continuity-of-government framework seems to extend the same idea: maintain a recognized chain of authority, largely out of public view, so that there is never a gap at the top.

From an engineering standpoint this is just a failover policy: continuity of service is the overriding value, and the identity of the process that takes over is incidental. But a government is not merely a service; it claims the right to make laws, levy taxes, and direct the use of force, and in ordinary times we understand those claims to rest on elections and the ongoing consent of the governed. I would like a careful reconstruction of the philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. Specifically, what view of legitimacy, or of what a state is, does the designated-survivor arrangement commit a government to? Does it strictly presuppose that authority resides in an institution or chain of offices that can hand power along independently of any current popular authorization, or is it compatible with a consent-based view once one remembers that the line of succession is itself a product of democratically enacted law? Does the practice split authority into two components—the entitlement to govern and the capacity to govern without interruption—and, if so, what does that split presuppose? And what would have to change in the practice if we rejected its implicit view and held instead that no one may exercise executive power without a direct and current public mandate?
