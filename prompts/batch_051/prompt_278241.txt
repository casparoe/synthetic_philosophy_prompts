ok here's the situation. I'm giving a paper at a political-theory workshop next month, and I already know the Q&A is where it'll get rough. My thesis is that the standard way people teach Machiavelli on fortune is wrong, or at least sloppy. The usual story treats virtù and fortuna as two separate inputs into a prince's success — basically skill versus luck — and then the only question is how much credit each one gets. I want to argue that for the new princes Machiavelli actually admires (Moses, Cyrus, Romulus, Theseus), he isn't dividing a success up into a "virtù part" and a "fortuna part." He's saying the two are different kinds of cause: fortune supplies the occasion, the raw matter, and virtù is the capacity to recognize that occasion and force it into a shape. So fortuna isn't the leftover after virtù gets its share; it's the material virtù cant fabricate on its own. Which means "did he succeed because of virtù or because of fortune?" is the wrong question.

The whole reading hangs on this passage, from chapter VI of The Prince (W.K. Marriott translation, public domain):

"And in examining their actions and lives one cannot see that they owed anything to fortune beyond opportunity, which brought them the material to mould into the form which seemed best to them. Without that opportunity their powers of mind would have been extinguished, and without those powers the opportunity would have come in vain.

It was necessary, therefore, to Moses that he should find the people of Israel in Egypt enslaved and oppressed by the Egyptians, in order that they should be disposed to follow him so as to be delivered out of bondage. It was necessary that Romulus should not remain in Alba, and that he should be abandoned at his birth, in order that he should become King of Rome and founder of the fatherland. It was necessary that Cyrus should find the Persians discontented with the government of the Medes, and the Medes soft and effeminate through their long peace. Theseus could not have shown his ability had he not found the Athenians dispersed. These opportunities, therefore, made those men fortunate, and their high ability enabled them to recognize the opportunity whereby their country was ennobled and made famous."

Specifically I lean hard on "owed anything to fortune beyond opportunity" and on that last line about high ability letting them "recognize" the opportunity. That's the claim: virtù is mostly recognition-and-shaping, not willpower conquering luck.

The audience is half historians of political thought, half analytic philosophers, with a couple of civic-republican types. The historians do the Cambridge-school thing and will hate that I keep glossing virtù as "agency" or "risk management" — they want it in Machiavelli's own idiom. The republicans worry my reading makes the virtous prince too passive, someone who just stands around until a flood or an exile hands him material; they want Machiavelli to be usable for active civic agency today. The analytic people will grant the textual point and then ask what causal claim, if any, I'm making about real politics — is "recognizing opportunities" just survivorship bias?
