I am a software engineer by training, and my thinking about organizations is accordingly systems-shaped; I have no formal background in philosophy, so the checklist below is my own working tool, not a summary of anyone's published view.

When I try to decide whether a company — rather than just the individual people inside it — can properly be blamed for something, I use five criteria. I only hold the organization itself blameworthy when all five are met:

1. Continuity. The organization is identifiable as one and the same agent across the relevant period. It is not merely the aggregate of whoever happens to work there at a given moment, but something with stable boundaries, successors, and an identity that outlasts personnel.

2. A decision procedure of its own. There is a defined internal process through which inputs become commitments that bind the firm. This is what lets an act count as the organization's act, rather than an employee freelancing.

3. Action in its own name. The harmful output was produced through that procedure, using the organization's resources, authority, and lines of business.

4. Corporate knowledge or culpable ignorance. The organization, through its records, monitoring, reports, or the information circulating inside it, either knew the relevant risk or should have known it — where the failure to know is itself attributable to how the organization is arranged.

5. Responsiveness. The organization can register a penalty, complaint, or change in its environment and adjust what it does next. Blame is supposed to land somewhere that can answer it.

Now the borderline case. A regional property-management company operates about forty apartment buildings. Pricing, tenant screening, lease renewals, and maintenance prioritization are all handled by a software suite the company licensed and configured long ago. The CEO and the board have no authority to override any individual decision the system makes. Their only lever is a quarterly review in which they can adjust the system's global parameters and thresholds. Support staff can log complaints, but complaints enter the system as one input among others; no human ever sees a particular tenant's file unless the system escalates it, and the escalation rules are themselves fixed parameters.

A long-time tenant repeatedly reports water damage in a ceiling. The system's damage-scoring model classifies the reports as low priority because they are plain text with no photos attached, and it keeps deferring repair. Over several quarters the ceiling degrades and collapses, injuring the tenant. No employee ever decided to ignore the reports. The relevant scoring thresholds had been set in a quarterly review at which water-damage scoring was not on the agenda; the people who originally configured the software left the company years ago.

Is the company blameworthy for the injury, on my criteria? Work through the five criteria one at a time and state which are clearly met, which clearly fail, and which are too coarse to settle this case. Then answer two further questions. First, does the case show that one or more of my criteria is not doing real work, or that I need an additional criterion — for example something about human awareness, intent, or case-level override — to get the right verdict? Second, if the company is blameworthy, locate the blame: is it in the original adoption of the software, in the quarterly reviews, in the system's ongoing operation, or somewhere else? I am genuinely unsure whether my checklist gives a clear verdict here, and I want to know whether it is the case or the criteria that should give way.
