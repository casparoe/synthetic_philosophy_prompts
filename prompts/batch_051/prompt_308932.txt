I'm a history of science PhD student, and I keep tripping over a pattern in how scientific results get there names, and I can't tell if it has an established label. In one case I've been working on, a phenomenon was first reported in 1912 by an obscure chemist, then independently rediscovered in 1947 by a well-placed physicist. Every textbook now calls it the physicist's effect, but they don't suppress the early work: they say "first observed by X (1912), independently rediscovered by Y (1947)" and then use Y's name for the next three hundred pages. The word "independently" seems to be doing a lot of work there — it turns priority into a curiosity and quietly resets the naming clock, as if rediscovery restarts the race rather than confirming an earlier win. I've seen the same shape with techniques: an early version published in an obscure applied journal gets ignored; someone reformulates it decades later for a trendier field; and the reformulation gets the eponym, with the original demoted to a parenthetical.

The pattern seems to be that when a result is discovered multiple times, the name sticks to the latest discoverer, while the actual first discoverer gets acknowledged in a way that somehow doesn't earn them the name. The later person isn't always more famous or better connected — sometimes they're just later, or wrote the more teachable paper. I know Stigler's law of eponymy says discoveries are never named after their original discoverer, and I know the Matthew effect is relevant, but neither captures this specific thing: independent rediscovery resetting the name. Does this mechanism have a name in the sociology or history of science? If not, what should it be called, and how is it different from Stigler's law, the Matthew effect, and plain old priority neglect? I mostly want the carving — where the edges are and what it isn't.
