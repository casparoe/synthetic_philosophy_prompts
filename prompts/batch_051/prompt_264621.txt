Imagine that an unwavering Schopenhauerian defender of compassion and an unwavering Nietzschean critic of pity have agreed, after a long period of talking past one another, to produce a single jointly authored statement recording what they can both assert and where exactly they part ways. You are to draft that statement as a neutral drafter whose wording each side must be able to accept for its own position.

For the purposes of the exercise, the Schopenhauerian is taken to hold roughly this: compassion (Mitleid), understood as the immediate participation in another's suffering that moves one to relieve it, is the sole genuinely moral motive; it runs from the restraint shown in justice to the positive disposition to help; and it both depends on and discloses a partial breakdown of the barrier between self and other. The Nietzschean is taken to hold roughly this: what ordinarily goes by the name of pity is a contagious, life-lowering affect that spreads and magnifies suffering rather than reducing it; it is often a disguised form of weakness, self-contempt, and resentment rather than a pure concern for the other; and it must be distinguished from forms of assistance that flow from strength, abundance, and discipline rather than from shared distress.

The document should read as a single work both parties could put their names to, and should follow this structure.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered section, each item of which both parties sign. It should cover: the surface phenomenon that both sides, pre-theoretically, would count as an instance of compassion or pity; what each side is not claiming; and what is at stake, meaning which judgments, practices, or norms actually depend on how the disagreement is resolved.

2. Disagreement table. A table in which each row isolates one point of live difference, stated in wording the corresponding side would accept for itself. The table must include at least these rows, and may include more: (i) whether compassion/pity is a single state or a family of states that share only a word; (ii) whether the state's typical instances reduce or increase the total amount of suffering; (iii) whether the state essentially involves condescension, self-deception, or a covert satisfaction in one's own sensitivity; and (iv) whether compassion is best classified as a motive, a virtue, a mode of access to metaphysical truth, or a symptom.

3. Cruxes and tests. For each row in the table, state the sub-question such that a confident answer to it would move at least one of the two parties; then specify, where possible, the observation, argument, or test that both parties accept in advance as bearing on that sub-question, and record each side's prediction about what the result will show.

4. Closing assessment. Identify which disagreements are substantive and which, if any, are partly or wholly verbal. In particular, state whether any dispute turns on the two parties using the words compassion, pity, Mitleid, or suffering in different senses, and explain how the document should therefore phrase each party's claim so that a merely verbal difference is not mistaken for a real one.

Constraints: produce the document itself. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to secondary literature, and do not rest any claim on historical biography. Write in a formal register.
