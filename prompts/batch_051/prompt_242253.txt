As someone who teaches philosophy of history, I have repeatedly encountered the slogan “the arc of the moral universe is long, but it bends toward justice” deployed in seminar rooms and in public political rhetoric as though it were a single, settled claim. In my experience it is made to do far too much work: sometimes it is cited as an empirical generalization about the long-run direction of social change, sometimes as a reason to accept present suffering, sometimes as a thesis about the built-in logic of institutions, and sometimes as a barely disguised article of faith. I want to get clear on what exactly one might be asserting in saying this, and which versions can survive scrutiny.

Please lay out, with as much precision as possible, the distinct claims the slogan could be making. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive empirical thesis, a metaphysical or teleological doctrine, a normative commitment, or something else; say what evidence or argument would be needed for it; and indicate whether the slogan, so interpreted, could actually support the uses to which it is ordinarily put—especially the inference from “there has been moral progress in some respects over long periods” to “present injustices will in time be corrected.” I am particularly interested in whether a purely descriptive reading could ever justify the practical trust people place in the claim, and whether the stronger providential or teleological readings are intelligible without any theistic or idealist metaphysics. If some popular readings are false or empty, I would like them identified as such.
