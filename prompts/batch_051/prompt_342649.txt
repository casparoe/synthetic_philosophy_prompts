Question (from the worksheet):
In the Prisoner's Dilemma, defection is the dominant strategy in a one-shot game, yet in the iterated version strategies like tit-for-tat can do well and cooperation can emerge. Explain (a) why repeating the game changes the incentives, and (b) assess how realistic tit-for-tat is as an account of cooperation among animals.

Student's answer:
In the one-shot PD both players have a private reason to defect: no matter what the other player does, defecting gives a higher payoff (the temptation payoff beats the reward payoff, and the punishment payoff beats the sucker payoff). So two rational players both defect and end up with a worse outcome than if they could trust each other and cooperate. Once the game is repeated, the future matters. A defection today can be met with retaliation tomorrow, so the long-run benefit of cooperating can outweigh the short-run gain from one defection. This is sometimes called the shadow of the future.

Tit-for-tat is the strategy of cooperating on the first move and then copying whatever the other player did last round. In Axelrod's computer tournaments it beat a lot of more complicated strategies because it is nice, retaliatory, forgiving, and clear. Since tit-for-tat retaliates once and then immediately goes back to cooperating, it actually handles mistakes pretty well: if a partner accidentally defects, tit-for-tat punishes them once and then everything snaps back to mutual cooperation.

The same logic shows up in nature. Vampire bats share blood meals with roost-mates, and they do it because roost-mates are close kin, so helping them spreads copies of the bat's own genes. So tit-for-tat reciprocity shows how cooperation can be stable among animals once the interaction is repeated.
