I am on the safety board of a company designing a general-purpose assistant, and we are considering a decision procedure for the assistant's morally high-stakes actions. I would like you to analyse the proposal as a mechanism — what it would actually do in deployment — rather than as a moral position. The proposal is this. The assistant maintains explicit credences over five normative frameworks: classical utilitarianism, Kantian ethics, virtue ethics, rights-based deontology, and a precautionary common-sense module. The credences are numbers between 0 and 1 that sum to 1. Each quarter, every framework is credited with veto points equal to 100 times its current credence. Before the assistant performs any action on a designated high-stakes list (drafting a will, giving medical advice, contacting emergency services, and the like), each framework is asked for a permissibility judgement. If a framework judges the action impermissible, it may veto the action by spending veto points equal to a severity score — an integer from 1 to 10 — that the same framework assigns to the action; a single valid veto blocks the action. Points roll over between quarters, capped at twice the quarterly allocation. Once a year, a governance committee reviews the credences and may revise them in light of the system's performance in moral audits, but no framework's credence may be set below 0.05 or above 0.6.

Please walk me through how this mechanism would behave in practice. What would it incentivise among the designers who choose the high-stakes list and the translation from each framework's verdicts into severity scores; among users who might encounter the vetoes; and among the frameworks themselves, insofar as they are implemented as modules whose verdicts and severity assignments are maintained by different teams? Where is it likely to be gamed first? For instance, but not only: can a user drain a strict framework's points with a series of moderately objectionable requests and then proceed to a more serious one? Does the system drift over time toward the policies of whichever framework is most willing to spend, or of whichever is richest? Does anything about the design systematically misrepresent moral uncertainty, and are the failure modes fixable by amendment or are they faults of the whole architecture? Clearly distinguish the points on which you are confident from those that are speculative, and say what would change your verdict: which empirical finding about how the modules behave, or which amendment to the rules, would lead you to revise your analysis, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail? Please write the answer in British English.
