I'm trying to work out what I actually think about when a dispute is "merely verbal," and in particular whether ontological disputes about what exists can ever be more than verbal. I've been collecting my gut verdicts on cases, and I want you to find the principle behind them, or tell me honestly which verdicts I have to revise.

Here are the cases as they feel to me.

1. Planet. An astronomer says Pluto is not a planet because it has not cleared its orbital neighborhood; a planetary geologist says it is, because a planet is just a body massive enough to round itself and orbit a star. They agree on every orbital, geological, and historical fact. My verdict: merely verbal.

2. Falling tree. One person says an unheard falling tree makes no sound, because sound is an auditory experience; another says it does, because sound is compression waves in air. They agree on the physics. My verdict: merely verbal.

3. Virus. Two virologists agree on every molecular fact about viruses; one says viruses are alive and the other says not, because they weight self-initiated metabolism, reproduction, and evolution differently. My verdict: merely verbal.

4. Free will. A compatibilist and a hard incompatibilist agree on every fact about how decisions come about, their causal history, and the brain. The compatibilist says we have free will because it is enough to act from our own uncoerced motives; the hard incompatibilist says we lack it because our actions are settled by the past and the laws. My verdict: substantive, not merely verbal. But I'm uneasy: I can see how to dissolve this by saying they mean different things by "free will," and that is exactly the move I happily make in cases 1–3. Why should this case resist the same treatment?

5. Tables. An ordinary speaker says there is a table here; a mereological nihilist says there are only simples arranged tablewise and no table. They agree on all the empirical facts about particles, forces, and perception. I cannot settle this one. Sometimes it looks like two readings of "there is," which would make it verbal; other times it looks as if one party is getting quantification itself wrong, which would make it substantive. I most want the principle to deliver a verdict here and justify it.

6. Numbers. A platonist says numbers exist as abstract objects; a nominalist says they do not, while accepting every mathematical theorem and every fact about mathematical practice. I have the same wavering as in case 5.

7. Corporations. A lawyer says a corporation is a person within the meaning of the statute; a critic says it is not a person in any sense relevant to moral blame. They agree on the statutes, the economics, and the social facts. My gut says this is not merely verbal, even though nothing non-linguistic is contested.

I have been testing a rough principle on these: a dispute is merely verbal just in case the parties agree on every non-linguistic fact in the vicinity and each would accept the other's sentence, understood with the other's meaning, as true. That cleanly gets cases 1–3. But it seems to classify case 4 as verbal too, and I do not want it to. So I considered adding that the competing meanings must be equally good, with no practical or theoretical stake in choosing between them. That rescues my verdicts in cases 4 and maybe 7, but it looks ad hoc, and it makes cases 5 and 6 depend on whether some "stake" exists that I cannot identify.

What I want from you: find the best principle behind this, or repair my rough one; state it precisely and say which clauses earn their keep in which cases. Tell me which of my verdicts it preserves and which it overturns. Then apply it to cases 5 and 6, and to a further case I cannot decide: two researchers agree on every physical and behavioral fact about a computer system but disagree over whether the system is conscious. Is their "conscious" behaving like "planet" in case 1, or like "free will" in case 4? If your principle groups the surviving ontological disputes as verbal, say so plainly and explain what that would and would not imply about whether those disputes matter.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
