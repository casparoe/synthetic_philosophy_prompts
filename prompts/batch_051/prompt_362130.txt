For a seminar on the ethics of imposing risk without consent I had to write down where I actually stand, and now I want to make sure the set isn't secretely incoherent before I defend it in front of people who will absolutely poke at it. Here's the profile I turned in:

(a) It's morally fine for me to drive to the grocery store, even though I impose a tiny (ballpark one-in-a-million) chance of death on pedestrians and other drivers I've never met, none of whom consented to that risk from me specifically.
(b) It would be wrong for me to hand someone a pill that has a one-in-1000 chance of killing them, without telling them, even if they'd never find out and even if refusing to do so would be a big hassle that falls entirely on me.
(c) The only difference between the driving case and the pill case is quantitative — probability, expected numbers, size of benefit, that sort of thing. I don't think a distinction like 'driving is ordinary and pills are weird' is doing any real moral work.
(d) So I don't think there's a sharp probability threshold where fine flips to wrong; any precise line I drew between 10^-6 and 10^-3 would be arbitrary.
(e) Consent matters to permissibility, not just to compensation afterwards — but I don't see how that squares with (a), since the people I drive past never consent.
(f) I'm not a consequentialist about any of this: I don't think you just multiply probability by severity, compare the expected numbers, and out comes the answer. I think the wrongness in (b) isn't exhausted by that arithmetic.

Which pairs of these are genuinely in tension, and which only look like they are? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. Then tell me which single revision — dropping or modifying one claim — would do the most to make the set hang together, and what it would cost me. I'd rather have a definite verdict than a menu of options.
