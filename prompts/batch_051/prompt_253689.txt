I am a philosophy professor presenting a paper to a workshop of international criminal lawyers and philosophers of law. The paper argues that the moral category of genocide should be recovered from Raphael Lemkin's original analysis in Chapter IX of Axis Rule in Occupied Europe (1944), and the passage that carries the main weight of my argument is this one:

"Generally speaking, genocide does not necessarily mean the immediate destruction of a nation, except when accomplished by mass killings of all members of a nation. It is intended rather to signify a coordinated plan of different actions aiming at the destruction of essential foundations of the life of national groups, with the aim of annihilating the groups themselves."

I read Lemkin here as claiming that the core of genocide is not killing but a coordinated attack on the conditions of a group's continued existence, so that certain campaigns which destroy a group's culture, economic life, or political institutions count as genocide even where the death toll is low. The audience will not be friendly: the international lawyers mostly treat the 1948 Genocide Convention as the authoritative definition and worry about legality, fair notice, and the special stigma of the term, while the philosophers think genocide earns its moral distinctiveness only if the category remains strict. Prepare me for the question period: which objections will they actually raise, in their own terms; which have real force and should be conceded up front; which are answerable, and with what; and what is the one reply whose success the paper's survival depends on?
