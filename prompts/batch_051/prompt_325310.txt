I'm a novelist — two books out, third one stalled, so of course I accepted a talk I shouldn't have — and next month I'm giving a lecture at a small literary foundation's annual craft weekend. The audience is mostly working literary novelists and short story writers, plus two editors from a serious quarterly and a translator. They are not philosophers and they do not love the AI debate; they mostly think of it as a tech-industry distraction from the real work of making sentences. But a lot of them now teach in MFA programs and are quietly grading stories that were partly written by chatbots, and they're unsettled in a way they can't quite articulate.

My thesis, or what I've talked myself into, is this: the reason AI-generated fiction bothers us isn't that the prose is bad or derivative, it's that a novel is a record of a life making choices under pressure, and what we value in it is the sense of somebody standing behind each choice as answerable for it. A language model generates text by sampling from a distribution of likely next tokens; there's no chooser and no moment of deciding, so nothing is authored in the way that matters. The person prompting it is closer to a patron commissioning variations than to an author. Bad or derivative fiction is still fiction; AI-generated text is not fiction at all, it's something else, and we should stop pretending the question is "is it any good."

My argument, roughly: (1) what makes a stretch of prose count as a story is that it embodies a series of human choices somebody could have made otherwise and would answer for; (2) authorship just is being answerable for those choices; (3) nobody is answerable in that way for what a model emits — the user didn't choose the sentences, the company didn't choose your sentences, the model can't answer for anything — so it isn't authored. I know there are holes in this.

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet for this specific room: what objections will I actually get from these people, phrased in their vocabulary, not the tech-policy or copyright objections you'd get at a conference? Order them by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do. For each one, give me a two-minute reply I can deliver in a crowded room, plus a note on where that reply is soft enough that I should pre-empt it or concede something. Include the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks — the one that would force me to redraw the whole line on the spot. And be honest about which objection I should just concede up front, because this crowd will smell a hedge and they'll probly respect the concession more than the dodge.
