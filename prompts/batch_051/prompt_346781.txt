In my nationalism and memory seminar we keep coming back to a weird asymmetry and I cant decide whether it's defensible or just a collective reflex.

Societies make some military defeats into their holiest memories and bury others as embarrassing footnotes, and the losing itself seems to do a lot of the commemorative work. Serbia doesn't have a national day for its medieval empire at its height; it has Vidovdan, built around a battle it lost to the Ottomans, with the legend that Prince Lazar chose a heavenly kingdom over an earthly victory. Masada, a suicide of rebels who lost, got turned into a founding symbol. The American South built its Lost Cause entirely around a defeat, and most people now read that as an apologetic myth for a wicked cause. Meanwhile Britain folds Dunkirk into national pride — a retreat recast as a moral victory — while the fall of Singapore in the same war is remembered mostly as a humiliation.

So the asymmetry is this: losing is sometimes treated as the thing that gives a cause special moral standing, and sometimes treated as pure shame, even within the same country and the same war. Is there a principle that produces both judgments, or is the only real principle that we tell the story that flatters us? I want the strongest principle you can find, tested against at least one case that seems to go the other way, and an honest verdict on whether we should keep sacralizing defeats or treat it as a mistake we dont quite notice.
