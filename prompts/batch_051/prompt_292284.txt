I'm a novelist, and I've been stewing on a parallel the whole time I've been revising. The old workshop advice to write the book you'd want to read looks to me like the golden rule aimed at readers: treat your reader as yourself, on the assumption that your own wants are a good enough stand-in for theirs. The golden rule has its stock failures, right? The masochist who'd hurt you because he'd want to be hurt, the person with unusual taste who gets other people wrong. The standard fix is the platinum rule: treat people how they want to be treated. But in fiction, the matching fix, write the book the reader wants to read, is exactly the advice writers get mocked for following: market-chasing, focus-grouped, crowd-pleasing. So either the two maxims aren't as parallel as they look, or the repair that's supposed to fix the golden rule goes bad when you export it to writing. I can't tell if its a real structural point or just me pattern-matching to avoid my next draft. Where does the mapping actually hold and where does it snap? Is write-the-book-you'd-want-to-read defensible despite the golden rule's problems, and if so, what feature of reading — consent? the fact that readers choose to enter a writer's sensibility? taste as judgment rather than preference? — does the real work?
