In a discussion about same-sex marriage, a friend kept falling back on the slogan "love is love" as though it settled the matter. His argument was: a man who loves a man is in the same situation as a man who loves a woman, so the law draws an arbitrary line when it lets only one of them marry. Another person in the conversation had just argued that marriage exists to do something beyond recognizing a couple's feelings — something about uniting the two sexes or providing for children — and my friend's answer was simply "love is love."

I am sure that one small sentence stands in for several different thoughts, not all of which support his conclusion, but I have trouble keeping them apart. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims "love is love" might be used to express? The ones I can dimly see are: a claim that the love in a same-sex couple is the same kind of thing as the love in an opposite-sex couple; a claim that love is what gives a relationship its value; a claim that marriage just is, or at least ought to be, the public recognition of love; and a claim that the state has no business preferring one kind of couple over another — though there may be others. For each reading, please say whether it is a platitude, a substantive claim that can be defended, or a claim that is probably false, and whether it actually answers the point that marriage serves a further public purpose. I would also like to know which reading the slogan has to express in order to do the work my friend was making it do, and whether that reading survives scrutiny. I am not looking for sources or legal commentary; I want the underlying structure of the phrase sorted out.
