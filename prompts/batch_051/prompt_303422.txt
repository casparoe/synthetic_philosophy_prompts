I have just spent the evening in a long, unresolved argument with a friend about Good's theorem on the value of information. His position was that the theorem settles the matter in principle: a rational expected-utility maximiser can never be made worse off, in expectation, by costless information received before a decision, because she is always free to ignore it. I resisted with cases in which information intuitively seems harmful — information that changes what one wants, information that cannot simply be forgotten once acquired, and cases in which other agents know that one has the information and act on that knowledge. He replied that my examples either altered the utility function itself, rather than lowering expected utility under fixed preferences, or else imported constraints that the theorem never assumed. I was left unsure whether this was a genuine defence of the theorem or a sign that it is nearly empty. Please write an essay that weighs the two positions and reaches a definite, well-justified conclusion about how Good's theorem should be understood: whether it is a substantive principle constraining rational information acquisition, or a narrow result that holds only under idealisations designed to exclude every case in which information appears to harm. Do not end on a non-committal note; defend a clear verdict.
