In Anarchical Fallacies, Jeremy Bentham works through the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and the Citizen and attacks the doctrine of natural rights—rights said to exist prior to and independently of any system of positive law. The passage I want you to use is his examination of the article that declares the end of every political association to be the preservation of the natural and imprescriptible rights of man, and that lists those rights as liberty, property, security, and resistance to oppression. Bentham's complaint is not merely that the article is false or vague, but that its formulation is strictly unintelligible as a statement of what already exists: for him, a right is a creature of enacted law, so an imprescriptible right that precedes all government is a claim made in a language without a referent. He also argues that declarations of this kind do practical harm by encouraging citizens to treat their own judgments about these shadow-rights as a standing license to resist actual law.

Now apply that argument to a specific legal practice. In several jurisdictions, courts have held that they may invalidate laws—including, in some cases, duly enacted constitutional amendments—on the ground that they violate unwritten constitutional principles. The basic structure doctrine is one example: a court treats certain features of the constitutional order as fundamental and prior to the text, even though the text does not specify them and even though the amending power was exercised lawfully according to the constitution's own procedures. Other courts have drawn on similar unwritten principles to constrain legislation.

What theory of rights and law does this practice presuppose? Does it commit its defenders to something like the natural-rights view Bentham attacks, or can it be reconstructed within a positivist theory of law in which rights are creatures of enacted or received norms? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and then say whether Bentham's objection in that passage reaches this practice or only a caricature of it. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the practice if Bentham's analysis of imprescriptible rights is correct: would the basic structure doctrine and analogous appeals to unwritten principles have to be abandoned, or could they be reframed as something Bentham would accept?
