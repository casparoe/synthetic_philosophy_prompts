I'm in physiotherapy training and one metaphor keeps cropping up in how we are taught to talk to patients about pain: pain is more like an alarm system than a damage meter. An instructor will tell a chronic pain patient that their nervous system is like a smoke detector — it can shriek when there is no fire (the story we give for pain persisting after the tissue has healed), and it can stay silent when there is a fire. The conclusion being drawn is usually therapeutic: don't take the loudness of the alarm as a reading of how much damage there is. Lately I've been going down a rabbit hole on pain asymbolia — the condition where people can report that a stimulus is sharp, located in their hand, and intense, but they don't seem to suffer, don't withdraw, sometimes even laugh — and I've started to wonder whether the alarm analogy can really carry the weight its being asked to carry. On the alarm picture, the badness of pain is essentially the badness of the warning plus the badness of whatever it warns about; if the signal stops being read as a warning (or the motivational pull gets detached from the sensation), the pain should stop being bad, which is roughly what asymbolia seems to show. But I have a nagging sense this imports a lot from smoke detectors into pain: a smoke detector's beep is an arbitrary convention, the beep itself isn't intrinsically awful, we dislike it because of what it portends and because it interrupts us. Is pain really arbitrary like that? And does the analogy actually license the idea that the sensory part and the badness can float apart, or does it just restate that idea in alarm vocabulary?

I'd really value an audit of this analogy from someone who works on philosophy of mind or pain. Could you start by brainstorming a few candidate mappings of the correspondence table (what plays the role of the sensor, the smoke, the siren, the person at home hearing it, the fire department, the false alarm), then settle on the most defensible one? After that I want: the places where the mapping breaks; which disanalogies matter for the intended therapeutic conclusion and which are harmless; and whether the further conclusion — that pain asymbolia shows the badness of pain lies in the affective/motivational response rather than in the sensory-discriminative component — actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If there's a better analogy that does the same work without smuggling in the arbitrariness of a detector's beep, tell me what it is.
