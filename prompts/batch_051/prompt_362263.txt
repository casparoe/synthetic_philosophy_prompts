I've been reading Lakatos's dialogue about Eulers polyhedron formula V − E + F = 2, and I put together an argument from it that now looks like it proves too much, because the conclusion seems to make even basic arithmetic conjectural. Here's the argument:

(1) In the polyhedron case, a proof of V − E + F = 2 was proposed; then counterexamples were found — a picture frame, a cube with a smaller cube hollowed out — and the concept of a polyhedron was stretched, or the hidden lemma was made explicit and built into the theorem (monster-barring, lemma-incorporation), so that the new cases either got excluded or changed the claim.
(2) If mathematical concepts get revised in response to counterexamples, then at any given moment a theorem is not true outright; it's true only relative to the current, possibly temporary, concept.
(3) But a proof is supposed to settle a determinate proposition once and for all. If the proposition's content can shift when a refutation shows up, then a proof never settles a fixed claim; it only marks the state of the current definitions.
(4) So mathematics is quasi-empirical: conjectures get tested against examples, proofs are fallible, and refutation is always possible.
(5) Nothing about this depends on polyhedra specifically; it applies to any concept, including number, prime, and addition.
(6) But claims like "2 + 2 = 4" or "there are infinitely many primes" are not open to refutation by counterexample the way the polyhedron formula was; they're certain.
(7) Therefore either Lakatos's picture is false, or all mathematical knowledge is insecure — and I can't accept that about elementary arithmetic.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to choose, I'd rather give up (5) than give up (6), since I find the idea that arithmetic could be refuted by a counterexample impossible to live with. But I want to know what I'd be paying. I'm also unsure whether the slide from "the concept changed" to "the theorem changed" is doing hidden work, whether (5) over-generalizes from polyhedra to arithmetic, or whether "quasi-empirical" is just another word for "fallible" and not actually a threat to certainty.
