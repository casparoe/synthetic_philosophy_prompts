The debate between retributivist and consequentialist theories of punishment has been running at full pitch since at least the late eighteenth century, and to me it has the unmistakable look of a stalled dispute: each side can state the other's view fluently, each side has a stock reply to every stock objection, and nobody's probability seems to have moved in years. I am not asking for a survey of positions or a verdict on which theory of punishment is correct. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself.

The passage I keep returning to is from the opening of Chapter XIII of Jeremy Bentham's An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789):

"But all punishment is mischief: all punishment in itself is evil. Upon the principle of utility, if it ought at all to be admitted, it ought only to be admitted in as far as it promises to exclude some greater evil."

That passage looks on its surface like a cost-benefit claim about a practice: only inflict the evil of punishment when doing so buys off a greater evil. But I think it is also doing something subtler. It quietly defines the only question that will count as a justification — whether the punishment "promises to exclude some greater evil" — and thereby treats the backward-looking question of what an offender deserves as, at most, a side-constraint rather than a possible ground. My working hypothesis is that this framing move, more than any disagreement about how much deterrence or incapacitation real systems buy, is what keeps the debate from moving. Bentham writes in the idiom of means-end rationality, while the retributivist, in Kant's canonical version, denies that punishment may be inflicted merely as a means to some further social good: punishment is to be imposed because the person has committed a crime and in proportion to it. That retributivist claim is not a rival prediction about consequences. It is a refusal to accept Bentham's tacit definition of what a justification of punishment would have to look like.

If that hypothesis is roughly right, what passes for a single debate may actually be several disagreements sharing one word. One layer looks stipulative or verbal: retributivists sometimes hold that the concept of punishment already includes desert, so a purely forward-looking practice would not be punishment at all but something else — social defense, coercive crime control. A second layer is substantive but meta-normative: the sides disagree about which considerations are even eligible to justify deliberately inflicted suffering. A third layer is straightforwardly empirical — how well deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation work — though this seems the least interesting layer, since both sides can condition on the same evidence without converging. And beneath it all there may be a bedrock moral intuition, something like the claim that it is intrinsically fitting that wrongdoers suffer in proportion to their wrongs, which neither side can argue the other into or out of. The proliferation of mixed views, especially limiting or negative retributivism, looks to me like an attempt to split the difference without settling any of this.

Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement. Which of these layers are genuinely doing the work, which collapse into one another, and which are merely verbal or frame-dependent? Once the distinctions are drawn, is there a residual question that still divides well-informed people, and if so, what kind of question is it — empirical, formal, interpretive, or irreducibly normative? Then say what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a new empirical finding, a reformulated question — or, if the honest diagnosis is that the impasse sits on an arational bedrock intuition and no progress should be expected, say that plainly and explain why. Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work on punishment from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading of Bentham or a contested theory choice as if it were the consensus.
