Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether Newton and Leibniz's simultaneous formulation of the calculus — and, as he pressed, Darwin and Wallace's near-simultaneous formulation of natural selection — shows that major discoveries are close to inevitable once a field is ready for them. He concluded that this dissolves the special credit we give to individual discoverers; I resisted, but by the end I was unsure what the institutions of science would look like if he were right, and that is what I want to think through now.

Consider the practice of scientific priority as it actually exists. We attach individual names to theorems, constants, elements, units, and species — sometimes accurately, sometimes not, and often to one person when many contributed. Prizes such as the Nobel are awarded as though discovery were an identifiable event attributable to one person or a small handful. Journals adjudicate priority disputes, sometimes with considerable bitterness, and patent systems grant rights to whoever files first or can demonstrate first invention. Yet the same scientific community readily acknowledges, in its historical and sociological moments, that discovery is collaborative, gradual, and heavily conditioned by the state of a field — which is precisely what the simultaneous-invention cases seem to show. The practice is oddly janus-faced: it treats discoveries as punctual, attributable, and ownable, while also treating them as the outgrowth of a community that made them all but inevitable.

I would like a reconstruction of the implicit theory of discovery, credit, and inevitability that this priority culture presupposes. Does it presuppose that a discovery is a discrete, datable event performed by specifiable individuals? That the discoverer deserves credit others do not, even if someone else would have made the same discovery a year later? Which parts of the practice strictly presuppose that picture, and which parts merely serve social functions — coordination, motivation, storytelling, institutional continuity — that would survive its rejection? And if one took the inevitability thesis seriously, what would actually have to change: the eponyms, the prizes, the priority disputes, the patent rules, or only the way we narrate and justify them afterward?
