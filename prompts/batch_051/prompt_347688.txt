I've been reading Aristotle's Politics, and in the Gutenberg edition the introduction quotes him as saying that the state is "a community of well-being in families and aggregations of families for the sake of a perfect and self-sufficing life." I've come to think that if you actually take this seriously, it pushes you toward sortition as the default way of filling most offices. Elections seem to select for people who are best at getting elected, not for people who will jointly aim at the common good; a lottery, at least under the right conditions, would represent the citizen body more faithfully and make officeholding a common burden like jury duty. I want to know what would count against this view. Not just the standard objection that randomly selected people lack expertise—I mean which background assumptions about human motivation, civic education, institutional incentives, and the nature of political judgment the position depends on. Which of these would a committed sortitionist concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss? I'd like the defeaters arranged from fatal to merely awkward, and I want an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. Also, please answer in American English, and no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—just continuous prose, like an email.
