The distinction between intent alignment and value alignment has become one of the more consequential fault lines in AI alignment work. Let me stipulate the terms. *Intent alignment* means making an advanced AI system reliably do what the humans who design, train, or operate it actually intend — where "actually intend" is a descriptive, psychological matter, with no requirement that the intention be wise, informed, coherent, or morally good. *Value alignment* means making the system reliably promote what is genuinely valuable, or what the relevant humans have reason to want, in a normatively robust sense. On the narrow view of the alignment problem, the target is intent alignment, and value alignment is either a separate, optional project or a disguised version of the same thing. On the wide view, intent alignment without value alignment is incomplete, because actual human intentions are too inconsistent, too context-bound, and too silent about novel cases to serve as a target across the situations an advanced system will face.

I want your actual credence in this proposition:

P: Any specification of intent alignment that is complete enough to determine what a system should do across novel and consequential circumstances will have to idealize away from the operators' actual, present intentions — for example, toward what they would intend if better informed, more coherent, or more impartial — and that idealization is functionally equivalent to value alignment. So the narrow intent-alignment framing does not avoid the value-alignment problem; it relocates it inside the word "intend."

Give me a number between 0 and 1. Don't tell me it depends on how the terms are defined; I've just defined them. Then decompose the number. I specifically want:

(1) The sub-questions your credence is built from, with a rough probability for each main alternative.

(2) How much of the total rides on whether an operator's actual intention can be given a stable, purely descriptive reading that still picks out a target when the system must generalize well beyond the cases the operator considered; how much rides on whether the needed idealization can remain minimal — for example, coherence plus revealed preferences — without growing into a substantive theory of what is good; and how much rides on whether the dispute between the narrow and wide views is ultimately verbal rather than substantive.

(3) The arithmetic or weighing by which these sub-questions combine into your final number. Your answer should be an argument with its weak points marked, not a survey.

(4) The sub-question your final number is most sensitive to: name the conditional probability that, if shifted, would move the total the most, and by roughly how much.

(5) What evidence, argument, or concrete engineering development could move you substantially — and in which direction.

To make the decomposition sharp, suppose two competent researchers currently sit at 0.25 and 0.65 on P. Diagnose where the disagreement between them most likely lives: in which sub-question, with what difference in their conditional probabilities?
