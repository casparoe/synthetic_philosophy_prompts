I've been circling around a pattern in arguments about Black art, and I can't tell whether it has a name or whether I'm just restating something obvious. A Black novelist spends years on a book about two sisters running a failing hotel, and in interviews she says she wasn't trying to make a statement about race. The reviews don't care: the book gets read as a portrait of Black life as such—warm and specific, or insufficiently political, or airing the community's dirty laundry. Same with a Black painter who makes all-white canvases and says the work is about surface and light, nothing more. The art world treats the refusal of subject matter as a subject: it's read as a statement about purity, or a capitulation to white formalism, or a radical insistence on freedom. In both cases the art gets drafted into a cause regardless of what the artist says, and the claim that she isn't making a political point is treated not as an exit but as one more political point.

I know Du Bois's view is often summarized as the claim that art is, in the end, always propaganda, and this feels adjacent. But what I'm tracking is narrower: the work gets turned into propaganda by its reception, even when the work itself is treated as not expressing a message; the artist's stated intention to opt out simply isn't allowed to count. Does that conscription effect have a name of its own? And is it actually what Du Bois meant, or is he making the stronger point that the work itself is propaganda before any audience gets hold of it? I won't be satisfied with a one-word label—mostly I want the edge between those two claims drawn carefully, and to know whether my phenomenon is doing any work beyond his.
