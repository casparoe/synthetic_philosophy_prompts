I am reading Plato’s Laws in a course on ancient political philosophy, and I have been trying to make sense of the passage in Book 1 where the Athenian Stranger defends the practice of wine-drinking at well-ordered symposia. As he presents it, the drinking party is not justified as relaxation but as a kind of controlled test: under the influence of wine, a person’s sense of shame becomes less reliable, and traits that are normally held in check come to the surface, so that the gathering provides a way of observing and training the souls of citizens. Drinking is thus treated as a civic instrument rather than a private pleasure.

I want to see whether that argument depends on the participants’ awareness of being tested. Take the same symposium, same wine, same elected ruler of the party, and same underlying educational aim, but suppose the participants sincerely believe the evening’s purpose is hospitality and group bonding; they have no idea that their behavior is being watched for signs of shamelessness or self-control. Does the wine-test still function as the Athenian says it does, or does its value collapse once the knowledge of being tested is removed? Could the deception make it a better test of character, or does it corrupt the educational function of the institution by treating the drinkers as objects rather than as participants in their own training? Work through what the Athenian would be committed to saying, and whether the variation reveals a hidden premise in his argument.
