I was prepping for my decision theory seminar and got stuck on something I'd like a fresh take on. The claim that deliberation crowds out prediction — roughly, that while I'm genuinely deciding what to do, I can't simultaneously stand outside myself and assign sharp probabilities to my own choice — seems obviously right to me in some moods. If I honestly assign probability .9 to my raising my hand, then in what sense am I still deciding whether to raise it? And if I do keep deliberating, the prediction I form becomes an input into the very choice its trying to predict, so it isn't a neutral measurement of an independent event. But in other moods I worry that this is just a symptom of a confused picture of agency.

Here's the thing I keep chewing on. The structure of this looks to me exactly like the reflexivity problem in economics and social science: once a metric or forecast about a system gets fed back to the people inside the system, they adjust their behavior to the metric, and the metric stops tracking what it was supposed to track — Goodhart's law, as its usually put. A self-prediction is a measurement taken by a measurer who sits inside the measured system, and the measurement changes the system just by being believed. Is this a genuine identity of structure — both cases being instances of the same self-referential-measurement problem — or is the parallel shallow? After all, in the Goodhart case agents are gaming an external measure that is used to evaluate or control them, whereas in the deliberation case there's no incentive to game anything; the trouble is the clash between the predictive and the deliberative standpoints. And if the parallel is deep, does anything transfer? Social scientists have developed ways of living with reflexivity — hedging, partial predictions, separating prediction from target — could any of those make self-prediction compatible with genuine deliberation? If it's superficial, what exactly is missing on one side?
