I've come round to the view that how realistic a model's assumptions are basically doesn't matter — what matters is whether the model predicts well. I mean this in roughly Friedman's sense, the so-called F-twist: a model can be good science even if its assumptions are false in obvious ways, provided it tracks the data, and insisting that assumptions be literally true is a bit of a mug's game. I'd like to know what would count against this view.

Don't just give me the standard line that unrealistic assumptions sometimes lead to bad predictions — I want the weeds. In particular, I'd like you to seperate different ways an assumption can be unrealistic: setting a small factor to zero, pretending a mechanism is simpler than it is so the maths works, or flatly misdescribing what drives the system. Does the F-twist's plausability depend on which sort you have in mind, and could it survive for some but not others? Also tell me which criticisms a committed defender would themselves concede as genuinely counting against the view, and which they'd dismiss as changing the subject or holding it to an impossible standard. I suspect the position can be insulated fairly easily — for instance by making predictive success vague enough that no model really fails — so give me an honest assessment of whether it has been insulated, and whether that's a problem for a view of this kind. I'd also like to know whether there's anything this view permits or predicts about model performance in new settings that a realism-about-assumptions rival wouldn't, and whether that's where the real test lies. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which you'd find most persuasive. I'd personally be swayed by cases where two models fit the existing data equally well but the one with more realistic assumptions makes the better novel predictions.
