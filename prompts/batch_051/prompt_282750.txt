The episode is the Rana Plaza garment-factory collapse of 24 April 2013; the lens is the "mutually beneficial exploitation" account of sweatshop labor. I want the two run against each other in both directions—first the theory over the case, then the case over the theory—rather than a one-way application of a moral vocabulary to a tragedy. To make the exercise self-contained: on 24 April 2013 an eight-story commercial building in Savar, on the edge of Dhaka, Bangladesh, collapsed during the morning work shift, killing more than 1,100 people and injuring more than 2,000; the building housed garment factories producing for export to Western retailers; the day before, inspectors had found deep cracks in the structure, and the banks and shops on the lower floors closed, but the garment workers were directed to return to their machines the next morning. The theory to be applied holds that a transaction can leave a worse-off party genuinely better off than their alternatives and still be exploitative, because exploitation consists in taking unfair advantage of vulnerability rather than merely in leaving someone worse off than before; a further thesis often attached to the theory is that when workers consent to terms that improve on their next-best option, third parties should be cautious about interfering with those terms. Begin by running the theory over the case: who are the parties, what exactly is the exchange, what were the workers' alternatives, and why does the theory classify the employment relation as both beneficial and exploitative? What would the theory, together with that non-interference thesis, say the various actors—workers, factory owners, Western buyers, the Bangladeshi state, consumers—faced, and what should each have done? Then run the case over the theory: which features of Rana Plaza does the theory explain or illuminate, and which does it strain to accommodate or leave outside its scope? Press specifically on the role of foreseeable lethal risk; the fact that the workers' choice to enter was made under a threat of losing scarce pay with no reasonable alternative; the brands' sourcing and auditing practices; and the background conditions that made the next-best alternative so poor. Finally, say what the episode shows about the theory itself—confirmation, anomaly, or a case it never anticipated—and where the theory is silent, say so rather than forcing it to pronounce.
