I help run an institution whose explicit purpose is to preserve a specific set of values far into the future, and I have adopted a fixed-point policy for its governance: whenever we can predict, from comparable institutions or from trends already visible in our own membership, that our successors will gradually abandon one of the founding values, I treat that prediction as a reason to strengthen commitments, entry filters, and self-binding rules, never as a reason to re-examine the value itself. A colleague objects that this is indefensible as an epistemic stance. If I really expect our more experienced, better-adapted successors to converge on different values after exposure to evidence and circumstances we have not yet seen, then, she argues, my own model of the future is telling me that our present values are probably parochial, and refusing to update on that is just status quo bias dressed up as stewardship. Is my policy defensible? Under what conditions should foreseeable long-run value drift be treated as a risk to be locked in against, and under what conditions should it instead be treated as evidence that the present values ought to change?
