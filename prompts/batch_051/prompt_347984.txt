Hey, I'm putting together a session on aggregation for a reading group and I want something I can steal. You know the classic dust-specks-vs-torture case: one person gets tortured for 50 years, or an astronomically huge but finite number of people each get a single dust speck in the eye, the kind you barely register and forget a second later. I want you to rank these candidate ways of handling the case from most to least defensable, and for each say in a few sentences what it does with the original case and with two twists I'll give at the end.

Candidates to rank:

1. Strict lexical priority: below some intensity, harms just don't count against pain above the line, no matter how many of them there are.

2. Simple additive aggregation: every unit counts, so a big enough pile of tiny hassles outweighs a torture.

3. Threshold realism about tiny harms: sub-threshold "harms" aren't real harms at all, so they aggregate to nothing.

4. Discounted aggregation: tiny harms count, but with a weighting scheme that makes them add up slower than linearly once they fall below the threshold.

5. Anti-aggregation across persons: individual harms can't be summed into a single social total in the first place, so the whole question is ill-posed as stated.

The twists: (a) same huge number of people but the speck is a little worse—say a genuinely painful but still trivial brief sting, clearly below torture; (b) the number of speck-recipients isn't fixed in advance, it could be arbitrarily large but finite, and someone asks whether there's a number at which you'd flip.

Rank the five by how well they survive the twists, and flag places where they unexpectedly agree with each other, or where two of them give the same verdict but for different reasons.
