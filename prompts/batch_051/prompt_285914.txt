Lately I've been chewing on a conflict between two things that both seem true to me. Criminal law plainly cares about consciousness: if you kill someone while sleepwalking, or during an epileptic automatism, or in some post-hypnotic state, you won't be held fully responsible the way you would if you were wide awake and aware of what you were doing. A lot of mens rea talk sounds like it's really about what was "in mind" — what you noticed, what you intended, what you were aware of — and "unconscious" actions are treated as less yours. But there's a whole family of views in cognitive science that says conscious experience is a user interface, a dashboard, a simplified summary your brain builds for some downstream consumer; the real work is done by processes you never experience. On those views, sorting culpability by what was consciously experienced starts to look like blaming the speedometer for the crash.

So what I want to know is: what theory of consciousness, and what theory of the link between consciousness and action, does the practice of grading responsibility by conscious awareness actually presuppose? I'm not asking whether the law is "right" in some cosmic sense. I want the implicit philosophy reconstructed: does the practice need a Cartesian executive self, or is it satisfied by something much weaker, like "the agent could bring the relevant facts to mind and act on them"? Is it coherent to run the sleepwalker cases and the normal cases on the same set of principles, or are the practices quietly inconsistent? And if we took the dashboard view seriously, what exactly would have to change in legal practice — the verdicts themselves, the way we describe responsibility, or just the folk-psychological curtain we keep in front of it? Be honest about where the practice is underdetermined, so that several different theories of consciousness could underwrite the same doctrine.
