I keep coming back to this argument between William James and W.K. Clifford and it bugs me alot that I can't figure out where the actual disagreement is. Not a philosophy person, just fell down an internet hole after a reddit argument and ended up reading both essays. In Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief," after the shipowner example, there's this bit that I keep rereading:

"The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him."

That feels rock solid: he's saying the morality of a belief isn't about whether the belief is true, it's about how you came to hold it. But then James, in "The Will to Believe," keeps describing cases where the evidence can't exist until you already commit — his example about being liked, where if you go in cold and suspicious that produces the exact coldness you were worried about, so the "evidence" is only there for people who first took a leap. That also feels obviously right, and it feels like it just walks around Clifford instead of answering him. So a hundred-something years of argument and nobody seems to have moved. My hunch is the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: Clifford is talking about beliefs where the evidence was sitting there and the person looked away, and James is talking about beliefs that are more like bets or commitments, where the believing itself changes what's true. Is that what's going on, or is it something else — a real disagreement about whether belief is even under our control, or a fight about what counts as evidence, or just two temperaments? And given your answer, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, some experiment, or is this just not the kind of thing that ends?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning after that, no preamble and no restating the question.
