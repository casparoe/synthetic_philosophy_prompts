Two people I know recently argued about price gouging, and after trying to steelman both sides I still cannot tell who had the better of it. The case was a man who, two days before a hurricane was forecast to make landfall, bought the last few portable generators at a hardware store and resold them from his truck for roughly four times what he paid. Maya said this was plainly wrong. Ben said it was not only permissible but close to the best thing the man could have done under the circumstances. The argument moved through four distinct claims.

1. Maya began with consent: a buyer choosing between an overpriced generator and a flooded house is not meaningfully choosing at all, so the exchange sits closer to coercion than to trade, and the seller is exploiting the emergency. Ben answered that the exchange is still consensual in the sense that matters, because the buyer would not pay unless he expected to be better off than without the generator; the alternative is that the generator sits unused or goes to someone with a weaker need, and the high price is precisely the signal that moves scarce goods toward high-value uses and draws new supply.

2. Maya replied that this treats the objection as if it were only about welfare and consent, when part of the wrong is expressive: an emergency is the one time a community is supposed to default to mutual aid, and price gouging converts it into just another occasion for profitable exchange, which changes what the transaction is. Ben said "what the transaction is" and "mutual aid" were doing all the work. If both parties prefer the exchange to their next-best option, then outside repugnance is an aesthetic reaction dressed up as moral criticism.

3. Maya answered that repugnance should not be dismissed that quickly. In cases like vote-selling or the sale of live organs, revulsion is widely taken to track a real wrong, and Ben owes her a principle for when moral disgust is informative and when it is mere bias. Ben replied that moral disgust has a known track record of ratifying whatever the local status quo is, and has been aimed at arrangements we now regard as benign; as evidence it is weak, so anyone who wants to condemn a voluntary trade needs to show a harm.

4. Maya said the harm is in the norm itself: permitted and normal price gouging teaches people to see disasters as profit opportunities, and that expectation gradually replaces the mutual-aid norms that make communities function in emergencies, which is a real social consequence rather than a private feeling. Ben replied that she had moved from a moral argument to an empirical prediction, and that the empirical predictions on the other side are better established: anti-gouging rules produce shortages, queues, hoarding, and black markets, with the same goods reaching the same desperate people less transparently.

At the end I still did not know how to weight the considerations. Please adjudicate the disagreement in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: one point each on (1) the consent-and-exploitation exchange, (2) the expressive objection and Ben's reply, (3) the dispute about whether repugnance is evidence of wrongness, (4) the dispute about norm erosion versus shortages, and (5) an overall verdict on who is more right once the settled points are separated from the contested ones. For each point, say whether you judge the move clearly mistaken, mixed, or clearly right, and throughout separate what is settled among people who know this topic from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
