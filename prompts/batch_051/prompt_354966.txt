I have been thinking about whether curiosity deserves its reputation as an epistemic virtue — whether knowledge is worth having for its own sake or whether the value is really in the activity of coming to know — and that led me to Hume's account of the love of truth in A Treatise of Human Nature, Book 2, Part 3, Section 10 ("Of curiosity, or the love of truth"). His psychological story struck me as mapping almost point-for-point onto the way machine learning researchers talk about curiosity-driven learning and intrinsic motivation. Here is the passage that set me off:

"The first and most considerable circumstance requisite to render truth agreeable, is the genius and capacity, which is employ'd in its invention and discovery. What is easy and obvious is never valu'd; and even what is in itself difficult, if we come to the knowledge of it without difficulty, and without any stretch of thought or judgment, is but little regarded."

The mapping I have in mind is this. Hume's claim that the pleasure of inquiry attaches to the exercise of one's faculties rather than to truth "merely as such" looks, functionally, like an intrinsic reward for learning progress or information gain rather than a terminal reward for arriving at true beliefs. His point that what is easy and obvious is never valued looks like the familiar idea that curiosity-driven agents should seek tasks of intermediate difficulty, where prediction error is reducible but not already eliminated. And his further claim — that a discovered truth must be "of some importance," but that importance contributes to pleasure only because it is "in some measure, requisite to fix our attention" — looks like the role of curricula, novelty filters, or utility gates that keep intrinsic-reward agents from locking onto meaningless but unpredictable noise (the noisy-TV problem).

I cannot tell whether this is a genuine identity of structure or just pattern-matching on the word "curiosity," and I want an argued verdict rather than a survey. First, is the analogy deep or superficial? In particular, does Hume's distinction between the process and the object of inquiry really track the distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic reward, or does it break down because Hume is giving a descriptive account of a human passion while reinforcement learning is engineering a reward signal for an artifact? Second, if the analogy is deep, what transfers? For example, does Hume's treatment of importance as an attention-fixer, rather than a source of pleasure in its own right, tell us anything about how to design curiosity rewards so they do not collapse into noisy-TV behavior — namely, that the remedy must look like a curriculum or a filter on which uncertainty counts, rather than a stronger terminal reward? Third, state the clearest point where the structures come apart: what would have to be true of human learners, or of reinforcement-learning agents, for the analogy to fail outright?
