The theoretical position I find compelling is the common prior doctrine, in its normative Harsanyi form and as sharpened by Aumann's agreement theorem: ideally rational Bayesian agents who share a prior over a fixed space of possibilities, and who have common knowledge of one another's rationality and posterior probabilities, cannot persist in disagreeing. Persistent disagreement among rational people therefore has to be explained by private information, by failures of common knowledge, or by boundedness — never by their starting points. This attracts me because it makes disagreement tractable and non-arbitrary: prior probabilities stop being a free parameter that can be adjusted to explain away any divergence in belief.

The intuition I cannot shake is that conscientious, fully rational inquirers can start from different prior plausibility weights over the very same space of hypotheses. A mathematically minded physicist and an empirically minded biologist can share the same public evidence and yet begin with quite different prior weights on simple field-theoretic versus messy mechanism-based explanations, because they weight theoretical virtues differently before any private information enters. Third, priors encode evaluative or aesthetic commitments about simplicity and naturalness, so disagreement in the prior is really disagreement in those commitments, not in probability.
