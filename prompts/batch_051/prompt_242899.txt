I spent most of my career as a chemist looking at instrument noise, and since I retired I've been reading about randomness and probability. The question I keep tripping over is whether you can ever say a finite sequence is random. The standard answer seems to be no — randomness is a property of a generating process, or of infinite sequences passing tests. But I've been reading about Kolmogorov complexity and I have a homemade idea I'd like someone to poke holes in.

I want to say randomness is not a property of the string at all; its a property of the string plus a "search budget." Define a finite sequence as b-random if no program shorter than the sequence that outputs it can be found within b steps of search. Choose b to be whatever an actual observer could afford: an afternoon on a laptop, a year on a cluster, whatever. Then the same hundred-digit string might be b-random for me but not for someone with a better search procedure — just like the first hundred digits of pi look random until you happen to have the rule "digits of pi."

What I like about this is it captures what we actually do in the lab: we call a signal random when we've tried and failed to find a shorter description. And it ducks the uncomputability problem, because you never claim to know the true shortest program — you just declare randomness relative to how hard you looked.

Is this a real concept or am I just reinventing something with a worse name? It seems to inherit all the arbitrariness of the search method and the description language, but maybe that's the point — maybe "random" is a three-place relation (sequence, observer, budget), not a one-place predicate, and the two-place versions are just special cases with the observer held fixed. Where does this break? Is there any way to make it non-arbitrary enough to be useful, or is it relativism with extra steps?
