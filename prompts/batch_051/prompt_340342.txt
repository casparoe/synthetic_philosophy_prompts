I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, mostly conservation and the occasional land-use fight, and I'm trying to turn years of notebook positions into something coherent. Before I do, I want a consistency audit. Here's where I've landed, roughly:

(1) An old-growth forest has value that isn't just about what it does for people — not its carbon, not its timber, not even the awe it produces. It matters in itself.

(2) But I also hold that value has to be value for some valuer. A universe with no valuers — no creatures with a standpoint or interests — isn't one where anything is good or bad; it's just physics with extra steps.

(3) I've defended biodiversity offsets in print as a practical compromise: if a mine destroys a peat bog, requiring the company to restore or create an ecologically comparable bog nearby is defensible, not a moral sellout.

(4) At the same time, I think it's genuinely bad — not merely sad in a sentimental way — if the last population of some obscure beetle with no known ecological role blinks out, even if nobody notices.

(5) I've also written that "wilderness" is a loaded, partly colonial category, but I dont think that weakens the case for protecting large, low-human-impact landscapes. If anything, I think it pushes the justification away from "pristine nature" and toward relationships — between communities and places, nonhuman ones included.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly? Rank the tensions rather than treating them all as equally serious. For each one, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair is. Also tell me where an apparent tension dissolves once you keep the senses straight. And if the whole request is misframed — say, I'm equivocating on "intrinsic" or smuggling in an assumption about valuers — say so plainly instead of playing along.
