For the past few years I have been trying, on my own, to think through what a future would have to be like to be genuinely good to live in — not merely safer, richer, or more exciting, but good for the person living it — and the discussions I join keep splitting into two positions that never move. One side treats the question as a problem of supply: eliminate scarcity, disease, and drudgery, make positive experience (pleasure, engagement, comfort, the absence of pain) reliably available, and the future will be good. The other side calls this a confusion of ease with goodness: smoothly delivered good experiences would make a life hollow, and a genuinely good future must preserve strain, risk, failure, and things worth caring about that can go badly wrong. Both sides seem to accept the same facts and both think suffering and boredom are bad; they would inspect the very same imaginary society and simply disagree about whether it is good. My working hypothesis is that the real dispute is not about what the future would be like but about what a good life consists in: one side locates value in how a life feels from the inside, the other in the shape of the striving and difficult activity a life contains, even when that striving feels bad moment to moment. Is that the right diagnosis, and is the underlying disagreement substantive, verbal, or two different questions under one heading? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a scenario, a change of question — or should I expect the standoff to persist?
