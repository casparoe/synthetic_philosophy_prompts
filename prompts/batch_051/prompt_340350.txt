I'm working on a paper about machine ethics and I keep realising that people use "ethical reasoning" in wildly different ways when the subject is an artificial system. Some mean any system whose behavior ends up aligning with moral constraints; others mean something closer to actually weighing moral reasons, the way a person does. I need a working definition of ethical reasoning by an artificial system — not ethical behavior or moral impact — that gives a defensible verdict for each of these cases: (1) a self-driving car explicitly programmed to minimize predicted casualties in an unavoidable crash; (2) a large language model that, given a trolley-style dilemma, gives a nuanced answer about rights and tradeoffs; (3) a mortgage-screening model trained on historical data that produces racially biased approvals; (4) a rule-based triage bot in an ICU that applies a hospital's written triage policy; (5) a reinforcement-learning agent that learns to share resources in a multi-agent simulation without ever being given moral categories; (6) a thermostat; (7) an automated theorem prover that proves a theorem in deontic logic.

My hunch is that (1) and (4) clearly count, (6) clearly doesn't, and (2), (3), (5), and (7) are where the disagreement lives. Propose the definition, run it through all seven, and flag any case where your definition gives a surprising verdict or where you'd have to bite a bullet. If you think the phrase is doing two jobs — one about the process inside the system and one about the designer's intent or the system's history — say so, give both definitions, and tell me which of my cases move between them.
