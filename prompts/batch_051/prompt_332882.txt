I run a monthly technical seminar for about sixteen software engineers at my company; the group is comfortable with probability and expected-value calculations but has no formal background in philosophy or game theory. I'd like to use a 75-minute session to run a hands-on activity built around the absent-minded driver problem: a driver knows in advance that, at each intersection on a route, he will not remember whether he has already passed an earlier intersection; the randomization plan that maximizes his expected payoff before the trip starts can conflict with the choice that maximizes expected payoff at the decision point itself, even though no new information has arrived. I want participants to feel that conflict in their own choices before anyone names it.

Please design one complete, runnable activity for this group, and commit to a single design rather than offering a menu of options. Give exact rules, materials, a point-based payoff scheme that doesn't require real money, how many rounds to run, what choices are recorded when, and how to keep players from accidentally destroying the key information limitation by comparing notes too early. The game should be arranged so that participants first commit to a strategy from a planning perspective, then later face the same decision from inside the scenario; the divergence between the planning-stage optimum and the action-stage optimum should appear in their actual scores or choices, not in a wrap-up lecture. Then give a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the formal structure of the problem: the ex ante plan, the action stage, the inability to distinguish decision points, and the shift from an unconditional expected-value calculation to a conditional one. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, and be explicit about which part of the idea the game cannot reproduce and the facilitator will have to supply directly.
