David Hume, in Section VII of An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, writes: "When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other."

I am preparing a handout for an upper-level metaphysics course on interpretations of Hume's theory of causation. Use this passage as the diagnostic starting point and write a decision tree that sorts a reader into one of five positions on what necessity amounts to in Hume: (1) strict regularity/reductionism, on which there is no more to causal necessity than constant conjunction plus a habit of the mind; (2) projectivism, on which necessity is a felt determination of the mind that we inevitably project onto the world; (3) thin skeptical realism, the modest "New Hume" view that Hume only claims we cannot discover power, not that there is none; (4) thick skeptical realism, on which Hume supposes there are real powers or connexions in objects though our idea of them is irremediably contentless; and (5) positivist quietism, on which the question of mind-independent necessity is empty because no possible impression could supply the idea.

Return exactly five numbered items, with no introduction or conclusion. Items 1-4 are the branch questions, each a concrete yes/no or forced-choice question that a student could answer by considering the billiard-ball case or Hume's own argument, not by recognizing the labels; after each question, state which answer-options route to which leaf. Item 5 is the leaf key: for each of the five positions, give one sentence stating its core commitment and another stating its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot fully separate with this passage alone.
