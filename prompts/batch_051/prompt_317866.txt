I have been puzzling over a practice in my county's criminal courts, which now use an algorithmic risk score to help decide whom to detain before trial. The score is built from a person's criminal history, age, employment record and a handful of other factors, and it outputs a number meant to predict whether they will be re-arrested if released. The court says it will rely on the tool only if the score is equally meaningful for everyone: among people with a score of seven, the proportion later re-arrested ought to be roughly the same whatever their race. Statisticians call this calibration, and the court treats it as the decisive test of fairness. But the court does not object when the same tool flags one racial group as high risk far more often than another, and it does not seem to care that the tool's specific mistakes — detaining people who would not have reoffended and releasing people who do — fall unevenly across groups. I find that combination strange, as though one kind of equality matters enormously and another does not matter at all.

I have also been told there is a mathematical catch: when groups reoffend at different underlying rates, no single score can in general be simultaneously calibrated, have equal false positive rates across groups, and have equal false negative rates across groups. If that is right, the court's choice to check only calibration is a real choice rather than a neutral technical requirement, and the other two properties have to be traded against it. What theory of fairness does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view of what makes a decision procedure fair, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say which rival views it quietly rules out. Then tell me what would have to change — in the tool, in the validation checks, and perhaps in how the court explains its decisions — if the court instead took fairness to be about the distribution of errors, such as who is wrongly branded dangerous and who is wrongly released.
