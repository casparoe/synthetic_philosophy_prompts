I'm giving a conference talk next month at a workshop on causation, responsibility, and law, and I want to be ready for a long, adversarial Q&A rather than improvising. The audience will be mostly analytic metaphysicians who work on causation and counterfactuals, several of them sympathetic to the idea that absences can be genuinely causal, plus two legal philosophers who work on criminal and tort liability and who assume that omissions must count as causes for the law to make sense. My thesis is that omissions are not causes in their own right. I defend a norm-indexed view: a claim that an omission caused an outcome is true only relative to a background schedule of expected or required actions, and the world's contribution is just a non-occurrence, which by itself is not a difference-maker in the way a cause should be. My main argument runs through contrast cases in which one and the same physical non-occurrence is judged causal under one expectation and non-causal under another; I take that covariation of causal judgment with expectations to show that the causal content is carried by the norms, not by a real relation in the world. I also press an overgeneration problem: if every absence whose occurrence would have prevented an outcome counted as a cause, then indefinitely many absences would count as causes of every event, and only a normative selection principle can stop that.

Prepare me for the Q&A as a prep sheet, not as a defense of the thesis. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely and how damaging each one is, and indicate whether each is likelier from the metaphysicians or from the legal philosophers. Phrase each objection as someone in that audience would actually put it, then give a two-minute reply, say what I should concede up front and what I should hold, and add an honest note on where my reply is soft. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks.
