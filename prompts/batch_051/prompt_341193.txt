In my epistemology reading group last week, two people spent most of the session going back and forth about accuracy-first epistemology and never reached a verdict. The background is that they were arguing about whether measuring credences by how close they are to the truth, via a scoring rule, can vindicate probabilism — the claim that rational credences obey the probability axioms. I was only half taking notes, so here's roughly what they said.

A: The whole project depends on a scoring rule, and there are infinitely many strictly proper ones — Brier, logarithmic, and so on. Strict propriety just means each probability function expects itself to be uniquely most accurate. The framework gives you zero guidance about which scoring rule is the right one, so any vindication is relative to an arbitrary choice. That's pretty weak.

B: But probabilism doesnt depend on which one you pick. For any additive, continuous, strictly proper scoring rule, every credence function that violates the probability axioms is accuracy-dominated by one that satisfies them — the bad credence is worse at every possible world. So the core result is robust across all candidate rules. You only get divergence on further norms, like regularity. That's a feature: the framework justifies exactly what it was designed to justify.

A: Sure, but why restrict to strictly proper rules in the first place? The dominance theorem only goes through because you've ruled out improper scoring rules in advance. And the standard justification for propriety is that a credence function shouldn't expect some other credence to be more accurate than itself. But that immodesty requirement is already a coherence norm, basically probabilistic already. So it looks circular. If you allow an improper but truth-directed rule — the absolute-value score, say — the dominance argument breaks down.

B: Propriety isn't a probabilistic assumption, though. It's an adequacy condition on calling something a measure of accuracy. If your current credences think some alternative credence is closer to the truth than they are, then the measure is telling you not to trust your own credences from your own point of view. That's not a measure of accuracy anymore. So there's nothing circular about requiring a genuine accuracy score to be proper.

A: That's the move I'm questioning. Accuracy should be about the actual world, not about whether your self-assessment is flattering. A rule like the absolute-value score is truth-directed and can measure distance from the truth; the fact that it recommends extremes from the perspective of some credence might just mean those credences are bad, not that the rule is defective.

Neither would budge. I'd love a third-party adjudication: who's right about which specific claims, where does each overreach, and what are they both missing?
