Question: Explain Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason. What are the two methods in conflict, why does each seem to have rational authority, and how, if at all, does Sidgwick think the conflict can be resolved?

Student answer: Sidgwick's dualism is the clash between egoism and utilitarianism. He argues that practical reason supplies two self-evident principles: that an agent should pursue her own good, and that she should promote the good of all. Since people in fact only ever pursue their own pleasure, psychological egoism gives the first principle extra support and makes it hard to dislodge. He spends much of the book showing that common-sense, intuitional morality, once cleaned up, coincides with utilitarianism, so the real conflict is egoism versus utilitarianism. He considers whether sanctions — social, legal, and religious — could reconcile self-interest with general happiness. But he thinks social and legal sanctions work only imperfectly in this life, and religious sanctions depend on belief in God and an afterlife, which rational intuition cannot prove. Since two self-evident principles cannot genuinely conflict, he concludes that egoism is the weaker method and settles on utilitarianism as the rational foundation of ethics.
