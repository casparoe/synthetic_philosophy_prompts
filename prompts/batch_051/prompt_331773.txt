I've been rereading that early-ish essay of Peirce's where he treats belief as a habit we fall into because doubt is uncomfortable, and compares four ways of getting belief to settle: digging in and refusing to hear anything contrary, letting an institution police what everyone thinks, following whatever seems agreeable to reason, and letting reality itself have the final say. I came away with a little argument that bothers me, because it seems to turn Peirce's own stated criterion against his preference for the last of those methods. It goes like this.

(1) Peirce says the whole point of inquiry is to settle opinion: to replace the itch of doubt with a fixed belief. (2) So any procedure that genuinely fixes belief satisfies his criterion for a good method of inquiry. (3) The first method — call it tenacity, just keeping a belief and shielding yourself from whatever might rattle it — really can fix belief for a person who succeeds at ignoring or avoiding contrary influences. (4) If two methods both fix belief, then by Peirce's criterion they are equally good, and no further standard is left by which to rank them. (5) Therefore Peirce has no non-arbitrary ground for treating the method of science as better than tenacity.

I keep feeling step 2 is doing too much work, or maybe step 3 is rigged with the "who succeeds" clause, but I can't make the suspicion precise. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails and what's the corrected version of that step? And if it somehow is valid, does that mean Peirce's preference for science really is just a preference?
