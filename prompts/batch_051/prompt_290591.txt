Two friends of mine, Mina and Sam, got into it at a bar after I brought up legal fictions — the old ones like "the king never dies" and the modern one that a corporation is a person. Mina pointed to one of those corporate plea deals where the company "admits" to fraud, pays a giant fine, and no human being is charged. She said the deal shows the law now treats the corporation as a real agent: it has beliefs, it can admit things, it can even be guilty. Sam said that's ridiculous — a corporation is just a legal fiction, a shorthand for moving money around without jailing anybody.

The back-and-forth, as close as I can reconstruct it:

Mina (1): You keep saying it's just shorthand, but once a court sentences a company partly on the basis of its "state of mind" or its "corporate culture," the court is reasoning from the fiction as if it were a thing with properties. At that point the fiction is doing real work, so I don't see why we shouldn't just say the corporation exists.

Sam (2): Because there's no state of mind in there. Every sentence about what the corporation intended is really a compressed sentence about a bunch of humans following a decision procedure. A legal fiction just saves breath; it doesn't add a new entity.

Mina (3): Compressed from what? Say the board splits four-four and the chair abstains, and the law still says the corporation intended the act. No human intended it. So the corporate intention isn't a compression of any human intention — the fiction is doing part of the work itself.

Sam (4): No, the law just imputes an intention from a subset of the human inputs. That's a rule about how to aggregate human acts. Doesn't follow that the company has a mind, anymore than a spreadsheet has a mind when it sums a column.

Mina (5): Fine, but a center of mass has no mind and no body either, and you'd call it real because it does explanatory work. If corporate intent explains why a fine was the size it was, why shouldn't it be just as real?

Sam (6): Center of mass is a bad analogy. It's fully determined by the masses and the distances. Corporate intent isn't determined by any facts about human mental states — that's exactly why it's a fiction. The analogy cuts against you.

Mina (7): Okay, money then. Money isn't reducible to paper or ledger entries, and no one thinks a dollar bill has mental states, but money is real because institutions treat it as real. The same institutions treat corporate intentions as real, so why shouldn't those intentions count as real too?

Sam (8): Because money is backed by a huge network of people who believes it's real and acts accordingly — the whole population uses it, not just lawyers. Corporate intentionality is only taken seriously inside legal documents. Nobody outside a courtroom thinks the company literally has beliefs. So the attitudinal base that makes money real is missing.

Mina (9): That's just false. Traders, journalists, employees and regulators talk constantly about what the company "believes" or "wants" or "decided," and they act on that talk. If a social kind is real when enough people treat it as real, then the corporation-as-intentional-agent passes that test.

I honestly couldn't referee it. Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the nine moves, say whether it's clearly right, mostly right, mixed, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong, and briefly why. Then say whether the center-of-mass analogy or the money analogy does more work for the person who used it, and whether it actually helps them. Finally, say who is more right overall, and what both of them are missing.
