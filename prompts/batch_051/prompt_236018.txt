I work at a legal tech company building a statute search engine, and something that's been bugging me is the way different teams talk about the same chunk of legal text. The backend team treats a statute like a function: there's a text, it has a parse tree, and if you know the rules of the language plus the definitions in the code, you can compute what it requires. But our actual lawyers keep saying we can't ship a feature without pulling in committee reports, floor statements, and sponsor statements, because the meaning is in the intent. Meanwhile some of the federal judges we target seem to refuse exactly that stuff, and instead argue over dictionary definitions from the year of enactment. I can't tell if this is just a professional norm or if each side is actually committed to a different theory about what a law is.

So my question is this. Pick either the textualist practice (excluding legislative history, focusing on ordinary public meaning at the time of enactment) or the purposivist practice (using legislative history and purpose to settle what a provision does), and tell me what philosophical commitments that practice presupposes about meaning, authorship, and authority. Like, does the practice strictly require some particular view, or is it compatible with several views? Does it secretly mix incompatible ideas, for example treating a text's meaning as fixed at enactment while treating its purpose as something that can shift with the context of the interpreter? And concretely, what would a software engineer have to build differently if we actually encoded the presupposed view instead of pretending all interpretation is just string matching plus relevance ranking?
