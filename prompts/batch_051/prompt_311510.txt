I'm taking a seminar on identity over time and got stuck on the part of Locke's An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter XXVII, where he talks about an oak. The idea, as I remember it, is that an oak goes from sapling to full tree while constantly losing and replacing particles, but it is still the same oak because it keeps the same continued organization suitable to an oak, whereas the mass of matter it is made of is not the same mass from one minute to the next. I'm not quoting, just working from memory, but that's the gist.

That made me want to settle my thinking about the Ship of Theseus. If you take Locke's criterion seriously, the ship with all its planks gradually replaced is the same ship the whole time, because its arrangement and function persist, while the original planks being reassembled in a warehouse would be a different thing—maybe not even a ship in the same sense, just a mass of matter in ship-shaped order. But I feel strange about that when the ship is a historical artifact. Imagine two rival museums: one displays the continuously maintained ship, and the other displays the original planks rebuilt into the original form. My friend says Locke gets the museum case wrong, because the rebuilt original planks are the real Ship of Theseus, and the continuously maintained one is a descendant. I want an essay that actually argues for a verdict. Weigh the Lockean answer against the intuition that the reassembled original planks matter. Don't end by saying both sides have a point—defend a view about what determines ship identity over time, and say which museum, if either, gets the real ship.
