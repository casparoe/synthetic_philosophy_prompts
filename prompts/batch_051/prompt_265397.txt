I'm a retired systems neuroscientist, and after a year sitting in on a philosophy of mind reading group I'm presenting next month in defense of heterophenomenology as the default method for the science of consciousness. My thesis is that verbal reports should be the primary data, but interpreted strictly as reports about how things seem to the subject — not as a read-out of some private phenomenal fact. That way cognitive neuroscience can hunt for the neural correlates of those seemings without first deciding whether qualia exist or whether subjects are infallible about their own minds. My positive argument is thin; it's mostly the burden-shifting claim that rival methods either smuggle in first-person authority or throw away the only thing that anchors the word consciousness to anything observable.

The audience is mixed. Two regulars are hard-problem philosophers, the kind who think the explanatory gap can't be closed by correlation; they'll say I've changed the subject away from consciousness itself, and they know the standard Dennett-style moves well enough to hit back. Three are working neuroscientists, my old crowd, who think verbal report is the least trustworty measure in the book and want everything anchored to task performance or neural decoding. A few others are uncommitted and will follow whichever side lands the cleaner punches.
