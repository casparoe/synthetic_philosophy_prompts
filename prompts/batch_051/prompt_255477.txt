At a recent reading group on modal metaphysics, two members got into an extended argument about how to interpret ordinary possibility claims about particular individuals. I am still not sure how to referee it, so I would like you to adjudicate their exchange. I have reconstructed it as faithfully as I can.

Callie defended counterpart theory. She began by saying that a sentence such as "I might have been a plumber" is best handled without assuming that the very same object exists in more than one possible world. On her view, the sentence is true in virtue of a counterpart of me in another possible world: an object sufficiently similar to me in the relevant respects, and that object is a plumber. The truth of the sentence does not require me myself to bear the property of being a plumber at another world. She said this dissolves the old puzzle of accidental intrinsics, since one object is never saddled with both my actual profession and the alternative profession at once.

Ravi objected that this changes the subject. When he says he might have been a plumber, he said, he means that there is a way things could have gone such that he — the very person now speaking — is a plumber. A similar but distinct person's being a plumber in another world does not make his sentence true; it makes a different sentence, about a different person, true.

Callie replied that Ravi's insistence on "the very person" is precisely what generates the problem of accidental intrinsics. If the same person exists in two worlds and is a professor in one but a plumber in the other, then one and the same person would have incompatible intrinsic properties. Ravi answered that this is a problem only if property possession is left unrelativized. The standard view relativizes property possession to worlds: the person has being-a-plumber at world w and being-a-professor at the actual world, just as the same person can be tired on Monday and rested on Tuesday without contradiction.

Callie countered that relativizing property possession to worlds is ad hoc and quietly changes the property involved. Being-a-plumber-at-w is a relational property, not the intrinsic property of being a plumber, so the view no longer preserves the original thought that I myself could have had that very property. Ravi then raised what he took to be the decisive objection. If counterpart theory is right, then the truth of "he might have won the election" concerns someone else's winning in another world, and the actual candidate is not the subject of the modal claim. The man himself might reasonably be unmoved by news that a very similar person in another world won; what he cares about is whether he himself might have won. A theory that cannot capture that, Ravi said, has lost the phenomenon it was supposed to explain.

Callie responded that this objection assumes the very notion it is meant to protect. What the candidate cares about is itself a modal state of affairs, and on her account "he himself might have won" is just as amenable to counterpart-theoretic analysis as "he might have won." To insist that he cares about himself and not a counterpart is to assert transworld identity as a datum rather than argue for it.

Finally, Ravi raised a more technical worry. Because the counterpart relation is a context-sensitive similarity relation, a single world can contain more than one counterpart of the same actual person, and different counterpart relations can be in play at different points in one piece of reasoning. He claimed this makes the logic of compound and iterated modal claims unstable: "I might have been a plumber" and "I might have been a lawyer" could both come out true while "I might have been both a plumber and a lawyer" comes out false, depending on how the context shifts. Callie accepted that result and said it is exactly what a context-sensitive account should predict: the two conjuncts can be evaluated under different counterpart relations, just as "everyone is here" can be true in one context and false in another. Treating possibility as rigidly univocal, she said, is the mistake.
