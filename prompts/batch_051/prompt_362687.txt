people in my reading group keep defending toleration of oddball lifestyles by leaning on mill's experiments in living: the idea that society needs unconventional people trying out ways of life, because that's how we learn which sorts of lives are worth living, and the messy failures are just the cost of running the experiment. i get the rhetorical pull but im starting to suspect the lab talk is doing a lot of the work. how far does the analogy actually hold? first give me the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the hypothesis, the experimental design, the data, the replication, the peer review, the control group, the funding, and the consent of the subjects. then say where it breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the toleration conclusion from the ones that don't (real lives don't isolate one variable, the experimenter and the subject are usually the same person, nobodys actually writing up the results). then the key question: once those are on the table does the argument for tolerating nonconformity survive, or was the experiment metaphor carrying it the whole time? and if it does survive, what's the actual argument once you drop the metaphor, and does it end up leaving more room for conformity than the reading group wants to admit?
