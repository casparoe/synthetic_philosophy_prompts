I chair the advisory board of an interdisciplinary research fund that supports collaborations between physicists and philosophers of science. We must decide on a proposal from an experimental group and a philosopher of physics who want to build a small information engine: a device that converts information about thermally fluctuating particles into useful work, deliberately framed as a modern laboratory realization of Maxwell's demon. The physicists would measure the energy and entropy flows involved in acquiring, storing, and erasing the bits that drive the engine, while the philosopher argues that the experiment would not merely illustrate an old result but would test whether the demon has genuinely been laid to rest by theory.

One member of the board, a condensed-matter theorist, urges rejection on the ground that the demon was exorcised decades ago, once Szilard, Landauer, and Bennett established that a would-be demon must pay a thermodynamic price somewhere — in acquiring information or in erasing its record — and that the second law of thermodynamics therefore survives intact. Another member, a philosopher of physics, replies that this textbook exorcism rests on disputed assumptions about erasure, reversible computation, and what it means for information to be physical, and that only a concrete engineered system can force those assumptions into the open. In advance of our meeting this Thursday I need a short written opinion to circulate from the board. Draft the decision memo: recommend one definite action — fund the proposal as submitted, fund it with conditions, or reject it — and make any conditions or grounds for rejection specific enough that the applicants could respond to them. Give the strongest consideration on the losing side its full weight, and reach a committed recommendation; indicate clearly how confident the board should be in it rather than ending by laying out the options.
