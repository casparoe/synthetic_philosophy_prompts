I've been reading Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding with a couple of friends, and we spent way too long on this paragraph from the essay "Of Miracles":

"In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence."

My friends insisted this is just a setup for his wider argument that miracle testimony can't outweigh uniform experience. But I think the balancing principle has much broader implications then they're admitting, especially for how I should handle weird reports in ordinary life—like a coworker telling me about a dream that predicted an accident, my aunt swearing her arthritis vanished after a faith-healing service, or viral videos of people speaking in tongues. What are the most important implications of this passage for how an ordinary person should react to these kinds of testimonies? I'm specifically puzzled about how to apply the balancing idea when the "experiments" aren't literally countable: my own experience is a continuous, fuzzy mass, while the other person's testimony is a one-off. Does the passage commit me to always discounting other people's reports in proportion to the strangeness of what they claim, or is that too mechanical a reading? I don't want to become either a reflexive skeptic or a gull. Please write your answer in American English.
