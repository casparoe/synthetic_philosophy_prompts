I keep going back and forth on a definition of intelligence I ran into recently. The version I find myself defending on odd days is the formal one: take the space of all computable environments or tasks, weight each environment by how simple it is to describe, so that simple environments count more than bizarre contrived ones, and define an agent's intelligence as its average accumulated reward across that whole space. I like it because it is clean, it does not smuggle in looking-human as a criterion, and it gives at least in-principle answers for ants, octopuses, corporations, AIs, whatever. But on even days I have this stubborn intuition that it hands high scores to exactly the wrong kind of system. A giant lookup table that has the right response stored for every environment in the space gets a perfect score, and so does a program that simply brute-forces every possible response until reward shows up. That feels like measuring credentials, not intelligence. I want to say intelligence must also involve compressed understanding, or doing well on environments that were not in the training distribution, or getting the same score with bounded compute and memory. But every time I try to build that in, I worry I am smuggling the human case back in and losing the clean generality of the original.

My friend says my intuitions are just folk-psychological residue, and the definition is doing exactly what it should: outcomes matter, mechanisms do not. Is he right that I should drop the intuition and accept that lookup tables are as intelligent as anything else on this definition? Or is the intuition tracking a real flaw in the definition? If it is a real flaw, what is the smallest amendment that fixes it while keeping the virtues that made the formal definition attractive, and how would I tell whether I have amended the theory or just gerrymandered it to keep my intuitions happy?
