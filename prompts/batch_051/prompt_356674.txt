I'm a working programmer with basically no formal philosophy background, so go easy on the jargon. I keep seeing people in programming circles lean on a picture I first met in SICP (Abelson and Sussman, *Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs*, ch. 3). The book opens the chapter on state with this:

"We ordinarily view the world as populated by independent objects, each of which has a state that changes over time. An object is said to “have state” if its behavior is influenced by its history. A bank account, for example, has state in that the answer to the question “Can I withdraw $100?” depends upon the history of deposit and withdrawal transactions. We can characterize an object's state by one or more state variables, which among them maintain enough information about history to determine the object's current behavior."

The analogy people extract from this is: real things have state, so the way to model them in a program is to give computational objects their own local state variables, and mutation is just the natural way to reflect that state changing over time. And the conclusion it gets used for is that mutable state isn't some avoidable accident of how we program; it's basically forced on us by how the world is, so its headaches (aliasing, order dependence, reasoning about time) are the world's fault, not a defect in the model.

I want to know how far that analogy actually holds. Give me the correspondence table first—what plays the role of the real object, its history, its state variables, and time itself. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. The parts I can't shake: real objects don't come with a fixed set of state variables; we choose which history to track, and different choices give different objects. Aliasing (two references to "the same" object) has no clean real-world analogue because two people can't literally be in two places at once the way two pointers can point to one cell. And time in a program is a sequence of assignments interleaved by a scheduler, not one global clock. Which of these disanalogies actually matter for the claim that mutation is "natural," and which are just nitpicks that don't touch the conclusion? Does the analogy explain why mutation is hard to think about, or does it hide exactly the parts that make it hard? If you know a better analogy, say it. Keep the whole answer under 450 words.
