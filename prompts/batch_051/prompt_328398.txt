I'm the co-president of my high school's computer science and economics club, and we've spent the past few weeks arguing about reports of companies whose pricing software seems to keep prices high in lockstep without anyone at either firm having directly agreed to anything. One member of the club says this is essentially unpunishable, because each program may simply be responding rationally to what the others do. Another says the deeper problem is that we cannot tell the difference between two algorithms that secretly colluded and two algorithms that independently learned the same profit-maximizing strategy. In response, I suggested a rule, and I would appreciate help thinking through how it would actually behave if a regulator adopted it.

Under my proposal, every seller using an automated pricing system would have to deposit a copy of its algorithm's decision rule with a new regulatory office before each selling period. The office would not publish these rules. Instead, it would run a very large number of simulated market scenarios, feeding identical inputs to each pair of competing rules: the same competitor prices, costs, demand shifts, inventories, and so on. If two rules produced prices within one and a half percent of each other in more than eighty percent of those simulations, the two sellers would be presumed to have colluded, and they would bear the burden of proving in a public proceeding that the similarity was only the result of separate, rational optimization against the same competitive environment. The office would publish only an aggregate similarity score for every pair of sellers, never the rules themselves.

How would this system actually play out under realistic assumptions? What would it lead companies to do with their algorithms, their prices, and their dealings with the regulator? Where would it be gamed first, and what form would that gaming take? What second-order effects should we expect on competition, on the design of pricing software, and on the behavior of the regulator itself? And is there any way to patch its likely failure modes without giving up the core idea of comparing decision rules rather than observed prices?
