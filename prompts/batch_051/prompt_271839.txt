I am a credit analyst and a member of a professional association whose Code of Professional Practice contains this rule, adopted in 1994:

"Rule 4.3. A member who relies on the output of an analytical tool in a report, opinion, or recommendation shall not adopt that output unless the member is able to explain, in the member's own words, the method by which the output was produced and why that method is appropriate to the matter at hand."

For most of my career this was easy: our group used a logistic-regression scorecard, and I could state the variables, their weights, and why they were chosen. Eighteen months ago we replaced it with a gradient-boosted model over several hundred features and, more recently, a large pretrained transformer model that scores default risk from the text of loan applications and account histories. I can describe the training objective, the data, the validation and monitoring procedures, and at a coarse level which kinds of features matter. But last month I signed a credit memorandum recommending that we decline a small-business loan because the model placed the application in the highest risk decile. If asked to explain, in my own words, the method by which that application got its score, I have nothing that is both faithful to what the model does and shorter than the model itself. I can say gradient-boosted decision trees over several hundred features, but that names a family of procedures, not the procedure that produced this output. The method appears to be the fitted model, and no one in our group can restate it in a usable compressed form.

The clause was written with regression models, spreadsheets, and rule-based scoring in mind, not opaque statistical models. Walk me through how it applies to my situation under the main approaches to interpretation: the plain wording, the evident purpose of the clause, what the drafters would likely have said if asked about models of this kind, and what a reasonable reader of the code today would take it to require. Then tell me whether I am in breach, whether there is a coherent reading under which explaining the model's validation, limitations, and monitoring counts as explaining the method, and which approach should win and why.

I want the analysis to avoid proving too much in either direction. A reading of method that requires a full statement of every learned parameter would make the clause unusable for most modern analytical tools; a reading that lets aggregate validation results substitute for an explanation may drain the clause of its content. My underlying worry is that the clause was written to prevent exactly the slow outsourcing of professional understanding that our group has now institutionalized, but I do not want to settle that from mood. Please write the answer in American English.
