In deciding whether a word is a preposition, I work with these criteria: (1) it takes a noun-phrase complement and forms a single constituent with it; (2) it expresses a spatial, temporal, causal, or similar relation between that complement and some other element in the clause; (3) it precedes its complement; (4) it is invariable; and (5) it belongs to a closed class. Now consider the English word ago. It combines with a time-expression complement (ten minutes ago), and the whole expression behaves as a single adjunct that can be fronted (Ten minutes ago, the phone rang). But ago follows its complement rather than preceding it (ago ten minutes is impossible); its complement is tightly restricted to measure phrases of time (long ago, a while ago, but not the meeting ago); and it cannot be stranded when its complement is questioned, as most English prepositions can (one cannot ask How many minutes did the phone ring ago?, though How many minutes ago did the phone ring? is fine). Traditional school grammar labels ago an adverb, while some reference grammars classify it as a postposition. Applying the five criteria one by one, does ago count as a preposition? If admitting it requires dropping criterion (3), does the resulting broader class—adpositions, in a preceding and a following variety—still pick out a natural kind, or does it become a label for a family resemblance? And if ago is left out as an adverb, what content does the adverb label retain beyond serving as the catch-all category?
