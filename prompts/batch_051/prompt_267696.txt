I spent nearly forty years as a field ecologist, mostly in salt marsh and limestone country, and I retired with a conviction I've never managed to shake: a salt marsh or a doline swamp has value in itself, not just because it stores carbon, filters water, or gives people somewhere to walk. Since I retired and started reading philosophy a few years back, I keep running into the objection that value has to be value for somebody, or that talk of "intrinsic value" does no real work in conservation disputes. I want to know what would properly count against my position.

Here's the position as I would defend it now. Wild, non-human nature has value that is not reducible to its usefulness for human purposes, and not merely relational either: even if every human interest vanished, the destruction of the last remaining wild places would still be a loss to something other than us. I don't mean the strong metaphysical claim that value is a weird non-natural property floating about out there; I mean the more modest claim that nature is worth protecting for its own sake, not only for what it does for people.

I'd like you to map the exposure of that view. What observations, arguments, thought experiments, intuitions, or developments would have to be true for it to be false or badly weakened? Sort them by decisiveness: from "this would finish the view off" down to "this is awkward but survivable." Include the things a defender like me should honestly concede count against it. And be willing to tell me if I've already qualified the position into unfalsifiability with all that "not merely" hedging—and whether its a problem for a view of this sort.

The pressure points I keep chewing on are these: the thought experiment where the last living human destroys the last wild places before he dies; the idea that a restored or recreated habitat stands to a wild one as a forgery stands to a painting; the argument that you can get equally good conservation outcomes by talking only about human benefit, which would make "intrinsic value" an idle wheel; and the worry that "nature" is itself a human category, so my view may be smuggling in the very valuer it claims to do without. Also tell me what the intrinsic-value view predicts about cases its rivals would treat differently—say, a deep-sea vent community no one will ever visit, a scruffy woodland with no scenic value and no carbon credit attached, and an expertly restored marsh that is functionally indistinguishable from the original. I suspect what would move me most is a demonstration that the view systematically leads to worse or more confused decisions in conservation practice, but I'd like to know whether that is the right thing to be moved by.

Oh, and please write the response in British English, not American.
