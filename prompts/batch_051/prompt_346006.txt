I am a PhD student working on the philosophy of photography, and I would like help thinking through a passage from C. S. Peirce’s 1894 essay “What Is a Sign?”. The passage reads:

“Photographs, especially instantaneous photographs, are very instructive, because we know that they are in certain respects exactly like the objects they represent. But this resemblance is due to the photographs having been produced under such circumstances that they were physically forced to correspond point by point to nature. In that aspect, then, they belong to the second class of signs, those by physical connection.”

I understand the surface classification: Peirce first seems to treat photographs as likenesses or icons, then says their resemblance is rooted in a physical or causal relation, which makes them indices. What I want to understand is how much work this observation can do in the transparency debate about photography. In particular, does the fact that a photograph’s resemblance is physically forced rather than produced by selective human intention give us a reason to say that we see the photographed object through the photograph, or does it only explain why photographs are unusually reliable likenesses? I would like help distinguishing what follows from the physical-production point from what would require a further premise about perception or reference.
