In our course on classical Indian epistemology we have been studying the Mīmāṃsā view that the Vedic injunctions give knowledge without depending on any human author: the Veda is apauruṣeya (not made by any person), and verbal cognition enjoys svataḥ prāmāṇya, meaning its validity is to be presumed unless a specific, concrete doubt arises about the speaker, the transmission, or the content. Let us adopt the following working criteria for treating a received liturgical text as authorless and intrinsically valid in that sense: (i) a lineage of orthodox ritualists recites and hands it down as an unaltered part of its inherited Vedic liturgy; (ii) no member of that tradition, when questioned, can remember the text's being introduced, composed, borrowed, or added at any point; (iii) it chiefly prescribes rituals rather than reporting ordinary factual states of affairs; and (iv) it is integrated into the established Vedic apparatus through the usual rules of ritual application or explanatory recitation. Now suppose that an old family of Vedic ritualists has transmitted a domestic rite for six generations, and the rite satisfies conditions (i)–(iv) in every publicly observable respect: no one now remembers its origin, its Sanskrit and ritual application are impeccable, and it is used in the family liturgy exactly as if it were immemorial. Stipulate, however, that six generations ago the family patriarch deliberately composed the rite, taught it to his children when they were too young to later recall its introduction, and instructed them to pass it on as if it had always existed; all evidence of this act has since been lost, and only we, as outsiders considering the case, know this history. Does the rite qualify as authorless and intrinsically valid according to the four criteria? If it qualifies, should we regard that as a reductio of treating authorlessness as an epistemic matter rather than a fact about origins? If it does not qualify, identify exactly which criterion fails.
