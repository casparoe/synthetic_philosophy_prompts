I've been trying to work out what Molyneux's problem actually shows, and I found the original passage in Locke's An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter IX, Section 8:

Suppose a Man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a Cube, and a Sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and t'other; which is the Cube, which the Sphere. Suppose then the Cube and Sphere placed on a Table, and the Blind Man to be made to see. Quaere, Whether by his sight, before he touch'd them, he could now distinguish, and tell, which is the Globe, which the Cube.

A popular philosophy blog I follow glosses it like this: Locke's point is that the newly sighted guy couldn't tell the cube from the sphere because shape is really a tactile concept, not a visual one. Before experience links the two senses together, vision only gives you light and color patches, so sight and touch have no common idea of shape at all. The blogger then says this is why Molyneux's problem proves the mind isn't born with categories like shape—it has to construct them from sense experience.
