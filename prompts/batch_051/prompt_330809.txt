I've been trying to understand the relationship between Curry's paradox and Stephen Yablo's claim to have produced a liar-like paradox without self-reference. Both involve an infinite apparatus of sentences, but they seem to pull in different directions. Curry's paradox, in its usual form, uses a sentence that says of itself that if it is true then some arbitrary claim follows; it is standardly taken to show that certain principles about truth and implication cannot all hold together. Yablo's construction, by contrast, is advertised as escaping the self-reference diagnosis entirely: each sentence in the infinite sequence says that all later sentences are false, and no sentence refers to itself. The paradoxical reasoning seems to go through anyway. Some people think this shows that the real source of the liar paradox isn't self-reference but something like non-well-foundedness or circularity at a higher level; others think Yablo's sequence is circular in a subtler way, or that the reasoning secretly relies on a fixed-point construction, or that the relevant notion of aboutness has to be broadened so that each sentence still refers to itself indirectly.

Write a dialogue between two equally competent participants at a logic seminar who disagree about what Yablo's paradox actually shows. One should defend the view that Yablo's construction genuinely eliminates circularity and thereby refutes the claim that self-reference or circularity is the source of the liar paradox. The other should defend the view that Yablo's construction does not achieve that: that it is circular in an implicit way, or that the paradox still depends on a circularity-explanatory principle such as the existence of a fixed point, or that the construction changes the subject. They should engage with each other's arguments rather than talking past each other, and they should consider how Curry's paradox bears on the issue, for example whether the availability of Curry-like reasoning with the same infinite structure strengthens or weakens each side. The dialogue should be substantive and avoid merely popularizing; it should use actual logical vocabulary where it matters, though the participants may disagree about how to apply it.
