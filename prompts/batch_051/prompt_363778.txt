My sister spent the summer in a reading group that was very into Calvin, and at Thanksgiving she and my dad—who grew up Arminian—got into it about predestination for four hours. I was mostly trying to pass the gravy, but now it's all I can think about, because I honestly couldn't referee. Here's the argument as best I can reconstruct it.

My sister: If God is sovereign and grace is what saves us, then the deciding factor in whether I come to faith can't be me. Otherwise salvation comes down to who had the good sense to accept the offer, and grace is just a boost people have to complete. That makes God's choice depend on ours.

My dad: But then God has decided some people will never be able to trust him, and he still holds them responsible for not doing the thing they were never enabled to do. That makes the gospel offer to them a sham, and it makes God punish people for being what he made them.

My sister: They're not punished for unbelief; they're punished for sin. God doesn't owe anyone the grace to repent. And the Augustinian point is that the will is in bondage: we freely do what we want, but what we want is fallen. So we're still responsible—sin is what we genuinely want, not something forced on us from outside.

My dad: But if my fallen wants are fixed, then in any given temptation I can't have done otherwise than sin. How can I be justly blamed for an act I couldn't avoid? You need the ability to do otherwise; that's what "free will" means. Arminius says God gives everyone prevenient grace that restores that ability, so the choice really is ours, and election is God's foreknowledge of who will freely believe.

My sister: If prevenient grace is resistible and everyone gets it, then the difference between saved and lost is still how each person uses it. That's sticking a tiny island of "free will" back into the story and saying election is God's approval of what you did with it. And if it's not resistible—if it guarantees faith—then you're back to Calvinism anyway. So Arminius hasn't solved anything, he's just moved the problem.

My dad: He's solved the justice problem, at least. In your view God could soften my heart but doesn't, so my being lost is ultimately his doing. Saying I "want" my sin doesn't help, because I never chose those wants and I can't change them; only God could. If the wants are just given, then "mine" is a label, not a justification.

My sister: You keep using "could" as if God were a parent with obligations to every child. He doesn't owe anyone a changed heart. And your wants are still yours—they're precisely what makes your choices evil. God's not intervening doesn't make him the author of sin any more than a judge not pardoning a criminal makes the judge the author of the crime.

My dad: A judge didn't make the criminal's character. But if the only difference between the pardoned and the condemned is a heart condition God alone can change, and he changes it in one and not the other, then the outcome is his selection, not mine. Prevenient grace at least leaves the final yes-or-no genuinely up to me, and election can still be first because God chooses, from the start, to save all whom he foreknows will freely trust him.

I genuinely can't figure out where each of them goes wrong, if they do. Please adjudicate this round by round: for each of their moves, say whether it's clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Finally, tell me how confident you are in your adjudication overall, and which single verdict you're least sure of.
