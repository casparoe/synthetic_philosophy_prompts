I'm a novelist, and at a dinner party last week I spent a hour as the quiet third wheel while two friends argued about whether anyone ever truly deserves blame. It started with a true-crime story Rachel had read, about a man who'd done something genuinely awful.

Rachel said he didn't choose his genes, his parents, his brain chemistry, or the early environment that shaped him, so how could he be truly responsible for what he did? An action comes out of the kind of person you are, so to be responsible for the action you'd have to be responsible for the kind of person you are. But that would mean you'd have to have created yourself, and nobody does that. It just regresses back and back. So nobody is ever truly responsible.

Jon said that was way too demanding. We hold people responsible for what they do with the traits they have, not for having those traits. A man with a bad temper didn't choose it, but if he refuses to get help and then assaults someone, we blame him for the assault. The responsibility attaches to what he does with what he's got.

Rachel answered that whether he notices he needs help, or can stick with it — all that is also made of unchosen stuff. You've just pushed the regress one step back.

Jon: Then your standard is one nobody can meet, so of course nobody satisfies it. Ordinary blame doesn't require self-creation. It's about whether the act expresses the person's own settled values and choices, not whether they authored those values out of thin air. We don't deny a novelist credit for a good book because she didn't choose her talent.

Rachel: She gets credit in a shallow sense. But the deep kind of blame — that this man deserves to suffer for what he did, not merely that punishing him is useful — needs him to be the ultimate source of who he is. Otherwise he's just a pipe the universe flows through.

Jon: Maybe that deep kind is a fantasy. But ordinary blame is what matters, and you haven't shown it's incoherent. The act came from him.

Rachel: "From him" is doing the work. If "him" includes a character he didn't form, then tracing the act back to him doesn't give the ownership that real blameworthiness needs. You're settling for the shallow kind and calling it ordinary.

Jon: I keep hearing that blameworthiness needs self-creation, but I don't see an argument. Maybe blameworthiness only needs an agent whose choices reflect who she is, full stop.

Then the conversation drifted to the trial and I never got to hear it resolved. I honestly couldn't referee it. Who is right about which specific points, where does each argument break down, and what are they both missing? Overall, who was more right?
