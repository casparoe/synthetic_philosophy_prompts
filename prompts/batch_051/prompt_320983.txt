I keep getting pulled in two directions while working through quantified modal logic. On one side there's a theory with real systematic virtues: ordinary classical quantifier rules, the usual modal principles, and one fixed domain of quantification across all possible worlds. It's beautifully simple — no free logic, no existence predicate, no separate accessibility conditions on domains. But with a single fixed domain, both the Barcan formula (∀x□φ → □∀xφ) and its converse (□∀xφ → ∀x□φ) come out valid, and the two together seem to leave the domain the same at every world. That means everything that actually exists exists necessarily, and nothing merely possible exists: I have to say I existed in every possible world, and that no third child of my parents who was never born is out there to be counted.

But I have an extremely firm intuition that I might never have existed — my parents could easily never have met — and that there could have been things that don't actually exist. I keep being told either to bite the bullet or to complicate the semantics, but no one makes the costs vivid for me. What I want is a negotiated verdict. Present the strongest version of the theory and of the intuition, and work out the actual structure: does the simple theory really force those conclusions, and does my intuition really conflict with the theory or only with a sloppy object-language paraphrase of it? Tell me what it would take for the intuition to be the thing I should discount, what it would take for the fixed-domain semantics to be the thing I should amend or abandon, and whether there's a reading of "could have existed" on which both survive. Then say which move you'd make and why.

Please write your answer in American English.
