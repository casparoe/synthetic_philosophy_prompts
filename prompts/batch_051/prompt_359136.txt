I'm taking a philosophy of mind and method course, and we keep being told that thought experiments are little intuition-extraction machines: set up a scenario, ask what you'd say, and the answer reveals the contours of your concepts. But that never quite fit my experience of being persuaded by them. The most persuasive ones seem to do something sneakier: they make some feature of the case feel like background scenery, something you can't vary while still talking about "the same case." Once a feature gets frozen into the background, it doesn't have to be argued for; it just shapes every subsequent judgment. The case doesn't defend the frozen feature so much as arrange things so questioning it feels like missing the point.

Can you explain this "freezing" mechanism in how intuition pumps persuade? Use one or two well-known intuition pumps—say, the violinist or the runaway trolley—to show how a pump can do its work by controlling what we treat as fixed rather than by changing the intuition itself. And then the part I actually need for a paper: when I'm doing the reasoning myself, how can I test whether a feature really deserves to be held fixed, as opposed to having been frozen just because the example was vivid, weird, or emotionally loud?
