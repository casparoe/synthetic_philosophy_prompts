At a dinner a few weeks ago, two former colleagues of mine — one a statistician, the other a control engineer — fell into an argument that has stayed with me. Both are quantitative by training, and neither has ever cared much for philosophy, but the disagreement turned on a question I now recognize as squarely philosophical: what, if anything, separates possibility from probability. I reproduce the exchange here as faithfully as memory allows, because I have not been able to adjudicate it on my own, and I would value a careful verdict.

(1) The statistician: "In a good probabilistic model, possibility just is positive probability. If an event gets probability zero, the only defensible thing to say is that it is impossible. Phrases like 'probability zero but still possible' are word games — what could the extra word possibly add that the number has not already said?"

(2) The engineer: "That cannot be right. Take a fair spinner with a continuous dial, and the event that the pointer stops exactly at 0.500…, that is, at the point, not in an interval. Under the uniform distribution a single point has measure zero, so its probability is zero. Yet every spin does stop at some exact point. So a probability-zero event not only can occur — one of them must occur on every spin. If probability zero meant impossibility, a spin could have no outcome at all."

(3) The statistician: "You are confusing a real event with an artifact of infinite precision. No measurement could establish that the pointer stopped exactly at 0.500…, and no sensible decision would be affected by whether it did. The events that observation, betting, and control actually deal in have positive probability. So for every purpose that matters, probability zero is impossibility. The 'possibility' you are left pointing at is a phantom of the limiting description, not something in the world."

(4) The engineer: "That is a retreat rather than a defense. You began with an unrestricted claim — zero probability means impossible — and have now quietly replaced it with a practical claim. Within the model, the event is perfectly well formed: it belongs to the σ-algebra, it has a measure, namely zero, and nothing in the formalism attaches the label 'impossible' to it. That label is yours, not Kolmogorov's. And if you do add it, you inherit the mirror-image problem: the complement of that point has probability one, so by your own symmetry you would have to call that complement necessary or certain. But 'the spinner does not stop exactly at 0.500…' is not a necessary truth; it is contingent and merely measure-one."

(5) The statistician: "I grant that every interpretation adds something to the bare formalism; that is not a sin. The question is whether the addition buys you anything. If you refuse to call probability-zero events impossible, you must allow that an event can occur although its probability is smaller than any positive number. What then does a probability of zero tell you? Nothing that can guide expectation. You have kept the word 'possible' at the cost of emptying the number of its use."

(6) The engineer: "A probability of zero tells you a great deal: that the event belongs to a class whose limiting relative frequency is zero, or, on the epistemic reading, that it merits zero credence. What it does not tell you is that the event cannot occur, because impossibility is a different sort of notion. Probability is graded — it runs from zero to one — and 'more probable than' is perfectly meaningful. Possibility, in its basic sense, is binary. A reduction of the qualitative to the quantitative is not impossible in principle, but you owe an account of where the grading goes, and you have not given one."

They then turned to me, as the only person present who now spends his retirement reading philosophy, and I honestly could not referee it cleanly. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. Rate each of the six moves on a simple scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, accompany each rating with a sentence or two of justification addressing the move as actually made, and then say which of the two positions is closer to correct overall, and what, if anything, both parties are missing.
