I spent much of yesterday evening arguing with a friend who is not a philosopher. He maintained that a tool shows what it really is only when it stops working, because only then do you stop projecting your purposes onto it and inspect it as an object. We went back and forth for a long time without settling the matter. As it happens, I am now grading a midterm in my undergraduate phenomenology course, and the answer below runs right up against the same issue, so I would like a careful second read before I commit to a score.

The exam question was: "Explain Heidegger's distinction between readiness-to-hand and presence-at-hand, and discuss what the breakdown of equipment reveals about the structure of the world, using the hammer example. (10 points)" One student answered as follows: "In Being and Time, Heidegger distinguishes two ways in which entities can show up for us. Readiness-to-hand (Zuhandenheit) is the mode of being of equipment as it is actually used. When I hammer, I do not first encounter a neutral object with properties and then interpret it as a tool; from the outset the hammer is something in order to drive nails. Its being is constituted by a network of references: the hammer refers to the nails, the nails to the boards, the boards to the shelf being built, and the shelf to Dasein's ongoing projects. The world, for Heidegger, is this referential whole, not a container full of objects. While hammering goes smoothly, the hammer withdraws; what is salient is the work to be done, not the hammer. Presence-at-hand (Vorhandenheit), by contrast, is the mode of being of things taken apart from such involvement — things regarded as bare objects with determinate properties like weight, shape, and color. Heidegger assigns breakdown a central role. When the hammer breaks or proves too heavy, the smooth absorption of use is interrupted. The hammer becomes conspicuous as unready-to-hand, and in this failure the context that had been operating implicitly lights up: I suddenly notice the nails, the boards, the bench, and the project as a whole. This shows that readiness-to-hand was never a property of the hammer in isolation; it was a projection we had laid over an underlying present-at-hand thing. In breakdown, the hammer finally becomes present-at-hand — I step back and inspect it as a mere object with measurable properties — and this detached inspection is the origin of theoretical science. Breakdown is thus what first discloses the world as a set of independently existing objects."
