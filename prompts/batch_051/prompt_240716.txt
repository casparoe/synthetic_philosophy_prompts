Act as a professional philosopher whose specialty is Gilbert Ryle and mid-twentieth-century analytic philosophy. Work slowly and deliberately: begin by brainstorming four or five candidate examples, then discard any that are only rewordings, special cases, or combinations of the items I list below, and present only your strongest candidate.

I retired from experimental physics and have recently been teaching myself philosophy. I am now working through Ryle's The Concept of Mind, and I want to be sure I understand his charge that the Cartesian picture of the mind as a ghost in a machine rests on a category mistake — a matter of assigning mental concepts to the wrong logical type rather than of getting the empirical facts wrong.

I am compiling a list of category mistakes, and so far I have these:

1. The visitor to Oxford or Cambridge who is shown the colleges, libraries, playing fields, museums, and administrative offices, and then asks where the University is.
2. The child watching a division march past who, after the battalions, batteries, and squadrons have been pointed out, asks when the division itself will appear.
3. The spectator at a cricket match who, after the bowlers, batsmen, and fielders have been identified, asks who is left to contribute team spirit.
4. The person who treats the average taxpayer as if he were a particular fellow citizen one might meet in a queue.
5. A physics student of mine who, after a pendulum had been dismantled into bob, rod, and pivot, asked which component carries the period.

As I understand it, these exhibit four different underlying structures: items 1 and 2 treat an organization as an additional unit of the same kind as the organized parts; item 3 treats a style or disposition as an extra performance alongside the concrete performances; item 4 treats a statistical abstraction as a concrete individual; and item 5 treats a dynamical magnitude as a material part.

I want one more category mistake that is not on this list and is not a variant, special case, or combination of anything on it. Ideally it should cross a category boundary different from all four of the structures I have just distinguished. After you give the example, state precisely which categories are being conflated, explain why the error qualifies as a category mistake rather than an ordinary factual error, and then say honestly whether it is philosophically illuminating or merely a classroom illustration. If you cannot construct an example that is genuinely new, say so and explain why.
