I've been going around in circles with a couple friends about what makes something a language, and I want someone to look at my gut reactions on cases and tell me what principle, if any, is pulling the strings—or which reactions I should ditch.

Here are my verdicts, in no particular order:

1. ASL is a language, full stop. I don't even want to argue about it.
2. A deaf kid born to hearing parents who never meets other deaf people or signers and slowly builds up a big gesture system, a few hundred signs with some regular word-order habits—a homesign system. My gut says it's language-like but not a language.
3. The deaf kids at that school in Managua in the eighties. The first group arrived with only improvised home gestures, and after a couple years they had a shared system that was mostly a big vocabulary plus loose pointing. I'd say that isn't a language yet. But the younger kids who came later and learned from that first group as little kids ended up signing with clear grammatical rules, and I'd call the later thing a language.
4. Signed English of the kind where you take ASL-type signs and put them into strict English word order, adding little signed morphemes for -ed and -ing. My gut: that's not a seperate language, it's just English running through the hands—even though it has grammar, because English has grammar.
5. Two hearing brothers who invent a private gesture code as kids, maybe 60 signs, no real grammar beyond point-and-label, only used with each other. Not a language.
6. Bee waggle dance. Not a language, whatever it is.
7. A deafblind person signing ASL into someone else's hands. My gut: still a language, but not a new one—just ASL in another channel.
8. Emoji-only messages, like a string of emojis meant to read "sad, coming home late." Not a language.
9. The improvised, mixed signing at international deaf events, where people with different national sign languages patch together a kind of pidgin. I genuinely don't know what my gut says; it changes by the day.

A friend of mine keeps insisting the line is grammar: a language has recursive rules for combining units, not just a bag of labels. But that gives case 4 a pass and I still don't want to call it a separate language, and case 2 has at least some regularities without making the cut for me. Another friend says the line is social: something becomes a language only when a community uses it as a first language and passes it along. That handles alot of my cases, but it seems to count case 4 as a language if a kid grows up with it, which I resist, and it makes the first-cohort Managua system count as a language as soon as the younger kids started picking it up, which also feels off to me.

So: is there one principled account that gets all nine of my verdicts, or close? What does your preferred principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a family with a deaf child and hearing siblings who together develop a genuinely grammatical sign system—full sentences, tense marking, the works—but it never spreads outside the family and dies out within two generations. Language or not? And if no clean principle gets me all nine verdicts, tell me which of my verdicts I should be most willing to overturn and why.
