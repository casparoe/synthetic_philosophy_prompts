I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, and one chapter of my dissertation concerns group belief revision under inputs that are conditional in form. I would appreciate a dispassionate mechanism analysis of the following scheme, which I am considering only as a running example, not as something I endorse.

Consider a standing prediction platform that maintains a single public consensus probability distribution over a fixed set of mutually exclusive and exhaustive outcomes—say, ten possible paths for a piece of legislation. Each week, any registered forecaster may submit any number of conditional probabilities of the form P(B | A) = q, read as "conditional on the outcome falling in A, the probability that it falls in B is q." Forecasters may not submit unconditional probabilities, and may not submit anything about the joint distribution beyond such conditionals. Submissions are public as they arrive. At the end of the week, the platform updates the consensus to the distribution that minimizes relative entropy (Kullback–Leibler divergence) from the previous week's consensus among all distributions satisfying the accepted conditionals; a moderator rejects or reconciles inconsistent submissions. Forecasters are paid at final resolution by a strictly proper scoring rule applied to each week's consensus, so their only instrumental goal is to move the consensus toward their own estimate.

Please work through how this institution would actually behave. In particular: when a forecaster wants to raise the consensus probability of a conditioning event A without ever reporting on A unconditionally, how does the Judy Benjamin structure—in which updating on a constraint about P(B | A) under minimum relative entropy can shift P(A) itself—function as an influence channel, and which conditional reports exploit it most efficiently? When many forecasters submit conditionals whose conditioning events overlap, does the process settle toward a stable distribution that aggregates dispersed information, or does it become path-dependent and agenda-driven? Where is the scheme gamed first—through the timing of submissions, the choice of conditioning events, or engineered inconsistencies left for the moderator—and what is the cheapest change, whether to the allowed inputs, the update rule, the scoring rule, or the moderator's role, that reduces the worst failure mode while preserving the idea of reasoning only from conditional evidence? I am less interested in whether the scheme is good in the abstract than in a precise account of its incentives and failure modes.
