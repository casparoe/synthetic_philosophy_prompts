Question:
W. E. B. Du Bois argues in his well-known essay on African American art that all art is propaganda and that he has no interest in art that does not serve as propaganda. Reconstruct his position by distinguishing two theses: first, a descriptive thesis that all art shapes social perception and so functions propagandistically; second, a prescriptive thesis that African American artists have a race-specific obligation to make their art serve racial justice. Then explain how Du Bois's standards of truth, beauty, and moral seriousness fit within this account, and explain the role of the white-controlled market for Black art in his argument. Suggested length: 400 words.

Student answer:
Du Bois's starting point is that art is never politically neutral. Any representation of Black life provides an audience with material for forming or reinforcing attitudes, so an artist cannot avoid responsibility by claiming purely aesthetic aims. The ideal of art for art's sake is therefore a luxury available mainly to those whose social position is secure enough that they can ignore the effects of their work. For Black artists under Jim Crow, Du Bois turns this descriptive point into a prescriptive one: African American artists must choose which cause their art serves, and he argues it should be racial equality. He also supplies a positive standard: Black art should present the truth of Black life, its beauty, and its moral seriousness, rather than the stereotypes white audiences expect. He argues that the white-controlled publishing and patronage system rewards those stereotypes and punishes dignified or truthful portrayals, so Black artists face structural pressure to produce propaganda for white supremacy even when they believe they are being apolitical. For this reason, Du Bois judges a work of art by whether it advances or retards racial uplift, and works that merely depict Black life without an explicit protest message are, on his view, failures.
