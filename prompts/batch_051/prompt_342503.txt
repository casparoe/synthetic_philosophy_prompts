I'm a PhD student working on mechanistic interpretability, and I've noticed I've fallen into a pretty rigid policy when I read papers. I basically never accept a claim like 'the model has learned a representation of gender' or 'this neuron tracks subject–verb agreement' unless the authors did some kind of causal intervention — ablation, activation patching, causal tracing, a knock-out experiment — and the model's behavior changed in the way you'd predict if that feature were actually load-bearing. Purely correlational results (a linear probe that classifies from the activations, a saliency map, a decoder trained on hidden states) tend not to move me at all; I file them as 'the model could be read as representing X' rather than 'the model represents X.'

I'm starting to worry this is just bias dressed up as methodology. Maybe I've implicitly committed to a manipulationist theory of content and I'm treating it as a neutral evidential standard. But the alternative — treating 'the network represents X' as supported by any decoder that can recover X from hidden states — feels way too promiscuous; cant that be gamed by training a probe on anything it can predict? Then an LLM would represent every feature I can come up with, which seems to make the word 'representation' useless.

So: is my policy defensible as a reading strategy for interpretability papers, or is it an undefended metaphysical assumption, and how would I tell the difference from the inside? I'd like the answer as a JSON object with these fields: 'verdict' (one of: defensible, partly defensible, indefensible), 'strongest_objection' (string), 'strongest_defense' (string), 'restated_rule' (a version of the policy that keeps the benefits without the costs, if one exists), and 'boundary_conditions' (list of situations where the policy is likely to mislead).
