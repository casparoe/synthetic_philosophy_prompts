Some philosophers and decision theorists have argued that an agent cannot assign well-defined probabilities to its own still-open choices: that the stance of predicting one's own action and the stance of deliberating about it are in tension. The claim is usually put as a conditional — if you really are deliberating, then your probability judgments about what you will do are either disguised predictions, reports of your present estimates of your reasons and weaknesses, or interventions that change the thing they purport to measure. Call this the thesis that deliberation crowds out self-prediction. I would like you to treat this as a claim that might be partly conceptual and partly empirical, and to do two things. First, design the strongest feasible test of the empirical component: specify a concrete choice setting, a method for eliciting subjects' probabilities over their own upcoming choices at several points between first facing the choice and deciding, and a criterion — for example calibration, the stability of the probabilities under further reflection, or subjects' own reports about whether assigning a probability altered the choice — that would count as evidence for or against the thesis. Be concrete enough that an experimenter could run it. Second, state precisely what a positive or negative result would and would not settle. Could a subject be perfectly calibrated and yet still be unable to predict herself in the way she predicts others? Could a poorly calibrated subject merely be bad at prediction rather than caught in a conceptual blind spot? Finally, identify the residual claim, if any, that no experimental outcome could touch, and explain whether it should be classified as a conceptual truth, a normative point about how one ought to deliberate, or something else.
