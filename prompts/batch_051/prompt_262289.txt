In a graduate seminar on welfare measurement, I watched an economist dismiss survey-based research on subjective well-being with the proverb "actions speak louder than words." The move was roughly this: people's stated values and reported satisfaction are cheap talk, whereas their choices—especially choices made under budget constraints or with real costs attached—reveal their true preferences; therefore utility and welfare should be read off choice behavior, and self-report data should be set aside. I suspect this one proverb is doing several distinct jobs at once, and I want them separated and evaluated.

Lay out the specific, precise theses that "actions speak louder than words" could be used to express within the revealed-preference approach to utility. Organize them as a menu, from the most modest—choices are one kind of evidence about preference, fallible and corrigible—through intermediate readings—choices systematically reveal preferences better than verbal reports because they force trade-offs; verbal reports are themselves actions, so the contrast needs refinement—to the strongest—a preference just is whatever a suitably consistent choice function maximizes, and talk of a "real" preference that diverges from stubbornly self-defeating or incoherent choices is vacuous. For each reading, state whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, and say exactly what that reading would and would not license about dismissing survey or self-report data in welfare economics.

I also want the same disambiguation applied to the technical side. My understanding is that the standard axioms of revealed preference work roughly as follows: if a subject's choices are consistent in the right way—satisfying the Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference in simple two-good choice problems, or the corresponding consistency conditions (such as the Strong Axiom or the Generalized Axiom) when there are many goods—then those choices can be represented as maximizing some as-if utility function. But I do not see how a representation theorem saying "these choices are consistent with maximizing some preference ordering" turns into "utility is nothing but choice," or how it rules out a distinct notion of a person's preferences that their choices only imperfectly express—because of weakness of will, addiction, ignorance, menu effects, or framing. So I want to know: does the strong definitional reading of revealed preference actually follow from the representation theorem, or is it a separate philosophical commitment that the axioms alone do not force? And which of the slogan's possible readings are needed to get from the theorem to the dismissal of survey evidence?

Please write your answer in American English, and do not include a bibliography or suggestions for further reading.
