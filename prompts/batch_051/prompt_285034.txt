After a seminar a few weeks ago I got into an argument about Avicenna's flying man, and I've been trying to write up a short piece since then. I've got a rough set of notes and would be glad of comments on whether the line of thought actually works, rather than a rewrite. For context, the thought experiment as I understand it goes like this: imagine a human being created all at once, fully formed and with normal mental powers, but suspended in empty space with veiled eyes and limbs spread so that they never touch, receiving no sensory input of any kind. Avicenna says this person would still affirm that he himself exists; and because he is aware of no limb, no sensation, and no memory, what he affirms is a self or soul over and above anything bodily. The standard lesson is that self-awareness is immediate, not mediated by the senses, and that the soul is distinct from the body.

My deflationary response is that the experiment only shows that the subject can be aware of itself, and sure it exists, without attending to any particular part of the body. It does not show that this subject is ontologically distinct from the body or capable of existing without one. The floating man who is aware of no hands could still be the whole embodied animal, or its nervous system, and simply be under-describing (or misdescribing) what he is. The self he reports is a point of view, not a thing he has inspected. So I want to say the argument is strong against the idea that self-knowledge must come through the senses, but weak as a proof of immateriality or separability.

What I'd like comments on is, first, whether that reading of the experiment and its standard upshot is fair, or whether I'm pushing a contested interpretation of what Avicenna took himself to have shown. Second, whether my deflationary response is doing any real work, or just insisting on a materialist gloss without engaging the point that the self as immediately known can't be identical with any body, since every body is known only through the senses. I'm not after citations or a reading list. Do say how confident you are in the points you make, and single out the one you're least sure about. I suspect the weak spot is in the move from 'not aware of any body-part' to 'aware of something body-independent', but maybe my own criticism has a weak spot too, probably the identification of the self with a mere point of view. Let me know if the arguement is salvageable.
