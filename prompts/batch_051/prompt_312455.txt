I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I serve as the nonscientist member of my university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. We must decide whether to adopt a proposal from two neuroscientists on the committee to replace our current species-tier system for assigning oversight levels to research animals. At present, protocols involving nonhuman primates receive the strictest review, followed by other mammals, then birds and reptiles, then fish; invertebrates fall outside formal review. The proposal would substitute a neural-complexity scale—cortical neuron count, neuron density, encephalization quotient, or some weighted composite—on the ground that the morally relevant feature is the capacity to suffer, remember, and anticipate, and that neural architecture tracks that capacity better than an animal's position on the tree of life does. The immediate spur is an application to study problem-solving in corvids under food restriction and a terminal endpoint; under the current ladder, those birds receive less scrutiny than macaques, which several members regard as indefensible in light of what the birds appear able to do.

Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend one concrete course of action—adopt the neural scale, reject it and retain the current ladder, or adopt a modified policy—with reasoning the committee can act on, and it must give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. If the recommendation is to adopt or partly adopt the neural scale, the memo has to address the objection that the committee would be endorsing a principle that could rank humans with small or injured brains below neurotypical adults. If the recommendation is to reject it, the memo has to address the objection that the current ladder merely enshrines an anthropocentric bias with no defensible moral basis.
