I've been going back and forth for weeks with a friend about whether a "global" history can be more than a patchwork of parochial histories with better marketing, and they've built an argument that I find suspiciously strong. I keep trying to reject it for what feel like motivated reasons, but I can't locate the step where it overreaches. Their argument runs: (1) Every history is a finite selection from an effectively unbounded past, arranged into an arc, and any arc must treat some events, places, and processes as load-bearing while everything else becomes background. (2) The distinction between load-bearing and background cannot be read off the past itself; it is imposed by the historian's questions, audience, inherited categories, and chosen scale — by a standpoint, in short. (3) Therefore every history, global ones included, is centered in some standpoint; a history told from everywhere at once would have no background, but a story with no background has no foreground either, so it would not organize anything and would not be a narrative. (4) Eurocentrism is one such centering: modern history is written as if Europe supplies the engine and the periodization, while the rest of the world enters as response, deviation, or raw material. (5) It follows that the only workable demand for a non-Eurocentric global history is a demand for other standpoints, or a plurality of them — a matter of who is translated, taught, and written into the syllabus — not an epistemic demand that the past be told from nowhere, which (1)–(3) foreclose.

What bothers me is the conclusion he draws next: to call a Eurocentric history a distortion is, on his account, to compare it with an undistorted history from everywhere at once, which the argument already rules out — so "distortion" is a confused charge, and all that remains is the political question of whose standpoint gets airtime. But this seems to prove too much, because it would make any selection whatever immune to epistemic criticism once it is labeled a standpoint. I can tell a difference between centering a history of Roman debt on senators rather than on dockworkers and writing a history of industrialization in which Europe is an autonomous engine while its colonial and Atlantic inputs are treated as external. I want the second to be mistaken, not merely differently centered. Where does the argument go wrong? I have a nagging sense that something slips at (5), between standpoints being inevitable and standpoints being all there is to say, but I cannot state it cleanly — and if the answer is to reject (2) or (3), I want to know whether I have thereby committed myself to saying the past imposes its own history after all. Please give me the diagnosis: which premise or inference fails, why it looked plausible, and what each way of resisting the argument costs.
