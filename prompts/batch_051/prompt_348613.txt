I teach an adult Sunday school class at a nondenominational church — about 15 people, a mix of charismatic and cessationist-leaning backgrounds, with a 75-minute session — and we have been working through the debate between cessationism and continuationism about the so-called sign gifts of the New Testament: tongues, prophecy, words of knowledge, and healing. The class divides along the standard lines. Cessationists treat the sign gifts as tied to the apostles and the founding era of the church, so that what continues today is ordinary providence and the Spirit’s work through Scripture; continuationists treat the gifts as ongoing distributions of the Spirit and point to Acts 2 and 1 Corinthians 12–14. I want to avoid a proof-text battle and test both positions against a concrete case. Here is a working list of criteria for counting a present-day event as the continued operation of an apostolic sign gift rather than ordinary providence, a natural event, or a merely private experience: (1) the event is the same kind of thing the apostles and early church practiced, not merely an answer to prayer; (2) it operates through an identifiable human instrument in whom the gift appears to reside; (3) it exceeds what nature, chance, placebo, or expectation can plausibly explain; (4) it edifies the congregation and points toward Christ rather than drawing attention to the instrument or adding a new doctrinal claim; and (5) it is sufficiently public and checkable that the community can judge the claim rather than simply taking the claimant’s word for it.

The case I want you to test against these criteria: over the past year a small congregation has recorded three clusters of events. First, a woman with stage III colorectal cancer received prayer with laying on of hands, and her follow-up scans showed marked tumor regression; her oncologist notes that she started a new immunotherapy regimen two months earlier and describes the response as unusually rapid but within the known range. Second, a visiting speaker told a woman he did not know that she was carrying guilt over a child lost in the second trimester and that this was not her fault; she later confirmed the facts to the pastor, though the speaker had spoken with her sister earlier that week. Third, several members have spoken in tongues during worship, and the interpretations offered were all generic exhortations about trusting God in a dark season, with nothing specific enough that a stranger could not have produced it. Taking the case as a whole, does it qualify as a continuation of the apostolic sign gifts in the New Testament sense? Work through the criteria one by one, flag where the criteria themselves underdetermine the verdict, and give your overall judgment. Also say which criterion is doing the most hidden work — where a cessationist and a continuationist would each need to amend or weight the list — and whether the case shows that the criteria need changing rather than merely that the case is hard.

Since I will use your answer to moderate the class, do not pretend to more certainty than the material allows. Finish by stating explicitly what would change your mind about the overall verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
