I am currently taking a seminar on Nietzsche’s Genealogy of Morals, and I keep returning to a passage from the Third Essay that seems to me to do a lot of work. The passage is from Friedrich Nietzsche, The Genealogy of Morals, Third Essay, section 1, in the 1913 Horace B. Samuel translation: “What is the meaning of ascetic ideals? In artists, nothing, or too much; in philosophers and scholars, a kind of ‘flair’ and instinct for the conditions most favourable to advanced intellectualism; in women, at best an additional seductive fascination, a little morbidezza on a fine piece of flesh, the angelhood of a fat, pretty animal; in physiological failures and whiners (in the majority of mortals), an attempt to pose as ‘too good’ for this world, a holy form of debauchery, their chief weapon in the battle with lingering pain and ennui; in priests, the actual priestly faith, their best engine of power, and also the supreme authority for power; in saints, finally a pretext for hibernation, their novissima gloriae cupido, their peace in nothingness (‘God’), their form of madness.”

I would like to test this passage against a borderline contemporary case. Imagine a secular movement, the Order of Clear Hours, whose members are not religious but practice strict silence for most of the day, sleep no more than five hours, eat only plain food at fixed times, abstain from alcohol, caffeine, and sexual activity, and describe their aim as “cognitive self-mastery.” They do not speak of sin, God, salvation, or moral duty, and they insist that their discipline is purely instrumental, a technique for removing distraction and achieving sustained attention. At the same time, they have a strong communal identity, a hierarchy of more and less advanced practitioners, and they distinguish themselves sharply from ordinary people, whom they call “sleepwalkers.”

Is the Order of Clear Hours an instance of what Nietzsche means by the ascetic ideal? Go through each of Nietzsche’s groups one by one and ask whether it fits. Where the fit is imperfect, say whether the case is genuinely outside his categories or whether the categories themselves need stretching. Then address whether the movement’s insistence on purely instrumental self-discipline should be taken at face value, or whether Nietzsche gives us reason to suspect a hidden evaluative commitment underneath it.
