I'm leading a discussion group on whether critical thinking is a moral value -- specifically whether doubt is a duty -- and I want to build the session around Shanley's film Doubt. The obvious reading is that Sister Aloysius is destroyed by her own certainty: she's sure Father Flynn molested a student, she has almost no evidence, yet she forces him out of the school, and in the final scene she breaks down and admits she's riddled with doubt. On that reading the film endorses the Clifford-style view that believing without sufficient evidence is plain wrong, and that doubt would have been the virtuous response. But the film witholds the key facts, so it also lets us read Aloysius as the only adult taking the danger seriously, and everyone else's insistence on more evidence as a kind of complicity. I want a reading of the film against the Clifford/James debate about whether there are momentous, forced choices where you're entitled to believe ahead of the evidence: where does the film push toward doubt-as-duty, where toward the Jamesian thought that suspending judgment is itself a costly act, and where does its ambiguity refuse to settle the argument? Point to specific scenes, and say what the film gets about doubt as a moral practice that the philosophy papers miss.
