You are an expert in Hegel's social philosophy and in the metaphysics of social statuses. Please think carefully, begin by brainstorming the different ways one might understand what it is for a status such as statehood or self-consciousness to be socially constituted rather than antecedently given, and only then settle on a considered answer.

I have been puzzling over an apparent inconsistency in the international practice of diplomatic recognition of states, and I would like you to reconstruct the theory of recognition that the practice itself presupposes. In their official statements, states overwhelmingly talk as if recognition were a matter of epistemic discovery: they say they have examined the facts and determined that the entity has a defined territory, a permanent population, an effective government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states, and they insist that their act merely acknowledges a statehood that already exists. But their conduct points the other way. Unrecognized entities are excluded from the United Nations and most international organizations, are often shut out of international financial institutions and courts, and generally cannot open formal embassies or conclude treaties on the same footing as recognized states; they materially govern territory and people, yet in international life they are treated as if the decisive thing about being a state had not happened until the established states say it has. The same states that call recognition a report often also insist that premature recognition is a hostile interference in another country's internal affairs, as if the act of recognizing could itself help manufacture a new state where none existed. International lawyers have names for the two poles, declaratory and constitutive recognition, but what interests me is the philosophy underneath.

My question is not which of these views is correct in international law, but what philosophical picture of the relation between being something and being recognized as that thing the practice as a whole commits its participants to, and whether that picture is coherent. Bring Hegel's account of recognition in the master-slave dialectic to bear. Hegel seems to hold both that self-consciousness only becomes what it is through recognition by another and that genuine recognition must come from an independent other who is already there to give it; recognition both creates the relevant standing and presupposes someone with standing to bestow it. Does diplomatic recognition instantiate a relation of just this two-sided kind, or does the practice secretly presuppose a more mind-independent conception of statuses, with Hegelian recognition as at most a rhetorical overlay? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and say what would have to change in it if the presupposed picture were replaced with the rival one. If you can, specify the test case that would most cleanly distinguish the two pictures.
