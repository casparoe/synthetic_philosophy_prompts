I cover science for a living, mostly longform pieces on animal behavior and the occasional book review, and I've been reading up on the history of animal psychology for a piece on how researchers decide what counts as evidence that an animal is "thinking." A popular book I picked up summarizes Morgan's canon—the Victorian-era rule from the psychologist C. Lloyd Morgan—like this, and something about it has been bugging me enough that I keep coming back too it:

"Morgan's canon says that when an animal does something that looks intelligent, you are required to explain it with the least sophisticated mental process that could possibly produce the behavior. If a dog learns to open a gate, you have to put it down to blind trial and error and cannot credit the dog with understanding how the gate works, because the less sophisticated explanation always wins. In practice this makes anthropomorphism unscientific, which is why a lot of modern animal-cognition research, with its talk of crow insight, monkey concepts, and ape theory of mind, is methodologically suspicious whenever it reaches for a rich mental explanation. At bottom the canon is a rule of evidence: the simplest explanation is the one most likely to be true."

I know enough from interviewing people who study dog and corvid cognition to feel like something in there is twisting the principle, especially the line about modern research being unscientific, but I can't say exactly where the summary stops being fair. Go sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful animal-cognition researcher or philosopher of science would accept, what they'd wince at, and what is just wrong. And does the claim about modern animal-cognition research actually follow from the accurate parts of the summary, or is that the place where the summary overreaches?
