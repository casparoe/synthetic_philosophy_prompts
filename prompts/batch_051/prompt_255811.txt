I am in the middle of reworking the normative-ethics portion of a course I teach, and I have discovered that I repeatedly invoke simplicity when comparing moral theories without being able to say exactly what role it is playing. The topic has become more than a syllabus problem: I am supposed to explain to students whether parsimony counts as a theoretical virtue in ethics, and why, and I do not want to teach a slogan I cannot defend.

Here are my considered reactions to a series of comparisons. First, when two theories generate exactly the same verdicts about the cases I care about, and one derives them from a single master principle while the other postulates three mutually independent principles, I find the one-principle theory clearly preferable, and I do not think this is a merely aesthetic preference. Second, when the one-principle theory gets a single serious case wrong—for instance, a maximizing consequentialist theory that requires me to punish an innocent person to prevent a destructive riot—I no longer care how simple it is; I prefer the pluralist theory that handles the case, even at the cost of added machinery. Parsimony, I seem to think, breaks ties after fit with my considered judgments has been secured, but it does not outweigh lost fit. Third, I do not count a theory as simple merely because it has one principle and a string of seven nested exception clauses; I would rather have a clean two-principle theory than that, because complexity can be hidden in the exceptions rather than in the principle count. Fourth, I distinguish between an additional principle that carries a substantial share of the explanatory load and one that exists only to mop up a single marginal case; a patch added solely to protect a theory from one counterexample feels like a defect. Fifth, however: I considered a pluralist theory that blocks a highly counterintuitive implication of a maximizing view by adding a constraint against treating a person merely as a means. That constraint was introduced precisely to handle the kind of case the simpler theory got wrong, and yet I do not experience it as a patch—I prefer the pluralist theory. I cannot say what separates this from the objectionable patch in the previous case. Finally, my demand for parsimony shifts when I turn to value theory rather than to principles of right: I am content to countenance a list of irreducibly distinct basic goods if each survives reflection, but I still reject a list that is open-ended in a promiscuous way, so simplicity has not disappeared; it has changed its form.

I would like a statement of the principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that best captures whatever is coherent in these verdicts about simplicity as a virtue in ethical theory. For each clause of the principle, identify which of the cases above motivates it. Then apply the principle to cases I have not yet settled: a theory with very few principles but a baroque procedure for deriving verdicts from them; an axiology that posits five basic goods standing in a well-explained structure versus one that posits two but needs extensive auxiliary assumptions to fit the data; and a single-principle theory that generates a uniform, indefinite regress of exception clauses. Finally, state explicitly which of my verdicts, if any, cannot be retained under any reasonably simple formulation, and what I would have to sacrifice to keep the rest.
