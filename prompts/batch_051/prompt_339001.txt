Lately I have been thinking about what some people in AI governance call pre-AGI bargains: agreements made now, while no one has yet built artificial general intelligence, about how the benefits, control, or transition costs of a future AGI should be distributed. The motivation is that once an AGI exists, the balance of power could shift quickly enough that any promises made earlier would become unenforceable, so the time to strike a deal is now, while the relevant parties still have roughly comparable leverage and something to lose. The difficulty is obvious: a promise whose fulfillment comes due only after one side has acquired overwhelming power may be worth very little, and there may be no third party capable of enforcing it. I keep returning to Thomas Hobbes when I think about this. Hobbes held that covenants made without a common power to enforce them are unreliable, because each party can reasonably fear that the other will defect when performance becomes inconvenient; he also held that some rights cannot be given up, and that a sovereign stands in a different relation to covenants than ordinary subjects stand to one another. I would like you to reconstruct, in your own words, how Hobbes would most likely have assessed pre-AGI bargains if the idea had been explained to him. Would he regard them as mere words, as rational but fragile arrangements, or as something whose bindingness depends on which parties are involved and on what enforcement mechanisms are already in place? Would he think there is a categorical difference between a bargain struck before the relevant enforcing power exists and an ordinary contract? Please do not turn the answer into a bibliography or a list of citations; I want a textually sensitive reconstruction of the position, not references. Please write in American English, and please say how confident you are in the overall prediction and single out the specific interpretive step you are least sure about.
