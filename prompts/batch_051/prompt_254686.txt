Suppose a country replaces its inheritance and gift taxes with the following scheme. Every resident has a lifetime register of gratuitous transfers received: inheritances, gifts above a small de minimis threshold, trust distributions, and below-market loans that are later forgiven. Each recorded transfer is taxed at a rate determined by two things. First, a progressive schedule applies to the recipient's cumulative lifetime receipts, so the more gratuitous wealth a person has already received, the higher the rate on further receipts. Second, the resulting tax bill is multiplied by a rank factor that increases with the lower of the giver's and the recipient's pre-transfer wealth percentiles. A transfer from the 95th percentile to the 90th percentile therefore carries a heavy surcharge, while a transfer from the 95th percentile to the 20th is nearly untaxed; transfers involving someone low in the distribution are cheap in both directions. The stated rationale is that equalizing transfers should be cheap but dynastic consolidation within the upper reaches should be expensive. Ranks are measured just before the transfer, using administrative records plus self-reported valuations for assets the state cannot see directly, with audits of a random sample of large transfers.

Walk through how this scheme would actually behave if enacted, rather than whether it is a good idea in principle. What would it incentivize among wealthy testators deciding how much to leave and to whom; among heirs who could receive early or late in life; among the merely comfortable; and among trustees, lawyers, and valuation professionals? Where is it gamed first: nominal transfers through relatively poor intermediaries who later pass the assets back; undervaluation of hard-to-value assets; splitting transfers across many recipients or many years to stay under thresholds; moving assets abroad; timing transfers before an expected rise in recorded rank; or some other channel? Which of these failure modes are fixable within the scheme, and which fixes would introduce new distortions? Then step back and ask whether the scheme's predictable failures indict its implementation or its underlying idea that inheritance taxation should track the relative positions of giver and recipient rather than only the size of the bequest. Finally, say which empirical claim or theoretical consideration would most change your overall assessment, and where your reasoning about the failure modes is least certain.
