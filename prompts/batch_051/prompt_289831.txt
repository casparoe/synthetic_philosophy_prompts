A situation keeps coming up in my own life, and an argument about it broke out between two colleagues, which I am hoping you can referee. I work from home, and for months I have ended each Sunday by resolving that the coming week I will write for two hours before checking anything online. Every Monday morning I check my messages instead, and by evening I am angry at myself for having done it again. When I described this over lunch, a friend who works in behavioral economics said it is not weakness of will at all; it is textbook hyperbolic discounting. On Sunday evening the costs of Monday's writing are still future and get discounted steeply enough that the resolution feels easy; on Monday morning those same costs are immediate and loom large, so the preference reverses. What I have is a time-inconsistent discount function, not a weak will, and the remedy is precommitment or changing my environment, not more self-reproach.

A second friend, trained in philosophy, pushed back: the discount pattern is just the mechanism of the akrasia, not an alternative to it. On Sunday evening I judge, all things considered, that writing before checking messages is the best plan. On Monday morning, even as I open the messages, I still hold that judgment — I can say "I should be writing right now." So at the very moment of choice I am acting against my own better judgment. Calling it a discount curve does not remove the conflict; it redescribes one and the same failure.

The economist answered: no, the point of hyperbolic discounting is that the reversal happens before the moment of choice, so Monday-me does not merely feel a stronger temptation — Monday-me genuinely judges that checking messages first is best. There is therefore no synchronic conflict between judgment and action, only a diachronic conflict between two stretches of the same person with two different better judgments. Akrasia requires one self to judge one thing best and do another at a single time, and you cannot find that here.

The philosopher answered: if that is right, then Sunday-me and Monday-me contradict each other about the very same future action, yet neither can ever be wrong on their own day, because whatever the present self judges is treated as authoritative. That is present-bias promoted to a theory. Worse, it makes akrasia disappear by definition: the man who swears at eight in the morning not to drink and then pours at eleven was never weak, only time-inconsistent. You have lost the contrast between the person who holds the line and the person who folds.

They went around like this for a while and eventually turned to me, but I have a stake in the answer and could not tell who was right about what.
