I am a novelist, and some years ago a defender of the cosmological argument from contingency tried to make the case with an image I have never quite shaken. He asked me to picture a manuscript copied from an earlier manuscript, itself copied from a still earlier one, and to suppose that the chain of copies went back forever. Every copy, he said, owes its wording to the manuscript before it; but if no manuscript in the chain is an original composition, then the content of the text — why these words stand in this order, and why there is a text at all — is never actually explained. The chain of contingent things, he continued, is like the chain of copies. The text of each manuscript plays the role of a contingent thing's existing as it does; the copyist's labour plays the role of one thing's producing the next; and the missing original manuscript plays the role of the necessary being in which the whole series must terminate. I suspect the analogy is carrying the argument rather than merely illustrating it. An author is an intentional agent, while the necessary being is not supposed to be a craftsman with a plan; an original manuscript is itself a contingent artifact, produced at a time and able to have been otherwise; and the explanation of a text's content is not obviously the same kind of thing as the explanation of a thing's existence. But I want the matter audited rather than deflected. Construct the correspondence in full — what maps to the author, the copyist, the book itself, the chain — and then identify where the analogy breaks. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the argument's conclusion from the ones that are merely incidental. Press especially hard on the move from each copy's being explained by its predecessor to the claim that an infinite chain leaves something unexplained, and on the further move from there being an original to that original's being a necessary being rather than simply the first contingent thing. Finally, say whether the conclusion that the series of contingent things requires a being that needs no explanation outside itself survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and propose a better analogy if one would serve the argument more honestly.
