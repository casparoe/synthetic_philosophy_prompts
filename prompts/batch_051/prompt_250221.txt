In my art history course we were taught a working definition of a forgery: a work is a forgery if (a) it is attributed to someone other than the person who actually made it, (b) it was made with the intention of deceiving viewers or buyers about that attribution, (c) it imitates the attributed artist's style or signature, and (d) the false attribution is the kind of fact that reasonably affects the work's value. I have been trying to apply this definition to a case that seems to escape it.

Suppose a painter's early masterpiece, the canvas that established her reputation, is destroyed in a studio fire. Thirty years later, working from photographs and her old preparatory studies, she repaints the picture with great care, matching the composition, brushwork, and even the fine cracks of the aged surface, and signs it with her own name. A gallery then sells the new canvas with a label giving the original's date and provenance, presenting it as the early work that survived; the painter knows about the label and remains silent.

Is this repainted canvas a forgery under the four-part definition? Work through each criterion and say honestly where the case falls. A classmate insists that it cannot be a forgery because the hand is the same; I am not so sure. Then consider whether the case reveals a gap in the definition itself — perhaps that a forgery can consist in a false history of the object rather than a false authorship — and explain whether the repainted canvas should be valued as the original, as less than the original, or as something else.
