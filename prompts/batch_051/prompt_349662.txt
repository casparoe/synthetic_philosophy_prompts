I've spent too long on rationalist blogs and forums, and lately I've been arguing with somebody about what the sunk cost fallacy actually says. The canonical example is the movie ticket: you pay $20, twenty minutes in you know the rest will be a bad time, and the standard answer is that the $20 is already gone and only future costs and benefits matter, so you should walk out. I'm not challenging the one-shot version. Here's the variant I can't get to the bottom of. A manager runs a long sequence of projects. At the start of each project she can greenlight it or not; after a first stage she gets a private signal about whether it will work out; then she chooses to kill it or carry it through a second stage. The board never sees the signal, only wether a project was killed, carried through and failed, or carried through and succeeded. By the time she chooses, carrying through has negative expected financial value for every signal — the only reason to keep going would be the effect on the board's posterior about whether she's a 'good judge' whose signals track the truth — and her future funding or promotion is an increasing function of that posterior. In the one-shot, private version the standard sunk cost analysis says kill. My question: in this repeated, observed version, should she sometimes carry through a project whose marginal expected value is negative? And if she should, has the sunk cost fallacy stopped being a fallacy, or is the sunk cost still doing no work while signal/reputation value does all the work? I'd like the response to run the one-shot analysis properly first, then give the cleanest signaling or game-theoretic analysis of the twist, and then mark which parts are settled among people who actually know this stuff and which parts are genuinely contested — please don't present a contested view as the consensus.
