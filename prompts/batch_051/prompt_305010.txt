Okay so I've got a homemade distinction I keep using in arguments and I want someone to actually beat on it, because it might just be me flattering myself. I call it 'front-loaded deathism' vs 'reactive deathism.' Front-loaded deathism is when someone holds, as a settled first-person policy, that mortality is good for them: death gives life its shape, an open-ended life would rot into boredom or triviality, the finitude is what makes choices meaningful. Reactive deathism is when someone only reaches for those arguments once radical life extension is actually being discussed — before that, the same person lives exactly like someone who wants more time: they take their meds, wear seatbelts, treat a cancer diagnosis at 50 as a tragedy and not as 'meaning.' My theory is that most of the deathism you see in longevity debates is reactive, and that the front-loaded stuff is usually a rationalization sitting on top of emotional self-defence: if you let yourself genuinely want centuries and then the tech never shows up, the letdown would be crushing, so you pre-emptively trash the prize. I've got a test I call the 'morning test.' You take someone who just said death is necessary for meaning, and you ask what they'd do if a doctor told them tomorrow morning they had six months left. If they say 'I'd make peace with it, and it would make every remaining day more vivid,' fine, that looks front-loaded. If they say 'I'd do anything for more time, I'm not ready,' I say their deathism is reactive. I'm aware of 'deathism' as a term from the life-extension crowd and of de Grey's 'pro-aging trance,' but I don't think either one is making exactly this cut. So tell me: is this a real distinction? Does it already exists under some other name I should know about? And where does it break — is the morning test just measuring fear of imminent death rather than anyone's view about mortality, and does that matter?
