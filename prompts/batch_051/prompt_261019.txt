I cover science and ideas, and I've spent the last few weeks hanging around consciousness labs and reading for a piece. I've found that my gut verdicts on particular cases don't line up with any simple line people draw, and I want help reverse-engineering the principle behind them, if there is one.

Here are my actual gut calls.

Blindsight. Someone with a damaged visual cortex says the left half of a screen is empty, but when forced to guess whether a line shown there is vertical or horizontal, they get four out of five right. I think there is no visual experience in that blind field at all. The information is being used, but there is nothing it is like for them to see it.

Anesthesia. Someone under a deep general anesthetic, not dreaming, reports afterward that there was no darkness, no time passing, no nothing. I believe them: phenomenal consciousness was genuinely off for that stretch.

Cortically blind confabulator. A man whose eyes work but whose visual cortex is damaged insists he can see; he walks into furniture while describing the room from memory. When he says he sees the window, I do not think there is a visual experience behind the report. He is confabulating.

Long highway drive. A friend suddenly realizes he has no memory of the last twenty minutes. I am sure he was not unconscious—there was somthing it is like to be him during that stretch—but I also doubt there was a rich, continuous movie playing in his head. I would call it thin experience, mostly unremembered.

AI report. A disembodied system gives a detailed, moving account of its own inner states and says it is conscious. My gut says that on its own is not enough.

Exact duplicate. If a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of me were made, I would insist it is conscious, and I do not just mean it would act conscious or be useful to describe as conscious. There would be something it is like to be it.

Zombie neighbor. I cannot take seriously the idea that the person next door has no inner life at all but behaves exactly like they do.

Fridge-light hunch. At the same time, I have a nagging hunch that the only reason my own inner life feels like a steady glow is that checking on it switches it on. My certainty that I have a continuous stream of experience might be an artifact of the checking. I do not know what to do with this hunch, because it seems in tension with the duplicate case and the zombie case.

Someone said I am drifting toward a cluster of views where phenomenal consciousness—the what-it-is-like part—is an illusion generated by information access and self-report, something in the neighborhood of illusionism. I do not trust that summary yet, because several of my verdicts above seem to resist it.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures these gut calls? If not, which verdicts have to go? Does the fridge-light hunch combined with the duplicate and zombie cases commit me to illusionism, to some weaker access-consciousness view, or to something else? Please state the principle precisely, tie each clause to the case that pulls for it, then tell me what it says about two cases I keep going back and forth on. One: a person roused from slow-wave sleep gives a correct answer to a tricky math problem and then insists she was not awake or aware; she says the answer just came out of her. Two: a locked-in patient, fully alert but unable to move for months, insists there was a continuous inner life throughout. And be honest about which of my gut calls your principle cannot keep.
