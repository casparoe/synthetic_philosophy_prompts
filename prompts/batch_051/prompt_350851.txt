Exam question:

Critically assess the following claim: when a researcher or journalist reproduces a short excerpt of propaganda in order to expose its persuasive technique, the reproduction should be classified primarily as counterspeech rather than amplification, and online platforms therefore have an obligation to distribute it at least as widely as they would distribute the original propaganda.

Student answer:

The claim is broadly correct. What separates counterspeech from propaganda is the speaker's critical aim, and this researcher has a critical aim. Because the excerpt appears inside a debunking commentary, the audience receives it in a critical frame, so its persuasive force is neutralized rather than reproduced. The classical free-speech tradition supports this conclusion: the remedy for bad speech is more speech, so down-ranking such critical republication would be paternalistic and inconsistent with liberal principles. Nor is there any real difference between a platform down-ranking content and the state censoring it, since both penalize a speaker because of what the speaker says. If a platform presents itself as a public forum, it should be bound by the state's duty to protect counterspeech. The only genuine limit is incitement: an excerpt that incites imminent lawless action may be restricted. Most propaganda, however, stops at abstract advocacy, so that exception will rarely apply. The reproduction should therefore receive the same protections, and the same distribution, as any counterspeech.
