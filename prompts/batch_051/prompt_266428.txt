I'm a software engineer, so I basically think in terms of logs, event sourcing, and when to buffer writes. I've been reading about this experiment in the science of consciousness called color phi, and I can't shake the feeling that it maps almost exactly onto a design choice I make constantly. I'm hoping someone can tell me whether this is a real structural parallel or just me pattern-matching my day job onto everything.

The setup as I understand it: a red dot flashes on the left, then a green dot flashes a short distance away. But people don't report two separate flashes — they report one dot that moves from left to right and switches from red to green roughly halfway along the path. The weird part is the timing: the color switch they report happens before the green dot actually appears. So it's as if the brain uses information from later to fill in the earlier experience. Fine. The part that got me is the two ways you can describe what "really" happened. Option one: you consciously saw something in real time, and then after the second dot arrived, that memory got edited — so you had an honest-to-god experience that got overwritten. Option two: consciousness is delayed a bit, like a tape-delay broadcast, so the experience was assembled after the second dot and you never had the unedited version. I think people call these Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions, which is great naming.

Now the software version. I build event-sourced systems where the event log is the source of truth. And there's a classic fork in the road when a projection or consumer has already seen something that turn out to be wrong. Either (a) you let the event go out as it happened and then append a correction event after the fact, so the log contains the original mistake followed by a fix; or (b) you hold events in a buffer for some window and only publish the corrected version, so the wrong event never existed in the log at all. That's... the Orwellian option and the Stalinesque option, near enough.

Here's my question. In software this is not a merely verbal distinction: it changes what auditors see, what can be replayed, what failure modes you get, and whether the wrong state was ever visible to a client. But in the color phi case, I've got a vague sense that the distinction might be empirically invisible — like there's no way for the person themselves, or anyone else, to tell which one is going on. That feels like the opposite of the software case, where the two are operationally different. Is the parallel deep, in the sense that the same underlying structure is at work and the differences are just about who can inspect the record? Or does it break exactly where it matters? Where does the mapping hold and where does it come apart — is it a genuine structural identity, a loose analogy, or just a confusion?
