I have been rereading Henry Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics, and I find myself wondering how far his arguments reach into a problem he never faced. Sidgwick says, in effect, that from the point of view of the universe the good of any one individual is of no more importance than the good of any other. He is explicit that this impartiality extends across time as well as space: just as my being here rather than there gives me no reason to count my own location as specially important, my being now rather than then gives me no reason to discount the happiness of people in other centuries. He treats temporal position as morally arbitrary in much the way that spatial position is.

What I would like you to do is predict, with careful reasoning, how Sidgwick would have come down on the modern debate about discounting future welfare in climate policy. Would he reject a pure rate of time preference as a violation of universal impartiality? Would he accept some practical discounting for uncertainty about the future, or for the possibility that future generations will be richer and better able to cope? And would he think that the non-identity problem—the claim that climate policy changes which particular people will exist—undermines the idea that we wrong future people? I am not looking for a biography of Sidgwick or a survey of his views; I want a reasoned extrapolation from his central ethical commitments to a debate he never directly addressed. Please write this as continuous prose, in the manner of a thoughtful letter or email, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
