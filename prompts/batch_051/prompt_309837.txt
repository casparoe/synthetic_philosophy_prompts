I work as an analyst for a state environmental agency, and my job is to review the computer models consultants send us before we use them to set pollution limits or decide where to spend flood-control money. The consultants always attach a list of assumptions, and alot of them are obviously false — fish all behave the same, the river is a smooth channel, people respond to price signals instantly, that kind of thing. My office is split down the middle. Half my coworkers say you can't use a model built on assumptions like that, period; the other half say every model has false assumptions and what matters is whether the outputs match the data. I once read an argument by an economist named Friedman that the realism of the assumptions doesn't matter at all, only whether the model predicts well. Sometimes that sounds right to me, but other times it sounds like a blank check to ignore any absurdity.

I get two or three of these models a month, and once we use one in a permit or a spending plan its hard to walk back. I'm not a modeler — I took stats in college but that's it — and my supervisor signs off on my memo without reading the equations, so the reasoning has to stand on its own. Write me a procedure my team could actually run on an incoming model. It should be a checklist or a decision tree in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure says reject, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values or risk tolerance rather than facts. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say what the procedure cannot decide.

Then run the procedure on three examples so I can see it working: (1) a fisheries model that assumes every fish in a stock is identical, (2) a flood model that assumes the river channel never changes shape, and (3) an economic-impact model that assumes people are perfectly rational and have full information. I want at least one case where the procedure tells us to accept a model we'd instinctively hate, or reject one we'd instinctively like. And please write the whole answer in American English.
