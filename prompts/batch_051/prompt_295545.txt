I am a retired Quaternary geologist, and I have been asked to give the eulogy at the funeral of my closest scientific colleague — a man who spent forty years dating glacial deposits and reconstructing past climates, and who was also, quietly and unshakably, a Catholic. I am not a believer. The room will contain two groups: his parish and his family, for whom the religious dimension of the day matters more than anything else, and our old colleagues from the field, many of whom are plainly skeptical and will hear any presentation of faith as a scientific hypothesis as a kind of category mistake.

I have lately been reading Wittgenstein on religious belief, and I would like the eulogy to be structured by his insight, as I understand it, that a religious conviction is not a provisional empirical conclusion that the evidence merely happens to favor. It is a different kind of commitment: not a hypothesis about one more fact in the world, but something more like a framework through which a whole life is lived. I do not want the speech to claim that his geology and his faith were two compatible theories about the same domain, because that already concedes the wrong picture. Nor do I want it to reduce his faith to a source of comfort or a moral attitude, which would be its own kind of condescension.

Please write the eulogy so that it can be delivered in about five minutes. Every sentence must be one I could sincerely say as a nonbeliever: I should not have to pretend to share his faith, but I also should not be allowed to turn it into something smaller than it was.
