I am a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I have been trying to use the units of selection debate to read Ursula K. Le Guin's short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas," but I worry the lens is doing too much work and I want a careful critic to tell me where it holds and where it distorts. In selectionist terms, the city's arrangement is easy to state: a single child is kept in extreme, ongoing misery behind a locked door, and the continued prosperity and general happiness of Omelas is said to depend on that child's condition; every citizen eventually learns this, most accept it, and a few simply leave. Read one way, the arrangement looks like a group-level adaptation carried at an individual-level cost—the city flourishes as a unit precisely because one of its members is made to suffer, the citizens who stay look like cooperators stabilizing a group strategy, and the ones who walk away look like defectors who neither reap group benefits nor bring about reform. Read another way, the gene's-eye view should make us suspicious: it tells us to identify the lower-level replicator whose interests the arrangement actually serves, and to check whether the city is a real vehicle of selection or just an environment in which individuals pursue their own strategies. The fact that the bargain is culturally transmitted to children at a certain age rather than biologically inherited is, I think, either a decisive disanalogy or a clue about the difference between genetic and cultural units of selection.

I want a reading that presses this in both directions. Where does the story genuinely illuminate the debate—for example, about what keeps a costly group-level trait from collapsing under free-riding, about whether the "group" is a real unit or a shorthand for correlated individual interests, and about how to individuate the beneficiaries of a costly trait? And where does the story resist or mislead: Omelas is not a reproducing population, the child's misery is stipulated as a condition of the city's flourishing rather than produced by any selective process, and the ones who walk away are not advancing a competing group strategy but only withdrawing. What does the story add to the units of selection debate that formal models and biological examples do not, and is it after all a fair test of the claim that groups, rather than genes or individuals, can be units of selection?
