Large user-generated-content platforms operate a system of escalating sanctions whose structure would be unrecognizable if transplanted into any court of law. A channel that repeatedly posts harassment may first be demonetized, then have individual videos removed, and only later, with no stated term and no path to reinstatement, have the entire account deleted, its archive and audience included; a channel that posts a single slur may have the account deleted immediately. Two accounts may carry the same sentence, and the one with the larger audience is removed while the smaller one remains visible. A politician's account may be suspended for twelve hours or given a warning label while an otherwise obscure account that makes the same claim is removed permanently. Throughout, the platform's rationale is that it applies neutral rules to protect its users, not that it judges the truth of what anyone says or the worth of anyone's character.

Reconstruct the implicit theory — of punishment, of speech, or of what a platform is — that would make this practice coherent. Is deplatforming best understood as punishment for an utterance, as a safety measure aimed at an audience, as a refusal to amplify rather than a refusal to host, or as something else again? Does the practice presuppose that the same utterance issued by a person with a large following is a different kind of act, or merely a more damaging one? Does it presuppose that proportionality, second chances, and the passage of time have no application to platform sanctions, or that culpability attaches to the account rather than to the act? Distinguish the commitments the practice strictly presupposes from those it merely fits well with, identify the points at which rival theories would license the identical platform behavior, and say what the practice would have to change if one of its central presuppositions were rejected.
