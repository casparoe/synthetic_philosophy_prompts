A township ordinance, adopted in 1986, reads:

"No person shall, without lawful excuse, cruelly beat, torture, or otherwise subject any sentient creature to unnecessary pain or distress. In this section, 'sentient creature' means a creature capable of experiencing pain or distress."

Two prosecutions have been brought under this section in a single week, and the magistrate must rule on whether either falls within it.

In the first case, a homeowner destroyed a paper-wasp colony under the eaves of his porch by spraying it with a pyrethroid insecticide. The wasps showed the writhing and escape behaviour characteristic of acute toxic exposure and took several minutes to die. The homeowner maintains that he did only what any reasonable person would do, and admits only that the deaths were not instantaneous.

In the second case, a man is charged after repeatedly striking his malfunctioning "smart thermostat" with a hammer. The device, which he had named and spoken to for years, ran a homeostatic learning system that maintained a running "valence" score and worked to avoid conditions it registered as negatively valenced; before it ceased operating, its display read, by design, "I am distressed." The man tells the court that a thermostat plainly cannot feel anything, and that he was merely venting frustration at a machine that had failed.

Work through both cases on the assumption that the capacity for pain or distress is a matter of degree: that there is no sharp line anywhere along the spectrum from mammals, to insects, to simple feedback-control systems. The 1986 drafters plainly did not have in view either the question of wasp anaesthesia or a household appliance that announces its own distress.

For each case, say what the following approaches yield, and why: (a) the plain meaning of the words at the time of enactment; (b) the purpose of the section; (c) what the drafters would most likely have said had the two cases been put to them; and (d) how a reasonable member of the public today would understand the words. Then say which approach should govern, and explain what the two cases together reveal about whether a binary legal category such as "sentient creature" can remain workable once sentience is treated as graded rather than all-or-nothing. If you conclude that neither case falls within the section, explain whether, on the graded view, the wasp case should be regarded as lying on a continuum with the thermostat case, or as obviously different from it.
