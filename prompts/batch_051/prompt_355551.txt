Suppose a country replaced the earnings-related portion of its social insurance with the following scheme. Every adult contributes a flat percentage of income to a mutual compensation fund, with no risk-rating of contributions. Anyone may file a claim for a misfortune, and a standing three-person tribunal classifies each loss as brute luck or option luck under this published test: a loss counts as option luck if the claimant voluntarily accepted a known risk or failed to buy reasonably available insurance against it; otherwise it counts as brute luck. Brute-luck losses are compensated in full at the tribunal's estimate of their value, and option-luck losses receive nothing, with the tribunal's decisions published as precedents in an ordinary prose digest. Treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral position. What would it incentivize among citizens, private insurers, employers, doctors, and the tribunal members themselves, and how would each learn to game the brute/option distinction? How would the published precedents alter the distinction over time, where would the scheme fail or produce second-order effects, and could the failure modes be repaired without quietly abandoning the underlying distinction? Write your answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
