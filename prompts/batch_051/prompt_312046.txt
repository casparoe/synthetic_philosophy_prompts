You are a philosopher of science who works on realism, reductionism, and levels of explanation. Think through the question in stages: first restate the phenomenon in your own words, then test candidate labels against it, and only then give your verdict.

I keep noticing a configuration across several fields, and I do not have a name for it. A chess engine can be predicted remarkably well by saying that it wants to protect its queen or is worried about its pawn structure, even though no one believes the machine has wants or worries. A heart is most economically described as a pump whose function is to circulate blood, even though no molecule in the heart has a function. And a financial bubble can be discussed as something that inflates and eventually pops, supporting real forecasts, even though no bubble is to be found in any individual transaction or ledger entry. In each case, something does genuine predictive and explanatory work at a coarse level of description, but seems to disappear when one descends to the physical constituents.

I have seen two characteristic mistakes about such cases. Some people conclude that, because the thing cannot be found among the constituents, it is merely a metaphor and not real at all. Others go the opposite way and speak as if the queen-protecting, the pumping function, or the bubble were an additional ingredient inside the system, alongside the circuits, molecules, or transactions. Both responses strike me as missing the same point, and I would like to understand exactly what that point is.

Is there an established name for the position that a thing of this sort can be real and genuinely explanatory even though it is invisible, or at least computationally unavailable, at the finest level of description? Is there a name for the first mistake, and a different name for the second? If the established vocabulary fits only approximately, please say so, mark exactly where it fits and where it strains, and give the edges of the phenomenon with boundary cases rather than a bare label.
