I am a senior editor at a general-audience magazine of ideas. Roughly once a month I receive a submission that invokes Gödel's incompleteness theorems to support a conclusion outside mathematics: about artificial intelligence, legal systems, physics, organizations, or the human mind. These pieces are often well written and popular with readers, but we have had to publish corrections when we let one through. Our desk has philosophical training but only an introductory acquaintance with mathematical logic, and we usually have about two working days to decide whether a claim may be published as stated, must be revised, or has to be killed.

I want a reusable editorial decision procedure, not a one-off ruling: a checklist or decision tree my desk could run on any submission that leans on Gödel's theorems. Please build it around what the incompleteness theorems genuinely show and what they do not show, translated into questions a non-specialist editor can actually apply. The procedure should specify, in order: the questions to ask; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where it stops with a clear no and what revision would clear the obstacle; where it allows publication with a disclaimer; and where it must hand the decision to a person with more logic training because the checklist cannot settle the matter. Mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated.

Then run the procedure on these three submissions:

1. An essay arguing that Gödel's first incompleteness theorem shows that the U.S. legal system must contain legal questions that cannot be decided within the system, because the system can reason about its own rules, so the law is necessarily incomplete.
2. An essay arguing that Gödel's theorem demonstrates that no artificial intelligence, understood as a finite rule-following system, can match human mathematical insight, because a human can see the truth of the Gödel sentence of any such system.
3. An essay that explicitly treats Gödel's results as an analogy: just as a formal system rich enough to talk about itself generates statements it cannot settle, a large bureaucracy's procedures will generate cases its procedures cannot settle, and this author disclaims any claim to have proven the latter.

For each case, say exactly what the procedure would do and why. Be sure to include at least one case in which the procedure would block a submission I would otherwise have been inclined to publish, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do — for example, whether it can assess the value of an explicitly analogical use, the literary merit of the piece, or the underlying philosophical question the author is really trying to get at.
