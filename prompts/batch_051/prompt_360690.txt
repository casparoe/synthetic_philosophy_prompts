I got appointed to my university's budget committee this year, which sounded boring but has turned out to be the most philosophically confusing thing I've done in a while. We're deciding whether to close the campus printmaking studio: it costs about four hundred thousand a year, serves maybe thirty majors, and the administration says the money would go to expanding counseling services, which everyone agrees are at capacity. The studio has existed for sixty years, and the faculty and students in it are basically despondent. They keep saying they would lose a community and a home, and that losing it would hurt far more than the counseling expansion would add. A friend who does behavioral econ says I should discount all of that talk: it's textbook loss aversion and the endowment effect, people overvalue what they already have, and a budget committee shouldn't let a known cognitive bias run the university. But I keep think that some attachments to long-standing institutions are legitimate — if a couple prefers to stay together partly because of their shared history, we don't call that a bias. So I need to figure out where the line actually is.

Please draft the short written opinion I'd circulate to the committee before our vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action (close, keep, or something in between like a phased reduction), and it should offer a general test for when resistance to losing something counts as a legitimate preference we should respect and when it's just an endowment-effect bias we should correct for. I want the memo to take the strongest argument on the losing side seriously rather than strawmanning it, and I'd like it written as continuous prose, like a letter to colleagues — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
