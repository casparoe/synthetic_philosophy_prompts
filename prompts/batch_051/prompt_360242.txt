I moved to this city for a dev job a few years ago, and the one local issue I keep getting pulled into is housing: every time someone proposes a mid-rise apartment building near me, a handful of long-time homeowners basically strangle it through planning-board meetings and noise complaints, and the renters who'd actually live in the new place never show up to fight for it. I was arguing with a friend about whether you could design the approval process so blocking new housing works like an expensive action instead of a default option. So here's the mechanism I've been turning over, and I'd like you to take it apart.

The rule: any project over 15 units can be forced into a binding neighborhood referendum if, say, 50 residents within a half-mile radius sign a petition. The referendum kills the project only if it passes with 60% of the votes cast in the same radius, but at the same time it automatically approves a "housing replacement levy." That levy is set per blocked unit (call it one-tenth of city median rent per unit per year, for ten years) and is assessed on every owner-occupier and landlord inside the radius in proportion to the assessed value of the residential property they hold there. The money doesnt stay in the neighborhood: it goes into a city fund that builds public housing elsewhere in the city, with sites chosen by the city council. So in principle a neighborhood can say no, but it has to buy back the housing it just prevented, and a neighborhood that blocks a lot ends up funding roughly equivalent units somewhere poorer.

I'm less interested in whether this is a good idea in the abstract than in how it would actually behave in a real city. Who actually ends up voting to block once the cost is on their own property-tax bill, and who stays home? How do developers game it, by tinkering with unit counts, phasing projects to dodge the 15-unit threshold, or nudging referendums they expect to fail so the levy scare kills future opposition? What happens to the city's own incentives, since the housing fund now collects money whenever a project dies: could the council start quietly preferring rejection revenue over approvals, and would that be fixed by sending the levy money to a different layer of government or into a trust? The obvious failure modes I can already see are rich neighborhoods treating the levy as a cheap exclusion fee, landlords passing the cost through to renters so renters pay for the veto that kept them out, and the "replacement" housing getting built somewhere with no political power. I want to know which of those actually dominates, plus any failure mode I'm missing, and for each one whether it can be patched without collapsing straight back into the current default-veto system.

The part I'm least sure about is which failure mode dominates, not whether the whole thing is workable. So wherever you land, tell me straight-up what would change your mind about that ranking, and which step of your reasoning you'd most expect to be wrong once the mechanism met an actual city council.
