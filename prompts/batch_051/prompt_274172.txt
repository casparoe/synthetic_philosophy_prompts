Last night I spent a long while arguing with a friend about sortition — specifically, whether a legislative chamber chosen by lot would be more democratic than an elected one. I came away still inclined toward the lottocratic side, but uneasy about one move I kept making. I repeatedly defended the idea by saying that a body chosen by lot is representative of the citizenry in roughly the way a random sample in survey research is representative of a population, and that the standard machinery of sampling therefore transfers to the political case. My friend replied that this is precisely the confusion: a sample is drawn in order to estimate facts about a population, whereas an allotted legislature exists to act, and its members are meant to stand in for other people rather than to estimate them. I could not tell how deep my analogy really went, and I want that sorted out.

The mapping I had in mind runs as follows. The citizenry, or the body of those with a claim to be represented, corresponds to the population. The list from which names are actually drawn corresponds to the sampling frame; eligible people missing from that list correspond to undercoverage. People drawn who refuse to take up a seat correspond to nonresponse, and where the pool is filled by volunteering, the act of putting oneself forward corresponds to self-selection. The practice of reserving seats by geography, sex, age, or other strata corresponds to stratification or quota design. The chance ways a particular allotted body diverges from the larger population correspond to sampling error, while systematic divergences produced by who opts out or is excluded correspond to bias. Finally, the claim that an allotted body represents the public corresponds, on my telling, to the claim that a sample represents its population.

I want to know whether this is a real identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion. If it is real, what transfers from survey methodology into the assessment of lottocratic institutions? For instance, in sampling, a larger sample shrinks sampling error but does nothing for nonresponse bias; would that mean a larger allotted chamber improves chance representativeness while doing nothing about the legitimacy problem created by people who decline to serve? And where does the analogy break, if it breaks — in the concept of representation itself, in the difference between estimating a property and making a decision, or somewhere earlier? Please name something the parallel would predict that turns out false, and keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on sortition or on sampling; where the statistical or philosophical points are contested, do not present one side as the consensus. I would rather have the breaks in the analogy laid out plainly than have either side of it defended.
