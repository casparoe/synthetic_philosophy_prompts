I have a rule I follow in arguments and I want to know whether it's defensible. Whenever someone's argument for doing something, or for treating some issue as enormously important, bottoms out in a literally infinite expected value — the St. Petersburg coin flip, Pascal's wager, arguments about infinite populations or an infinite future — I treat the whole argument as broken and refuse to act on it. I dont even try to fix it by saying my utility is bounded or I should look at finite approximations or whatever; I just stop listening. My friend says this is just an ad hoc bias: the infinity is usually an idealization, and the right move is to do the bounded version, not throw the argument away. Last week he caught me doing this with an effective altruism style pitch about the infinite future, and I couldn't say why my rule was better than just not wanting to do the math. So is my policy defensible? When, if ever, should a literally infinite expected value actually move someone, and is there a better version of the rule I should adopt instead — something like always truncate infnities, or only act on finite approximations?
