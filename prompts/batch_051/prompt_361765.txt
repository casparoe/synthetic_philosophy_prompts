I'm a PhD student working in moral epistemology. I keep noticing an asymmetry in our practice that we rarely defend. If a physicist tells me that a certain particle cannot decay, I am behaving exactly as I should in believing her; if a moral philosopher tells me that a familiar practice is wrong, and I form my view on that basis alone, most of us think something has gone wrong—I have outsourced my moral judgment or failed to think for myself. Yet the two cases look structurally parallel: in each I am trusting a more reliable agent about a difficult subject in which I am not an expert. I want the strongest candidate principle that could justify this asymmetry, and an honest verdict on whether it survives. The obvious candidates are that moral knowledge cannot be transmitted by testimony; that what matters in the moral case is understanding rather than mere true belief; that moral judgment requires an affective or motivational component that testimony cannot supply; or that the asymmetry is only a confusion. Please test whichever principle you favor against cases on both sides—for instance, a parent telling a child that cruelty is wrong, a person who changes their mind after hearing victims testify about an injustice, and a doctor who defers to a clinical ethicist about end-of-life care—and then say whether we should keep, revise, or abandon the asymmetry.
