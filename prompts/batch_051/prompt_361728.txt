Ok, here's a constitutional design question. A country is drafting a new constitution and the delegates are deadlocked over whether unelected judges should be able to override the legislature, so they settle on this compromise. The constitutional court can strike down a statute on fundamental-rights grounds. But the legislature can then pass a "reinstatement act" by a three-fifths vote in both houses. The act must (a) name the exact right being limited, (b) include official projections about what the law will actually acheive in measurable terms, and (c) include a sunset clause of no more than four years. The act goes to an independent statistical office, which must publish a review after two years comparing outcomes with the projections. If the office finds the law has underperformed by more than a set margin, the law lapses immediately unless the legislature passes another three-fifths vote within sixty days. The constitutional court cannot strike down the reinstatement act while it is in force, but it can still review the implementing regulations made under it.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than asking whether its a good compromise in the abstract. What does it incentivise in the legislature when they draft statutes and projections, in the court when it writes rulings, in litigants choosing cases, and in the statistical office? Where does it get gamed first, and are any of the failure modes fixable without gutting the whole thing? And for the response, write in American English.
