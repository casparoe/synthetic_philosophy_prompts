I got into an argument with a friend about whether legal proof is really about probability, and it kept going in circles, so I'd like a careful worked-through treatment. Take People v. Collins, the California case from 1968 (the crime itself was a few years earlier, in LA). An elderly woman was robbed on the street, and witnesses described a white woman with blonde hair in a ponytail and a Black man with a beard and mustache driving off in a yellow car. Malcolm and Janet Collins matched the description pretty well. At trial, a math instructor testified that if you multiplied estimated base rates for each feature — yellow car, beard, mustache, ponytail, interracial couple, and so on — you got something like one chance in twelve million that a random couple in Los Angeles would fit all of them. The jury convicted, and the California Supreme Court reversed, mostly on the familiar grounds that the frequencies were made up and the multiplication treated correlated features as if they were independent.

But I want to look at the case through the naked statistical evidence lense, not through the product-rule stuff. Assume the prosecution had done the statistics correctly: the base rates were defensible, the dependencies handled, and the number really did mean what it claimed. Would a conviction on that evidence alone still be problematic, and if so why? Map Collins onto the blue bus and the gatecrasher cases as carefully as you can — what is the bus company here, what is the reference class, where does the one-in-twelve-million number actually live. Then reverse the direction: what does Collins, treated as a naked-statistical-evidence case, do to the standard explanations for why bare statistics are insufficient for legal proof — that proof must be individualized, that a base rate supports no causal story, that the reference class is arbitrary, that a procedure which convicts on population frequencies would knowingly convict innocent people? Does the case vindicate one of those explanations, or does it suggest the real problem in Collins was never about individuation at all, just about jurors being bad at math? I care less about doctrine than about what the episode reveals about the difference between proof and betting odds.
