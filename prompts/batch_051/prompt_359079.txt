I'm putting together a prompt for a philosophy club session with adults, roughly a dozen people, meeting for ninety minutes. We've been reading around the idea of purity and sanctity as moral values—so Haidt's moral foundations work, some Mary Douglas, and the question of whether disgust and sacredness should be admitted into ethics or are just noise from our evolutionary past. I want an activity that gets the group to feel, not just discuss, how the boundary between pure and impure gets built socially, and how violations of that boundary can feel real and demanding even when nobody can give a non-circular justification for them.

In the first part of The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life, Durkheim says religion divides the world into two domains, the sacred and the profane. He writes:

The two classes are not merely distinct from one another, they are opposed to each other. [...] It is not merely that they are placed at different points in the hierarchy of things; they are separated by a kind of contrary relation. The sacred thing is par excellence that which the profane should not touch, and cannot touch with impunity.

I want the activity to make people experience that "cannot touch with impunity" force directly, before we name it. Design a game or structured role-play, say forty minutes of activity and then a debrief, where the group ends up enforcing a purity boundary that they themselves invented, on the spot, and where the rule against touching seems to acquire a weight that feels out of proportion to any instrumental rationale. The boundary should not be about hygiene or safety if possible; I want it to be about something arbitrary, like a mark on a card or a side of the room, so that afterward we can ask: why did it start to feel wrong to cross it? Give me concrete rules, setup, materials, what the facilitator does and doesn't say, what will predictably go wrong, and how the debrief should link specific moments in the activity back to Durkheim's claim and to the broader question of whether sacredness belongs in ethics. The tricky part: I don't want participants to know they're being set up to manufacture a purity rule, because then they'll self-consciously perform it. It should emerge from the game mechanics or the roles as a stable equilibrium.
