Some friends and I have been going back and forth about epiphenomenalism, and I've arrived at an argument that seems to show the position saws off the branch it sits on. I can't find the exit, but I also don't believe the conclusion, which makes me suspect something has gone wrong. Here is the version I keep writing out:

(1) Phenomenal states — the hurtfulness of pain, the reddishness of seeing red — are real, distinct from their physical correlates, caused by those correlates, and causally inert: they never help produce behaviour, speech, or further mental states.

(2) If such a state is distinct from and causally inert with respect to its physical correlate, then a world physically exactly like this one but with the phenomenal state absent is genuinely possible.

(3) A belief about a contingent fact counts as knowledge only if it is counterfactually sensitive to the fact it represents: had the fact not obtained while the belief-forming process remained fixed, the belief would not have formed.

(4) Take my belief that I am in pain. In the possible world from (2), the physical process that normally produces that belief runs exactly as it does here, so I would still believe I was in pain although the pain was absent. By (3), my belief is not knowledge.

(5) The same problem attaches to the judgments that usually support epiphenomenalism in the first place — for instance, that there is something it is like to feel pain, that this something could be absent while the physical facts stayed the same, and that no amount of third-person description fully captures it. Each is a judgment about phenomenal states. By (2) and (3), each is produced by physical processes while the phenomenal facts themselves stand idle, so the epiphenomenalist's own supporting evidence is not knowledge.

(6) A view whose characteristic supporting evidence is rendered unknowable by the view itself is rationally self-undermining: accepting it severs the evidential connection between the person and the doctrine.

(7) Therefore, if epiphenomenalism is true, no one can rationally accept it; and if it is false, no one should. Either way, the position undercuts itself.

I want to know which step, or which transition, you would reject, and what rejecting it commits one to. I have a prior that (3) is roughly right, because I rely on something like it to say that a lookup table or a thermometer does not know in the relevant sense; but I also do not want to deny that I know I am in pain when I am. Can the epiphenomenalist keep both causal inertness and first-person knowledge, or does the argument show that epiphenomenalism forces a revision in epistemology, and if so, how costly a revision? Please name the step or inference, explain why it looked safe, and price the main alternatives. Also say how confident you are in your diagnosis, and identify the part of your answer you are least sure about.
