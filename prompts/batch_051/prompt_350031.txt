I have a standing rule for credences about propositions whose truth is settled by mathematics but unknown to me. When I cannot verify such a proposition myself, I assign it a credence of exactly one half and decline to change that number in response to other people's reports of proofs I cannot follow. I apply the rule uniformly: to a conjecture and its negation if I meet them separately, to conjunctions of several such conjectures, and even to statements that follow by a few minutes of reasoning from things I already accept, provided I have not actually performed that reasoning. My defense is that a finite reasoner cannot be required to see all entailments, and that testimony about a proof I cannot check is a report of someone else's computation rather than evidence about the theorem itself. Last month this mattered in practice: a collaborator invoked a bound that follows from Goldbach's conjecture, and because I held the conjecture at even odds I treated the bound as an open assumption in our design.

Is this policy defensible for a bounded agent who cannot compute the logical consequences of her beliefs in real time, or is it a confusion about what probability means in the first place? If it cannot be defended as stated, can it be repaired into something better — for instance, a default that respects the logical relations I do notice, plus a rule for when someone else's checked proof should move me?
