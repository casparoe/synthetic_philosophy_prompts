In March 2018, an Uber self-driving test vehicle operating in autonomous mode struck and killed a pedestrian in Tempe, Arizona. A human safety driver was in the driver's seat but had been watching a video, and the NTSB later found that the vehicle's built-in automatic emergency braking had been disabled and that Uber's testing program had serious safety-culture failures. Arizona prosecutors declined to hold Uber criminally responsible; the safety driver was charged with negligent homicide and ultimately pleaded guilty to endangerment. Analyze this episode through the lens of the responsibility-gap thesis from machine ethics: the claim that increasingly autonomous systems can produce situations in which no human or institution satisfies the ordinary conditions of moral responsibility, so that blame cannot be properly assigned to anyone. I want a disciplined mapping rather than the standard hand-wringing. Identify the candidate responsible agents—the safety driver, the engineers who configured the system, the managers who set testing policy, the company, the regulators—and run each through explicit criteria such as control, foresight, and reasonable care; say which features of the episode the gap thesis explains and which it misdescribes; and argue explicitly for whether this is a genuine responsibility gap or rather ordinary many-hands diffusion of responsibility of a kind that long predates automation. Then use the legal aftermath, especially the charging of the human operator rather than the company, to say what the case shows about whether the law hides, fills, or simply relocates such gaps—and what one concrete revision to the responsibility-gap framework this episode should motivate.
