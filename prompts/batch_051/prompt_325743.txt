im in a metaphysics course this term and we've been reading Aristotle's sea battle chapter, and I keep coming back to an idea I want to get checked before I use it in my essay. I call it the difference between "hard" and "soft" truth for future statements. A future statement like "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is softly true if, as things actually turn out, it will be correct. It is hard true if there is something already in place now—like a battle plan so specific and locked in that the commanders could not reverse it—that makes it true before the fact. On my view, bivalence holds at the soft level: either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there won't, and one of those is already correct in the way a bet is already won or lost even before the race is run. But determinism only follows if the statement is hard true now. Aristotle, I think, is denying hard truth for future contingents while keeping soft truth. That seems to solve the sea battle problem without abandoning classical logic.

But I'm worried this is either already in the literature under some name I don't know, or that it's just a metaphor. Is "hard vs soft truth" basically the same as the Thin Red Line view, or as the difference between truth and present evidence? And where does it break? I can't tell if soft truth is doing any work beyond saying "the future will turn out one way," which is the very thing future truth was supposed to explain. Give me the strongest objections, and tell me whether my distinction is a real one or just a comforting way of restating the problem.
