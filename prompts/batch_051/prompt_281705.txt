In philosophy club we've been reading the part of A Theory of Justice where Rawls splits envy proper from resentment: envy is a vice, resentment is a moral response to injustice, but he also says envy can become excusable when an inequality is bad enough that it undercuts your self-respect and you could hardly be expected not to feel it. I started playing with this and built an arguement that now feels like it proves too much, and I cant find the wrong step.

(1) A feeling of hostility toward someone better off is either resentment, when it is a response to an injustice, or envy proper, when it is just hostility toward the other person's advantage.
(2) When the advantage is shaped by injustice, the feeling is justified if it counts as resentment, and at worst excusable if it counts as envy.
(3) In any real society, nearly every advantage is shaped by some injustice somewhere in its background: inheritance, unequal schooling, past discrimination, and so on.
(4) So in any real society, nearly every hostile feeling toward someone better off is either justified resentment or excusable envy.
(5) So almost no one is ever properly criticisable for envy, and the idea that envy is a vice is left with no cases to pick out.
(6) But that is absurd. People plainly can be criticised for envying a friend's slightly higher essay mark or a sibling being more charming, where no injustice worth the name explains the feeling.
(7) Therefore at least one of (1) to (4) has to go.

Which premise do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep (1), because the resentment/envy distinction is the part of Rawls I'd rather not lose.
