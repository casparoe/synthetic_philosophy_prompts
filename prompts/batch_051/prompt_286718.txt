I keep coming back to a concrete case in the philosophy of mathematics, and I want it handled as a case study rather than as an armchair argument. The case is the nineteenth-century arithmetization of the continuum: Dedekind's construction of the reals as cuts in the rationals, the Cauchy-sequence alternative, the shift from treating the line as a geometric given to treating the real numbers as whatever satisfies certain completeness axioms, and then much later the proof that the continuum hypothesis — every subset of the reals is either countable or has the cardinality of the continuum — is independent of the standard axioms of set theory. Analyze this episode through the lens of the question whether the real numbers were discovered or defined. Give me a disciplined mapping rather than a survey. Which moves and results in the episode count as evidence for the discovery picture, which count as evidence for the definition picture, and which are neutral between them? What exactly does it show that Dedekind cuts and Cauchy-sequence constructions yield isomorphic copies of one structure, or that the second-order completeness axioms characterize the reals uniquely up to isomorphism while the first-order theory admits nonstandard models? What is the right conclusion to draw from the independence of the continuum hypothesis, and what is the tempting but wrong conclusion? Then run the analysis in both directions: what does the episode establish or at least suggest about the discovered-versus-defined question, and does the lens confirm itself against the history, break down, or get revealed as too imprecise to do real explanatory work? In particular, say where the lens illuminates the episode and where it misdescribes it by imposing a dichotomy the participants' actual practice does not fit. I am especially interested in the places where the case is silent, and where the common deflationary move that the dispute is merely verbal would paper over a real issue.
