I am giving a paper next month at a university humanities center workshop, and I want to prepare for the question period rather than be ambushed by it. The paper defends a version of the Williams–Appiah line about moral revolutions, though I develop it in my own way. My thesis is that major moral transformations—I use the decline of dueling in Britain and the abolition of the British slave trade as my two case studies—are not best explained by the arrival of new or better moral arguments. The arguments against both practices had been publicly available for generations while the practices persisted; what eventually changed, I argue, was not the stock of available arguments but the social meaning of the practices, above all whether they conferred or withdrew honor. On my account, moral argument is not inert, but it does its work only when it attaches itself to a live shift in what a community admires or shames. Argument moves people through honor, not apart from it, and treating the argument itself as the engine of moral revolution mistakes a necessary accompaniment for a sufficient cause.

The audience is genuinely interdisciplinary and will not be impressed by cheap shots at rationalism. It includes two intellectual historians of the long eighteenth and nineteenth centuries—one who works on Atlantic slavery and abolition, the other on aristocratic and military codes of honor; a political scientist who models informal norms as coordination equilibria and is generally suspicious of explanations that put "culture" or "identity" in the driver's seat; and a moral philosopher who works on peer disagreement and on the relation between explaining a moral belief and justifying it. They are careful and collegial, but each of them will want to know whether my account survives contact with their discipline.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet for the discussion period. I want the objections ordered roughly by how likely and how damaging they will be, phrased the way these particular people would actually put them, with a two-minute reply to each and a note on where the reply is softest. Make sure the political scientist's objection about interests and the moral philosopher's objection about debunking are both there if they are the serious threats I think they are. Finally, include the question I should privately hope nobody asks, phrased in the voice of whichever audience member is most likely to find it, and explain why it is the dangerous one.
