The frame problem began as a technical difficulty in symbolic AI planning: how to represent everything that does not change when an action is performed, without writing down a separate axiom for each non-change. Philosophers later generalized it into a puzzle about how any finite cognitive system determines which of its standing representations are relevant to an update and which can safely be ignored. A later development appears to undercut the original setting: the systems now best at handling open-ended dynamic situations are large language models, which are not symbolic planners, do not keep an explicit database of world facts, and do not run inference over a set of axioms; their competence comes from predicting continuations of text. Does this development dissolve the frame problem, or merely relocate it? In answering, identify which premise of the classical problem is actually under pressure, and say whether a repaired version of the problem remains for learned, non-symbolic systems — for instance in their training, their evaluation, or their long-horizon reliability. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
