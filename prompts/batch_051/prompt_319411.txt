I'm preparing for a seminar on the ethics of resource allocation, and a colleague has pressed an argument on me that I cannot seem to escape. It concerns the familiar importance–tractability–neglectedness heuristic for comparing causes. He claims that, taken literally as a guide for individual donors, the heuristic predicts an allocation of resources that its own advocates would reject, and therefore proves too much. I find each step plausible, but the conclusion seems to imply that cause prioritisation is either self-defeating or not worth doing, and I suspect something has gone wrong somewhere.

Here is his argument. (1) The expected marginal value of a donation to a cause depends on how important the cause is, how tractable it is, and how neglected it is: other things equal, a cause is a better giving opportunity when comparatively little effort or funding is already directed at it, because the cheapest and most effective opportunities are still unclaimed. (2) So, for any donor deciding in isolation, the best available cause will ordinarily be one that is still fairly neglected. (3) If every donor follows this reasoning, a cause becomes less neglected as soon as it begins to attract significant funding, and its expected marginal value to the next donor falls accordingly. (4) In the resulting equilibrium, each additional donor faces a landscape in which the previously best causes no longer look especially neglected, and the rational response is to seek whichever serious cause is currently least funded; donors therefore spread themselves across causes roughly in inverse proportion to the funding those causes already receive, moving on as soon as others arrive. (5) But an allocation of that kind forgoes the returns to concentrated, coordinated effort: specialist labour markets, shared institutions, accumulated operational knowledge and infrastructure that only arise when many people deliberately work on the same problem. (6) Hence a community that all followed the importance–tractability–neglectedness heuristic would systematically under-invest in causes that are highly important and tractable once those causes had become well funded, and would disperse its efforts across whatever happened to be least fashionable at each moment. (7) So either the heuristic is not a complete decision procedure and must be embedded in some larger theory of collective allocation that it cannot itself supply, or the neglectedness component recommends a pattern of giving that the importance and tractability components should rule out. (8) Either way, the heuristic as usually presented cannot be the right tool for ranking causes.

I cannot tell whether the argument makes an unnoticed inference at some point, or whether it is valid and I should simply abandon the ambition of having a single heuristic rank causes. Before I settle on a reply, I would like someone to identify the precise step at which the argument fails, or, if it is valid, to say which premise I should abandon and what the repair would cost. I would most like to keep the idea that neglectedness genuinely raises the expected value of a donation, and I would least like to concede that we must all pile into the same few causes for the sake of coordination; but if the argument really does force that dilemma, I want to understand why. Please write your answer in British English.
