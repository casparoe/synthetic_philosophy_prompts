I want a working definition of a term I use with confidence in conversation but cannot operationalize: abandonment, as applied to software and other creative works. I don't mean the narrower legal category of the orphan work, where a rightsholder cannot be identified or located, though the two notions are clearly adjacent. I mean the practical judgment people make when they call a project dead, unmaintained, left for good, or fair game for someone else to revive.

Let me give you the cases I want the definition to get right. I have confident verdicts about only the first four. (1) A proprietary desktop application whose publisher went bankrupt two decades ago, whose copyright was never transferred, and which can no longer be legally purchased or legitimately obtained anywhere. That one is abandoned. (2) A freeware utility whose author posted a final-release notice saying they were done with the project and has made no change since. That is abandoned too. (3) A commercial game that its studio still sells, patches, and supports, but whose online servers have been shut down. That is clearly not abandoned. (4) A widely used open-source library with no commits in three years whose maintainer still answers issues and says the project is feature-complete and stable. Not abandoned either, I would say.

I cannot decide about the rest. (5) A public repository under a permissive license whose only maintainer died two years ago; nobody else has write access, and the code itself remains intact. (6) A polished web application that is still live and usable, whose owning company dissolved, and whose last deployed change was five years ago. (7) An out-of-print novel whose author is alive, reachable, and says they would license a reprint if a publisher made a serious offer, but no edition can currently be bought. (8) A property whose rights-holder insists every few years that a sequel or update is coming, has shipped nothing in fifteen years, and refuses offers to let others work on the property.

Propose a definition of abandonment, run it through all eight cases, and say where it delivers the verdict I already hold and where it forces a surprising verdict or no verdict at all. If the cases suggest that the word tracks several different things at once — an intention to return, ongoing maintenance, a reachable rights-holder, a practical path back to use — tell me which of those the definition ought to track when everyday use pulls in different directions, and revise the definition accordingly. I am after a working conceptual boundary, not legal advice about the underlying rights.
