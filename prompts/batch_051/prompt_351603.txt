I am a retired physical chemist who has only recently turned to serious reading in moral philosophy, and I have been turning over a question that sits awkwardly between my old scientific instincts and my new philosophical interests. Like many scientists of my generation, I watched the replication crisis unfold in social psychology with a mixture of recognition and unease, and it has made me wonder what becomes of philosophical arguments that rested on the older, shakier experimental literature.

The argument I have in mind is the situationist critique of virtue ethics. As I understand it, the critique runs roughly as follows. Virtue ethics assumes that ordinary people can cultivate stable traits of character—honesty, compassion, courage—that manifest themselves across very different circumstances. But decades of social-psychological experiments appeared to show that behavior tracks trivial features of the situation far more reliably than it tracks any such trait: whether one is in a hurry, whether one has just met with a small piece of good luck, whether an authority figure is standing nearby. From this the situationists concluded that the robust dispositions virtue ethics requires are rare or nonexistent, and that an ethics built around their cultivation rests on a false empirical psychology.

What unsettles me is that much of the empirical basis for this critique has since become suspect. The replication movement has cast serious doubt on a great deal of the small-sample, surprising, context-dependent social-psychology work of that period, and the situationist case drew heavily on exactly that genre of finding. I want to know whether the situationist critique of virtue ethics survives this development. Does the argument still go through, does it need repair, or is it effectively dead? In answering, I ask you to identify which premise of the critique actually does the work; to say whether the argument can be rebuilt on stronger evidence—large-scale experience-sampling, longitudinal studies, personality psychology—or instead survives mainly as a conceptual burden-of-proof argument once the dramatic experimental demonstrations lose credibility; and to distinguish what the replication crisis genuinely damages from what it leaves untouched. Please also say how confident you are in your assessment and single out the point about which you feel least sure.
