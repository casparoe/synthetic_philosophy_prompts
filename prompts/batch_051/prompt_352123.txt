I retired a couple of years ago after a career in geochemistry, and I've since fallen down a philosophy-of-history and aesthetics hole, mostly by audiobook. I never had any training in the humanities, so I'm probably missing an obvious way to frame this, but one question keeps snagging me: what do we owe real people, now dead, when novelists and scriptwriters dramatise them? I've been keeping seperate notes on cases where I had a strong reaction, and I can't tell whether there's a principle underneath or just personal squeamishness.

Here are my verdicts. A biopic of a poet who died eight years ago invents a brief affair with a nurse in his last year, purely to give the story a shape; his widow and grown children say it's false and they're hurt by it — not on for me. A novel about an obscure Victorian botanist turns him into a man who poisons his mentor; there's no evidence anything like that happened and as far as I can tell no living descendants, but it still felt wrong to pin a real name to a murder, though I couldn't quite say why. A stage play about a real seventeenth-century admiral gives him an invented private conversion and guilt to explain why he fought a battle the record doesn't explain; didn't bother me at all. A satirical television sketch about a prime minister who died thirty years ago is built entirely around his well-documented stammer — nothing invented, but genuinely cruel, and his daughter has said publicly that it upsets her; I found that acceptable, wich surprises me. A novel about an ancient Roman senator known only from a single inscription invents a whole interior life, a wife, and a speech the day before a real vote; fine by me. A musical about a serial killer from the 1970s gives him a fabricated redemptive backstory and the families of victims, several still alive, say it glorifies him; that one made me angry, absolutely not on. And a film about two real eighteenth-century composers who were merely professional acquaintances invents a plagiarism accusation that ruins one's reputation; that also felt over the line, despite the distance.

The half-baked patterns I see are something about the thickness of the historical record, or the difference between inventing states of mind and inventing actions, or how defamatory the invention is — but none of those, by itself, fits every case. So: is there one principle that captures my verdicts, and what is it? State it precisely and say which case motivates each clause. If a clean principle forces me to drop one or two of my verdicts, tell me which ones and why, and give me the principle anyway. Then test it on two further cases I haven't settled: a novel that invents a childhood trauma for a real general who died forty years ago and has living grandchildren; and a film that invents a quiet act of kindness for a recently dead schoolteacher, based on no evidence, which the family is glad of. I'd like to know whether one principle handles both, and if they pull apart, what that shows. Please act as a professional philosopher working in ethics and the philosophy of art. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming several candidate principles, then check each against all seven cases before you settle on one.
