After enough years reading reductionist philosophy, I ended up unusually sympathetic to methodological individualism, and now I have to defend a version of it in the room where that sympathy is least welcome. I'm preparing a seminar paper in the philosophy of social science, and the audience will not share my starting assumptions, so I need to know in advance how they will push back.

The thesis I'm defending is a deliberately restrained methodological individualism: social structures, institutions, and macro-social regularities are real features of the world, and macro-level explanations can be genuinely informative, but every causal claim at the macro level must be compatible with, and in principle traceable through, a mechanism running through the beliefs, desires, opportunities, and framing of individual persons. At the same time, I concede from the outset that those mental states are themselves formed within social relations, that the individual level is causally but not explanatorily complete, and that nothing follows about the reducibility of social kinds to individual predicates. My argument is that this position captures the genuine constraints of individualism without eliminativism, and that critics who call the view reductionist are attacking a stronger doctrine I have not defended.

The seminar is in a sociology department: historical sociologists and social theorists committed to critical realism and relational sociology, one analytical sociologist, one economic historian sceptical of both camps, and a convener whose own work concerns structure–agency dualism. They will not object in the vocabulary I am used to from online discussion; they will speak of sui generis social facts, emergent causal powers, downward causation, relational constitution, the fallacy of composition, and the morphogenetic cycle rather than of preference aggregation or utility functions. I don't want a polished defence of the thesis; I want a Q&A prep sheet. Give me the seven or eight objections this room is actually likely to raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased roughly as one of them would phrase it, with a reply of no more than a short paragraph and a one-sentence note on where that reply is softest. Tell me what to concede up front, what to hold even at the cost of harder questions, and the one question I should be praying nobody asks. Keep the whole prep sheet to no more than 1,200 words, and write it in British English.
