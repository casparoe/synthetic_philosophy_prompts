I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am preparing a piece on the philosophical arguments that circulate beneath debates about taxing labor income. In conversations with defenders of strong property rights, I keep meeting a particular analogy, drawn from Nozick's entitlement theory and his Wilt Chamberlain thought experiment. The thought experiment runs roughly as follows: take any distribution you consider just, then allow people to spend their resources freely — the classic case is fans each paying a quarter to watch Wilt Chamberlain play basketball. The resulting distribution, however unequal, is also just, because it arose by just steps from a just starting point. So any 'patterned' principle of justice, which requires the distribution to exhibit some ongoing shape, must continually interfere with people's voluntary choices in order to keep the pattern in place. The analogy attached to this argument is that taxing the earnings of labor is morally on a par with forced labor: under threat of penalty, the state lays claim to a portion of the time and effort a person has spent working, just as a coercive employer would.

For my piece, I need an audit of how far this analogy actually holds. Please give me:

(1) A correspondence table: what in the taxation case maps onto what in the forced-labor case — the conscripted worker, the hours worked, the coercer, the threat, the beneficiary, the product taken, and anything else you think belongs in the mapping.

(2) The places where the mapping breaks down, stated precisely. Among the candidate disanalogies I want you to evaluate are: taxation falls on income rather than on specified hours of labor, so it also reaches non-labor income such as gifts, capital gains, and bequests; the legal order that defines and enforces the property and contract rights generating pretax income is itself a coercively provided public good; taxpayers have at least partial exit and voice, whereas the paradigmatic forced laborer has neither; the taxpayer also benefits from the very system that does the taxing; and the tax leaves the worker free to adjust how much and in what way to work, rather than commandeering particular hours. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended inference from the ones that do not.

(3) A verdict on whether the conclusion the analogy is used to support — that taxation of earned income is morally on a par with forced labor, and therefore that patterned or redistributive principles are illegitimate because they cannot be maintained without continuous interference with liberty — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. In particular, assess whether the Wilt Chamberlain argument can establish its conclusion on its own, or whether it silently leans on the forced-labor analogy at exactly the point where it treats the pretax distribution as a natural baseline.

(4) A brief note on what residual point the analogy can still carry if the strong conclusion fails, and whether some better analogy would do the work more honestly.

No citations or bibliography are wanted; treat the argument on its own terms, and write for an educated but non-specialist readership.
