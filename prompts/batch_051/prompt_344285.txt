I am a high school student writing a term paper for my American literature class, and I want to read Nathaniel Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter through the philosophical debate about standing to blame. As I understand the debate, blame requires more than a wrongdoer and a true accusation: the blamer also needs standing. Someone can lose standing by being complicit in the same kind of wrongdoing, by condemning in others what one excuses in oneself, or by using blame as an instrument of some other aim; several philosophers hold that such an accuser may still state the truth while lacking the standing to blame. The novel seems to test this idea through different characters: the magistrates and townspeople, who condemn Hester Prynne from a position of presumed communal purity; Arthur Dimmesdale, who is secretly the partner in her sin and conceals it; and Roger Chillingworth, whose grievance is personal and turns into revenge. I want a reading that goes beyond merely calling the Puritans hypocrites. Which characters does the novel treat as having or lacking standing to blame Hester, and which scenes convey that? Consider in particular the early scene in which Dimmesdale, speaking as her minister, is called on to persuade Hester to name her partner while he himself stays silent, and then his later public confession on the scaffold. Does the novel accept the philosophical move that a hypocrite can make a true accusation without being in a position to level it, or does it suggest that a failure of standing corrupts the accusation itself? Where does the novel complicate or resist the standing framework—for instance, in Hester's own refusal to accuse others, or in the fact that the punishment is carried out by a community acting as a body rather than by individual accusers? I would also like an honest account of where the reading breaks down, and a closing thought on what the novel adds to the standing debate that the usual philosophical examples about individual hypocrites do not capture.
