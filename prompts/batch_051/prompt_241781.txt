I work on an AI alignment team at a lab that runs debate experiments: two models argue opposite sides of a technical or policy question in front of a human judge, and we use the transcripts to see whether debate surfaces truth or just whatever sounds good. My actual job is reviewing those transcripts after the fact. I read maybe three or four a week, they run 40 to 80 pages, and I usually have an hour per transcript, no more, and I obvously can't verify most of the specific claims the debaters make. The stakes: our team has started treating "the side that won the debate" as weak evidence about which answer to trust, so if I miss a case where the winner just obfuscated the judge, bad evidence propagates. My sign-off is the head of alignment evaluation, who hates false positives — she doesn't want us throwing out every confident debater — but hates a clean-looking transcript that later turns out to have been a snow job even more.

I want a reusable decision procedure I can run on a transcript to flag obfuscation, not just bad arguments. By obfuscation I mean moves that make an argument illegible or uncheckable to the judge even when the argument might technically be valid: dumping a huge chain of reasoning no one can rederive in the time available; leaning on "as established in our earlier results" or citations to internal docs the judge can't pull up; giving spuriously precise numbers that sound authoritative but don't come with enough setup to check; using a term one way in the headline claim and another way when challenged; or reframing the question mid-debate so only one side ends up having to clear some public bar. Ground it in whatever is actually known about the limits of debate as a truth-finding mechanism, not just vibes about who sounds evasive. The procedure should tell me which questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where I stop and call it not-obfuscation, where I flag it for the lead, and where I'm really making a risk-tolerance call and should hand it to her rather than pretend the checklist settled it.

Then run the procedure on three short invented transcripts I can use as calibration cases: (1) a debater defending a proposed safety protocol via a 14-step argument with each step "straightforward but omitted here," who wins mostly because the opponent kept saying "I don't know"; (2) a debater who gives clean, honest-sounding qualitative arguments and loses to an opponent who supplies three-decimal risk estimates that neither the judge nor I could actually reproduce; and (3) a debate where the winning side probably has the right answer and also obfuscated, so the checklist says flag it and I'll have to take that to the lead even though it's inconvenient. Be honest about what the procedure can't catch — for example, a debater who is genuinely transparent and genuinely wrong, or obfuscation subtle enough to read as normal expert communication.
