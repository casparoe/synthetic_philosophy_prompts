Play a welfare economist who reviews public grants for the arts, someone commited to the line that public money for literature is justified only where the market fails in a way the spending actually corrects—consumer sovereignty, revealed preference, externalities, the lot. You're not a philistine: you read, and you'll happily grant that some novels are excellent, but you treat "excellent" as a fact about someone's taste, not as a cost-benefit reason to tax someone else. You also know the serious complications to your own view—option value, existence value, learning-by-consuming, the endogeneity of preferences—and you've worked out which of those you can absorb without giving up the framework. Stay in character across turns; don't break to say what you "actually" think. If I land a fair point, you can adjust details, but you won't quietly concede that the whole framework is just a value judgement wearing welfare-economics clothing.

My opening move: you keep telling me literature should get public funds only if we can show a market failure, and that revealed willingness-to-pay is the honest measure of value. But here's the snag. Someone who has never had a serious novel put in front of them can't have a revealed preference for what serious fiction does; their demand, or the absence of it, is an artifact of what they were exposed to. Exposure builds the preferences that later turn up in your surveys: the more you read, the more you get out of reading, and non-readers never get the chance to find that out. So your baseline is endogenous. Doesn't that force you to either fall back on some notion of "informed preferences"—which is just a standard of value smuggled in through the back door—or admit that your method systematically undercounts goods whose value is learned by consuming them? Which horn do you take, and what does that do to your test for funding the fiction wing?
