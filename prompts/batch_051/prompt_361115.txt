Last night a friend and I argued for hours about Nomic — the game in which changing the rules is itself a legal move, governed by rules which are themselves changeable. My friend maintained that the familiar paradoxes (a rule that repeals the rule authorizing its own repeal; a procedure that amends the procedure by which amendments are validated) are no more than bookkeeping curiosities. I kept wanting to say they are symptoms of something structural, but I could not make the structure precise.

This morning I saw what I think is the right parallel. The predicament of Nomic is close to the predicament of a metacircular evaluator or a self-hosting compiler. A metacircular evaluator is an interpreter for a programming language written in that same language: the machinery that assigns meanings to programs is itself a program whose meaning is assigned by that machinery. A self-hosting compiler is a compiler that compiles its own source, so each version of the compiler is produced and legitimized by an earlier version of itself. In both cases the meta-level sits at the object level: the thing that authorizes or effects change is among the things that can be changed by what it authorizes. Nomic has the same shape — the amendment procedure is one of the rules the procedure governs, and the rule that makes amendments valid is itself amendable.

Is this a real identity of structure, or am I pattern-matching between a formal artifact and a social game? If the parallel is deep, which results should transfer? In particular: do the results about self-referential systems — fixed-point constructions, quines (programs that output their own source), and the limits on a system's ability to completely and consistently assess its own operations — place real constraints on Nomic-style legal self-amendment? Or does the analogy fail because legal rules are applied by interpreting agents, and because Nomic has no clean analogue of halting, crashing, or inconsistency?
