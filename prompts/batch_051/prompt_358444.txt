I am a reporter who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I also serve on the magazine's editorial standards board. We must decide this week whether to approve final copy for a reported feature on the linguistics and psychology of slurs. The writer has asked permission to print in full the anti-Black slur usually referred to euphemistically as the n-word, in three places: (i) in a block quotation from a Black research participant who describes being called the slur and explains why she does not use it herself; (ii) in the author's own exposition, where the term appears only as an italicized linguistic example in a passage distinguishing what the word denotes from what it expresses; and (iii) in a pull-quote displayed in the online preview and prepared social-media posts. The board is split. One side holds that all three are metalinguistic occurrences, comparable to quoting a word in a dictionary, and that disguising the orthography would withhold from readers the very evidence the article asks them to assess. The other side holds that the slur's power to demean is a stable, conventional feature of the word itself, which neither quotation marks nor a writer's intention suspends, and that reprinting the word in a large-circulation venue gives that harm a second life. Draft the short decision memo I will circulate before Friday's meeting. The memo must recommend a concrete course of action: print all three, print none, or print with specified conditions, treating the three occurrences as potentially different rather than collapsing them into a single yes or no. In the reasoning, address the strongest consideration on the losing side, and take seriously both the semantics of the word and the pragmatics of publishing it in this venue. The board is comfortable with technical distinctions such as use and mention, quotation and endorsement, and standing meaning versus speaker-intended effect; the memo should put those distinctions to work, not simply recite them.
