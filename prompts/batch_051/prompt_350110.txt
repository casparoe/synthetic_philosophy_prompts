I'm trying to reconstruct what I actually think about whether death is bad for the one who dies, and I want to work from my verdicts on cases rather than from a theory I've already absorbed. I started by trying to take the old Epicurean thought seriously — that death cannot be bad for the one who dies, because the person and their death never overlap in time — and I found I couldn't dismiss it as quickly as I expected. For years I've defaulted, mostly by inertia, to a deprivation account: death is bad for you to the extent that it deprives you of good life you would otherwise have had, and neither awareness of the loss nor its timing matters. But I keep discovering that my own judgments don't all fit that view, and I'd like you to find the principle behind them, if there is one, or tell me which to give up.

My verdicts:

1. A 30-year-old is killed painlessly in their sleep; their life is going well and they had every reason to expect decades more of it. This is my clearest case: it is severely bad for them, even though they never anticipated it or felt anything.

2. An instant, painless accident kills a 30-year-old with no warning. I can't locate any difference from case 1: equally bad. Anticipation matters not at all.

3. A one-week-old infant dies painlessly. This strikes me as terrible, for the infant, even though the infant had no plans and no grasp of a future. The badness seems to lie in the whole prevented life, not in anything the infant experienced or wanted.

4. A 95-year-old dies peacefully in their sleep after a complete and fulfilled life. I don't think this is bad for them, or only negligibly so. A view that handles 1–3 should also explain why 4 is different rather than just declaring it smaller.

5. The time before I was born. I do not treat the fact that I didn't exist in 1800 as a misfortune for me; it isn't on the ledger. But if nonexistence is bad exactly because it prevents possible good life, pre-natal nonexistence should be a misfortune of the same kind as dying at 30, and my gut says it plainly isn't. I know the standard move — an earlier birth wouldn't have been me — but I've never found it fully satisfying, and I'd rather see whether the principle you extract handles the asymmetry without special pleading.

6. A close friend solemnly promises an author to publish their manuscript if anything happens to them; after the author dies, the friend does nothing. I am confident the friend wronged the author in a real sense. Was the author harmed? I keep going back and forth. My strongest impulse is yes — the author's actual interests were set back, though no subject remains to undergo the setback — but calling it harm strains the word.

7. After an author's death, a stranger publishes a widely read biography full of damaging falsehoods; the author's reputation is destroyed, and no loved one survives to be caused pain. I don't think the author is harmed, though what the biographer does is wrongful. I cannot yet say what distinguishes 6, which feels as if it reaches the dead person, from 7, which doesn't.

Cases 1 and 5 are the pair I most want a principle to reconcile, and 6 and 7 are close behind. I'd also like the resulting account to say something precise about a further case I haven't listed: a person is painlessly destroyed at the very moment a perfect physical duplicate of them is created, with the same memories, values, and psychology, and this duplicate goes on to live the life the original would have lived. My unreflective sense is that this is less bad for the original than plain death at the same age, but I don't know whether that intuition is sound or just confusion about identity.

Is there one account of the badness of death that delivers all of these verdicts? If not, which should I overturn and why? State the account precisely enough to test it, say what it predicts for the duplication case, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. I would rather have the simplest principle that gets most of these than a baroque one that saves every verdict by adding a clause per case.
