I keep flip-flopping on a debate I see in rationalist-adjacent circles, and I want an essay that actually commits to a position instead of doing the both-sides thing. The question is whether, when you want someone (or a whole community) to actually change their behavior, the better lever is guilt or shame. The standard line I absorbed is that shame is the toxic one and guilt is the grown-up one: shame targets the whole self and makes people hide, lie, or get defensive, while guilt tracks the act and pushes toward apology and repair. But there's a revisionist take that's been nagging at me, and it says guilt is a luxury good. If someone doesn't already share your values, guilt has nothing to hook onto; shame, by contrast, can be imposed from the outside by an audience, so it works even on people who think they did nothing wrong. On that view, shame is the only thing that keeps large, low-trust, semi-anonymous spaces from collapsing into defection, and the reason it gets a bad rap is that we're judging it by the standars of intimate, high-trust relationships where guilt actually functions.

The thing I most want a verdict on is the second-order question: is 'should this culture run on guilt or shame' even a real choice? You can't decide to feel guilt instead of shame, and a group can't just install a moral emotion by fiat. Or maybe it can, slowly, through what it makes public, what it punishes, what it praises, and who it lets save face. If you were designing the norms of a community from scratch—an online forum, an EA-adjacent org, a friend group with exacting standards—would you try to cultivate more guilt or more shame, and what would cultivating it even look like? Please also keep apart the acute pang of shame from the standing practice of shaming, and guilt-the-verdict from guilt-the-feeling; I suspect some of the disagreement comes from people using the same words for different layers.

Weigh the main arguments on both sides, then defend a conclusion rather than ending on 'it depends.' A scoped or intermediate conclusion is fine—like 'guilt for the inner ring, shame for the boundary,' or the reverse—as long as you actually land on one. And don't turn it into a survey of the psychology; I want your actual position.
