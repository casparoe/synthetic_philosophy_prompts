Common-law criminal courts routinely strike down statutory offences for vagueness on the ground that an ordinary person must be able to tell in advance which conduct is prohibited, so that enforcement does not become a matter of police and prosecutorial discretion. The same courts uphold offences defined through terms whose application is contested and heavily context-dependent: dishonesty, gross negligence, reasonable suspicion, and the like. What view of linguistic meaning and of statutory interpretation does this standing practice presuppose? In particular, does the doctrine presuppose that statutory terms have a stable, pre-adjudicative core of meaning that competent speakers share and that can guide conduct, or can a contextualist about legal meaning endorse the practice without self-deception? Reconstruct the implicit theory carefully, distinguishing what the void-for-vagueness doctrine strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, identify the points at which the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and say what would have to change if the presupposed picture of meaning were abandoned.
