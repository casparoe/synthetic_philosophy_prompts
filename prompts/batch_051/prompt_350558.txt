I am an undergraduate currently taking a course in the philosophy of history, and I have reached a point where two things I find deeply attractive appear to be in direct tension. I would be grateful for help thinking through which side should give way, or whether the conflict is avoidable.

On one side, I am drawn to the covering-law account of historical explanation. It offers the systematic virtues I tend to trust: it unifies explanation across the natural and social sciences, it supplies a clear criterion of adequacy—an explanation should show that the event was, under the circumstances, to be expected on the basis of general regularities—and it gives me a way to diagnose why a mere chronicle, which lists one thing after another, fails to explain. When I read a piece of historical writing and cannot find any implicit lawlike generalization, this account tells me that the explanation is, at best, a sketch waiting to be filled in. That rigor is satisfying.

On the other side, I have a firm and nearly unshakeable intuition that some of the best historical explanations are narratives, and that their explanatory force is not the force of a covering law. A narrative seems to explain by tracing a particular sequence of human actions, choices, accidents, and shifting circumstances, and by making the outcome intelligible through the way those elements hang together—for instance, by showing which possibilities were live at each point, which choices foreclosed others, and how a fragile process held together or came apart. An account of why the First World War broke out in the summer of 1914, for example, appears to get its explanatory power from the concrete plot—the assassination, the ultimatums, the mobilization schedules, and the leaders' misjudgments—rather than from subsuming the event under a general law about how wars arise. I cannot convince myself that this intuition is merely a psychological effect of storytelling, and I cannot find the relevant laws that would convert such narratives into covering-law sketches.

My question is how to negotiate between these two commitments. Should I debunk and abandon the intuition that narrative explains, concluding that narratives are acceptable only insofar as they are incomplete covering-law sketches? Should I amend or abandon the covering-law account instead, holding that historical intelligibility is autonomous and not reducible to nomic expectability? Or is there a reading on which the two are compatible—for example, one on which they answer different questions about an event, or one on which narratives explain how something was possible rather than why it was necessary? I would especially like to know what would justify each of these moves, and how one could tell whether the apparent conflict is real or an artifact of the way I have framed it.
