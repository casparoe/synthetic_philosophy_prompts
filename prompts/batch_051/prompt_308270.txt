I'm putting together a handout for a decision-theory study group on the two-envelope problem, and I keep going in circles on one specific thing. The setup, just so we're aligned: two sealed envelopes, one contains exactly twice as much money as the other, I pick one, and before opening it I call its unknown amount x. The usual reasoning then says the other envelope is equally likely to contain 2x or x/2, so its expected value is (1/2)(2x) + (1/2)(x/2) = 1.25x, and I should switch. But the very same argument works from the other envelope, so each one ends up looking better than the other, which can't be right.

The diagnoses I've collected so far are these, and please don't give me a reheated version of one of them:

(1) The equivocation diagnosis — x flips its referent between the two cases: in the "2x" branch x is the smaller amount, in the "x/2" branch x is the larger amount, so 1.25x isn't the expected value of any single fixed quantity.

(2) The impossible-prior diagnosis — the equal-probability step presupposes a flat (or better, doubling-invariant) distribution over an unbounded range of possible envelope contents, and no such normalized probability distribution exists.

(3) The infinite-expectation diagnosis — on the priors that make the reasoning feel natural, the unconditional expected contents of an envelope are infinite, and comparing 1.25 times infinity with infinity doesn't yield a real advantage.

(4) The conditioning diagnosis — once you actually see the amount, whether switching pays depends on the posterior probability of the two candidate pairs, and under any proper prior there will be amounts you shouldn't switch on; the "always switch" policy can't beat the symmetry once you condition properly.

(5) The utility diagnosis — the calculation is in money, not utility, and if utility is concave or bounded, the expected utility of switching needn't exceed that of staying.

I'm farely sure these cover the standard moves, but I want one more that is genuinely different in structure — not a rewording of the equivocation or impossible-prior lines, and not just a special case of the conditioning point. If there's a diagnosis that comes at the whole puzzle from a different angle, what is it and what does it add that these five don't? And if you honestly think anything else collapses into one of the five, say that too, and tell me which one it collapses into.
