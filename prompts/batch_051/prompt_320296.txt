I have recently moved into a job where I make decisions under uncertainty with slow and noisy feedback, and after a costly bout of overconfidence a colleague who trains in Brazilian jiu-jitsu suggested I look into a program that teaches decision-making the way a dojo teaches a martial art. The program's core claim, as I now understand it, is that rationality is not a fixed trait but a set of component skills that can be built through deliberately designed exercises, which its instructors call katas: short, repeatable drills that isolate a single movement of thought, such as noticing the base rate, taking the outside view of one's own plans, or asking what evidence would change one's mind. The stronger claim is that skill built in these drills transfers to decisions that look nothing like the exercises, the way a boxer's drilled footwork is supposed to show up in an unscripted fight. I am less interested in whether this particular program delivers than in whether the underlying claim is even the kind of thing that can be tested, and, if it is, what an honest test would look like. Please spell out the strongest concrete test you can design that would distinguish the claim that a general capacity for rational thought has been trained and transferred from the much weaker claim that people have simply gotten better at the drills themselves. Tell me exactly what pattern of results would count as evidence for each, and then be as precise as you can about the residue: what a positive result would and would not settle, including whether what transfers deserves to be called rationality or only a loose collection of narrow habits, and whether doing well on your test tasks would actually tell me anything about my decisions in the wild. I would also ask you to tell me what would change your own assessment of the martial-arts analogy: which kind of finding, or which argument about what rationality is in the first place, would most shift your verdict, and where the test you propose is most likely to fail. Please write your reply as continuous prose, in the manner of an email or a letter, without headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables.
