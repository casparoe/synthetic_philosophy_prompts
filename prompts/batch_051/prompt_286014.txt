A team researching coordinated disinformation campaigns has spent several months documenting how a manipulated video — which falsely appears to show a public official destroying mail-in ballots — was seeded, edited, and amplified across social platforms. The report's lead author wants the final publication to embed the full video, framed by time-stamped annotations that dissect its cuts, lighting, and sound design, as well as platform-specific data about how it spread. The team's other senior researcher opposes this. She agrees with nearly every empirical finding in the report, but argues that reproducing the video in the published version, however carefully framed, delivers the propagandists exactly what they wanted: an authoritative-looking copy of the artifact that can be clipped, screenshotted, and recirculated without the annotations, lending it a second life inside the very networks the report is trying to expose. The disagreement is not about whether the video is false and harmful, but about the ethics and downstream consequences of reproducing propaganda for the purposes of critique.

Write a dialogue between these two researchers in which each tries to talk the other out of their position. Neither should be a strawman: each should be able to state the strongest version of their own view and anticipate the other's objections. The dialogue should involve genuine back-and-forth, with the characters responding to each other's actual points, conceding partial ground where appropriate, and pressing each other on their weakest commitments. Among the issues the dialogue should broach are: whether understanding how a piece of propaganda works requires encountering the artifact itself or only an accurate description of it; whether critical framing can ever be guaranteed to travel with the artifact as it circulates; whether a researcher whose report becomes a vector for the original content is culpable for that downstream misuse; and whether there is a principled line between quoting an artifact for analysis and amplifying it for an audience.
