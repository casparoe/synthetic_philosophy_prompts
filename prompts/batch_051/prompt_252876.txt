I am a retired natural scientist, and philosophy is a late enthusiasm of mine, so I would welcome a careful audit of an argument I recently met in a popular book on strategic thinking. The book summarizes the centipede game roughly like this. Two players take turns either taking a larger share of a growing pot of money for themselves and ending the game, or passing the pot to the other player, which makes it grow. If both players pass until the end, each ends with more than either could obtain by taking at her first opportunity. Backward induction says that a rational player will stop this process immediately. At the final move, the player whose turn it is would take rather than pass, since that yields her more; the other player, foreseeing that, would take on the turn before; and this reasoning unwinds to the very first move. Since game theory assumes that people are rational and selfish, the theory predicts that the first player takes straightaway. Yet in laboratory play people commonly pass for several rounds and walk away with more than immediate taking would have given them. The author concludes that backward induction is refuted as a guide to rational play. His claim is that once the players can see that continuing is best for everyone, no sensible account of rationality can tell them to stop at once, and that the fault therefore lies in game theory's assumptions about rationality rather than in the people who play the game.

I find this argument suspicious, but I do not yet know exactly where it goes wrong, and I suspect the centipede puzzle turns on some distinctions the book does not draw. Could you tell me which elements of the summary are accurate, which are common simplifications, and which are genuine misrepresentations that a specialist in backward induction would object to? Does the author's conclusion follow from the accurate parts of the summary? I am especially interested in how much of the apparent paradox depends on treating a normative account of rational play as a descriptive prediction about laboratory subjects, and how much rests on assumptions about the players being certain of one another's rationality. As you answer, please mark clearly which parts of the diagnosis are settled and which remain genuinely contested among game theorists and philosophers, and do not present a contested resolution as if it were the consensus view. I would also prefer your reply in continuous prose, as one would write an email or a letter, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
