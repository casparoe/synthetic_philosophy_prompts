Working on Sen's liberal paradox for a seminar paper, and I keep running into the same four standard ways of dissolving it. The ones I've collected so far are: (i) Gibbard's game-form move, where individual rights get reinterpreted as protecting the availability of certain strategies or moves, rather than requiring the social ranking to defer to my preference between two particular outcomes; (ii) the domain-restriction fix, where you rule out profiles with "meddlesome" preferences, so nobody is allowed to care too much about how someone else exercises their protected choice; (iii) the Pareto-weakening fix, where Pareto only gets to bite on comparisons outside anyone's protected personal sphere, and inside that sphere individual decisiveness just wins; and (iv) the side-constraint picture, which stops trying to build a single social ordering that satisfies both rights and Pareto, and instead treats rights as constraints that social choices may not violate whatever the ranking says.

I'm trying to find a fifth way out, and I don't mean a reworded version of one of these four, or a combination of two of them. It should be a genuinely different diagnosis of what's going wrong, or a different place to break the theorem. Then give me your honest read on whether the move actually works, or whether it quietly smuggles the conflict back in somewhere else. If you think these four really do exhaust the standard map, you can say that too, but only after actually trying to find something else.
