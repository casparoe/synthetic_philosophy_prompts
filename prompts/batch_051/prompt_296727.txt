I am an undergraduate enrolled in a course on rational choice and the foundations of probability, and before I submit a short position paper, I would like an audit of the following set of commitments I have been defending in discussion section. I am worried that they pull against one another, but I cannot locate the worst tensions.

My commitments are these:

(1) A rational agent's degrees of belief can be represented by a single real-valued credence function satisfying the probability axioms.

(2) The Dutch book theorem is the strongest available argument for why credences ought to satisfy those axioms.

(3) The force of the Dutch book argument is not prudential: nobody is actually threatened by a cunning bookmaker in ordinary life, yet this fact does not weaken the norm.

(4) I am drawn to a depragmatised version of the argument: the existence of a Dutch book is a symptom of incoherence rather than the source of its badness.

(5) Degrees of belief are partly defined by their role in decision-making under risk, but I also think they are conceptually prior to preferences, since preferences are preferences over acts, and acts are individuated by their outcomes across a state space that already presupposes probabilities.

(6) In the Allais problem, I share the classic preference pattern: I prefer a certain $1,000,000 to a gamble with a 10% chance of $5,000,000, an 89% chance of $1,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing; but I also prefer a 10% chance of $5,000,000 and a 90% chance of nothing to an 11% chance of $1,000,000 and an 89% chance of nothing.

(7) I regard the preference pattern in (6) as rationally permissible, not as a mistake to be corrected.

Which of these commitments are in genuine tension, and which tensions are only apparent? Please rank the tensions by severity, and for each say what the least costly revision would be and what it would cost. I am less interested in a general survey than in an audit of this particular package. Please write your answer in British English.
