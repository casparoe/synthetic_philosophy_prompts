I am a novelist. My most recent novel follows a young magistrate in a country emerging from a military dictatorship, and in the course of writing it I was forced to settle, at least provisionally, what I believe about the relation between law and morality. I have agreed to give a lecture to law students in which I will present these commitments, and before I do I want a consistency audit of them. I am not asking for a defense of any single thesis and not for an intellectual history; I am asking where the set strains.

My commitments are these:

1. A rule is valid law in virtue of how it was made, not in virtue of its content. A statute requiring citizens to inform on their neighbours to the regime was therefore valid law, however wicked it was. That is a claim about what law is, not about what anyone should do.

2. The rule of law is a moral achievement, not merely an instrumental convenience. Official power should be exercised only through rules that are public, general, prospective (governing future conduct rather than retroactively punishing past conduct), consistent with one another, possible to obey, stable, and actually followed by officials.

3. Those rule-of-law standards are ideals, not tests of validity. A particular law can violate them and still be law. But I also want to say that a regime whose practice consists in secret decrees, retroactive criminal statutes, and trials that ignore its own announced rules has crossed some line and should be described as organized coercion rather than as a legal system.

4. There can be a moral duty to disobey a valid law when that law is grossly unjust, and this duty is fully compatible with admitting that the law in question is law.

5. After such a regime collapses, it can be right to prosecute the informers under new retrospective statutes for conduct that the old regime's valid law had permitted or even required. In the closing scene of the novel, my magistrate signs just such a conviction, and I remain convinced that he does the right thing.

6. In hard cases judges do not merely apply settled rules; they must make new law, and they properly draw on moral and policy considerations in doing so.

Which of these actually pull against one another, and which merely seem to? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each give the cheapest revision and a note on what that revision costs. Then tell me which single revision would make the whole set hang together most cleanly, and what it would sacrifice.
