I'm a novelist, deep in a draft of a book set partly in a sleep clinic. One of my characters has enrolled in a study that's just the Sleeping Beauty setup: a fair coin is flipped Sunday night; heads means she's woken once, on Monday; tails means she's woken twice, Monday and Tuesday, and the drug they give her erases the memory of any previous waking so each waking feels like the first. The wrinkle for my plot is that on each waking, before she can find out the day, she has to accept or decline a real bet on the coin at even odds, and a lot of the novel hangs on whether she takes it. I don't want to cop out and have the character say something like "there are thirders and halfers and the question is unsettled" — I need her to commit, and I want the reasoning I put in her head to be defensible.

So give me a number: your own credence — not a survey of the debate — that the rational credence for Beauty to assign to heads on any given waking, in the standard fair-coin one-waking-versus-two version, is 1/3 rather than 1/2. Then decompose that number into the sub-questions that are actually doing the work, with a rough probability for each: whether self-locating evidence like "I am awake now" can be updated on in the same way as ordinary evidence; whether the even-odds bet she's offered is a good test of the right credence, given that tails makes her place the identical bet twice; whether the answer is stable if you scale the tails wakings up to ten, a thousand, or a million (and why or why not); and whether "the rational credence about the coin" is determinate at all once you seperate it from what she's supposed to do with the belief. Combine them however they should combine, but show the combination, and then tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to and what would move it the most. If there's a question my character should be praying no one in the scene asks her, name it too — that's usually where the novel will get interesting.
