I'm taking a course on moral philosophy and war, and we read G. E. M. Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet *Mr Truman's Degree*. I want to check whether the following reconstruction of her argument against Oxford's proposal to award Truman an honorary degree is valid, and if not, where exactly it goes wrong. I am not quoting her; I am reconstructing what I take her central line of thought to be.

(1) It is always murder to choose to kill innocent people as a means to one's political goals. (2) The bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki chose to kill large numbers of civilians, who were not combatants and not unjust aggressors, as a means of producing Japan's surrender. (3) Truman, in authorizing the bombings, chose those killings as a means to his goal. (4) Therefore Truman committed murder. (5) A university should not confer an honorary degree on someone whom it would be wrong to honor, and it is wrong to honor a murderer. (6) Therefore Oxford should not have conferred the degree on Truman.

I suspect the questionable move is (3), because it slides from "Truman chose an act that he knew would kill civilians" to "Truman chose the killing of civilians," and those are not the same. But I also find (1) stronger than I expected, and I wonder whether Anscombe's actual view is that the distinction between intended and merely foreseen civilian deaths is unavailable here, because the bomb's whole point is to destroy a city. So: is the reconstruction valid? If not, identify every step that fails and explain the correct version of that step, and say whether the argument can be repaired while keeping Anscombe's conclusion.
