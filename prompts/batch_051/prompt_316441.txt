Over the past couple of years I have been developing an idea I call presuppositional ratcheting, and I would like an honest appraisal of it. The starting point is the familiar accommodation phenomenon: when an utterance presupposes something not yet in the common ground—for instance, a speaker says that even John's laziness is holding him back—hearers often quietly add the required presupposition, that John is lazy, to the conversational score rather than stopping the conversation to object. My proposal is that this is not neutral bookkeeping. A presupposition enters the common ground without having been asserted, so it never has to discharge the ordinary burden of justification that an assertion about John's laziness would invite. At the same time, the score is sticky. Once the presupposition sits in the common ground, later utterances can build on it, and the cost of challenging it later is higher than it would have been at the moment of accommodation, because now the challenge amounts to rewinding a stretch of shared conversation. I therefore think accommodation works as a one-way ratchet: propositions can be inserted cheaply as presuppositions and become progressively more expensive to remove. I suspect this is part of how prejudicial or self-serving background assumptions establish themselves in ordinary talk without anyone ever having to assert them.

I know the standard view says accommodation operates only within limits—the presupposition must be plausible, relevant, and more or less uncontroversial—and that common ground is not the same as individual belief. My response is that these constraints tell us when the ratchet turns, not whether it turns. What I want to know is whether this mechanism already exists under another name, whether it is in fact distinct from standard accounts of accommodation and common ground, and where it breaks down. If you think I have overstated the asymmetry, I would especially like to hear the strongest reason you can give for thinking that conversations do not in fact ratchet in this way, together with the clearest counterexample you can construct.
