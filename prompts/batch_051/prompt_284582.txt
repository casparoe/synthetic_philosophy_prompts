I have spent most of my life working in the physical sciences, where one is trained to ask what mechanism makes an instrument work before trusting that instrument. Since retiring I have begun to read philosophy, and a long-familiar legal practice now strikes me as philosophically unstable precisely because its mechanism is unclear. In an English court, a witness may either swear an oath on a holy text or make a solemn affirmation; the two are treated as legally equivalent, and a witness who lies under either is exposed to the same penalty for perjury. On its face this is puzzling. Swearing an oath looks like an appeal to a power beyond the court, a way of placing the speaker under a sanction that no human tribunal could fully administer; the old story in which even the gods are bound by an oath sworn on the river Styx is the extreme form of that thought. If the practice presupposed such a view, one would expect the affirmation to be a second-class substitute, tolerated for those unable to swear but not in itself equally binding. Yet the law treats the two as equivalent. What theory of the binding force of an oath does this dual practice presuppose? Is the law quietly committed to the view that the religious form is an optional shell around a perfectly secular core — a performative act, a social bond, a matter of conscience or personal integrity — and, if so, why does the shell survive? Or does the practice presuppose inconsistent things at different points? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, and in doing so separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. I am less interested in the law as such than in what the law's own practice commits it to about why oaths bind.
