I teach ethics, and over the last few years I've been keeping track of my own reactions to the cases that get filed under "purity" or "sacredness." They don't line up with my official harm/autonomy views, and they don't line up with each other either. I want to know wether there's a principle underneath them, or whether I'm just rationalizing my disgust. Here are the cases, verdicts included.

1. Two middle-aged siblings have sex one time. They're careful, it's fully consensual, neither is harmed, nobody else ever learns. I think it's wrong. I can't give a harm-based story that would convince my students, and I've stopped pretending I can, but I won't give up the verdict.

2. A man's dog is killed by a car, and he butchers and eats it at home, alone, reasoning that wasting the meat would be worse. Nobody else is affected. I don't think this is fine. Not "he should be punished," but not fine.

3. A man buys a chicken at the store, has sex with it before trussing it, then roasts and eats it alone. I find it deeply gross, but when I try to locate the wrong I come up empty. My verdict: not a moral matter.

4. A woman spits into a glass of water and then drinks all of it. Fine. Disgusting to watch, but no more than that.

5. A man uses his own torn national flag to clean a clogged toilet. I feel a flicker of something, then it passes. Fine.

6. A boutique sells jewelry set with hair it says came from victims of the Nazi camps; down the street, a museum displays the same kind of remains behind glass with names and documents. I think the museum's display can be proper, even important. The boutique is not fine.

7. A man has his foot amputated, and he and a close friend cook and eat it together, with the man's repeated, informed consent beforehand. I'd decline the invitation, but my verdict is: fine.

8. A man inherits an urn containing the ashes of a woman he never met; he empties the ashes into the kitchen bin and keeps the urn for flour. I don't think that's fine. (I don't think keeping the urn with the ashes still inside, on a high shelf, is especially problematic.)

The case I keep going back and forth on: a company will press your dead spouse's ashes into a diamond you can wear. Sometimes it feels honoring, sometimes it feels like turning a person into an accessory.

So: is there a single principle, or a small family of them, that captures all of this? Which of my verdicts would I have to abandon? Then test the account you end up with on a new case or two of your own devising, and tell me where it would push me somewhere I won't like going.
