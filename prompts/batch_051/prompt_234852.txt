In a seminar I teach on the philosophy of privacy, a student recently cut off a discussion of ambient workplace monitoring and commercial data collection by saying: "If you have nothing to hide, you have nothing to fear." He meant it as a complete rebuttal to the claim that pervasive observation can be objectionable even when it causes no concrete injury: if the observed person has done nothing wrong, observation can cost them nothing, and so they have no legitimate ground to complain. Several other students nodded, and I realized the sentence was functioning in the room as a full argument rather than as a slogan in need of analysis. I plan to devote the next session to taking it apart, and I want the analysis worked out in advance.

The sentence can be read in several different ways depending on what "having something to hide" amounts to (literal legal exposure, embarrassing facts, discreditable facts, or anything at all that one would rather not reveal), on what "fear" ranges over (state coercion, social sanction, manipulation, discrimination, or the mere unease of being watched), and on what kind of claim the conditional is (a factual generalization, a prudential rule, a normative principle, or a conceptual point about the meaning of privacy). Lay out the main readings as a menu, and for each say whether it is a harmless platitude, a plausible but contestable claim, or simply false. Then identify which reading, precisely, is doing the argumentative work when the slogan is deployed to defeat an objection to surveillance or data collection, and formulate the strongest version a sophisticated defender could retreat to.

Please keep the response under 900 words, organized as one brief section per reading with a one-sentence verdict at the end of each section. I am less interested in a general essay on the value of privacy than in a precise disambiguation of this one sentence and the argument it is being made to carry.
