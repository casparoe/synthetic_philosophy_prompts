my rule with alien life is: any argument that goes "the universe is big / atoms are countless / wherever matter and space are, worlds form" gets zero weight from me, no matter how carefully stated. my friend brought up Lucretius the other day, De Rerum Natura book 2, where after arguing that space is infinite and the seeds of things are bottomless he writes:

[quote]
And if their force and nature abide the same,
Able to throw the seeds of things together
Into their places, even as here are thrown
The seeds together in this world of ours,
'Tmust be confessed in other realms there are
Still other worlds, still other breeds of men,
And other generations of the wild.
[/quote]

my friend says i'm being too quick, because Lucretius's point is that if the material conditions are the same everywhere, we should expect more of the same thing, not just one earth, and that should shift my prior at least a little. i said no: before we had any data, a general principle like that told us nothing because it can't distinguish a universe with 10 worlds from 10^10^10 or whatever. who is right? is there a version of my rule that's defensible, maybe "zero weight until it makes a differential empirical prediction" rather than ignore it forever? how would i tell if i'm just being paranoid about sweeping philosophical arguments instead of actually using evidence well?
