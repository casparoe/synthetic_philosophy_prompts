Lately I have been reading about machine learning on my own, and I came across the no-free-lunch results. As I understand them, they show that if you average over every possible pattern the future might follow, no rule for predicting from past data does better than any other. I have been trying to connect this to the old puzzle about whether experience can ever justify our expectations about what has not been observed, and I have ended up with an argument that seems to prove too much. I cannot find the mistake, and I would like to know where it goes wrong.

(1) A rule for learning from experience is rationally preferable to another only if it does better in some respect that matters, and the only respect that matters is predictive success.
(2) The no-free-lunch results show that, averaged over all possible patterns the future might follow, every predictive rule scores exactly as well as every other.
(3) Since we have no way of knowing in advance which possible pattern our world will follow, the only even-handed way to compare two rules is to average over all possible patterns.
(4) Therefore no predictive rule is rationally preferable to any other. In particular, ordinary induction from past regularities is no better than a rule that predicts tomorrow's weather by drawing lottery numbers.
(5) But that conclusion is absurd. In practice, rules that respect the regularities of experience have a vastly better record than arbitrary rules, and science appears to succeed precisely because it relies on them.
(6) So at least one of (1), (2) and (3) must be false.

I would most like to keep (1) and (5), since giving up either seems to leave no rational basis for choosing any policy of belief over another. But I cannot see how to escape from (2) and (3) to (4). Which step should I reject, why did that step look so plausible, and what does rejecting it cost for the standing of induction?
