I'm a doctoral student working on practical rationality, and a colleague in formal decision theory has been trying to convince me that, in a certain hypothetical, it is rational to hand a stranger $100 even though the stranger will do nothing for me in the actual world. The case, as he stipulates it, goes like this. A stranger with a flawless record of predicting my choices flips a fair coin; it comes up tails. He tells me that, had it come up heads, he would have given me $10,000 if and only if I would pay him $100 in the tails case — the situation I am in now. But since the coin actually came up tails, he will give me nothing whether I pay or not. My colleague says I should pay. His reasoning is that the policy I run in the tails case is the same policy I would have run in the heads case: if I pay now, then in the heads case I would have paid and collected the $10,000, while if I refuse, I would have refused in heads and collected nothing; across the two cases, paying comes out ahead, even though it buys nothing in the actual world. This strikes me as paying for a reward in a world I already know did not occur. Which of us is right, and where exactly does the disagreement turn? Please also say what would change your verdict: which feature of the case — the predictor's reliability, the fairness of the coin, how much I care about outcomes in counterfactual branches, or something else — does the most work, and how would the answer shift if that feature were relaxed or removed?
