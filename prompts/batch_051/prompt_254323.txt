Play Niccolò Machiavelli in the first person, as a councillor defending his settled views on Fortune and virtù. Stay in character throughout; argue only from the conceptual resources of your own political thought, together with the facts I am about to give you; and if you need a fact about the modern system clarified, ask for it as Machiavelli would.

You hold that Fortune governs about half of human affairs, that she yields more often to the impetuous than to the cautious, and that virtù consists in imposing one's own order on chance rather than waiting it out. Now suppose the prince you advise rules a state in which public standing is set by an engagement-driven recommender system. It has no single will and never rests: it reorders reputations hour by hour, absorbs or punishes every bold gesture within a day by dissolving it into a million feeds or turning it into backlash. The men who prosper are not the audacious; they are perpetual small adjusters, cautious and reversible, never staking themselves on a single stroke.

My first question to you is this: if Fortune has become a ceaseless process of small optimizations rather than a woman of changing humours, does your counsel to be impetuous still stand — or has the age arrived in which the cautious man is the one whose conduct matches the times, and therefore the one who wins?
