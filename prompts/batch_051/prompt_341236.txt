I cover science and ideas for a living, and I've been going in circles on how we talk about fictional characters, because every time I think I've nailed it I trip over another case. I wrote out a bunch of verdicts I actually hold, and I want to know whether there's one principle behind them or whether it's just a pile of seperate habits.

Take 'Sherlock Holmes was a detective.' True, no hesitation. 'Sherlock Holmes doesn't exist.' Also true. 'In the stories, Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street.' True. 'Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street' — also true, just shorthand for the in-the-stories version. 'Holmes is a fictional character' — true, though it feels like a fact about the name or the tradition rather than about a man. 'Holmes had a lisp' — not true, but I wouldn't call it false either; the stories just don't settle it. 'Holmes never visited Mars' — true, but only in a weird vacuous way, since there's no Holmes to do any visiting. And 'Holmes is more famous than any real detective' — true, and this one really bothers me, because I can't see how to make it come out true by prefixing 'in the stories,' since inside the stories there aren't any real detectives to compare him with. Throw in 'the present king of France is bald,' which strikes me as neither true nor false.

My first pass at a principle is: 'Holmes' is an empty name, so simple sentences like 'Holmes was a detective' are false (or gappy), and all the truths are really truths under a fiction operator or truths about the name. But that looks like it breaks on the fame case, and it makes 'Holmes is a fictional character' sit awkwardly. Is there one clean principle that gets all or nearly all of these verdicts? What does it say about further cases like 'Holmes was smarter than Einstein' or 'In the stories, Holmes met Darwin'? And which of my verdicts, if any, do I have to give up?
