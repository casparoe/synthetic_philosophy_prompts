I am a software engineer designing a platform that turns written argumentation into a ranked competitive sport, and I want a failure analysis of the scoring mechanism before any of it is built.

The proposed rules are as follows. Twice a week, a match pairs two players, who are then assigned opposite sides of a randomly drawn policy resolution from a community-maintained question bank; players never choose their side. Each player writes a short, anonymously presented case for their assigned side. A panel of sixty volunteers recruited from the site's readership records a probability that the resolution is correct before reading the cases, and records a second probability after reading both cases. A player's match score is the number of percentage points by which the panel's average probability moves toward that player's assigned side, with movement in the opposite direction counted as a negative score. Ratings use a continuous Elo-style update keyed to the match score, and feed a public leaderboard. To keep panelists from reporting artificially weak priors so that large swings are possible, each panelist is paid according to how close their initial probability is to the average final probability of the other fifty-nine panelists.

Treat this as a mechanism to analyze rather than a proposal to endorse. What does the scoring rule actually reward, and how does that differ from what the site would claim it rewards? Where does this get gamed first, and what do the first successful gaming strategies look like on the part of the debaters, the panelists, and the people who write and curate the question bank? What trait does the public leaderboard end up selecting for, and does the forced random assignment of sides mitigate or sharpen that selection? Finally, name the one or two failure modes you would act on first, and say whether they can be fixed without destroying whatever the game was supposed to be good for.
