I run a small discussion circle at our public library, and none of us has formal training in philosophy. For the last several weeks we have been arguing about whether anyone can ever genuinely deserve praise or blame, and we keep orbiting a line of thought that I have since learned is associated with Galen Strawson and is usually called the Basic Argument. In my own words, the argument is this. What you do issues from the kind of person you already are: your beliefs, desires, temperament, and habits. If you were to be ultimately responsible for what you do, you would have to be ultimately responsible for that underlying cast of mind. But being responsible for that would require having shaped it yourself at some earlier point, and that act of self-shaping would itself have issued from the cast of mind you had then. The demand never binds; there is no self-made starting point. So no one is ultimately responsible for how they are, and therefore no one is ultimately responsible for what they do. The argument appears to cut against this kind of responsibility whether or not determinism is true, because an undetermined event would not by itself make you the original source of your own nature.

I would like a sorting instrument for people who have just been walked through that argument. Please write a decision tree that places them among the main positions one can take in response. Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of labels: each branch point should be a substantive question or a concrete scenario, phrased so a person can answer from their own instincts without already knowing which position the answer tends toward. Place the actual positions at the leaves, using their usual names if they have them and adding a one-sentence gloss. After the tree, list any combination of answers the tree permits that no one actually defends, and say whether each is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Make sure the tree can land people on at least these positions: one that accepts the argument and holds that deep blame and praise are never deserved, so our practices should change even if we cannot fully live that conclusion; one that accepts the argument but keeps ordinary blame and praise anyway because they do needed social work without requiring ultimate desert; one that rejects the argument by denying that responsibility for an action requires being responsible for the character behind it; one that rejects the argument by holding that a person can be a genuine originator of some decisions in a way that stops the regress; and one that accepts the argument and also treats determinism as an independent reason for skepticism about free will and responsibility, so the two considerations are complementary rather than redundant. Split any requested leaf that is really two positions; if two requested leaves cannot be separated by any question you can build, say so rather than forcing a branch.

For at least the first three branch points, draw your diagnostic questions from these concrete cases, adapted as needed: a driver who, over years, built a habit of running red lights almost without noticing; an adult who was severely neglected as a child and now answers criticism with sudden rage; a person who spends a decade deliberately reforming a cruel streak until genuine kindness becomes second nature; and a person who performs a single uncharacteristic act of selfless courage on impulse. The questions should elicit gut reactions about whether the person deserves blame or praise and about what would have to be true for that desert to be real, not about whether the respondent calls themselves a compatibilist.

Aim for roughly nine to fourteen leaves and no more than eight branch points. For each leaf, end with one sentence naming the most awkward commitment that position carries.
