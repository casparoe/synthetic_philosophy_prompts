I manage a small software team, and we are in the middle of a migration that is forcing us to settle on a single point in time from which all of our timestamps will be counted — our epoch. The obvious choice is the one our existing systems already use: midnight UTC on January 1, 1970. But the discussion has spiraled in a direction I did not anticipate. One engineer argues that 1970 is essentially arbitrary, borrowed from an old operating-system convention, and that if we are rebuilding our time handling anyway we should choose an epoch with historical weight, such as the traditional founding date of our country, or perhaps a proposal he once read about that starts the calendar near the beginning of the Holocene so that most of recorded human history falls on the positive side of zero. Another argues that the only defensible anchor is astronomical: we should count from a precisely defined celestial event, so that our calendar is tethered to nature rather than to a particular culture's political or religious memory. A third insists the whole debate is confused, that an epoch is like driving on the left or the right — a pure convention with no fact of the matter — and that we should just choose whatever minimizes engineering effort and future confusion.

I have to break the tie, and I have realized I am no longer certain the third engineer is right. Is there any sense in which one zero point for a calendar is more correct than another, or is the choice of an epoch entirely a matter of convenience? Does it matter that some candidate epochs refer to events no human could have witnessed, or that one of them would force us to insert a year zero where the familiar calendar has none? I want to give my team a reasoned answer rather than merely casting a deciding vote.
