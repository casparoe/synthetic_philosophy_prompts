I have no academic background in philosophy or history, but I keep returning to a question I cannot settle: whether a society has a duty to remember its worst acts, or whether forgetting is sometimes the more humane choice. Let me make the question concrete. Imagine a country of about ten million people that, fifty years ago, went through a civil conflict in which one part of the population killed or expelled another. Tomorrow the government enacts a policy it calls the Right to a Clean Future: all state archives, school textbooks, public monuments, and broadcast records about the conflict are removed from public use and destroyed. Private memory is left untouched—families may keep letters and photographs, tell their own stories, and pass down whatever they wish. Nothing else changes. Predict, with reasoning, how public memory and political culture look two generations later. Which accounts of the conflict get transmitted, to whom, and in what distorted forms? Does the official silence inflame old grievances or bury them? Walk me through the mechanisms and incentives—of politicians, teachers, families, victim and perpetrator communities—rather than telling a story. Then tell me how confident you are in each link of the prediction, single out the point you are least sure of, and say what would have to be true for the policy to succeed in producing the clean future it promises.
