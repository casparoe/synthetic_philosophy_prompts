I am a retired physical chemist, and a habit I cannot shake is the sense that the deepest results are impossibility results. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy, particularly formal political theory, and Arrow's theorem has struck me as having the same texture as the second law of thermodynamics or the uncertainty principle: a small set of conditions, each independently plausible, cannot all be satisfied by any procedure for aggregating individual rankings into a complete and transitive social ranking, at least when there are three or more alternatives and the rankings are treated as purely ordinal, with no interpersonal comparison of utility admitted. The conditions are unrestricted domain, the weak Pareto condition, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship. What I have not settled is what the theorem actually shows about democratic judgement, and it occurs to me that an older writer might help me see it more clearly.

The Marquis de Condorcet had already identified the central difficulty in his mathematical work on majority decisions: pairwise majority preference can run in circles, so the 'majority will' need not be coherent. But he died well before Arrow proved the general impossibility. My question is the counterfactual one: what would Condorcet have made of Arrow's theorem if it had been put to him?

My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that he would have conceded the formal result at once and then read it as a reductio of Arrow's framework rather than as a limit on rational collective judgement. Condorcet, as I understand him, regarded voting in large part as a search for the alternative most likely to be correct or genuinely best, with voters as fallible but competent judges whose errors could be handled probabilistically; he also thought the intensity and grounds of preferences mattered. From that standpoint, Arrow's theorem demonstrates what goes wrong only once one has already forbidden everything that makes collective judgement tractable. Yet I am unsure of this, for two reasons. First, Condorcet's own pairwise procedure is itself an ordinal method that violates independence of irrelevant alternatives and can fail to yield a transitive social ranking; he had noticed the cycles, but it is not clear he had a remedy within an ordinal framework. Second, his probabilistic defence of majority rule treats voters as instruments for discovering a truth, whereas Arrow's framework treats preferences as sovereign; Condorcet himself moved between epistemic and preference-based language, and it is not obvious how he would have adjudicated between them under pressure.

I should like a reasoned reconstruction, not a guess about his temperament. Which of Arrow's conditions would Condorcet have found least defensible, and on what grounds? Would he have located the impossibility in collective decision-making itself, or in the restrictions Arrow placed on the information a voting rule is permitted to use? And how far can one responsibly extrapolate the views of a historical figure confronted with a formal result stated in a vocabulary — ordinal utility, independence of irrelevant alternatives, transitivity of social preference — that he did not possess? In answering, I would be grateful if you would engage with the uncomfortable fact that Condorcet's own method, made precise as an aggregation rule, also falls within the scope of Arrow's impossibility, since that is the strongest reason to doubt my hypothesis.
