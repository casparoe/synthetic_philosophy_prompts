I'm taking a social and political philosophy course, and this week's reading is on Habermas's theory of communicative action and the ideal speech situation. A friend who took the course last year sent me the one-paragraph summary she wrote for herself when she was studying, and I have a nagging sense it gets the broad strokes right while flattening a few important distinctions. I'm not confident enough in my own reading of Habermas, though, to say exactly where the problems are. Here is her summary:

"For Habermas, genuine communication is only possible if everyone tacitly presupposes an ideal speech situation in which all participants have equal rights to speak, question, and criticize, and only the merits of the arguments themselves—not power, status, or pressure—determine the outcome. That is why, on his account, whenever you discover that someone was silenced or a conclusion was forced through by pressure rather than reasons, you cannot count what happened as real agreement. In any genuine use of language, speakers also raise validity claims—to truth, normative rightness, and sincerity—that listeners can always in principle challenge and ask them to redeem with reasons. From this he concludes that a statement is true if it would be accepted by everyone in such an ideal situation, so truth simply is idealized consensus. The political payoff is that legitimate decisions are those reached through actual face-to-face deliberation approximating the ideal speech situation, and the task of democracy is accordingly to move as many social decisions as possible into that kind of discourse. When critics object that all this is utopian, Habermas's response is that the ideal speech situation is not a real place or a guaranteed outcome but a presupposition we already make whenever we argue seriously, and real arguments can be more or less free of power."

I'm not looking for reading suggestions or citations; I want a careful accuracy audit of this characterization. Please go through it and tell me which parts a careful reader of Habermas would accept as fair, which parts are defensible but common simplifications, and which parts Habermas would likely reject or sharply qualify in his considered view. In particular, I'd like to know whether the two conclusions she draws from the summary—the account of truth as idealized consensus, and the claim about what democratic legitimacy requires—actually follow from the accurate parts of what she said. I'm inclined to think the step from ideal acceptability to truth is too quick and the democracy part sounds too direct, but I'd like to know whether that suspicion tracks Habermas's actual position or just mine.
