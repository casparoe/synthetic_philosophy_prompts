At a philosophy of mind seminar I attended a few weeks ago, two doctoral students got into a long argument that no one in the room seemed able to settle. Dev was defending epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness: the view that felt experience — the character of pain, red, salt — is real, is caused by brain processes, but itself causes nothing at all. Rosa took the view that epiphenomenalism of this kind makes first-person reports about one's own experience impossible to trust, and that this is not a side issue but an internal inconsistency in the position.

The exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Rosa: "Your view says that this pain I am in right now does nothing: it does not cause me to wince, to withdraw my hand, or to say 'I'm in pain.' But then my judgement that I am in pain is not caused by the pain. And if the pain does not produce my judgement, why should the judgement tell me anything about the pain? You have cut the causal link between first-person reports and the very thing they are reports of."

(2) Dev: "That only looks like an objection if you assume a belief is trustworthy only when it is caused by the fact it represents. That assumption is too strong. A thermometer's reading is caused by the expansion of a fluid, not by the temperature itself, yet it reliably indicates temperature. Similarly, the neural state that causes my report could reliably track the pain. That is all I need for first-person knowledge. The experience need not do the causing for the report to be a reliable indicator."

(3) Rosa: "The thermometer case is disanalogous in exactly the respect that matters. Temperature just is mean molecular motion, so the same quantity that expands the fluid is the quantity being reported. On your view, by contrast, the felt pain is something extra, over and above the neural state. I can understand a mechanism that tracks the neural state, but there is no mechanism that tracks the extra felt quality — especially if that quality is causally inert. And here is the sharper point: if the report is caused only by the neural state, then a duplicate with the same neural state but no pain would make the same report. Your account cannot tell the case in which the report is correct from a case in which it is radically wrong, because everything observable is the same."

(4) Dev: "Every theory faces a version of that duplicate worry. A brain in a vat says 'I have hands' exactly as I do, but that does not by itself undermine ordinary perceptual knowledge unless we already accept an implausibly demanding view of knowledge. My claim is only that, in the actual world, the neural state lawfully co-occurs with the experience — say because the experience is identical with the state, or because a psychophysical law links them. Given that lawful co-occurrence, the report is reliably correct. I do not have to rule out the duplicate; I only need the actual correlation."

(5) Rosa: "But the correlation is now doing all the philosophical work, and it is exactly what your view makes mysterious. If the experience is epiphenomenal, the correlation cannot be explained by the experience causing the report. If the experience is not identical with the neural state, then appealing to a 'psychophysical law' simply restates the correlation in the form of a law; it gives no account of why the report should track the felt quality. And notice the deeper problem. Your evidence for epiphenomenalism comes from introspection: experience seems to have a felt character, while our best science seems to need only physical causes. But if introspection is itself just a neural report that would occur whether or not the experience existed, then your access to that felt character is no evidence at all. Your view pulls the ladder up behind itself."

(6) Dev: "That final step overreaches. A report can be justified even when it is not caused by the state it reports, so long as the process producing it is reliable. My visual belief that there is a red tomato is justified even if I cannot rule out the possibility that my colour experiences are inverted relative to yours. Likewise, my introspective reports are justified because they are produced by a reliable neural process, even if the experiences themselves do no causing. And my case for epiphenomenalism does not depend on introspection being caused by experiences; it depends only on my taking my experiences at face value as initial data. If the resulting view implies that those experiences do not cause the reports, that is a surprising consequence to be absorbed, not a contradiction. The paradox dissolves once we abandon the causal theory of introspection."

At that point the seminar ran over and they had to stop. Afterwards, both of them asked me what I thought. I honestly could not say who had the better of it, so I am passing the question on. Please go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate it: for each numbered move, say whether and why it is right or wrong; say what, if anything, both parties are missing; and say who ends up in the stronger position overall. Write your answer in British English.
