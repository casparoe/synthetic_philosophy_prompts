Draft a sequence of eight to ten questions, each with one immediate follow-up, for an author-meets-critics session with an epistemologist who defends a strict conciliationist view of peer disagreement. The position at issue is this: when two inquirers are epistemic peers with respect to some proposition—equally intelligent, equally informed, equally conscientious, and in possession of the same first-order evidence—and they arrive at incompatible attitudes toward that proposition, each should substantially revise toward the other's attitude, and in its strictest form each should suspend judgment, because a peer's assent is evidence of equal weight and one's own prior conviction cannot be used to break the symmetry without violating epistemic independence. Assume the guest has just summarized the view at length, so the questions need not restate it.

The questions should form a sustained, knowledgeable cross-examination rather than a survey. They must include at least one question in each of the following areas:

- self-undermining and regress: what the view says when the target of disagreement is conciliationism itself, or about the appropriate response to discovering disagreement about what the evidence supports;
- the stopping problem: once one has conciliated to an intermediate or suspended state, what licenses treating the original dispute as resolved without covertly reimporting the first-order reasoning that was just set aside;
- disputed peerhood: what conciliationism requires when the other party denies that the two inquirers are peers, including cases where peerhood itself is the subject of higher-order disagreement;
- the relation to uniqueness and permissivism: whether conciliationism covertly assumes that a body of evidence permits exactly one rational doxastic response, and what it predicts if permissivism holds;
- stakes sensitivity: whether the rational pressure to conciliate should vary with the practical or epistemic stakes of the disputed proposition, and whether the view can respect the fact that inquirers treat low-stakes and high-stakes disagreements differently;
- asymmetric treatment of agreement: why a peer's disagreement diminishes one's justification while a peer's agreement is not treated as licensing unbounded upward ratcheting of confidence.

For each main question, the follow-up should be keyed to the reply the guest is most likely to give. Likely replies include appeal to the independence principle, a distinction between first-order and second-order evidence, a distinction between full and partial peers, and companion-in-guilt arguments against rival views; the follow-ups should press each of those replies at the point where it is weakest—for example, asking whether the independence principle applied one level up undermines one's confidence in one's own assessment of peerhood, or whether the first-order/second-order distinction can be drawn without invoking the very reasoning whose authority the view has disclaimed. Do not include pleasantries, biographical questions, or requests for reading recommendations.
