Before I read one more take on whether misleading is just as bad as lying, I want the actual spec: what does a theory of the moral difference — or lack of one — have to do rather than just pick a side? Assume it's meant to ground ordinary blame and tell us how to word an honesty norm inside a community, not to solve all of speech-act theory. Give me the desiderata: the cases it has to handle (outright lies, technically-true-but-misleading, paltering where someone picks true statements to create a false impression, omissions the hearer expected filled in, failed attempts), the distinctions it can't collapse (asserted vs implicated, intention vs uptake, successful vs attempted deception, misleading vs bullshit, one-off act vs policy), and which of these requirements are non-negotiable vs just one camp's taste. For each requirement give me the motivating case.

I especially want your take on this: 'don't say the literal false' feels like a measure that got goodharted. People palter and then say 'I didn't lie', and the norm stops correlating with what anyone actually wanted from it — accurate beliefs — even though nobody broke a rule. So include or reject a rephrasing-invariance requirement: hold intent and harm fixed, move the false proposition out of what's asserted and into what's implied, and the theory has to say whether blame should shift, and why. I'm half-convinced 'literally true' only works as a defence because it's a cheaper-to-adjudicate Schelling point, and that had better not be doing moral work without an argument. Wich single desideratum does the most damage to the standard views? And if some pair of requirements can't be jointly met, say so — that's the part I'd most want.
