Here's a distinction I keep using and want pressure-tested, by experiment if possible. I think two different structures get run together under the heading of family resemblance, and it's a mistake. In a 'chain' concept, each new member resembles the last one enough to join: A shares something with B, B with C, C with D, but A and D have nothing at all in common. In a 'star' concept, there's a central exemplar and the other members resemble that exemplar one-by-one, though they needn't resemble each other. If a concept is a chain, the edges do the work and theres no core; if it's a star, there's a core, just not a necessary-and-sufficient definition.

The claim I'd like tested is that 'game' is overwhelmingly a star concept, and that what looks like chain structure is mostly an artefact of people using different peripheral examples. Chess and football sit at the centre; something like Ring a Ring o' Roses or seeing who can hold their breath counts mainly by sharing one obvious feature with that centre.

How would you test whether an ordinary concept is star-shaped rather than chain-shaped? I want a concrete design, the result that would favour each structure, and some account of how to stop the test just reflecting whichever examples I happened to pick. Then the hard bit: what would even a clean result fail to settle — is the star/chain contrast a fact about the extension, a fact about how concepts are represented in people's heads, or something the task itself produces? I'd rather know whether the distinction earns its keep before I hang a theory on it.
