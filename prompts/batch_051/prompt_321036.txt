"Weitz argued that 'art' is an open concept and that no list of necessary and sufficient conditions can capture what makes something an artwork. Explain his family-resemblance argument for this, and then discuss how the institutional theory of art responds to that argument. Should aesthetics continue looking for a definition of art, or should it give up on definitions?"

Here's the student's answer:

"Weitz's argument draws on Wittgenstein's idea that some concepts are held together only by family resemblances rather than a shared essence. Just as games have no single unifying feature—some involve competition, some chance, some skill, some none of these—artworks likewise have no property that all and only artworks share. Since art is inherently creative and open-ended, any definition will either leave out some art or let in some non-art. Weitz therefore concludes that a real definition of art is impossible. The institutional theory, as Danto formulated it, agrees in effect with this anti-definitional conclusion. It says something is art if and only if the artworld—gallery owners, critics, curators, and historians—confers on it the status of being a candidate for appreciation. But this does not count as a definition in the sense Weitz declared impossible, because it gives no independent criterion for which objects deserve that status, and it explains the artworld only by already appealing to the idea of art. So the right view is a modified institutionalism: art has no essence, and its boundaries are settled case by case by the artworld's evolving practice."
