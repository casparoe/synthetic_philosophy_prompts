I recently got put on my university's animal care and use committee, and the policy documents are driving me a bit nuts. Any protocol using live vertebrates goes through full ethical review, and the checklist has questions about pain, distress, enrichment, endpoints, all of it. But there's a cutoff that nobody on the committee can quite justify: cephalopods like octopuses are excluded from review entirely, and insects don't count as animals at all under the definitions. I've been reading Bentham's An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, and he says: "Other animals, which, on account of their interests having been neglected by the insensibility of the ancient jurists, stand degraded into the class of things." Our policy seems to be doing exactly that to octopuses and bees, but the official reasons are all practical: staffing, workload, tradition, what other universities do. So my question is: what philosophical commitments does a policy like this actually presuppose about which beings can have interests, or about whether some are merely legal things? Does the vertebrate invertebrate line track anything ethically defensible, or is it just a bureaucratic inheritance with no coherent theory underneath? And what would the policy have to look like if the committee took Bentham's sentence seriously?
