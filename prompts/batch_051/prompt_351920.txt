I am a high school senior, on the editorial board of my school paper and in a philosophy seminar that has just spent several weeks on Kierkegaard's attack on the daily press and the modern public. Before I present next week, I have to write a short statement of my own position, and I want an audit of whether the commitments I have ended up with actually hang together. I would rather be told they do not than defend a loose package in front of the group, but I also do not want to discard something just because it sits awkwardly next to something else on first reading.

Here is the set:

1. I accept Kierkegaard's core complaint about the public: in his sense, the public is an abstraction rather than an agent. It has no will, takes no responsibility, cannot be addressed or rebuked as a someone, and talk of what the public thinks is often a way of concealing that particular persons think particular things.

2. I also accept his diagnosis of the daily press. By making everyone a commentator and no one a committed actor, and by speaking anonymously to an anonymous mass, the press encourages leveling, the erosion of genuine qualitative distinctions and of the seriousness of individual choice.

3. Nevertheless, I hold that a free press, including the right to publish and to speak anonymously, is necessary for democratic accountability, and I have defended that position as an editor.

4. I hold that the deepest ethical and religious truths are first-person truths: their validity has nothing to do with how many people accept them, and everything to do with how a single individual appropriates them.

5. I hold that citizens have a duty to stay in public life, to vote, argue, organize, and try to persuade majorities on questions of justice, rather than treating politics as too corrupt to touch and retreating into private inwardness.

6. I hold that responsibility attaches only to the choices of individual persons, so that a social outcome arising from thousands of small anonymous choices cannot rightly be blamed on any one person, or on society as such.

I cannot tell where the real tensions are, and I do not want to give up a view merely because it is in dialogue with another. Please audit the list for me. Order any genuine tensions by severity; for each, say exactly which commitments are incompatible, what the least costly revision would be, and what that revision costs. Also tell me which pairs only appear to be in tension and why they are not, since I want to keep as much of this as I can without fooling myself.
