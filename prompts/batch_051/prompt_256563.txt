I'm leading a seventy-five-minute session for a reading group of law students and working programmers, none of whom have studied legal philosophy or formal logic. The topic is Nomic: a game in which the rules include rules for changing the rules, so that a move made under a rule can alter the very rule that authorized it. From that single feature I want the session to make the following puzzles feel forced rather than imported: the self-amendment paradox (a rule applied to itself), the abolition paradox (using the amendment procedure to delete the amendment procedure), the puzzle of a rule that declares itself unamendable, and the more general separation between a rule's validity and its content.

Don't explain Nomic or these puzzles to me; plan how I should explain them. Act as a philosopher of law who has taught the logic of rule systems to mixed audiences of lawyers and programmers, and think carefully about the dependency structure before committing to an order of presentation; in particular, anticipate the failure modes of a naive order. The audience is sharp but impatient with formalism for its own sake.

Your plan must specify the one idea that has to be in place before any of the puzzles can be stated, the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what, the single concrete rule-change scenario that carries the whole session, the misconceptions the audience will predictably bring or form and where each should be defused, the check that shows the puzzles have landed, and what to leave out.

Structure the response in exactly these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

5. **End-of-session check** (90 words). One question or task, and what a correct response would show they understand the paradoxes as paradoxes rather than just the game mechanics.

6. **Deliberate omissions** (80 words). What to leave out at this level and a sentence I can use to decline without making the omissions feel like failures.
