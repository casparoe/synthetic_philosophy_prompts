I'm a grad student who got drafted to run a mini reading group on Buddhist ethics for three final-year philosophy undergrads. They've each done one standard intro ethics course—Mill, Kant, a bit of Aristotle—and they keep trying to fit every view into one of those three boxes before they've understood it. Next meeting we're unpacking this passage from the Buddha's advice to Rahula at the Mango Stone in the Ambalatthika-Rahulovada Sutta (MN 61), Thanissaro Bhikkhu's translation:

> Whenever you want to do a bodily action, you should reflect on it: 'This bodily action I want to do — would it lead to self-affliction, to the affliction of others, or to both? Would it be an unskillful bodily action, with painful consequences, painful results?' If, on reflection, you know that it would lead to self-affliction, to the affliction of others, or to both; it would be an unskillful bodily action with painful consequences, painful results, then any bodily action of that sort is absolutely unfit for you to do.

I want the session to land on the idea that this test is soaked in consequence-talk but still isnt obviously consequentialist in the sense they know: the reflection is first-personal rather than a calculation of total welfare, the agent's own affliction matters but not as a unit to be aggregated, and the master category is skillful/unskillful kamma rather than the good. Plan the ninety minutes for me rather than explaining it to me. What do they need to have in place before the passage looks like a problem rather than just a nice rule? What one concrete case should carry the whole discussion, and what does that case need to have and not have? Which misconceptions will they reach for—I'd bet on 'oh, so the Buddha is a utilitarian'—and where in the sequence should each be headed off? Is there anything I should flat-out refuse to settle at this level and say so? And give me one question for the last five minutes that would show whether they've got the distinction rather than learned the word.
