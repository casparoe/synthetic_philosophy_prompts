Over the past year I have developed a rule for myself in political arguments, and I am no longer sure whether the rule is keeping me honest or is just a sophisticated way of refusing to engage. The rule is this: when someone describes an act as terrorism, I do not allow that word to carry any moral weight for me until the speaker can state a definition of terrorism that does two things—it marks some difference between terrorism and other kinds of political violence, and it does not simply amount to "political violence I oppose." In practice, this means I set the label aside, ask whether the speaker would still use it if the same act were committed by a government or a group they supported, and hold off on endorsing the moral judgment the label seems to carry until the definition is actually produced. I adopted the rule because the word seemed to function as a conversation-ender: once an action is called terrorism, people stop asking questions about motives, about violence by states, about who counts as a civilian, or about what the relevant difference is between a bombing and an officially sanctioned military operation.

My brother, who is in college and tends to play devil's advocate, says the rule is intellectual bad faith. He argues that a term can be perfectly meaningful even when a person cannot define it on the spot, and that demanding a definition before I take the underlying claim seriously is really a way of ruling out conclusions I find uncomfortable. I do not think he is entirely wrong, and that is why I am writing this out. Is my rule defensible as an epistemic practice, or is it a bias dressed up as a principle? If it is partly defensible but partly dishonest, is there a revised version of the rule that keeps the good part without the bad faith? And how could I tell from the inside whether I am using the rule to think more clearly rather than to protect a view I already hold?
