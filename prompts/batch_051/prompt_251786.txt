What even is a function in biology? I want a working definition of 'function' that gets these cases right, and I'd rather the definition do the work than just be told the etiological and causal-role camps disagree.

The cases:
1. A heart pumping blood.
2. A brand-new mutation in a single individual that happens to make it a bit more heat-tolerant, in a population where it hasn't spread or been selected yet.
3. A trait that was originally selected for one thing and is now selected for something else (say feathers, thermoregulation first, flight later). Does it have the original function, the current one, both?
4. The redness of blood — a byproduct of oxygen-binding chemistry that does nothing itself.
5. A cave fish's degenerate eye, which no longer forms images but did in its ancestors.
6. A duplicated gene that isn't expressed, does nothing, and is just drifting.
7. A trait that does something useful for the organism now, but got fixed by drift, not selection.
8. The human appendix, which is mostly a liability but maybe helps reboots the gut microbiome after diarrhea. I honestly don't know whether to call that a function.

My hunches: (1) clearly has a function, (4) and (6) clearly don't, and I keep flip-floping on (2), (3), and (7). Propose the definition, run it through all eight, and tell me where it surprises you or where you'd bite the bullet. If you end up thinking 'function' splits into two notions (roughly, what a trait was selected for vs. what it does now), give me both definitions and say which cases land differently under them.
