I'm a retired physicist who's only recently started reading philosophy, so I still reach for back-of-the-envelope expected-value sums, and there's an asymmetry in how ordinary people—and honestly, how I myself—react to two muggings, and I can't get it to sit still. A man in an alley points a gun and says, your wallet or your life. I hand it over, and everyone says that's obviously rational. Another man on the street says, give me five dollars or I'll simulate a trillion trillion copies of you, each tortured for a trillion years. I tell him to get lost, and everyone says that's obviously rational too. But the second threat, taken at face value, is so much worse than the first that the five dollars has essentialy infinite expected value, while the first is worth a few hundred at most. So ordinary judgment says: comply with the small, credible threat, and ignore the enormous, incredible one. Is there a principle that generates both verdicts, or is one of them a mistake? Give me the strongest principle you can find and an honest verdict on whether it works. If you say the difference is just that the second man's claim is wildly improbable, defend that against the version where he first makes me feel a brief, unmistakable jolt of pain at the exact moment and place he predicts, and then repeats his offer.
