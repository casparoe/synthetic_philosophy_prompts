A position paper I'm reading defends functionalism about the mind by leaning on the analogy that the mind is to the brain as software is to hardware. The author's argument, in outline, runs as follows: a program can be characterized at the level of its computational states without specifying the circuitry that runs it; the same program can be implemented in vacuum tubes, silicon, or other substrates; so the mental states a system instantiates are multiply realizable; therefore mental kinds are not kinds of physical material but kinds of computational or causal organization. The author takes this to dispose of the mind–brain identity theory and to show that psychology does not reduce to neuroscience.

The paper seems to be getting a lot of its force from the analogy rather than from an independent argument for computationalism, and I want to know how much weight it can actually bear. Please audit the analogy in detail. Give the correspondence table explicitly: what is supposed to map to what, from the mental/physical side to the software/hardware side, and which features of software are doing the argumentative work. Then identify where the mapping breaks down: which features of the mind are not captured by the software picture, and which features of software are quietly imported into the theory of mind without justification. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the author's two conclusions from merely surface differences. Finally, say whether each conclusion—multiple realizability, and the claim that mental kinds are functional rather than physical kinds—survives once the analogy is set aside, and what work the analogy was doing that the conclusions actually required.
