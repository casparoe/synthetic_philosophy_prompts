So I've been poking through some old Turner for a piece I'm working on about American exceptionalism, and I keep tripping over this passage from Frederick Jackson Turner's essay "The Significance of the Frontier in American History," where he quotes an 1890 census bulletin:

"Up to and including 1880 the country had a frontier of settlement, but at present the unsettled area has been so broken into by isolated bodies of settlement that there can hardly be said to be a frontier line. In the discussion of its extent, its westward movement, etc., it can not, therefore, any longer have a place in the census reports."

Turner reads that dry bureaucratic announcement as the closing of a great historic movement. For him, the existence of free land, its continuous recession, and the advance of American settlement westward explain American development.

Here's the thing I want to work through. American exceptionalism is often talked about as one idea, but it seems to me it splits into at least two: an empirical/historical thesis that frontier conditions produced a distinctive American character, economy, and democratic institutions, and a normative/ideological claim that America has a special moral status or world-historical mission that persists even after those conditions vanish. I want you to compare those two versions, using the census passage as the hinge. Am I wrong to think the normative version could survive the death of the historical thesis? Or does it secretly depend on the frontier story even when its not supposed to?
