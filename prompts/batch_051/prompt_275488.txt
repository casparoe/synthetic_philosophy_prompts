I am the editor-in-chief of a serious independent magazine, print and online, that publishes reported features, essays, and a small number of unsigned institutional editorials. I read roughly twenty submissions or pitches a week, and my deputy editors and I decide what to run, what to revise, and how to frame it for readers. The stakes are the magazine's editorial identity and the kind of reader it addresses, not only traffic or subscriptions. I have been reading Kierkegaard's attack on the daily press and on what he took to be its peculiar product: an anonymous thing he called the public, an abstraction composed of everyone and no one, in whose name opinions circulate without any particular person being answerable for them. On his account, the press trains its readers in limitless chatter about everything and commitment about nothing; it levels distinctions by treating the trivial and the momentous with the same detached, knowing tone; and its real alternative is address to the single, concrete individual, called to self-examination and decision rather than to spectatorship. I want to put that diagnosis to work without turning the magazine into a pulpit.

Please write a reusable editorial decision procedure, grounded in the distinction between copy that addresses a concrete individual and copy that feeds the public-as-abstraction, that my editors can run on any submission or planned piece in about fifteen minutes. The deliverable should be a sequence of questions in the order they should be asked, with criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no or a revise, and where it hands the decision to a named person because the question is one of judgment rather than rule. Preserve the distinctions in Kierkegaard's critique that actually change an editorial decision, and drop or compress the ones that do not; but the procedure must be usable by line editors who have not read Kierkegaard, so explain each test in plain editorial language. Flag explicitly every step that is a judgment call dressed up as a checklist item.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

1. A commissioned essay arguing that the serious life is a trap, that every cause is ultimately a performance, and that the honest stance is ironic distance from all commitments.

2. A reported news piece about a sharp moral controversy in which each side's claims are reported almost entirely through what anonymous supporters are saying online, with the reporter's own assessment withheld and no attempt to say what the disagreement would require of a person who held either view.

3. Our own weekly unsigned roundup of sharp, funny, much-shared takes on the week's events, which is one of our most-read features.

At least one of these should return an answer I will not like, and the procedure should make clear why that answer follows anyway. Finish with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do: the ways it will misfire, the kinds of pieces it will wrongly pass or wrongly kill, and why the underlying distinction cannot be turned into a mechanical test.
