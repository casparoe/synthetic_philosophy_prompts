I've come to believe that the familiar polysemy of ordinary words is largely not semantic. On this view, a word such as 'open' has one highly abstract standing meaning, and the many dictionary senses—open a door, open a meeting, open an account, open water, an open question—are generated by context, world knowledge, and general reasoning applied to that common core rather than stored as distinct lexical entries. Treat this as the position under examination and tell me what would count against it. I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward. What phenomena would force the conclusion that distinct senses are genuinely lexical—for example, divergent antonyms across uses (shut a door, close an account, adjourn a meeting, a closed question), derivational or inflectional behavior that tracks one sense but not another, differences in truth-conditional contribution, cross-linguistic gaps that pattern by sense, or the felt oddness of zeugma and the workings of puns? Which of these would a committed one-meaning theorist concede as genuine counterevidence, and which would they re-describe as merely pragmatic? And has the view insulated itself by assigning every hard case to 'context,' and if so, is that a problem for a thesis about where semantics stops and pragmatics begins—or for the question of whether words have meanings at all?
