I have been reading Locke's _Some Thoughts Concerning Education_ after seeing it described as one of the roots of modern liberal parenting, and I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a settled word for. Locke spends a great deal of the book telling parents to instil habits and self-denial in children before they are capable of reasoning, and he is quite frank that this early stage relies on the child's fear and reverence for the parent rather than on understanding. He tells parents to keep children in subjection whilst young, and to become more familiar with them as they grow older and come to the use of reason. He also says that children should be won over by esteem and credit rather than blows, that learning should be made a play rather than a task, and that the parent's authority should gradually be relaxed until the child is treated as a friend. The same structure seems to recur in different areas: an early stage of imposed discipline justified by the child's present incapacity, followed by a deliberate handing over of control as the child becomes capable of it, with the parent's authority being self-limiting rather than permanent. Is there an established name for this specific two-stage pattern, and for the view of parental authority that goes with it — where the authority is legitimate only insofar as it works to make itself unnecessary? I know this is adjacent to paternalism and to "authoritative parenting" in developmental psychology, but I'm after the precise concept for the authority-that-dissolves-itself structure. If there is no name, what would you call it, and where are the edges: what distinguishes this from ordinary education on the one hand and from manipulation or grooming on the other? I am not looking for a bare label; I'm interested in the carving. If I'm reading the structure into Locke too neatly, or conflating different things he says for different reasons, I'd rather be told that than have a tidy answer that fits only a forced version of the text.
