I am a novelist at work on a near-future political novel, and I need to know whether one of the institutions I have invented would actually function as I imagine, or whether it would collapse under its own incentives.

In the novel, the country has replaced its environmental permitting system with a welfare-based test for land use, administered by an agency called the Wild Welfare Exchange. Any project that converts or degrades a natural area above a de minimis size must first obtain a net-welfare permit. The Exchange commissions an assessment that models, over a forty-year horizon, the expected change in the aggregate welfare of all sentient wild animals affected by the project, expressed in welfare points. For each affected species, the model estimates population trajectories under the project and under a no-project counterfactual, assigns each individual-year a signed value — negative when the model judges the life in question to contain more suffering than enjoyment — and sums over the affected individuals across the horizon. A permit is granted if the applicant's projected sum is non-negative, or if the applicant purchases enough offset credits to bring the total to non-negative. Offset credits are issued for funded interventions in the same ecological region, such as vaccination, supplementary feeding during drought, predator control, or fencing that keeps animals out of newly built infrastructure, whose projected welfare-point surplus relative to an additionality counterfactual is certified by the very office that assessed the original project. The panel that sets species weights and the models themselves are revised every five years, but permits already granted are not reopened.

I am less interested in whether this system is justifiable in principle than in how it would actually behave. I would like you to walk me through its incentives and its likely failure modes in concrete terms. Who would first invest in learning to game the models, and what would their opening moves be? What happens once developers work out which kinds of land-use change the models treat as welfare-improving? How would the market for offsets develop, and what would count as a good offset project in practice? How might the weight-setting panel be captured, and by whom? Where would political pressure on the Exchange accumulate first? Could the obvious failure modes be repaired without abandoning the welfare-point framework entirely?

If the mechanism as I have described it is incoherent, or if some part of the task as posed cannot be done well, I would rather you tell me so plainly than supply a polite but artificial analysis. I can revise the novel's machinery; what I need to know is which parts, if any, describe a plausible institution and which parts rest on an assumption that no refinement could make workable.
