I'm taking a seminar on diachronic rationality this term, and we spent two weeks on money-pump arguments for why preferences ought to be transitive. Everyone in class keeps re-describing the same toy case: someone has a strict cycle of preferences over three bundles, a con man trades them around the loop, they pay a fee at each step, and they end up back where they started with less money. That's fine as far as it goes, but it leaves me with a nagging worry: is the irrationality in the *preferences* or in the *choice procedure* the person uses? For my term paper I want to make the case that a money pump can run on the choice rule while the underlying preferences stay perfectly acyclic.

So here's what I'd like you to build. Construct a money pump where the agent's underlying strict preference relation is acyclic—there's genuinely no cycle of 'strictly better than' anywhere in their values—and yet the pump extracts real money from them. The engine of the pump should be the tie-breaking or aggregation rule they use when their values don't give a verdict: I suspect something like three ordinary criteria they refuse to weigh against each other, with a pairwise-majority rule (or another fixed rule—no framing effects, no preference change, no time discounting, no dishonest salesperson). The objects should be ordinary enough that a student could actually face the choice—used phones or laptops, that kind of thing—and no lotteries. The agent has to be free to stop at any point, and the losses should come from small price differences they willingly pay on each swap becuase the current rule says the new item beats the old one. After you set up the scenario, do the check for me: list the options and the underlying criteria, state the partial preference order, explain why the strict preference relation really is acyclic, give the tie-breaking rule for each pair, and walk through each offer and why the agent takes it. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if you make the tie-breaking rule consistent across all pairs, and finally give me the revealed-preference theorist's comeback: they'd say the choice cycle just *is* the agent's preference cycle, so the distinction I'm banking on is fake. I want an honest answer about whether that reply collapses the case, or whether there's a seperate kind of money pump that targets choice rules rather than preferences.
