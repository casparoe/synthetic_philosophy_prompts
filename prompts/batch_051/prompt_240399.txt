A friend who works in security policy and I got into an argument about whether nuclear crises are more stable than they look, and he kept coming back to the same picture: two nuclear powers in a standoff are like two drivers playing chicken, and the one who visibly throws the steering wheel out the window wins. His claim was that this isn't just a colourful way of speaking—it's the actual logic of deterrence: the state that convinces the other it cannot or will not back down gets its way, so a reputation for being a little irrational, or a domestic setup that makes backing down politically impossible, is an asset. He brought up the Cuban Missile Crisis as the standard example, with the usual lesson that Khrushchev backed down and therefore credibility is everything.

I'd like you to take that analogy seriously and test how far it holds. First, give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the two cars, the speed, the cliff, the steering wheel, the act of swerving, and the spectators in an actual nuclear confrontation. Then work through where the mapping breaks. I've got my own hunches—chicken is one-shot and roughly symmetric, while nuclear standoffs are repeated, the two sides have different arsenal sizes and risk tolerances, nobody has perfect information about the other side's intentions, and there are command-and-control accidents, allies, and domestic audiences in the background—but I want you to be systematic, and to say which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusions people draw and which are just surface differences. Then the core question: which of the usual lessons survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Specifically, (a) that the more committed or less scared side has the bargaining advantage, (b) that a reputation for irrationality or for being unable to back down is useful, and (c) that crises are stable because both sides understand this logic. Finally, say what a better analogy would be, if one exists, and what the chicken picture is quietly smuggling into the story that a real nuclear standoff doesn't have.

Also, as you go, tell me how confident you are in the main claims and single out the point you're least sure of—even if that's the part where you recommend a better analogy.
