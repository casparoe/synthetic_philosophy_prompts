I am writing a paper for my high school philosophy class on the Liar paradox and the main theories of truth that respond to it, and I have been trying to construct a parametric variation that tests whether those theories track the difference between paradoxical and non-paradoxical self-reference. I would like you to run the analysis in detail.

Consider a list of n written sentences S_1, ..., S_n, with n ≥ 1, and a nonnegative integer k. In the “at least” family, every S_i asserts: “At least k of the sentences in this list are false.” In the “at most” family, every S_i asserts: “At most k of the sentences in this list are false.” Work within a classical background rule: a sentence is true just in case what it says is the case, and false otherwise.

First, give the complete bivalent analysis. For each family, determine all consistent assignments of truth and falsity to S_1, ..., S_n as a function of n and k, showing the reasoning. Identify which parameter values recover the original Liar sentence and which recover the Truth-teller.

Second, apply two standard responses to the Liar, case by case. (1) A Tarski-style response: it is illegitimate for a sentence to apply “false” to sentences of its own language in this self-referential way, so the relevant sentences should be ruled out. (2) A Kripke-style truth-value-gap response, specified by the following groundedness test: a sentence is grounded true if its truth follows from the non-semantic facts alone (that there are exactly n sentences, the value of k, and what each sentence asserts); grounded false if its falsity follows from those facts alone; and otherwise neither true nor false.

Finally, state the philosophical upshot. Does the parametric change make these responses agree where the original Liar divided them, or does it reveal a new disagreement? If they agree for some values of n and k and come apart for others, specify exactly where the boundary falls, and say which feature of the original Liar each response is tracking or ignoring.
