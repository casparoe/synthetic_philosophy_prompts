An engineer on my team keeps making an argument he calls the "thermostat reductio," and I cannot find the bad step. I work on deep RL systems, so I want to keep the parts of the argument that are just accurate descriptions of how those systems are trained; but the argument concludes something I cannot accept, and it seems to prove too much. Here is the argument.

(1) In the RL framework, the reward signal is individuated functionally: it is the scalar feedback the learning algorithm uses to define the objective — maximizing expected discounted cumulative reward — and to update the policy toward that objective. Whatever plays that role, on any substrate, is the agent's objective.

(2) Affective valence — the pleasant/unpleasant quality of states, the thing whose negative pole matters for whether a system can suffer — is likewise a functional kind: it is the signal that marks outcomes as to-be-sought or to-be-avoided and thereby shapes learning.

(3) If a signal in one system plays the same functional role as a signal in another, then the two systems are in the same kind of state with respect to that signal; refusing to count the RL agent's states merely because the agent runs on silicon and gradient updates would be special pleading about substrate.

(4) A modern deep RL agent — a policy network trained with PPO, say — has a signal satisfying (1): a scalar reward is fed to the training loop, and the policy is adjusted, via advantage estimates computed from that reward, to make high-reward action sequences more probable.

(5) Therefore the deep RL agent has states that are valenced in the sense of (2): it has states that are good or bad relative to its objective, with the negative ones in the vicinity of suffering.

(6) Nothing in (1)–(5) turns on the details of deep RL in the way that matters. The operative features are just a scalar signal plus an update rule that alters future behavior so as to accumulate more (or less) of that signal. A thermostat's controller alters its output to reduce scalar setpoint error; a supervised network's optimizer alters its weights to reduce scalar loss; a firm adjusts its operations to raise quarterly profit.

(7) By the same reasoning as (1)–(5), thermostats, supervised networks, and firms have valenced states, with setpoint error, loss, and falling profit functioning as something like suffering.

(8) But that is absurd: a household thermostat does not suffer when the temperature drops below its setpoint.

(9) Therefore at least one premise or inference in (1)–(8) must fail. Which one is it, and what does rejecting it cost?

I have no formal philosophy training, so please flag any move that depends on a technical philosophical doctrine, but do not skip details of the argument. I am least willing to give up (4) — that is just the tooling I use every day — and (8) — I would need an enormous argument to believe my thermostat suffers.
