A friend and I recently argued far too late into the evening about whether the causal exclusion argument is best read as a threat to mental causation itself or only to the causal relevance of mental properties within non-reductive physicalism, and we never quite resolved it. I am now grading an exam on exactly this material, and I would like a careful evaluation of the student answer below. Please assign a grade (a letter grade or a score out of 10 is fine) and provide the specific, point-by-point comments that justify that grade. I want the comments to distinguish clearly between what the answer gets right and where it goes wrong, with attention to the precise formulation of Kim's principles and the level — events or properties — at which the argument is supposed to operate.

Exam question: "Reconstruct Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument against non-reductive physicalism, and explain whether it forces the conclusion that mental properties are epiphenomenal even if mental events are token-identical with physical events."

Student answer: "Kim's causal exclusion argument aims to show that non-reductive physicalism cannot accommodate mental causation. Non-reductive physicalism says that mental properties supervene on physical properties but are not identical with them. The argument turns on the causal closure of the physical, which says that every physical event is determined by a prior chain of physical causes, so a physical event's cause must already be fixed within the physical domain. Now suppose my craving for water causes my hand to reach for the glass. The hand movement is a physical event, and closure guarantees that it has a sufficient physical cause in the preceding neural activity. But the craving, on the non-reductive picture, is a distinct mental property realized by that neural activity; so the movement would appear to have two sufficient causes, the neural event and the craving. Kim's exclusion principle says that no event can have more than one sufficient cause at a time except in a genuine case of causal overdetermination. Since it would be unreasonable to accept that every ordinary piece of behavior is systematically overdetermined, Kim concludes that the physical cause excludes the mental one. Applied across the board, this means mental events never cause physical events, and the mental is epiphenomenal. A non-reductive physicalist might reply that the purported problem dissolves once we see that the mental event and its physical realizer are the same event picked out under different descriptions. Kim's response, however, is that once mental events are identified with physical events, mental properties become reducible to physical properties, and that is no longer non-reductive physicalism. The identity reply therefore gives up the position it was trying to defend. I think the argument succeeds in forcing the non-reductive physicalist to accept epiphenomenalism about the mental."
