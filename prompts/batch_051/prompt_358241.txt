I'm taking a course on ancient philosophy and we've just gotten to Parmenides, and I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether the Eleatic denial of change is just a historical curiousity or whether there's a live argument underneath it. I tried to reconstruct the reasoning in a clean modern form, and it now unsettles me:

1. You can think or speak only about what is. (To think of nothing is not to think at all, and to speak of nothing is to say nothing.)
2. So "what is not" — non-being — is not something you can think or speak about.
3. But any thought or statement of the form "x is not F" is about what is not: it is about x's not being F.
4. Therefore no thought or statement of the form "x is not F" is possible.
5. Yet change requires something of the form "x is not F" to be true: when soup cools, the very same soup is hot at one time and not hot at a later time.
6. And what cannot be thought or said cannot be. (The Parmenidean principle that thinking and being go together.)
7. Therefore change is impossible.

I can't find the slight of hand. Step 1 feels airtight, and step 5 just seems definitional, but the conclusion can't be right. I suspect the same machinery would also rule out plurality, since "A is not B" is a negative claim too, but change is the part I'm stuck on. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher, ideally one sympathetic to Parmenides but not already committed to the conclusion, and tell me exactly which numbered step, if any, fails. Don't just say "equivocation": show me the competing readings of "what is not" and "about," and trace what happens to the rest of the argument on each reading. Start by brainstorming the candidate weak points (I keep flipping between 3 and 6) before you settle on the diagnosis, and be specific about whether the argument fails once or twice over.
