I'm in a seminar this term on democratic theory and epistocracy (we've read Brennan, Estlund, Landemore, that crowd), and I also sit on the governance committee for my housing co-op, which is about 300 people run as a direct democracy. So I want to make the class do actual work for once.

Our co-op votes on everything at general meetings, one member one vote, and it's been going badly in a specific way: people show up and vote on the budget, or a maintenance contract, or a lawsuit settlement, without having read the documents. There's been some expensive mistakes. One faction now wants to move to a "competence-weighted" system where your vote counts in proportion to how much you demonstrably know about the issue (they literally call it epistocracy, because one of them took the same class). Another faction says any departure from one-person-one-vote destroys the legitimacy of the co-op and would just entrench whichever members have spare time and the right kind of background.

The committee has been asked to draft a reusable procedure—a checklist or a decision tree in words—that the co-op can use, issue by issue, to decide whether a decision goes to the full membership on a straight majority vote, or whether some issue-specific competence gate or vote weighting applies, and, if so, how that gate gets designed and audited. Constraints: it has to be usable by members who won't read political theory and won't sit through long deliberative exercises—say 10 minutes of orientation per vote. It gets used about twice a month. The stakes are real (rent, evictions, the mortgage). And at the end of the year the whole membership has to vote on whether to adopt the thing, so it can't be a poison pill.

Ground the procedure in the actual debate, don't just tell me "be reasonable about who votes." I want to know which distinctions from the literature survive being translated into a 10-minute procedure: issue-specific competence versus general competence, ex ante legitimacy versus ex post correctness, and the demographic objection—that any test of political knowledge tends to track education/class/race and quietly disenfranchise people. Mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated, and say where the procedure will predictably misfire.

Then apply it to three proposals currently on our agenda: (1) whether to refinance the building's mortgage (a 20-page term sheet, and you'd need to know what a prepayment penalty and a break-even point are); (2) whether to adopt a noise policy with fines (everyone lives there, no technical background needed to have a real stake); and (3) whether to spend $8,000 on a mural in the common room—make sure the procedure forces the committee to swallow something it doesn't like on that last one.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, especially where a competence gate would be self-serving, or where the epistemic gains are too small to justify the democratic cost.
