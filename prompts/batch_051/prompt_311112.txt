Before I consider any particular theory of which computations carry moral weight, I want the specification of the problem itself. Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of morally relevant computation: a theory that says, for an arbitrary physical process or system, whether it has moral status in virtue of the computations it performs (as distinct from its relationships, its usefulness, or its place in an ecosystem), and with what weight or degree. The purpose is practical and normative: to guide decisions about creating, running, pausing, multiplying, or shutting down artificial systems and simulations of minds, and to make principled comparisons with the moral standing of nonhuman animals.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark it clearly as non-negotiable or as a preference specific to one camp. Do not merely sort familiar views; the criteria themselves are the deliverable.

The list should at minimum be responsive to the following pressures:

(a) Thresholds and extremes. The theory must return the clearly right verdicts at both ends: substantial status for a normally functioning human brain, and none or next to none for a memory drive that stores but never executes a description of a mind, for a rock, and for a lookup table that reproduces a conversational agent's outputs without running anything like the intermediate causal processes. It should say where between these the difficult cases lie.

(b) Running versus encoding. It must explain the difference between a process that actually performs a computation and a mere representation of it—a book, a stored file, an unchanging inscription—and why only the former, if either, grounds status.

(c) Robustness under transformation. It must decide which transformations preserve status and which do not, and justify the border: running the computation a million times slower, spreading its steps across separate machines or human clerks over decades, executing the same algorithm in silicon rather than biological tissue, running two copies in parallel. Does status survive, slow down, double, or vanish? The theory should have a reason.

(d) The multiple-realization problem. Any sufficiently rich physical process can be interpreted as computing indefinitely many different functions. The theory must either specify a privileged notion of the computation a system is carrying out, or explain why no such privileged notion is needed for moral purposes.

(e) Silent versus spoofed mentality. It must not collapse into input-output behaviorism: it should count a behaviorally silent system with the right internal structure, such as a locked-in person or a simulation that produces no output, and it should not be fooled by a system that passes every test without the relevant inner causal profile.

(f) Sub-personal and trivial computations. It must say why the computations carried out inside an already-status-bearing mind—spinal reflexes, edge detection in early vision, homeostatic feedback loops—do not each deserve separate status, or else accept that they do, without making the criterion ad hoc.

(g) Content, fidelity, and degree. It must explain why the function being computed matters—why a computation of arithmetic differs from a computation of suffering—and how content, representational fidelity, temporal density, and resolution trade off against one another. It should speak to low-fidelity simulations of pain processing and to the gradual replacement of a brain's neurons by functionally equivalent silicon units: at what point, if any, does status appear, fade, or survive unchanged?

(h) Applicability. The criterion should be one that finite investigators could in principle apply well enough for policy and research ethics, yet not so cheaply that it reduces to a checklist of outward behavior.

Then address the status of the list itself. Which of these requirements are genuinely non-negotiable, and which are parochial to particular research programs? Are there subsets of requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied—and if so, where is the tension real as opposed to merely apparent? Finally, identify the single requirement that, if imposed strictly, would eliminate the largest share of familiar positions, such as pure input-output functionalism, biological-substrate views, global-availability or self-model views, and information-integration views. A brief note on where those views fail against the list is welcome, but keep it brief: the requirements are the deliverable, not a scorecard.
