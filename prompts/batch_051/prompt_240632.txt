The question I set was:

"Mill is usually read as the great defender of individual liberty. Yet his later writings contain steadily more sympathetic treatments of socialism. Explain, with reference to his distinction between the natural constraints on production and the social arrangements of distribution, how much of the socialist programme Mill came to accept, where he stopped short, and what route of transition he favoured."

And here is the student's answer:

"Mill's late position is best described as a qualified socialism. He accepted the socialist claim that private ownership of the means of production is not a fact of nature but a social arrangement to be judged by its results, and he thought the existing wage-system was defective because workers were shut out of ownership and control. In the Autobiography and the unfinished chapters on socialism he frames the problem of the future as one of reconciling extensive individual freedom with collective ownership of the world's natural resources and a fair sharing of the gains from cooperative production, so he plainly was not defending the status quo. He also keeps the distinction between the constraints on production, which are natural, and the arrangements for distribution, which are conventional. Where he stopped short, on this reading, was the administrative machinery of revolutionary socialism: he thought a centralized planning state would threaten liberty and require more public spirit than people currently possess, and he worried about incentives under common ownership. His stated route was gradual, through profit-sharing and eventually firms owned and run by the workers themselves. But because he classified distribution as conventional, he reasoned that the only real obstacle to socialism was the historical entrenchment of property, and he concluded that a complete legal transfer of the means of production to the community should be carried out as soon as public opinion allowed, with cooperative associations serving as a temporary testing-ground. And since he located the threat to liberty in the wage relation rather than in any possible arrangement of common ownership, he thought that once that relation was abolished the problem of combining socialism with individual liberty would have largely resolved itself."
