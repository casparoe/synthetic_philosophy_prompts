I'm a PhD student working on the formal side of AI alignment and on what impossibility results can and cannot show about 'alignment in principle.' I keep noticing a structural parallel that I haven't seen anyone press, and I'd like help deciding whether it is deep or superficial.

The parallel is between Arrow's impossibility theorem and a family of arguments that alignment is unsolvable in principle. Arrow's theorem, as I understand it, says that with three or more alternatives, no aggregation procedure can turn arbitrary individual preference rankings into a transitive social ranking while satisfying all of unrestricted domain, Pareto efficiency, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship. The dialectical shape of that result is: set down four innocuous-looking requirements, prove they conflict, and thereby force a debate about which requirement to relax. Now consider an alignment-side argument with the same shape. Take a goal-directed system that should (i) work across a genuinely open-ended range of tasks and goal statements, not just a curated test distribution (the unrestricted-domain analogue); (ii) choose A over B whenever every reasonable reading of the stated goal ranks A above B (the Pareto analogue); (iii) have its comparison of A and B depend only on what the goal says about A and B — not on framing, correlated features, or unmentioned side effects — so that it cannot be steered into specification gaming (the independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives analogue); and (iv) not simply enact one fixed hidden objective or one party's preferences regardless of the stated goal (the non-dictatorship analogue). One can argue that no implementable system satisfies all four, and on the strength of that argument claim that alignment is impossible in principle.

But I suspect the analogy is doing illicit work. In social choice, Arrow's result was not the end of democratic theory; it showed that exact aggregation has no solution and pushed the field toward relaxations, approximations, and domain restrictions, and democracies limp along with imperfect aggregation. Does the alignment-side impossibility have that same status — a result about jointly satisfying ideal axioms, compatible with systems that are approximately goal-faithful under the right assumptions — or does 'in principle' mean something stronger there, because the relevant failures are not preference cycles but policies a human would reject outright, which would make approximation itself unsafe? I keep going back and forth on this with colleagues, including some social choice people, and we end up talking past each other.

Please structure your reply in five titled sections, in this order: (1) 'The mapping' — about a paragraph: formalize the parallel as charitably as you can, including each axiomatic correspondence; (2) 'Where the parallel holds' — one to two paragraphs: the strongest case that the two impossibility results have the same epistemic status; (3) 'Where it breaks' — two to three paragraphs and the longest section: the disanalogies, especially what 'in principle' means on each side and whether approximation is equally tolerable in both; (4) 'A prediction that fails' — a short paragraph: name something concrete the analogy would lead one to expect about alignment that is false or unsubstantiated; (5) 'Verdict' — two to four sentences: whether the parallel is deep, superficial, or a category confusion, with the core reason.
