Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about the Judy Benjamin problem, and I came away suspecting that the debate over it has stalled for structural reasons rather than because anyone is bad at calculating posteriors. The setup, as we were considering it, goes like this. Judy is a paratrooper dropped at night into a region divided into four quadrants: Red Headquarters, Red Second, Blue Headquarters, and Blue Second. Initially she assigns a credence of 0.25 to each quadrant. A radio message tells her that, conditional on her being in Red territory, the odds are three to one that she is in the Headquarters quadrant rather than the Second quadrant; the message gives no corresponding information about Blue territory. The question is how she should revise her four probabilities.

My friend argued that the message should be treated as a constraint on the posterior, and that the only principled way to select a unique posterior is to minimize information gain relative to the prior; this yields a posterior in which the probability of being in Red territory shifts slightly. I argued that Adams conditioning is better: it leaves the probability of being in Red territory unchanged and only adjusts the ratio between the two Red quadrants. Each of us could do the arithmetic, and each of us could see that the other's conclusion followed from a prior choice of update rule, but neither of us could justify that choice without simply assuming the point at issue. In the wider philosophical debate, the same pattern seems to repeat: there are several formally consistent rules for updating on conditional information, and their proponents appear to talk past one another. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about probability theory but about what it means to learn something in the first place — whether a conditional probability is information about Judy's location, a constraint on her posterior, or something else. I would prefer a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a survey of the rules. Is this a disguised dispute about the metaphysics of information? About the role of the learning event or protocol? About what rational credence changes are supposed to preserve? And given your diagnosis, is there anything — a theorem, a formal distinction, a sharper example — that would count as progress, or should I expect the debate to remain stalled because the question is underdetermined? I would appreciate an answer in American English.
