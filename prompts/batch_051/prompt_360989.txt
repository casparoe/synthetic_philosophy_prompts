I'm a philosophy professor, and I've caught myself following a policy I'd like you to evaluate. When I'm facing a real decision — whether to leave a job, how to vote on a departmental motion, whether to send a risky email — I refuse to assign probabilities to my own choices. I'll happily assign probabilities to other people's choices, to chance events, even to my own past choices, but never to the thing I'm currently deliberating about. The reason is that forming a credence about what I'll decide feels like stepping into the role of a spectator toward myself: the prediction crowds out the deliberation, and the number is either a leak from a decision I've already half-made or a way of binding my future self under cover of forecasting. But I also believe I'm a physical system that other people can model, and my colleagues sometimes predict my votes better than I do. So the rule may just be a bias protecting a flattering picture of my own agency at the cost of accuracy. Is this policy defensible, or is it a shortcut dressed up as a principle? If a cleaned-up version is defensible, state the best version of it and say where it stops applying.
