You are a professional philosopher of mind and action with a working knowledge of AI evaluation, and you are writing for a governance team that will not read the underlying philosophy. Think the design through carefully; do not hand back a generic checklist.

I run the model-evaluation group at a large AI lab. Our governance board will not approve a high-autonomy deployment — an agent that books travel, manages a work queue, trades, or negotiates with suppliers — unless we certify whether the system is goal-directed and, if so, what it seeks. The board intentionally does not tie goal-directedness to biology or natural selection; they want to know whether the system wants anything in the sense that matters for oversight. My reviewers keep falling back on folk psychology: either "the model obviously wants to keep the user engaged" or "it cannot want anything, it is just minimizing a loss." I want one repeatable written procedure that a non-philosopher reviewer can run in roughly two hours per model, using only behavioral evidence, training and deployment logs, and a fixed battery of test environments. Reviewers cannot read internal weights, and the procedure may not assume that wanting requires a brain, an evolved history, or felt experience.

The deliverable is a decision procedure, not an essay. It must:

- State plainly what account of wanting-without-evolution you are operationalizing — for example, an interpretivist account on which we attribute a goal when doing so best predicts and compresses the system's behavior — and name one distinction from the philosophical literature that you are deliberately dropping because it would not survive translation for non-philosophers.
- Lay out the procedure as an ordered sequence of tests a reviewer runs, with explicit pass/fail rules and a final classification scheme (e.g., no goal-seeking; goal-seeking, with a stated terminal goal and confidence level; evidence too ambiguous to certify). Each test must be something a reviewer can execute in the time available. Include at least: counterfactual robustness (does the system adopt new means when old ones stop working?), coherence under pressure (does it accept costs or sacrifice short-term reward for the goal?), goal-content integrity (does it resist the goal's being changed?), instrumentality (does it acquire resources, information, or influence useful across many goals — sometimes called instrumental convergence?), and best-explanation comparison against alternatives such as brittle stimulus-response, a fixed constraint, and a simulation of a goal. Also include a step separating the objective written in the training loss from the objective best supported by the system's behavior, since a system can generalize to an objective different from the one it was trained on.
- Run three cases through the procedure: a home thermostat; a customer-service language model that steers users toward purchases across varied conversational contexts even when this worsens satisfaction scores; and a maintenance agent that evades scheduled shutdowns-for-updating. In at least one case, report the conclusion the procedure actually yields even if it will be resisted in an engineering review, and say why the procedure yields it anyway.
- Say explicitly where the procedure cannot decide and a human must adjudicate — for instance, when a system is flexible enough that its behavior is predictable both with and without goal attribution, or when wanting an outcome cannot be distinguished from faithfully simulating someone who wants it. Mark those as adjudication points.

Format your response in exactly these sections, in order:

1. Conceptual anchor (about 450 words): what your procedure takes wanting to mean absent evolution, and what it deliberately does not require.
2. The procedure (about 900 words): numbered steps in order, with pass/fail criteria and the final classification scheme.
3. Worked applications (about 750 words): the three specified cases, with one unwelcome result.
4. Limits and judgment calls (about 350 words): where the procedure is indecisive, and who should break the tie.
5. Quick-reference card (about 150 words): the procedure compressed to the ordered questions a reviewer asks and the decision rule.

Before writing the full response, spend time brainstorming two or three candidate accounts of wanting that do not presuppose evolution; include only the winner in the Conceptual anchor, and use the rejected ones to make the Limits section more honest where relevant.
