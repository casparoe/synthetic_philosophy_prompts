I'm a high school student doing an independent study on practical rationality, and I've been trying to get clear about a puzzle called Kavka's toxin puzzle. In the version I'm working with, a reliable billionaire offers you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a painful but harmless and nonlethal liquid tomorrow at noon; the money will be deposited before noon regardless of whether you actually drink. The intuitive difficulty is that the intention seems worth a million dollars, so forming it seems rational in one sense, but drinking the liquid at noon seems obviously irrational once the money is secured, and many people also think you cannot genuinely intend to do what you know you will not do. Before I decide where I come down, I want a specification of what any adequate theory of the rationality of intention would have to get right. I am not asking for a bibliography or a summary of who said what; I want the requirements themselves.

Specifically, please write the desiderata that a theory of rational intention must satisfy if it is to handle this puzzle and its close variants. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it. I take it the list will have to include things like: accounting for the apparent gap between the rationality of forming an intention and the rationality of the action intended; respecting the difference between intending, wishing, predicting, and merely saying you intend; and not making intentions into magical sources of reasons while also not making them irrelevant to later action. But I would like you to check which of these are genuine requirements and which are just habits of thinking about the puzzle, and whether any pair of requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. Please rank them by how much they constrain the available theories, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are optional.

Finally, because I want to test how solid this list is, tell me where your reasoning is least secure: which desideratum you would be most willing to drop or revise, and what argument or counterexample would change your mind about it.
