I am the president of my school's student government, and I make decisions at least once or twice a week about whether to push a proposal now, postpone it, accept a compromise, or spend weeks quietly building support. The stakes are small by adult standards but real to us: a badly timed push can cost us a meeting with the principal, a faculty ally, or a policy we have worked on for two years, and my faculty advisor, whose sign-off is required for anything we do, will block anything she thinks is reckless. I have been reading Machiavelli, and his distinction between fortune and virtù seems to describe my situation exactly if I understand it correctly: fortune is what lies outside my control, especially timing and the changing moods of other people, while virtù is not ordinary moral virtue but the capacity to act decisively, judge circumstances accurately, and adapt as conditions change. What I cannot do is convert that distinction into a procedure I can actually run in the few minutes before a meeting, when I am tired, when I cannot explain the theory to the students I lead, and when the decision will not wait.

Do not write me a summary of Machiavelli. Write a reusable decision procedure — an ordered set of questions or tests with plain-language labels — that tells me when Machiavelli's framework says to act boldly and when it says to wait, prepare, or let circumstances develop. Ground the procedure in what Machiavelli's account of fortune and virtù actually provides, including his idea that one should use calm periods to build the banks and channels that will contain fortune when it floods, and his worry that the same temperament can succeed in one set of circumstances and fail in another when the times change. Build in a step that separates what can be prepared in advance from what must simply be met as it arrives, and a short list of signs that I am confusing virtù with recklessness or with stubbornness.

Then apply the procedure to three decisions I actually face. First, a sympathetic teacher has just been transferred, and I have to decide whether to force a vote this month on the outdoor-lunch pilot the administration has been ignoring. Second, the vice principal has offered a weak compromise on a different proposal, and I have to decide whether to take it now or risk losing it by waiting for a better moment. Third, a loud and popular proposal is about to go forward with strong student support, but I believe advancing it will cause the faculty to retaliate by killing an unrelated project I care about. Give at least one worked example in which the procedure reaches a conclusion I will not like. Finally, say explicitly where the procedure stops being Machiavelli and becomes a judgment call, and where a small student government is different enough from Machiavelli's political world that the analogy should not be pressed. Please write the response in American English.
