I am a philosophy PhD student, and in my metaphysics reading group last month two people in my cohort—Priya and Daniel—spent most of the evening on Hempel's dilemma for physicalism. I have reconstructed their exchange as faithfully as I can from my notes:

Priya: Physicalism is only a definite claim if "physical" has a definite extension. If it means "whatever current physics quantifies over," then physicalism is hostage to a theory we know to be incomplete. Current physics has no settled account of quantum gravity or dark matter, and earlier physics was repeatedly overturned. On that reading, physicalism is almost certainly false.

Daniel: Then take "physical" to mean the entities and properties that a completed, ideal physics would posit. Physicalism says that everything is among whatever final physics describes.

Priya: That makes the thesis empty. We do not possess the completed physics, so you cannot specify which properties count as physical. Worse, if finished physics had to posit fundamental phenomenal or proto-phenomenal properties to account for consciousness, your reading would count those as physical. Physicalism would then exclude none of the positions it was supposed to exclude; it would come out true no matter what the world contains.

Daniel: That follows only if we put no prior constraint on what ideal physics can posit. Constrain it as a science that never posits fundamentally mental entities—the science of the non-mental, whatever that turns out to contain. Then ideal physics cannot simply absorb phenomenal properties.

Priya: That is a via negativa definition. You have defined "physical" through "non-mental," and physicalism collapses into "there are no fundamental mental entities." That is not an account of what the world is made of; it is only the denial of one rival.

Daniel: A denial of a rival can be fully substantive. "No fundamental mental entities" rules out panpsychism, idealism, Cartesian dualism, and property dualism in one stroke. That is a great deal of content.

Priya: It does no work beyond that anti-dualist claim, and it is asymmetric. You are defining the physical negatively while every other domain gets its content positively. If "physical" just means "not fundamentally mental," it is no longer the notion that does work when we say physics studies the physical, and it cannot underwrite the claim that the entities physics actually describes exhaust reality.

Daniel: Perhaps physicalism is better treated not as a crisp doctrinal thesis but as a commitment to explaining the mental using the methods of the physical sciences. On that reading, the dilemma is a reason to drop the demand for a precise definition, not a reason to abandon physicalism.

Priya: That saves the label but changes the subject. Physicalism, as it figures in the debate, is a metaphysical thesis about what exists. If you replace it with a methodological policy, you are no longer contesting the dualist's claim.

Daniel: Maybe the lesson is that the usual debate was ill-posed. If both horns of the dilemma are unacceptable, the right response is to reframe the question rather than accept the less bad horn.

Priya: That is tantamount to conceding that physicalism, as a metaphysical thesis, cannot be formulated so as to be both non-trivial and probably true.
