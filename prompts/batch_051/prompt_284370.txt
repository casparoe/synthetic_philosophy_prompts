Okay, I need a short explainer I can adapt into a handout for my philosophy of religion seminar. Topic: the Trinity and the logic of identity. Skip the usual "how can one be three" hand-waving — my undergrads have already seen that, and it mostly produces metaphors. I want to get into the weeds on one specific fork in the dialectic.

Set it up like this. The creedal claims are: the Father is God, the Son is God, the Holy Spirit is God, but the Father is not the Son. If "is" is numerical identity, transitivity gives you Father = Son, contradiction. So the obvious fix is that "is" here is the "is" of predication, not identity. Fine. But then "God" starts behaving like a common noun or kind term, like "human": three persons divine in something like the way three humans are human. And now the pressure is: why isn't that just three gods? Social trinitarians more or less eat that bullet; others insist the divine nature is a single individual substance the persons somehow share.

The actual thing I want you to explain is the third option: relative identity. Geach's line is that there's no bare "a = b"; there's only "a is the same F as b." So the Father and the Son can be the same God but not the same person, and the contradiction dissolves because the sortal differs. Please unpack, in order: (a) exactly how this blocks the transivity argument, (b) what it does to Leibniz's law and to counting — e.g., does "how many gods?" still have a determinate answer, and why should a true "same God" claim fail to liscence substitution in, say, reasoning from "the Son is God" to "the Son is the Father," and (c) what the defender of absolute identity would say is going on — are we solving the problem, or quietly redefining "same" until it stops biting? It's fine if you end up skeptical of relative identity, but give the strongest version before you push on it.
