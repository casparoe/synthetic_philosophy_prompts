When people defending a moral difference between lying and misleading want to explain why misleading is less bad, they often reach for the analogy that misleading stands to lying as letting die stands to killing. The analogy is made to do real classificatory work: both pairs are taken to place a positive intervention on one side and a mere failure to prevent on the other, so that the person who only misleads is like a bystander who lets a process run rather than an agent who starts it. From this classification a moral conclusion is drawn — that misleading is in general less wrongful than lying, and is not a violation of the same duty.

Audit the analogy rather than accepting or rejecting it outright. First, spell out the intended mapping: in the conversational situation, what is supposed to correspond to the death, to the fatal process running in the victim's body, to the killer's intervention, to the bystander's letting the process run, to the victim, and to the victim's body itself? Then state where the mapping breaks. In particular, examine whether the difference between asserting something false and allowing one's true statement to generate a false belief really lines up with the difference between commission and omission, and whether the hearer's act of inference is being treated as a merely natural process when the speaker may have deliberately engineered it. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended moral conclusion from those that are incidental.

Finally, say what survives. Does the analogy actually support the claim that misleading is generally less wrongful than lying, or was it carrying the whole argument? If the analogy fails in its intended use, say what a better analogy would capture that this one cannot.
