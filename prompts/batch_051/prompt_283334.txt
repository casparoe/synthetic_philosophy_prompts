I keep coming back to a disagreement with some people on the rationalist-adjacent internet about hate speech laws, specifically why the US and Europe ended up with such different regimes despite both having liberal democratic traditions. The standard story is that the US is committed to a principled free-speech absolutism descended from Mill's argument from fallibility, while Europe is willing to trade some speech off against dignity, social cohesion, and the memory of the 1940s. I suspect that's a just-so story about principle versus expediency.

I was rereading On Liberty, chapter two, and this passage stuck with me: "He devolves upon his own world the responsibility of being in the right against the dissentient worlds of other people; and it never troubles him that mere accident has decided which of these numerous worlds is the object of his reliance, and that the same causes which make him a Churchman in London, would have made him a Buddhist or a Confucian in Pekin." Mill is making a psychological claim here about how people's confidence in their own norms is socially referenced. So here's the conceptual claim I want to test: the difference between US and European hate speech doctrine is better explained by differing epistemic reference groups — different "worlds" that make each side's norms feel like settled, obvious truths — than by differing principled commitments about speech versus dignity.

I want a concrete experimental or observational design that could actually pull those two explanations apart. What result would favor the epistemic-reference-group story, what result would favor the principled-commitment story, and what residual part of the distinction would remain untestable even with a clean result? I'd especially like to hear about natural experiments or designs that avoid just asking people their stated reasons, since stated reasons are themselves likely to be post hoc confabulations from the local epistemic world.
