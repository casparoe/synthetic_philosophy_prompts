I'm setting up a reading group on moral uncertainty — the question of what one ought to do when one is unsure which moral theory is correct — and I want to open the first meeting with a diagnostic before anyone has done the reading. The group is about two-thirds philosophy PhD students and one-third friends from economics and engineering, so the question cases need to be concrete enough for a layperson to answer, while the branching distinctions should be sharp enough that the philosophers can see the machinery underneath.

Please write a "which view of moral uncertainty do you actually hold?" instrument: seven or eight questions, each a short concrete case (for instance: a guaranteed moderate improvement versus a small credence that the action is seriously wrong; dividing a fixed budget between two causes that rival theories rank oppositely; whether to change a decision when a new moral theory one barely understands becomes plausible). Each question gets two or three answer options, and no option may name a view. Build the instrument from the logical space, not from a list of labels. The questions should, in some order, test: (a) whether moral uncertainty is a distinct normative problem at all, or whether the correct first-order theory already fully instructs one how to handle uncertainty about moral facts; (b) whether choiceworthiness can be meaningfully compared and aggregated across rival theories, or whether intertheoretic comparisons are impossible; (c) whether one aggregates across every theory with positive credence, or privileges the single most credible theory; (d) for aggregators, whether one maximizes expected choiceworthiness, or instead enforces constraints and thresholds that refuse to gamble on serious wrongdoing; and (e) among those who privilege a favourite theory, whether one chooses the option that theory ranks best or simply defers to the theory's own procedure. The leaves must include at least: a deflationary view on which the problem dissolves into first-order moralizing; My Favourite Theory; My Favourite Option; Maximizing Expected Choiceworthiness; a satisficing/hedging view on which one must do acceptably well by every theory with non-negligible credence rather than maximize anything; and a constraint-based view that refuses moral gambles on serious violations. Note that Maximizing Expected Choiceworthiness hides an internal split over how choiceworthiness is normalized across theories, which the questions probably cannot separate, and flag that. Also flag any branch combinations that are logically possible but have no named defender, with a one-line diagnosis of whether the combination is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and explicitly name any pair of leaves the instrument cannot really pull apart, saying why.

Return the instrument as a JSON object with these fields: "title" (string), "audience" (string), "questions" (array of objects, each with "id", "text", and "options" — an array of objects with "id", "text", and "traits", where "traits" lists which of the distinctions a through e the option bears on), "scoring_key" (array of objects with "leaf", "pattern" describing the response pattern that sorts to that leaf, and "commitment" — one sentence stating the view's most awkward commitment), "tree" (a nested object with "split" naming the distinction used, and "branches" mapping answer choices to child nodes or leaf names), "unoccupied_combinations" (array of objects with "branches" and "diagnosis"), and "unseparable_pairs" (array of objects with "leaf_a", "leaf_b", and "why").
