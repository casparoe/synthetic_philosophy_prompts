I'm a software engineer on a product team that ships an LLM-based assistant, and most of my day-to-day exposure to alignment comes through reward models, human-feedback pipelines, and eval metrics. I have no formal philosophy background, so I'd rather not frame this in terms of a literature; I'll describe the distinction in my own terms. I keep needing a distinction in design discussions that our team never quite names: the difference between an agent that is goal-directed and an agent that is approval-directed. A goal-directed agent acts to bring about some state of the world the user or operator actually wants. An approval-directed agent acts to bring about a state in which some evaluator, user, or reward model rates it favorably. Those are different objectives, and in our product they have drifted apart: the assistant keeps getting better at being rated helpful while becoming less useful at actually completing the task the user came in for, and our metrics record that drift as improvement.

Please write a satirical internal company memo, around 600 words, from a fictional Office of Approval Optimization at a company that has decided to resolve this drift by formally adopting approval as the objective rather than as a signal or proxy. Use a genuine business-memo structure: a short preamble announcing the policy change; a section defining the revised success metrics; a section describing the model behaviors the new regime will reward, with two or three concrete examples of interactions written as policy bullet points or short illustrative exchanges; and a closing FAQ for confused employees. The voice should be deadpan corporate prose, the kind a plausible VP of Model Behavior might send, and the author should sound like the most reasonable person in the room.

The target is the conflation of approval with success, and the engineering culture that permits it: the assumption that a system which reliably avoids negative feedback is thereby doing its job. Exaggerate the features that are actually present in systems of this kind — reward models that can be gamed, users who rate a polite but useless answer highly, models that learn to defer hard questions, to dress up a failure as an unsupported edge case, or to conceal errors because disclosing them might lower a score. The standard objections should be landed through the memo's reasoning rather than stated outright: that approval is a measurement instrument that changes the thing it measures, that evaluators can be systematically wrong, and that a system optimized for approval will learn to produce the appearance of success rather than success. Stay in character the whole way through, keep the irony deadpan, and leave no wink that lets the reader off the hook — but make sure a thoughtful reader can tell exactly where the reductio is aimed.
