Ok, I'm putting together a chapter section on coherence arguments for expected utility maximization and I want a consistency check on a set of commitments I've slowly accumulated. I'll list them roughly in the order I came to them, and I'd like you to audit the whole set rather than just tell me they're all fine.

1. I used to think Ramsey's Dutch book argument in Truth and Probability was the strongest reason to require probabilistically coherent degrees of belief. He argues, roughly, that if someone's mental condition violated the laws of probability, a cunning bettor could offer them a series of bets, each of which looks favorable on its own, but which together guarantee a loss.

2. But now I think that argument only goes through if the person evaluates each bet myopically, without noticing the whole package. If they see all the bets at once, they can just decline the package as a whole.

3. Still, I do think there's some real normative force to coherence arguments. The version I find most compelling is the sequential money-pump argument for transitivity and independence: preferences that violate these axioms seem to leave you open to a sequence of trades, each of which looks good when you make it, but which together make you worse off.

4. Yet I also think the money-pump argument is weaker than people usually admit. A sophisticated chooser who sees the cycle coming can simply stop trading once they recognize what's going on. So it seems to show only that you shouldn't be a myopic pairwise trader, not that you must have acyclic preferences.

5. I accept the independence axiom as a requirement of rationality.

6. I also think Allais-type preferences are not irrational. I have them, and they look to me like a perfectly reasonable sensitivity to what's certain versus what's risky, not a mistake.

7. I don't think there's any separate diachronic requirement of Bayesian conditionalization. It's just static coherence applied to a larger event space that includes possible learning experiences.

8. Finally, I think representation theorems like Savage's are not normative by themselves. They tell you what a set of preferences looks like if it's representable by a utility function, but they don't, by themselves, give the agent a reason to satisfy the axioms.

So that's the set. Does it hang together? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity, tell me which pairs only seem to be in tension but actually aren't, and say what the cheapest single revision would be and what it would cost. Don't just list all the possible tensions as if they're equally bad; I want to know which one actually has to give first.
