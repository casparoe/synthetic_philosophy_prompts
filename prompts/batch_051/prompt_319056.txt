I am a novelist, and for the past year I have been researching a book built around a forced choice: an ordinary character, with no special tie to any of the people involved, must divert a deadly threat from five strangers onto one stranger, or let it run its course. I have come to think that the most demanding treatment of this material is the position associated with John Taurek — roughly, that the greater number of people who would be saved does not, by itself, make saving the greater number what one ought to do, because the claims or losses of distinct persons are not the sort of thing that can simply be added together.

As part of the research, I will be conducting a long-form public interview with a philosopher who has spent much of a career developing and defending a broadly Taurekian view of these cases. I need eight to ten questions that will not let the guest restate the position, but will force work in the places where it is hardest to hold. Please order the questions to build pressure, and cover at least the following, not necessarily in this sequence:

1. what the no-aggregation claim amounts to in the guest's version — whether greater numbers are said to be wholly irrelevant, merely not decisive by themselves, or decisive only when something further is added;
2. the role, in that version, of a fair procedure such as tossing a coin, and how equal concern for each person is supposed to be satisfied by giving each the same chance when the procedure itself does not treat a larger group as having greater weight;
3. the pairwise-comparison line of thought — comparing the one with each of the five one at a time — and whether it really motivates the view, or instead leaves the choice indeterminate or open to a cycle once the comparisons are combined;
4. the objection that five people, each bearing a claim of the same weight as the single person's, must together constitute more reason to act, and whether the guest has anything to offer against this beyond reasserting that claims do not add;
5. cases in which the harms differ in kind or severity rather than only in number, and whether the view can handle these without collapsing into a blanket refusal to compare harms at all;
6. special relationships, and whether the equal concern the view seems to demand is in tension with ordinary partiality toward family and friends, or whether the view can draw a principled line around it;
7. whether the view is meant to govern institutions and rule-makers — a hospital allocating scarce treatment, a government deciding where to build a flood barrier — or whether the individual case and the institutional case come apart, and if they do, what explains the split;
8. finally, what in a nearby ordinary case would count against the view, and whether the guest can describe a rescue case in which he would abandon the no-aggregation claim rather than extend it.

Do not include biographical questions or invitations to introduce the view from scratch; assume the audience has already heard a short statement of the position. For each question, give the answer the guest is most likely to make, and then the follow-up that answer invites — ideally one that shows the reply concedes more than the view can afford, or depends on a principle the view has not independently earned.

At the end, add a short note saying how confident you are in your characterizations of the view and of these likely replies, and identify the single point you are least sure of.
