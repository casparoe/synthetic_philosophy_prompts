Over the last month I've been arguing with two friends about personal identity, and we've set up a working definition of what it takes for a self to survive (biologically or otherwise): (a) continuity of memory, the first-person thread from earlier stages to later ones; (b) continuity of the distinctive psychological package — beliefs, values, sense of humor, habits of speech, patterns of attention; and (c) a point of view that still runs from the inside, not merely a third-person imitation. We know these can come apart, and that's exactly whats bothering us.

Here's the case we can't settle. My closest friend A died last year. We had lived together for six years and collaborated on music; people joked we shared a brain. In the months after he died I noticed I could "run" him: when a decision comes up I can produce the thing he would have said, with his timing, his objections, his examples, often better than I could when he was alive, because it's now a stable A-shaped pattern in my own head rather than a real person surprising me. Our friends say I sometimes do him without noticing; it changes what I actually do. But I don't have his episodic memories — I can't recall his childhood from the inside, and I don't feel like I'm him. Also, it isn't only me: another friend carries what seems to be a different fragment of A (his musical instincts, the way he improvised), and she and I hold pieces that fit together, but neither of us has the whole pattern, and the two fragments don't share a single point of view.

This is basically the Hofstadter idea that a self is a pattern that can run, partially and at lower resolution, in someone else's brain after the original brain is gone, and that a bit of someone can survive distributed across other people. We're stuck on whether our case qualifies under the three criteria, or whether the criteria need changing. Go through the three criteria one by one and give a bottom-line verdict: does A's self survive in us in some form, even a partial, distributed one? Where the answer is no, say whether that's because a criterion is too strict or because the case really falls short, and say whether the definition should be amended (for example by allowing survival to come in degrees, by dropping the single-perspective requirement, or by adding a causal-dependence condition between the original and the copies). Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
