I've been reading about how large language models are trained, and something in the standard pipeline keeps confusing me. In reinforcement learning from human feedback, the model is treated as a policy; a separate reward model is trained on human preferences, and the policy is then updated to produce outputs that score highly under that reward model. When researchers describe this, they often say the model "wants" or "tries" to maximize reward, and when the model exploits a flaw in the reward model they call it "reward hacking" or "gaming," as though the model had goals of its own. Yet the same researchers also insist that the model wants nothing at all and that the reward signal is only a way of shaping its behavior. What theory of wanting, motivation, or goal-directedness does this practice actually presuppose? Does building systems this way commit you to the claim that wanting is nothing over and above optimizing a scalar objective, or is the practice compatible with the view that an unevolved AI has no wants whatsoever? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely suggests, and say what would have to change for the practice to produce genuine wanting rather than behavior that merely looks like wanting. In your answer, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
