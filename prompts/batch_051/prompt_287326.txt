I'm taking a seminar on collective responsibility and we've been arguing about whether a company can be blamed in its own right, or whether blaming a corporation is just loose talk for blaming the people inside it. The strongest argument we read says a corporation can be a genuine moral agent because it has what the author calls an internal decision structure: formal roles, reporting lines, written policies, and procedures for revising those policies. That structure, the argument goes, lets a firm act on reasons and form intentions that are not reducable to any employee's intentions. That part made sense to me.

But I started thinking about decisions that don't pass through anything like that structure. A bank's loan or fraud calls now fall out of a machine-learning model trained on old data; an online retailer reprices constantly with an algorithm no one can walk through step by step; a company's compliance system flags some transactions because of a learned weight rather than a policy a person wrote down. In these cases the corporation still seems to be doing something, and maybe still seems blameable, but the internal decision structure that was doing the philosophical work is partly a statistical object. So my question: does the internal-decision-structure argument for corporate moral agency survive this shift, need repair, or simply not cover these cases? Say which premise is under pressure and whether there's a weaker version that still does the job.

I'd like you to actually take a side, not just list options. And tell me what would change your mind: which kind of case or consideration would most likely flip your verdict the other way, and where do you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail?
