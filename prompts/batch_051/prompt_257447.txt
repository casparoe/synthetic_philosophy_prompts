Put yourself in the role of a historian of philosophy who also knows the contemporary bioethics/transhumanism stuff. I want you to do a kind of anachronistic thought experiment about Jeremy Bentham.

Here's the setup. There's a view in contemporary circles — sometimes called the hedonistic imperative — that says biology is basically the enemy. The capacity to suffer is an evolutionary kludge, and we should use genetic engineering, neurotechnology, and whatever else becomes available to phase out suffering entirely. Not just reduce it: literally abolish the biological substrates of pain, depression, fear, chronic anxiety, you name it, maybe across the whole living world. This goes beyond anti-natalism or garden-variety animal welfare; it wants to change organisms themselves so that the category of suffering no longer applies to them.

So here's the question: if we could sit Bentham down, hand him a neuroscience textbook and some recent debates, would he sign on? On one hand he's the guy who made the minimization of pain and the maximization of pleasure the sole test of law and morals, and he had zero deference to whatever happened to be natural as a moral category. On the other hand, his whole reform program uses pain — in punishment, detterence, and even the Panopticon — as a tool to be administered rather than just extinguished, and he's an empiricist about human psychology, so he might worry about tinkering with the very machinery that produces the preferences and aversions his calculus needs as inputs.

Think carefully. Start by brainstorming both sides: the case for Bentham-as-bioabolitionist and the case for Bentham-as-cautious-reformer, including what resources in his actual known views each side would lean on (no need for exact quotations). Then settle on which Bentham you think would win out, and flag exactly where contemporary anti-suffering movements go beyond anything Bentham could have anticipated — or where Bentham's own commitments would force him into positions he never had to confront. Feel free to make it messy; I want the tensions left visible.
