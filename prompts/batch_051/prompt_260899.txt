Two members of a decision theory reading group I sometimes sit in on got into an argument last week, and it has been nagging at me because each of them seemed clearly right at one moment and clearly overreaching the next. They were arguing about whether a rational person's degrees of belief must always be representable by a single precise probability function. Dana defended imprecise probabilities: the idea that evidence can underdetermine credences, so that in some cases the rational belief state is a set of probability functions rather than one number. Mark defended the orthodox view that rational credences are precise.

Dana opened with a case. You know that a coin is biased, but only that the bias goes one way or the other: either heads has a 60 percent chance or tails does, and nothing tells you which. She said the evidence does not single out 0.5, or 0.52, or any other point; what it supports is the whole interval from 0.4 to 0.6, and insisting on a single number would be manufacturing information you do not have. Mark replied that no one can act from an interval. Suppose you are offered a ticket that pays $10 on heads and costs $5. If you buy it, you must be behaving as if the probability of heads is above one half; if you decline, you must be behaving as if it is below. So the imprecise agent, he said, is just a precise agent who hides the precision until the moment of choice. Dana answered that this confuses permissibility with commitment. Under an E-admissibility rule — an option is permissible if it is optimal under at least one probability in the set — buying the ticket is permissible, because it is best if heads has probability 0.6, and declining is permissible, because it is best if heads has probability 0.4. A state that licenses both actions without selecting between them, she said, is not a disguised point estimate; it is a different kind of state, with different guidance. Mark came back with two objections. First, an agent who regards a whole range of trades as acceptable can be exploited: if each link in a sequence of small sales and buybacks is permissible on its own, a trader can cycle you through deals that leave you worse off no matter how the coin lands. Second, the interval by itself gives no guidance at all; everything doing the actual guiding is the decision rule you attach to it, and that rule could just as well be attached to a single precise credence, so imprecision does no independent work. Dana replied that the money-pump objection assumes a myopic agent who evaluates each offer in isolation, whereas a careful agent can treat the whole sequence as one package and refuse the cycle. And to the second objection she said the interval is doing representational work: it records exactly how much the evidence supports, while the precise Bayesian's point is the thing doing unearned work by pretending the evidence is sharper than it is.

Neither of them would move, and they both turned to me to settle it, which I could not do on the spot. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate each move — Dana's opening case, Mark's reply about action, Dana's E-admissibility answer, Mark's two objections, and Dana's final response — from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating, and then say who is right about what overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Please write your response in American English.
