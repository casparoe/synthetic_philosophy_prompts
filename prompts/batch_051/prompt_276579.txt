In a philosophy of mind course I'm taking, my professor gave a quick summary of Dennett's view of the self, and I wrote it down like this: "The self isn't a soul or a little person sitting in your head; it's a center of narrative gravity, like the center of gravity of a physical object. A center of gravity doesn't exist as a concrete physical thing, but it's a useful fiction that lets you predict how the object will behave. Likewise, the self is a fictional character your brain creates to make sense of the stream of your life, and there's no deeper fact about who you are underneath the story. So Dennett is basically saying the self is an illusion — and since it's just a story, any narrative you construct about yourself is as real or true as any other. It would also seem to mean that asking whether someone has a distorted self-image or a 'true self' doesn't really make sense."

I'm inclined to accept the first part — the self not being a little homunculus seems right — but the last part seems like it would make Dennett say some weird things about self-deception and responsability. My professor is usually pretty careful, so I don't want to just write the whole thing off. Is this summary fair to Dennett? Which parts would he accept, which are subtly off, and does the "self is an illusion" conclusion actually follow from the accurate parts?
