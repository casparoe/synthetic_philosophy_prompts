So I've been going down a rabbit hole on theories of concepts — the prototype story, exemplar views, the theory theory — and I keep coming back to the same passage in Hume, which I want to make the load-bearing wall of an essay about Borges's "Funes the Memorious" ("Funes, His Memory"). I'm not an academic, just someone running a small reading group, and I want to come in with a reading that's actualy sharp rather than the standard "memory is isolating" stuff.

The passage is from Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature, Book 1, Part 1, Section 7, "Of abstract ideas":

It may therefore be thought, that here is a plain dilemma, that decides concerning the nature of those abstract ideas, which have afforded so much speculation to philosophers. The abstract idea of a man represents men of all sizes and all qualities; which 'tis concluded it cannot do, but either by representing at once all possible sizes and all possible qualities, or by representing no particular one at all.

What I'm trying to figure out is what Funes does to this picture. After his accident Funes remembers everything and is bothered by the fact that the same word "dog" covers the dog seen from one angle at 3:14 and the same dog seen from another angle at 3:15, because for him those are just two different particulars. The narrator says Funes was incapable of general, platonic ideas. On its face that reads like a refusal of abstraction: here's someone with more particulars than anyone, and he can't do the thing Hume says we all do. But Hume's whole point in the passage is that the abstract idea isn't really a separate abstract thing — it's a particular image made to stand in for others. So is Funes a refutation of Hume or a strange confirmation? Or does he show that the "habit of applying the term" can't do all the work, since he has the term and the similarity (he remembers everything), yet the habit never gets off the ground? I also want to know whether the modern views — prototypes, exemplars, theory theory — actually handle him better, and whether Borges's details (the numbering system, the dog at different times of day, the mirror) anticipate those debates. I'd like a reading that lets the story push back on Hume where it should, without pretending Funes is easy for the modern theories either, and that ends by saying what the story adds to the philosophy-of-concepts debate that the arguments alone miss.
