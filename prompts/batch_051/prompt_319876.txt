A friend and I have been going back and forth about free will, and he keeps leaning on a line of argument he attributes to Galen Strawson, something about how nobody can be truly responsible for anything because you would have to be the cause of your own self. I've tried to reconstruct the argument into numbered steps so I can actually evalute it, and now I'm worried I've either made it too strong or the original really is that strong. Here is my reconstruction:

(1) An agent is morally responsible for an action only if it was really up to her whether she did it.
(2) An agent's actions flow from the way she is: her beliefs, desires, values, temperament, and so on.
(3) So if an action is really up to her, then the way she is, at least in the respects that make a difference, must itself have been really up to her.
(4) But nothing about the way she is was ever really up to her. At birth, her starting constitution was given to her by genes and luck. Later, it changed through environment, other people, and her own earlier choices; and each earlier choice was itself just a product of how she already was at the time, which was not up to her either.
(5) Since every aspect of her self can be traced back to factors that were not up to her, no action is really up to her.
(6) Therefore no one is morally responsible for anything.

Part of me thinks this is valid and people only resist the conclusion because it is unpleasant. Another part suspects step 3 or step 4 is cheating, maybe by sliding from an ordinary notion of control over actions to an impossible demand that you be self-made from nothing. Can you go through it step by step and say whether the argument is valid, and if not, exactly which step fails and under what reading? If it is valid, tell me whether a compatibilist has any legitimate place to push back, and where.
