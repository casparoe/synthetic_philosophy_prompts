I am an undergraduate serving as a grader for the moral psychology course I am taking this term. One of the midterm questions asked students to engage with the psychopathy-and-moral-knowledge debate, and I have to assign a grade to an answer that is fluent and confident but that I want to assess claim by claim before finalizing the score. The question was worth 20 points.

The exam question was: "Do psychopaths know that what they do is wrong? Answer with reference to (a) motivational internalism and (b) the moral/conventional distinction."

The student answered as follows: "Psychopathy is typically treated as a counterexample to motivational internalism, the thesis that a person who sincerely judges an action to be wrong will thereby have at least some motivation not to perform it. Psychopaths can use moral vocabulary competently, and in interviews they will often say that assaulting someone is wrong, yet their behavior shows no corresponding motivation to refrain. On the face of it, then, psychopaths are people who know that a certain act is wrong while being entirely unmoved by that knowledge. The standard reply is that this appearance is misleading: psychopaths do not really have the relevant moral knowledge, because they lack the concept of moral wrongness. The evidence comes from the moral/conventional distinction. Normal subjects treat moral violations such as hitting someone as wrong independently of what any authority says, and they justify that judgment by appeal to the victim's welfare; conventional violations such as talking out of turn are treated as wrong only because an authority prohibits them. Studies of psychopathic subjects fail to preserve this distinction, treating moral violations as if they were authority-dependent. This has been taken to show that a psychopath's use of 'wrong' is merely conventional, much as a chess move can be 'wrong' relative to the rules. If that is correct, then a psychopath never forms the genuinely moral judgment that internalism concerns, and psychopathy is no longer a counterexample. I think this reply is basically right. The empirical finding that psychopaths cannot tell moral from merely conventional rules strongly suggests that their apparent moral knowledge is not knowledge of moral wrongness. The deeper lesson is that moral knowledge requires more than the ability to repeat a rule: it requires the affective capacity to appreciate the authority-independent status of moral wrongs, and this is exactly what psychopaths lack."

(3) "Grade and Justification": a score out of 20 with a letter grade, and a few sentences explaining where points were lost. (4) "Comments to the Student": 75 to 125 words of constructive feedback written as though I would put it on the exam itself.
