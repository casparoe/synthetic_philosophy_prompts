I have been trying to think clearly about ritual substitution—the logic by which a goat, a prisoner, or a sacrificial offering comes to stand in for a community's wrongdoing—and I keep returning to a principle I have started calling the conservation of guilt. The principle says that within the economy of a ritual, guilt behaves like a conserved quantity: it cannot be annihilated, only moved. When the high priest lays his hands on the scapegoat and confesses the people's sins over it before sending it into the wilderness, the ritual is not merely expressing a wish or symbolizing a hope for cleansing; it is performing a kind of moral bookkeeping in which a determinate quantity of guilt is transferred to a bearer chosen because it can be expelled or destroyed. The same logic seems to me to underlie blood sacrifice, the crowd that finds a scapegoat for its anxieties, and even some forms of punitive justice, in which the sentence works to locate the wrong in the offender so that the rest of the community can stop carrying it. The principle earns its keep, as I see it, by explaining something a purely expressive or symbolic reading leaves out: why it should matter that the goat is actually sent away, and why rituals that merely announce forgiveness often feel comparatively weightless.

I want an honest assessment of whether this is a real concept or just a metaphor in costume. In particular: Is the principle coherent, or does it smuggle in a quasi-physical picture of guilt as a substance that moves between persons—and does that picture survive scrutiny? Is this already worked out under another name in the study of ritual or moral thought, some version of substitutionary atonement, scapegoat theory, or something adjacent, and if so, what, if anything, does my formulation add? And where does it break? I can already see that it may make punishment look too much like just another transfer mechanism, and that it may fail to explain rituals that seem to dissolve guilt rather than relocate it. Give me the strongest objections and a judgment about whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
