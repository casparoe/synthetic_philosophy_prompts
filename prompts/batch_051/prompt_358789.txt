I am a journalist writing a long-form piece on the debate over why some nations are rich and others poor, where the rival explanations are institutions, geography, and culture. A recurring move in the economic-history literature I have been reading is to treat certain historical episodes — the drawing of a colonial border, the uneven reach of a disease, the navigability of a river — as 'natural experiments', and then to reason from them in much the way one would reason from a randomised controlled trial. The implicit analogy is that history has already assembled something like a treatment and control group and assigned the treatment approximately at random, so the researcher's task is simply to read off the effect.

I would like this analogy audited before I rely on it in print. Set out the mapping explicitly: what is meant to correspond to the randomising device, the treatment and control arms, the sample, the follow-up period, and the causal quantity being estimated. Then work through where the mapping breaks. A real randomised controlled trial earns certain features: assignment that is independent of potential outcomes, no interference between units, a single well-specified treatment, balance on unmeasured confounders in expectation, and an exclusion restriction by which the randomising device influences the outcome only through the treatment. Which of these does the historical episode import without earning, and which of the failures are fatal to the intended use rather than merely untidy? Finally, say whether the conclusion this analogy is usually enlisted to support — that institutions can be cleanly separated from geography and culture as a cause of national wealth — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and offer a better analogy for historical causal inference if one is available.

Please write the answer in British English.
