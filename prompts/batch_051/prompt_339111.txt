I am a novelist planning a book set in a near-future polity whose government has consolidated all welfare decisions into a single Allocation Board. The Board is bound by one statutory rule. For every candidate recipient of scarce assistance — pain relief, psychiatric care, life support, prosthetics, and the like, whether human or nonhuman — a trained assessor assigns two scores. The first is an estimate of the recipient's level of consciousness, from 0 for no awareness to 1 for ordinary human wakefulness. The second is an estimate of the recipient's expected net happiness over the period the assistance would cover, assuming the assistance is provided, from -1 for a life dominated by suffering to +1 for a life of maximal happiness. The recipient's moral weight is the product of the two scores, and the Board must allocate resources so as to maximize the sum of these products across all candidates; no other consideration may enter.

I need to understand how this rule would actually operate, because the novel's plot turns on its second-order effects. Analyze the incentives it creates for assessors, for the relatives and advocates who petition on behalf of recipients, and for the Board's administrators. Work through the edge cases that strike me as unstable: the fully conscious recipient whose expected happiness is deeply negative, the barely conscious recipient who is content, and the recipient whose consciousness score could be changed by medication. Say how the multiplicative form distributes resources differently from an additive formula or a threshold rule that requires consciousness to pass some bar before happiness is counted at all. Finally, tell me which of the scheme's failure modes could be repaired by amending the formula and which are intrinsic to letting consciousness and happiness multiply.
