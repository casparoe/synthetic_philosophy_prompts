I have been trying to sort out whether, and in what sense, a person can assign probabilities to their own choices while the question is still open, and I keep meeting the same small set of positions. I want you to give me one more that is not on this list. The positions I already have are these.

One: deliberation crowds out prediction. From inside a choice, treating one of your available actions as more or less probable than another is not a further piece of information; it is a covert way of making or announcing the decision, so the deliberative attitude and the predictive attitude cannot occupy the same moment.

Two: the probabilities are well-defined but decision-theoretically idle. On the evidentialist picture, what matters is the probability of outcomes conditional on each action, and the unconditional probability of the action itself either cancels out or double-counts. The Newcomb-style cases only seem to involve self-prediction because evidence about one's likely action is correlated with the predictor's move, and that correlation, not the self-credence, does the work.

Three: the causalist or interventionist position. Choosing is setting the action, not observing it, so treating your own future action as a chance event to be forecast confuses seeing and doing.

Four: prediction at the level of policies. You can be uncertain about which rule, policy, or precommitment you will adopt or stick with, and that is coherent, but the object being predicted is the policy rather than the output of the very deliberation you are currently running.

Five: the outside-view position. For choices that are sufficiently distant or governed by known dispositions, you can predict your future self the way you predict anyone else, from base rates and track record; the prohibition is local to the first-person deliberative stance, not absolute.

Six: ratifiability as the place where self-prediction properly enters. Instead of feeding a probability for your action into the initial expected-utility comparison, you check after a candidate choice whether that choice still looks best conditional on the fact that you made it, so self-prediction appears as a stability condition on decisions rather than as evidence.

I keep feeling there is a seventh position that does not reduce to any of these, and I do not want to fool myself by dressing up a restatement as a discovery. Give me one additional position, argument, or move in this debate. It must be genuinely different in structure from all six, not a rewording, special case, or combination of them, and it must not be the bare thesis that the question is confused unless you can make that thesis do work none of the six do. I am not asking for an attribution or a reading list, only the position itself.
