I recently sat in on a graduate seminar on Anselm's ontological argument. Afterward, I overheard two of the participants, Nadia and Tomas, arguing about whether Gaunilo's perfect-island parody does real damage to Anselm. I reconstructed their exchange as faithfully as I could. I would like you to adjudicate the exchange point by point: go through their actual moves in order rather than giving your own preferred reconstruction of the debate, say for each substantive point who is right about that specific point and why, and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing. Do not simply declare an overall winner; the point is to sort the good moves from the bad ones as they are actually made.

Here is the exchange.

Nadia: Gaunilo's parody is stronger than people give it credit for. It doesn't have to be a perfect analogy; it has to show that Anselm's argument has a form that, if sound, proves too much. Replace God with an island — an island such that no greater island can be conceived — and run the same steps. If such an island existed only in the understanding, then a greater island could be conceived, namely one that also existed in reality. Therefore the greatest conceivable island exists in reality. That conclusion is absurd, so something in Anselm's form is doing too much work. The burden is on the Anselmian to say what.

Tomas: The parody fails before the absurd conclusion even matters, because the phrase "an island such that no greater island can be conceived" doesn't pick out a coherent object. Islands are the kind of thing that can always be improved: more fresh water, gentler waters, better anchorages. There is no upper bound to island-greatness. Anselm only applies the reasoning where the concept carries an intrinsic maximum. God, as Anselm conceives him, has perfections that do have maxima — knowing all truths, having all power. So the island is disanalogous exactly where the argument's force is located.

Nadia: But I can simply stipulate an upper bound. Take a definite list of island-excellences — fresh water, safe harbors, fertile soil — and define the perfect island as an island whose island-excellences no island surpasses. Then I have a coherent object in the understanding, and the argument still forces its existence. If you object that island-excellences can always be improved, I add the clause that no island surpasses it in any of them. At that point your disanalogy charge is just a rule forbidding parodies.

Tomas: The stipulation "and no island surpasses it" doesn't give the concept content; it attaches a cap to qualities that have no natural maximum. For knowledge, the maximum is internal to the quality: knowing all truths is not a point on a sliding scale of knowing-more, it's an endpoint specifiable independently of any stipulation. For island-qualities, improvement is open-ended under any description. The island needs a cap to appear maximal; God's perfections don't. That is a structural difference, not an ad hoc one.

Nadia: Even granting that, the engine of Anselm's argument is the premise that existing in reality is greater than existing only in the understanding. That premise is doing the real work, and it works for islands exactly as well as for gods. If existence is a greatness-making feature, the greatest island must have it; if it is not, the greatest being need not have it either. Gaunilo's point then stands: the argument moves only because "greater" is allowed to slide from "greater relative to a concept" to "greater, full stop." The island exposes that slide.

Tomas: I don't accept that it works exactly as well. For Anselm, the existence-in-reality premise is not a general principle that adding existence improves any concept. It is applied to a being whose concept demands unsurpassability simpliciter, not merely unsurpassability for a member of a kind. If such a being existed only in the understanding, it could be conceived to be greater — the same being, but real. That is a failure to satisfy its own concept. An island cannot get this result, because for any island the standard of comparison is other islands, and no candidate fixes what an unsurpassable island would be. So the premise is not available to the island at all.

Nadia: But that just relocates the magic. You have built "would be greater if real" into the concept, and then used that content to conclude that the thing is real. The fool in the psalm is not failing to understand the concept of an unsurpassable being; he is denying that anything answers to it. The island's real lesson is that definitional content of this kind never settles whether anything satisfies the definition. If it can't settle the island's existence, it can't settle God's existence — unless we already grant that the Anselmian God is a specially privileged concept, which is exactly what the fool disputes.

Tomas: The fool has the concept, but Anselm's point is that he has not noticed what the concept demands. The reductio is that denying reality to the unsurpassable being is to conceive of it as surpassable, which contradicts the content of the concept. The island never yields a comparable contradiction, because the phrase "no greater island" does not fix a determinate standard of island-surpassability. I am willing to grant that the parody kills the generic version of the argument — existence improves any concept, so the best member of any kind must exist. That generic version deserves to die. But Anselm is not running it.
