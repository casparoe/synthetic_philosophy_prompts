Here's an argument I've been fiddling with for a linguistics-adjacent philosophy seminar, and I cant find where it goes wrong. The conclusion is mad, but every step by itself looks fine to me.

(1) The grammar of a language just is the system behind what its fluent native speakers actually do. If the rules in a grammar book diverge from what millions of native speakers say every day, the book is wrong, not the speakers.

(2) So a construction is ungrammatical in a speaker's own language only if the regularities of native-speaker usage exclude it.

(3) But the usual "grammatical errors" aren't excluded by usage. "Between you and I", the split infinitive, singular "they", double negatives: these appear constantly, across dialects and registers, in the speech and writing of fluent people — often produced by the very people who'd tell you they're wrong if you asked.

(4) So by (2), those forms are grammatical, and calling them errors is a mistake.

(5) Now take any supposed grammatical error in a native speaker's own language. Either fluent native speakers regularly produce it in the relevant way, in which case (3)–(4) show it's grammatical; or they dont, in which case it isn't a live part of the language at all but a one-off slip or a nonce form — not an error of grammar, just a performance glitch. Either way, the category "grammatical error" is empty.

(6) Therefore no native speaker ever makes a grammatical error in their own language. But that can't be right: it would put "between you and I" and "between you and me" on exactly the same footing, and make criticising someone's grammar in a job application incoherent.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd rather keep the idea that there really are grammatical errors (whatever they turn out to be) than hand over premise (1) in full generality, but I don't want you to just wave a style guide at it.
