So I'm in this reading group and the whole semester has basically been the Enlightenment on trial, and last week a friend of mine made the standard defense by leaning hard on Kant's coming-of-age picture. His version: Kant treats enlightenment as humanity finally leaving behind a self-incurred immaturity, the state where we'd rather let a guardian — a pastor, a physician, a book, a prince — do our thinking for us. The unenlightened are like someone who could walk perfectly well but keeps clinging to the furniture becuase they're scared of stumbling, and the Enlightenment is supposed to be the moment the species lets go and dares to use its own understanding. His punchline was that the whole Adorno- and Foucault-style critique of the Enlightenment as a new form of domination is almost incoherent, since leaving childish dependence can't be domination; dependence was the domination and autonomy is its opposite.

But I'd spent that week with the critics and it started to feel like the analogy is exactly where the argument goes wrong, not a neutral illustration of it. Actual children don't exit tutelage on their own schedule; guardians decide when they're ready, administer the tests, and set the standard for what thinking-for-oneself is supposed to look like. The species doesn't come with a predetermined adulthood, so what looks like growing up can just be swapping one set of guardians for another that's better at producing people who police themselves. So I don't trust my friend's punchline, but I also think it's too cheap to just say "analogies are imperfect" and call it a refutation.
