I've been going in circles on a contractualist argument and I half-suspect its got a trick in it. Scanlon-style contractualism says an act is wrong just in case it would be ruled out by a principle that no one could reasonably reject. Now take a choice: you can either save one person from a serious injury, or cure a thousand people's mild headaches, not both. Here's the argument:

(1) In that choice, consider the principle P: "save the one injured person."
(2) P gives each of the thousand headache-sufferers a reason to prefer the alternative principle ("cure the headaches"), since it leaves them with a headache they could have been spared.
(3) A reason of that kind is a complaint against P.
(4) So each of the thousand has a complaint against P.
(5) If each of the thousand has a complaint against P, then P is a principle that someone could reasonably reject.
(6) So P is reasonably rejectable.
(7) So, by the contractualist criterion, saving the one is wrong.

That conclusion feels wrong to me, but I can't tell whether the argument is valid and my gut is just being aggregative, or whether one of the steps is doing something illicit. Which step fails, if any? If it's valid as stated, say so; if not, name the first step that fails and explain exactly why. Then tell me how the contractualist handles the case where the thousand people would each suffer a serious injury comparable to the one person's: why can they say save the thousand there, without sneaking aggregation back in?

At the very end of your reply, give a machine-readable verdict on its own line: \answer{valid} or \answer{step N}, where N is the number of the first failing step.
