Act as a professional philosopher working in applied ethics and the philosophy of public space. I am a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and I want the full set of constraints on the table before evaluating any more particular arguments about taking down statues or renaming streets. The discussions I have seen usually start from whichever case is most sympathetic to one side—a slave trader, a colonial administrator, a Confederate general, a wartime prime minister—and then induce a general principle from a single instance, with the other side accused of moving the goalposts every time a new case is introduced. I want to avoid that.

Write the desiderata that an adequate theory of public commemoration must meet. The theory should cover statues, building names, street names, and commemorative plaques, and should say when it is permissible, required, or impermissible for a polity to install, retain, recontextualize, or remove them. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it. Then mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and justify those marks. If some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say so explicitly—that tension is the part I most want to see. Finally, identify the single requirement that does the most work to exclude otherwise popular theories of commemoration, and briefly justify it.

Start by brainstorming the hardest cases and strongest counterexamples before extracting the criteria. Do not turn the answer into a list of historical examples or a bibliography; I am interested in the specification itself.

A few half-formed constraints I have seen or entertained, which you should treat as raw material to incorporate, sharpen, or reject:

- A statue on a pedestal in a main square, a working building that happens to carry a name, a street name used for navigation, and a plaque on a wall are importantly different in how imposing, honorific, and unavoidable they are. A one-size-fits-all theory needs an argument, not an assumption.
- It must be possible to honor an achievement without honoring every agent associated with it. A theory that cannot say we can commemorate a public accomplishment without thereby endorsing all the acts of its protagonists seems suspect.
- The meaning of a monument is not fixed by the intentions of those who erected it; it has a social afterlife, and different publics read it differently. A theory that treats original intent as decisive is probably wrong descriptively and possibly wrong normatively, but I am unsure how much of that is normative rather than empirical.
- Public commemoration consumes scarce public space and maintenance budgets and imposes itself on passersby who did not consent. A theory should say whether this raises the bar for what deserves commemoration, and how.
- The theory should not be restricted to the safely long dead: we also name laws, buildings, and streets after living or recent figures, and that practice has different epistemic and incentive properties.
