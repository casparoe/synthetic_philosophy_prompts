I am a philosophy of mind PhD student working on constitutive panpsychism, and I keep noticing that debates about the combination problem are settled in advance by which desideratum the author decides to treat as load-bearing. I do not want to reproduce that pattern. Before I commit to any account, I want the requirements list worked out in full.

Constitutive panpsychism holds that the conscious experience of a human being, or of any other macro-level subject, is constituted by the experiential properties of its fundamental physical parts, with nothing over and above those micro-level facts. The combination problem is the charge that this cannot be rendered intelligible: however tightly one packs a collection of small subjects, the result looks like a collection, not a single subject. Please give me the desiderata that any adequate solution to that problem must satisfy, rather than another restatement of the problem or a survey of the literature.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and explain why a theory cannot quietly drop it. I want particular attention to several things. First, which distinctions a solution cannot collapse: for example, the difference between one subject having a complex experience and many subjects having experiences in concert, or the difference between a phenomenal quality being constituted and its merely being caused. Second, which empirical constraints a solution must respect: that consciousness in humans tracks certain kinds of integrated neural activity while being apparently absent in others, such as the cerebellum, dreamless sleep, and general anaesthesia, and that subjects can divide in split-brain cases and dissociative conditions. A solution must explain how macro-subjects have reasonably sharp boundaries without an ad hoc stipulation. Third, the structural constraint: macro-experience has structure, such as a unified visual field and distinct modalities, while the micro-experiences said to constitute it are typically taken to be simple; the theory must account for where that structure and those novel qualities come from without smuggling them in from the physical side or treating the micro-experiences as already containing every macro-quality in miniature. Fourth, a solution must not merely rename the difficulty by positing a primitive bonding relation and declaring the problem solved; if a solution invokes such a relation, the desiderata should force it to say what the relation amounts to and what evidence would show that it obtains.

Then mark which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are better understood as commitments of particular camps: for instance, whether a solution must be fully reductive, whether it must secure a causal role for macro-level experience, or whether it must handle fission cases rather than setting them aside as a separate problem. Finally, tell me whether some subset of the non-negotiable desiderata is plausibly jointly unsatisfiable, and if so which of them a constitutive panpsychist should be most tempted to weaken, and why. If you briefly score the main candidate responses against the list, keep that part short: I care more about the criteria than the scorecard.
