Two friends of mine got into an argument after an epistemology seminar on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and I honestly couldn't referee it. Quick setup in case it matters: in the lottery paradox you rationally believe each individual ticket in a million-ticket lottery will loose, but you also know one will win, so a rational person seems to hold inconsistent beliefs. In the preface paradox an author rationally believes each sentence in her book, but she also writes in the preface that somewhere in the book there is certainly an error, so again a rational person seems to hold inconsistent beliefs.

The exchange went like this. (1) Maya said they're the same puzzle wearing different clothes, so any solution has to handle both at once or it's ad hoc. (2) Jon said no: in the lottery you know for certain that exactly one ticket wins, so your reason for believing the conjunction is false is deductive, whereas in the preface the number of errors could be zero, one, or several, and your reason is only inductive, based on knowing you're a fallible human. (3) Maya replied that the difference doesn't matter for the paradox, because in both cases you rationally believe each member of a set and rationally believe the negation of the conjunction, which is the inconsistency that needs explaining; fallibility is close enough to knowing one claim is false. (4) Jon answered that "close enough" is doing all the work there, and the two cases have different escape routes: the lottery pushes you to reject closure of rational belief under conjunction, while the preface pushes you to say rational belief doesn't require certainty or even high probability, and forcing one treatment flattens that distinction.

Please go through move by move and say who's right about what. Are the two paradoxes really the same problem underneath, and does it matter for which fix you should prefer?
