I am trying to work out what I actually think about gratitude, and about what we owe to benefactors, to luck, and to the past. My reactions to particular cases feel stable, but I cannot find the principle that generates them.

Here are the cases. A colleague stays late, unasked, to help me finish a report; she gave up an evening and gained nothing. I think I owe her genuine thanks and should look for an opportunity to help her later. A donor I cannot identify pays off my student loan in full. I would not say I owe any particular person, but I feel a diffuse obligation to be generous to some stranger when I can. A friend gives me a costly piece of equipment and I suspect the gift is meant to leave me unable to refuse his later requests; the benefit is real, but I feel no gratitude, only that I am being managed. My parents made sacrifices before I was born so that I could be educated; I feel deep gratitude and would help them now without counting, but I resist describing this as a debt to be settled. I learn years afterward that a member of a hiring committee argued for me against a stronger-looking candidate without my knowledge; I feel retrospective gratitude and regret that I never thanked her, but I would deny that I now owe her anything specific. A tire blowout on the highway leaves me unhurt through sheer luck; I am intensely glad, but I do not think I am grateful to anyone. I do not feel grateful to my great-grandparents for the choices that made my existence possible; those choices were not made for my sake, so appreciation seems right and gratitude seems forced.

My tentative idea is that gratitude answers to being treated as someone whose good was pursued for its own sake, while indebtedness answers to the cost the benefactor bore; luck and the unreachable past can be objects of appreciation but not of gratitude. That idea fits several verdicts but breaks on others. By its lights I should feel indebted to my parents, since their cost was enormous and their care was for my sake, yet I do not. And the anonymous gift becomes hard to explain, since a debt with no identifiable creditor seems different in kind from a debt to my colleague.

I want a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts, or at least captures most of them while being honest about which it cannot. Please state each candidate precisely, identify which of my cases motivate each clause, and where my tentative idea fails, repair it rather than simply rejecting it. Then run the candidates through all seven cases, say which of my verdicts they force me to revise, and tell me what they predict about a case I keep going back and forth on: a dog wakes me during a house fire and saves my life, without any intention to save anyone; I feel something closer to gratitude than to mere appreciation, but I cannot tell whether that feeling is coherent.
