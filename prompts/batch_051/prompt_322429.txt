My friend and I recently spent an evening arguing about whether the doctrine of original sin can survive what population genetics has discovered about human ancestry. The classical Augustinian version of the doctrine, as it developed in the Western tradition, treats original sin as inherited guilt: every human being is born culpable for the first transgression because all humanity was somehow included in Adam's sin and descends from him. For guilt to be transmitted in that literal way, the human race must have passed through a single original pair. My friend insists that this assumption is now empirically discredited: the standard population-genetic picture is that Homo sapiens descends from a large interbreeding ancestral population rather than from a single breeding pair, so there is no genealogical bottleneck through which inherited culpability could propagate. I want to know whether the original argument actually collapses. Which premise does the genetic finding genuinely bear on: the monogenetic descent premise, the transmission principle, or the very idea that guilt can be inherited? Is there a repaired version that keeps the doctrinal core, for example by treating original sin as a transmitted corruption of the will or as a structural condition of being formed by already-sinful practices rather than as inherited personal culpability, and would that be a repair of the same doctrine or a replacement of it? Identify what the classic argument needed to be true, and say whether the premise removed by the genetic result was load-bearing or only historical packaging.
