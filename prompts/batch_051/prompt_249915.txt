I'm a philosophy PhD student working in philosophy of psychiatry, and I'm putting together a teaching module on the concept of disease. The backbone of the module is the divide between naturalism and normativism. On the naturalist side, a disease is a value-free matter of biological or psychological dysfunction: a condition is a disease when some system fails to function in the way characteristic of a normal member of the relevant class. On the normativist side, the concept of disease is ineliminably evaluative: to classify a condition as a disease is, at least in part, to judge it a harmful or undesirable state, and the classification often tracks social expectations rather than any purely biological norm. There are also hybrid positions on which disease requires both a dysfunction and a judgment of harm, and I want the discussion to keep these options separate.

For one session I want to work through this with the film One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest. The ward plainly treats behaviors and personalities as symptoms, and the film plainly invites us to see much of that as institutional control rather than as medicine. But it also gives us patients whose suffering is real, and it shows treatments that harm them. I do not want the session to collapse into the crude claim that the film proves psychiatric diagnosis is nothing but social labeling. So here is what I would like from you: a careful reading of the film through the naturalism/normativism divide. What thesis about the concept of disease or mental illness does the film most plausibly dramatize—that the diagnoses in the ward are misapplications of a value-free dysfunction concept, that the concept actually operating in the ward is normative through and through, or something else? Which characters, scenes, or choices carry weight for each side of the debate, and where does the film resist an easy reading? In particular, does the film help us separate the thesis that institutions pathologize deviance from the thesis that the concept of disease itself is evaluative, or does it blur exactly that line? Finally, what does the film add to the philosophical debate that the standard papers and toy examples do not? I would rather you keep both the philosophy and the film honest than force a clean verdict.
