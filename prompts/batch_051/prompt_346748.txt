I've been reading a book on risk and decision-making that a friend recomended, and there's a chapter on something called ergodicity economics that I keep going back and forth on. The author summarizes the core idea roughly like this. Standard economics tells you to evaluate a risky choice by its expected value: list the possible outcomes, weight each one by its probability, and take the average. But that average is computed across an ensemble of parallel copies of you, and you don't actually live as a bunch of parallel copies—you live one single path through time. What should matter instead is the time average, meaning how your wealth actually grows along a single unfolding history. He gives the standard example of a coin flip where heads increases your money by 50% and tails decreases it by 40%. The expected value is positive, so ordinary expected-value reasoning says the bet is good, but someone who takes that bet over and over with their whole bankroll will go broke almost surely, because the time-average growth rate is negative. From this he concludes that the whole apparatus of risk aversion in expected utility theory was just a fudge for using the wrong average, and that a rational person should turn down even a small version of the bet—indeed, that maximizing expected value is systematically irrational for anyone making risky choices.

I find the broad idea pretty compelling—averaging over parallel copies of myself does feel psychologically beside the point—but the leap from "repeated all-in play ruins you" to "never take even a small version, expected value reasoning is just irrational" feels too quick to me. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate from what is a common simplification or overstatement, and say whether the conclusion about the small bet actually follows from the time-average point.
