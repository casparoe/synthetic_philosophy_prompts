Take the standard bystander-at-the-switch trolley case, in which you can divert a trolley away from five people trapped on the main track onto a side track occupied by one trapped person. Now vary the case as follows: the side track is instead a disused spur on which twenty people are walking, each with an independent one-in-twenty chance of being killed if you divert, and none has consented to being put at risk. The five on the main track will certainly die if you do nothing. Compare how an act-consequentialist, a threshold deontologist who refuses to kill one to save five but accepts that sufficiently high stakes can outweigh some constraints, and a contractualist who asks whether non-consenting people could reasonably reject the principle behind your action would analyze the original case and this variation. In particular, does replacing one certain identifiable death with an expected one death spread across twenty non-consenting people change any of the three verdicts? Where does each view place the boundary beyond which aggregated small risks, imposed without consent, become morally equivalent to a single certain death—and should that boundary be drawn over expected lives, ex ante risk, worst-case outcomes, or something else?
