I am a novelist, a few years into researching a novel set in the near future, when therapies that slow or reverse human aging have just become available to ordinary people. In interviews with doctors, hospice workers, potential readers, and friends, including some who work in longevity research and some who call themselves transhumanists, I keep running into a stance I have no precise word for. It is not the claim that the technology will fail, arrive too late, or be distributed unfairly, although those objections are often stapled to it. It is a positive defense of mortality. People tell me, often with real feeling, that death is what gives a life its narrative arc, that without a final page no chapter would be urgent, that the scarcity of time is what makes love, ambition, or achievement matter, that a life of several hundred healthy years would necessarily become flat, repetitive, or self-absorbed because the old drives would have no deadline, and that wanting to push the horizon back is itself childish, greedy, or a failure of wisdom rather than prudence. Some of the same people will admit, a minute later, that they would accept a treatment for the cancer or heart disease already growing in them. I have also noticed something psychological about the pattern: the arguments get more confident and more elaborate as the technology gets closer. When life extension seemed like science fiction, people treated it as a fantasy; now that it is discussed as an engineering problem, they surround it with moral objections and speak as if loving mortal life required refusing the remedy. My drafts have started reproducing all of this, because my characters keep making these speeches and I keep writing them, partly because I half-believe them myself.

What I want to know is whether this cluster is one phenomenon with an established name, or whether I am mixing several separate positions together. If it has a name, where are its edges? How is it different from ordinary risk aversion, from status quo bias, from a religious conviction that an afterlife makes earthly longevity beside the point, from a judgment about the merits of this particular technology, or from the sour-grapes move of devaluing what one doubts one will have? If it has no established name, I would welcome a coined term with a definition and boundary cases, so that I can see what belongs inside the concept and what merely looks similar from a distance.
