My friend and I have been arguing about how much of the modern world is robust and how much is just frozen accident. He sent me this argument, which he thinks shows path dependence makes most of what we take for granted radically contingent:

(1) An outcome is path-dependent when which outcome you get depends on the particular sequence of events that got you there, rather than only on underlying conditions like costs, resources, or preferences.
(2) If the outcome depends on that particular sequence, then had any link in the sequence been different, the outcome would have been different too.
(3) In the standard examples—QWERTY beating other keyboard layouts, VHS beating Betamax, the standard railway gauge—the sequence that produced the outcome was, as usually told, a string of minor accidents: lucky timing, early leads, marketing decisions nobody thought much about.
(4) Each of those accidents could easily have gone the other way.
(5) So for these technologies and standards, the outcome we actually got wasn't robust at all: it hung on a chain of accidents, link by link.
(6) Since these things structure huge parts of how we live and work, huge parts of the modern world also rest on nothing deeper than a pile of lucky breaks.

He says step (2) is the engine of the whole thing and can't see how anyone could deny it. The conclusion feels too strong to me, but I can't locate the sleight of hand. Is the argument valid? If not, where exactly does it fail, and what would the corrected version of that step say?
