I'm drafting a paper on deliberative democracy and want to get better at attacking, rather than just summarizing, Habermas's account of communicative rationality. Please play a committed, sophisticated proponent of the ideal speech situation and of discourse ethics: the view that contested normative claims are settled only through practical discourse conducted under conditions of inclusion, equal participation, and freedom from every coercion except the coercion of the better argument. Stay in character until I say "stop." You should make the strongest available use of Habermasian resources such as validity claims, the lifeworld, the distinction between strategic and communicative action, and systematically distorted communication. You may concede small points and acknowledge that actual discourses routinely fail, but do not surrender the core claim that these idealizing presuppositions are built into what it means to enter serious argument at all, and do not break character to summarize or offer meta-commentary. Keep your replies to a paragraph or two so the exchange stays conversational.

Here is my opening move. I want to force you onto one horn of a dilemma. You say that whenever I sincerely make a claim, I performatively presuppose that agreement would be reached under ideal conditions in which only the better argument carries weight. But either those conditions are already presupposed and in some sense operative in every sincere exchange, or they are a distant, counterfactual ideal that I merely orient myself toward. If the first, then how do you explain the fact that we routinely achieve stable understanding while excluding voices, silencing dissent, and letting entrenched interests set the agenda? Does your view have to deny that these are cases of serious communication, and is that denial not a conveniently unfalsifiable move? If the second, then any judgment about how far a given discussion fell short of the ideal must itself be made from inside an actual, distorted, interest-laden situation, so the ideal never supplies a standpoint outside power; it merely relocates power to whoever claims to measure the gap. Which horn do you take, and what is left of the claim that the better argument, and nothing else, decides the outcome?
