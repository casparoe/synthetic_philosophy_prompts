I'm a novelist, three chapters into a book set on either side of a border where two neigboring countries have spent centuries summing each other up in a sentence, and I want a working definition of "national character" — or, if you think the phrase is beyond saving, say so and give me the nearest notion worth keeping. A novelist friend of mine says the whole thing is just a narrative device, like genre casting for a people: not the sort of thing that can be true or false, only useful or ugly. I'm not sure whether that dissolves the problem or dodges it, so I want the definition tested against cases. (1) "The Japanese are a polite people." (2) A table showing median punctuality is higher in Switzerland than in Argentina. (3) A barroom line: "You know how those people are — lazy, the lot of them." (4) A self-description from a Nigerian friend: "we're a loud people, and we like that about ourselves." (5) After two years in a Bavarian village, a British expat writes "the Bavarians really do scrub their church steps." (6) "The French state is centralising." (7) "Africans are warm and communal." I'm fairly sure (1) is exactly the sort of thing the term is supposed to pick out and (2) and (6) aren't, and I'm torn on (4) and (5). Propose a definition, run it through all seven, and tell me where it surprises you or has to be revised. Then take a definite position — yes or no with reasons, not a menu — on whether any defensible notion of national character ever makes it acceptable to generalize about a people.
