Yesterday evening I had a long argument with a friend who studies comparative politics, about which countries have genuinely undergone democratic backsliding and which have merely had severe but self-contained episodes. She refuses to state a definition: she insists she goes case by case, and that any definition would silently import the very judgments we were contesting. So I wrote down the verdicts she did commit herself to, together with the facts she treated as decisive.

Her verdicts:

1. Hungary, 2010 onward. A party with a constitutional majority entrenching itself through repeated constitutional amendments, a packed constitutional court, a rewritten electoral system, and much of the independent media converted into loyal outlets. She says: backsliding.

2. Poland, 2015–2023. The constitutional tribunal subordinated, judicial appointments and judicial discipline politicised, public broadcasters turned into party instruments. She says: backsliding.

3. Turkey, 2014 onward. Judicial independence eroded, critical media taken over or closed, the post-coup state of emergency used for wide purges, and a presidential system entrenched with few counterweights. She says: backsliding.

4. Czechia, 2017–2021. Serious conflicts of interest and hostile treatment of journalists, but no packing of courts, no manipulation of electoral rules, no takeover of public broadcasting, and no campaign to capture the institutions that checked the government. She says: not backsliding.

5. Singapore, recent decades. Dominant-party rule, restricted civil liberties, constrained electoral competition — but, she insists, it never had a liberal-democratic baseline to slide from. She says: not backsliding.

The case we argued over, on which she refused to commit, is the United States during the Trump administration (2017–2021). On her own account of the record, there was a sustained effort to overturn a lost election: repeated assertions of mass fraud, pressure on the Department of Justice to treat the election as corrupt, a call urging a state official to come up with the votes needed to reverse his state's result, a scheme to have the vice president decline to count certified electoral votes, and an attack by supporters on the Capitol during certification. Yet courts rejected the challenges, state officials mostly refused to comply, the vice president certified the outcome, media remained independent, and power was transferred on schedule.

Suppose she is to remain consistent with the five verdicts above. What should she say about the United States? Work out which rule she is implicitly running; if her five verdicts underdetermine that rule, state the live candidates, say what each predicts for the United States, and give the best-supported verdict. Identify the closest analog among the five earlier cases, and specify the further test case that would most cleanly settle the choice between the remaining rules.
