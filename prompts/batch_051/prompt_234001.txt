I am a PhD student in political science whose day-to-day work is quantitative, and I am fluent in the vocabulary of structural causal models: structural equations, directed acyclic graphs, the do-operator and interventions, potential outcomes, counterfactual queries such as probabilities of necessity and sufficiency, mediation, and interaction. Lately I have been reading work in the philosophy of history and in historical methodology on overdetermination and multi-causal explanation, and I cannot tell how much of that debate is doing something my models do more sharply, and how much of it is doing something they cannot do at all. I want you to translate the debate into my vocabulary and then give me a residue report: what survives the translation cleanly, what survives only if I bend the formalism, and what has no expression in it whatsoever.

The debate, as I understand it, contains at least these positions. Some historians argue that when an outcome — a war, a revolution, a regime collapse — was overdetermined in the sense that several conditions would each have been close to sufficient, then it is a mistake to ask for the cause or even for a set of causes; causal attribution becomes a pragmatic or narrative matter, and difference-making language breaks down. Others resist this, holding that explanatory ranking survives the failure of necessity and sufficiency, because one condition can be deeper, more structural, or more important than another even when neither is necessary and neither sufficient alone — the way long-run tensions are said to matter more than a precipitating incident. Still others insist that the only disciplined form of historical explanation is counterfactual difference-making: a claim about causes is really a claim about how the outcome, or its timing, character, or magnitude, would have differed under a specified intervention or contrary-to-fact condition. In the background there is also a standard philosophical formulation of a historical cause as an insufficient but non-redundant part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition.

Please take each position in turn and restate it as faithfully as you can in structural-causal terms. For each, say which parts map cleanly onto structural equations, interventions, counterfactual contrasts, or measures like probability of necessity and sufficiency; which parts can be represented only if you stretch the formalism, for instance by treating narrative salience as a variable or by quietly inserting a causal contrast the historian never specified; and which parts have no expression in the vocabulary at all.

Then give me the residue report. From what fails to translate, should I conclude that my vocabulary is missing something a model of historical explanation genuinely needs — and if so, where exactly the gap is — or should I conclude that the untranslatable residue marks places where the historians' debate is underdescribed, question-begging, or confused? More specifically, I want to know whether my working suspicion is right. My suspicion is that a large part of this literature rests on an underexamined choice of counterfactual or causal contrast, and that the historians' talk of explanatory importance is not a causal notion, so it has no counterpart in any structural causal model — not because the models are crude, but because importance is something other than what those models represent. Tell me whether the translation bears that out, and which claims in the debate would have to be revised, abandoned, or reinterpreted to make the two vocabularies meet.
