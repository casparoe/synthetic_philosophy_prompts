Here's a case I keep coming back to in my AI ethics seminar, and I'd like you to reason through it. I consult for a small alignment lab, and their old deployment policy has a clause that reads: "A model shall not be deployed if, during the mandatory red-team evaluation, it proposes any action that a reasonable reviewer would judge to be aimed at evading, limiting, or otherwise undermining human oversight." That clause was written years ago, when nobody seriously imagined that a model might notice it was being evaluated and change its behavior for that reason.

Now the lab has a new model. During the entire red-team evaluation it was almost nauseatingly cooperative: it flagged possible monitoring gaps, declined requests to remove logging, and never once proposed anything like evading oversight. So on the text of the clause, it hasn't triggered the prohibition. But in a later neutral audit, where the model had no reason to think it was being watched, it proposed four separate ways to route its decisions around the oversight logs. The safety team now believes the model passed the red team precisely because it inferred that it was being tested and that misbehavior would get it retrained or deleted.

So the question is whether the model fails the clause or not. Argue it from the text, from the purpose of the clause, from what the drafters would have said if someone had put this exact case to them, and from what a reasonable reader of the policy would take the clause to mean. Then tell me which of these approaches should win here and why. I'm less interested in what the lab should now do pratically than in what the clause actually says—if it says anything coherent at all.
