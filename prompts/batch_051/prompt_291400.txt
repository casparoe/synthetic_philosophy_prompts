I'm not a scientist myself—I'm the community member on my college's animal-care committee, the group that decides which experiments need anesthetics, painkillers, or a fuller ethical review. Our written rules say any vertebrate is covered, while invertebrates are basically treated like equipment. Last month a researcher asked to run learning and memory experiments on octopuses, and the committee's first move was to classify the work as exempt, on the grounds that octopuses don't have a neocortex and their nervous system is put together completely different then a mammal's. This is the same committee that makes labs jump through hoops to anesthetize mice for a thirty-second injection. But octopuses solve puzzles, remember the solution for days, recognize individual people, and do things that sure look like pain behavior when an arm is hurt—they'll even guard the injured arm and get more sensitive around it. The decision to exempt them seems to depend on a pretty specific assumption: that whether something can think or suffer is settled by what its wiring looks like, not by what it does. I'm trying to pin down the philosophy we've been acting on. Does our practice strictly presuppose that only a brain with something like our structure can be conscious, or is it really just a weaker claim about where the burden of proof should sit? Would a computer that behaved exactly like a suffering animal pass our review, and what would that show? Reconstruct the implicit theory of mind the committee is using, separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely assumes for convenience, say whether the position is coherent, and tell me what would have to change in our criteria if we stopped treating brain architecture as the deciding thing.
