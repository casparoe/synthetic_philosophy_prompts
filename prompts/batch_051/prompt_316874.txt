You are an experienced designer of philosophy classroom exercises and an applied ethicist working on the ethics of the family and childhood. I teach a sixteen-student upper-level undergraduate seminar and have eighty minutes to work with. In the previous session, students examined two positions that pull against each other: the proposal that parenting competence should be licensed, roughly as driving or adoption is, and the child's right to an open future, understood as an interest in reaching adulthood with a sufficiently wide range of life options still intact rather than having them foreclosed by the adults who raise them. Last year I ran this material as a structured debate, and it collapsed into the predictable pro-licensing versus anti-licensing sides; I want something that does not let them stay outside the problem.

Design me a single-session structured activity — a licensing-board simulation, a role-play, a decision game, a forced-choice exercise, or some hybrid — in which the participants themselves hold and must exercise the licensing power. The central idea should arise from what they do, not from what I say: they should first construct and apply their own criteria for an adequate parent, then encounter cases in which those criteria either fail on a technicality for parents they would otherwise accept, or pass every explicit test for parents whose approach to raising a child forecloses futures in ways the group itself finds objectionable. Use the rules, roles, incentives, or time pressure to manufacture the collision rather than relying on discussion afterwards. Please give me the full setup and materials (cards, profiles, scoring sheets, or prompts as needed), the roles or positions, the exact rules and constraints, an exact minute-by-minute timeline for the eighty minutes, and any real but low stakes — time, status, a small privilege, not money — that make the choices feel like choices.

Then give me the debrief. It should map what actually happened back to the philosophical distinctions: between setting a floor of parental competence and imposing a state ideal of a good childhood; between protecting a child from harm and preserving a child's open future; and between the adults' interest in raising a child in their own tradition and the child's later interest in being able to reflect on and revise that tradition. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for example, groups that smuggle in a thick conception of the good while insisting they are being neutral, or that confound their own preferences with the child's interests — and how I should handle each failure in the moment. Be frank about which feature of the real licensing debate the activity cannot reproduce, and what the facilitator must supply by instruction to make up for it. Before settling on a design, think carefully about the sequence of psychological moves: brainstorm two or three candidate mechanics, say which one you chose, and explain why that one best makes the participants feel the force of the open-future objection rather than just hear it. The students should not hear the names of the positions until the debrief.
