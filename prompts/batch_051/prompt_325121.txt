I’m rereading Foundation with a few mates from a book club and we kept coming back to the same argument. The book presents Hari Seldon’s psychohistory as a genuine science of long-run human history: if you have enough people and they don’t know the predictions, you can plot the fall of an empire and the rise of its successor. That already sounds quite close to what cliodynamics claims, at least in its softer versions—Turchin’s secular cycles, structural-demographic pressures, elite overproduction and all that. But there’s a difference: Asimov seems to need the subjects to be ignorant of the prediction for the science to work, whereas real macrohistory, if it works at all, has to keep working even when people have read the paper. I’d like you to read the novel through that lens and say where the science in the story is genuinely cliodynamic, and where its actually just Laplace-style determinism in disguise.

The part that’s troubling us is the Mule. In the plot hes framed as the unpredictable individual, the random mutation or whatever, that a science of mass trends cannot foresee. But a structural historian would not necessarily put him outside the system: elites fight over state capacity, and a usurper like the Mule emerges from exactly the sort of political crisis the Seldon Plan predicts. So does the novel make a false dichotomy between individual agency and macro-prediction? Or does it actually show something true, which is that even if you can model structural cycles, you cannot predict the shape of the crises they produce? I want to be able to explain to my group whether the novel is a good intuition pump for thinking about cliodynamics or a bit of a trap.

Could you write me a reading of the book along those lines, maybe 900 words, in a fairly informal style? The group is mixed—some historians, some scientists—so I’d love it if you unpacked the sense in which each side might feel vindicated or annoyed by the comparison.
