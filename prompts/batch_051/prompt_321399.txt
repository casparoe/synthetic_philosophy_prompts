I've been going around in circles on this and I want a careful verdict rather than a side. I find the no-trade argument genuinely compelling: if two Bayesian agents start from a common prior and only differ by private information, then a public offer to trade is itself evidence about the other person's signal — if they're selling, that's evidence their signal was bearish, so you should revise your price down; they know you'd revise, and so on, until no mutually acceptable price survives. So a market populated only by rational informed agents shouldn't trade at all, and price changes should happen without anyone trading. But I also can't shake the firm intuition that actual markets do aggregate dispersed information — I don't just mean insider trading; I mean ordinary price discovery in something like a stock or a betting market, where I feel like lots of individually small pieces of knowledge end up in the price. I don't want to be told the theorem is right or the intuition is right. I want a negotiated account of which way each side should move: which assumption (common priors, no pre-existing risk-sharing motives, no noise or liquidity traders, pure information differences, something else) is doing the real work, and once you relax it does the no-trade result collapse into something much weaker or does it survive in modified form? Should my market intuition be debunked as me mistaking hedging, consumption smoothing and noise for information aggregation? Or is there a reading on which both survive — the theorem governing what information differences alone can achieve, and real markets aggregating information only because one of those other things is present? Spell out what would justify each of those moves, and close out with what an honest epistemology of markets ought to claim. Answer in British English, please.
