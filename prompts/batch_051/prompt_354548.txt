I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a peice about citizens' assemblies and term limits, and there's something I can't get straight. The same political system seems to run on three different theories of what qualifies a person to make binding decisions for others.

On the one hand, we let random citizens sit on juries and, increasingly, on citizens' assemblies that draft actual policy, and the standard defense of that is that ordinary judgment is sound and that professionals become captured or self-interested. On the other hand, we elect legislators on the theory that some people are unusually good at politics and should make a career of it. And then many of those same systems impose term limits, forcibly removing exactly the people who have racked up the most experience. Ancient Athens, I'm told, went further and filled most offices by lot, on the grounds that elections are aristocratic because they favor the rich, well-connected, and verbally quick.

So: what view of political knowledge, virtue, or representation does each of these practices actually presuppose? Are sortition and career legislatures strictly incompatible, or does the conflict dissolve under a better description? Is there a single coherent position that licenses sortition, elections, and term limits all at once — or does one of them have to be a mistake by the other practices' own lights? And what would have to change in the practices if we took the best account seriously?

Please answer as a professional political philosopher who works on institutional design. Think carefully: start by laying out the candidate presuppositions and how they conflict, then commit to the one account you think is best and defend it. I don't want a survey of options; I want a definite answer, with the places you're least sure about flagged honestly. Since I'm a journalist, not a specialist, spell out any technical terms. Please write in American English.
