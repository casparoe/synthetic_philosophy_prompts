In my historiography seminar the tutor keeps reaching for the past-is-a-foreign-country line, the one about people doing things differently there, to shut down arguments about whether we can judge people in 16th-century England by contemporary standards. Her line is roughly: a historian's job is to understand the locals on their own terms, not to issue moral verdicts from outside, and present-day judgements are just tourist complaints. I half agree, but the analogy feels like its doing a lot of unexamined work, and some of it is probably doing the presentism debate for us on the sly.

Could you audit it for me? Give me the correspondence table first: what maps to the foreign country, the traveller, the language barrier, the customs, the guidebook, the return flight, and whatever else is doing work in the analogy. Then the disanalogies: where does the past not behave like a foreign country at all? I suspect the analogy quietly imports a few things: that both places exist at the same time; that the traveller has a home to return to which is not itself made out of the country being visited; that the locals know their own customs in a way a translator could recover; and that the visitor can leave without changing what it means to be home. Also, is the historian more like a traveller, a translator, an ambassador, or a descendant of the locals? Then the verdict: does the conclusion she draws from it — that present-day moral judgement of the past is unhistorical, or that we can and should bracket our own standards — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was it being carried by the analogy the whole way? And if it doesn't survive, tell me what a better analogy would be, or whether presentism is better argued without the travel metaphor at all.
