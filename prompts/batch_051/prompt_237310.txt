I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and last week I watched two people I respect get into a genuinely hard argument at a conference reception, and I've been chewing on it since. You're a professional philosopher who works on blame and moral responsibility, so I'd like you to think carefully about it. Maybe start by brainstorming the best version of each side before making any judgments.

The trigger was a senator (you'll recognize the type) who wrote a furious op-ed about a mayor caught taking kickbacks, while the senator himself is currently under investigation for nearly identical self-dealing. The two people, call them Maya and Daniel, argued like this:

Maya said the senator's op-ed counts for nothing because he's guilty of the same thing. "You lose the standing to make that criticism when your doing it yourself."

Daniel said that's just the tu quoque fallacy. The fact that the senator is corrupt doesn't show the mayor isn't corrupt, and doesn't show corruption is okay. A smoker can tell you smoking causes cancer.

Maya said blame isn't just a statement of facts; it's a demand that someone account for themselves and change. A demand like that only has force if the person making it is willing to live under the same standard. The senator exempts himself from the standard while trying to enforce it on the mayor, so his demand is hollow. That's a point about standing, not logic — a judge with a financial stake in a case is disqualified even if she'd rule correctly.

Daniel said that's exactly the wrong comparison. A judge's verdict has legal force, so we need safeguards. Everyday blame has no force behind it; it's just one person registering that a wrong happened. If only the upright could blame, then almost no one could blame anyone. Besides, some of our most important whistleblowers are compromised people.

Maya said nobody's asking for purity. There's a difference between being generally imperfect and actively doing the exact thing you're condemning while you keep doing it. That second thing is hypocrisy, and it specifically destroys standing. A person who used to take kickbacks, came clean, and stopped would be in a different position.

Daniel said that line just smuggles the sainthood requirement back in under another name — now you have to have reached moral compliance before you can talk. And if the senator's factual claims are right, the public still learns something true about the mayor. Why throw away true information because the messenger is a creep? If anything, an active corrupt player has insider knowledge, so he might be better at spotting the schemes.

Maya said standing isn't about maximizing information for bystanders; it's about the relationship between the blamer and the blamed. Blame is partly a relational act, and being lectured by a fellow wrongdoer adds an extra wrong. The mayor can legitimately say "you, of all people?" — and the fact that this reply makes sense shows the senator's demand misfired. It's not just that we dislike the messenger.

Daniel said "you, of all people?" is itself just a complaint about the speaker's character, not an answer to the charge. If the mayor is corrupt, the statement "the mayor is corrupt" isn't made false or irrelevant by the person who says it. At most we should conclude they're both corrupt, not that the senator's criticism is worthless.

Then they had to go to a panel, and I never got a resolution.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the steps (Maya's opening, Daniel's reply, and so on), rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify the rating in a sentence or two. Then tell me who is more right overall, and — this is what I really want — what both of them are missing. Don't just give me a generic "both have a point" answer; I want you to use the specifics of what each of them actually said.
