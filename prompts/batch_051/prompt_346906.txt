Ive been turning over something about hard choices and want it checked before I try to write it up. By hard choices I mean the kind where the options are neither better than each other, nor worse, nor exactly equal — where ranking them feels like it would misdescribe at least one of the things you care about. Not the cases where you're just uncertain or missing information, but the ones where more information wouldn't settle it.

The theory, which I'm calling the wear of choosing, is this: incommensurability is not mainly a feature of two values sitting out in the world; it's a feature of a chooser who hasn't yet traded those values against each other. The first time you have to choose between, say, moving city for a job and staying near your ageing parents, the options feel non-comparable. But if you've made versions of that same trade half a dozen times, you end up with a private exchange rate — maybe not one you could state out loud, but you behave as if one career move is worth about two Christmases missed, or whatever it happens to be. Each choice deposits a little sediment of precedent, and after enough sediment the hardness is gone. Three predictions fall out. A novice chooser and a veteran chooser facing the same underlying values will experience different levels of hardness. Veterans will be able to produce rates where novices insist that the categories simply don't compare. And veterans will choose faster but not better, because the rate is just scar tissue, not wisdom. I know this sounds like I've rediscovered the point that people learn their utilities, but I don't think that's quite it — the claim is meant to be about the hardness itself and where it goes.

So: is this a real distinction or just a metaphor? Does it already exist under another name I should know about? And where does it break — what's the strongest case of a hard choice that doesn't get easier with repetition, and does that refute the theory or just limit it?
