ok so I've been trying to pin down my actual views on hope and optimism for a reading group, and I want a consistency check before I have to present them. Here's the list as it stands:

(1) Evidentialism: a belief is justified only if it's proportioned to the evidence. So optimism, understood as a standing tendency to overestimate the probability of good outcomes, is an epistemic vice.
(2) Hope isn't optimism in belief form: you can hope for an outcome while assigning it very low credence. So (1) doesn't automatically indict hope.
(3) Hope can be a moral virtue, and sometimes even admirable, in people who face very bad odds and would otherwise stop acting—the organizer with no chance on paper, the patient, the parent of a kid who keeps failing. In those cases it can be right to hope against the evidence.
(4) It can be rational all-things-considered to cultivate that kind of hope even when you know your credence is low, because the motivational effects of hope give you practical reasons to be in the hopeful state.
(5) Epistemic norms and practical norms can diverge without contradiction: an attitude can be epistemically improper but practically right to have.
(6) But I also think hope that depends on not looking at the evidence is worse than hope that stays informed—and worse in a specifically epistemic way, not just a practical one. If the only way to keep a hope alive is not to look at the numbers, I want to say the hope is less rationally respectable, maybe even not all-things-considered rational despite its practical benefits.
(7) I'm an involuntarist about belief (you cant believe at will), but I think hope is more like a policy or orientation that can be deliberately cultivated.

I have the vague sense that this list can't all be right at once, but I can't locate the problem, and I don't want to just flag everything as equally conflicted.
