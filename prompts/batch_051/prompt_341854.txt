I'm a community college instructor, and I've noticed I have a policy forming about people in my life, and I can't tell if its a decent heuristic or a dumb prejudice. The policy is that I treat every small broken promise — being twenty minutes late without texting, saying "I'll send you that article tonight" and never doing it, accepting a dinner invite and cancelling an hour before — as evidence about a single underlying trait, call it "shows up for people." I don't let context blunt the inference much. If you were late because your kid puked, that counts as an emergency and I don't count it. But "work ran long" gets maybe a quarter of the weight of a no-excuse flake. After about five counted incidents I basically stop relying on that person for anything that matters, even though I've never actually seen them break a big promise — I might never get to observe a big promise with them at all.

My partner says I'm loading all these different actions onto one trait and dressing up an ethical judgment (it's wrong to flake) as an epistemic one (it predicts future flaking). He says small obligations are cheap to break precisely because they're small, so that domain might just be where people reveal their least conscientious self, while still being wholly reliable about big ones — and that I'm basically sentencing people on traffic tickets before they've committed a felony.

I can feel he's partly right, but I also think a pattern of small breaches is the only cheap evidence I'll ever get before a big breach, and maybe the only affordable one. So: is my policy defensible as a predictive heuristic? What would have to be true about cross-situational consistency — or about how people allocate their reliability — for me to be licensed to extrapolate from small promises to big ones? And is there a restated version that keeps the predictive value without the moral smugness? Please give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, in American English.
