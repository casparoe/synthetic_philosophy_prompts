So I’ve been reading Goodhart's 1975 paper, Problems of Monetary Management: the UK Experience, because I had to build a “customer support quality” metric for my company. There’s a line in the paper — I dont remember the exact wording and I wont trust my memory — where he says, roughly, that an observed statistical regularity only stays useful as a target if you don’t use it as one; once you put control pressure on it, the relationship it had with the thing you care about gets knocked loose. Not necessarily because people are scheming, but because the system changes around the target.

That led me to a homemade concept I call “metric sedimentation.” The usual Goodhart story says: a metric starts out tracking what you care about, people game it, and now the readings diverge. But I think that understates things. My claim is that after a high-stakes metric has been used long enough, it stops being a proxy altogether and becomes a small institution: its local meaning gets partly constituted by the gaming and defensive reclassification around it. The metric and the behavior settle into each other. What the score “means” to the people being scored is the set of moves that raises it, not the property it was originally introduced to track. So it doesn’t just stop measuring the original goal; it becomes the goal of a different practice.

Example: our support score started out as “resolved customer issues.” Six months in, agents are closing duplicate tickets, making micro-calls to turn complaints into “positive outcomes,” and learning which survey phrasing bumps the number. Internally, the score now functions as “how well you move the system’s levers,” not “how well you helped the customer.” I dont think that’s just individual corruption; the score is training everyone into a different kind of work.

So my question: is “metric sedimentation” actually a distinct idea, or am I just restating Goodhart and Campbell under a new label? I want a candid assessment, not a pat on the head. Where exactly does the concept break? Give me the strongest counterexample you can: a case where a high-stakes metric stayed a decent proxy for a long time, or where the sedimentation metaphor misdescribes what actually happens. And if the whole framing rests on a confused picture of what a metric is, say so plainly.
