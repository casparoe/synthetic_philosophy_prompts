In the rationalist-adjacent and economics-blog discussions I follow, the open-borders position is often compressed into a single consistency argument: immigration is just free trade applied to labor. The claim is that if you accept the standard economist's case for free trade in goods, you are committed to something close to unrestricted labor mobility—that a border control is a tariff on labor, and that the same comparative-advantage logic predicts even larger gains from letting people move to where their work is more productive. The argument is used mainly to shift the burden of proof: someone who endorses free trade in goods but still favors immigration restrictions is supposed to be engaged in special pleading.

I would like an audit of that analogy rather than a list of costs and benefits of immigration. Start with the correspondence table: what is being mapped to the imported good, the tariff, the non-tariff barrier, the transport cost, the exporting firm, the consumer, and the adjustment mechanism. Then identify where a migrant diverges from a shipment: the migrant is the seller arriving together with the good; the migrant can draw on host-country institutions, age into public services, form households, vote, and alter the political equilibrium in a way a container of goods cannot. For each divergence, say whether it breaks the consistency argument or only complicates the welfare calculation—whether it is a leak in the analogy or merely a nuance the analogy omits. Finally, give a defended verdict on whether the free-trader-with-border-controls position is genuinely inconsistent, and whether the analogy was doing the real argumentative work in the first place.
