Since being appointed to the grants committee of a family foundation that gives away most of its income, I have had to spend a great deal of time deciding what a morally excellent life actually demands, and the old question of whether a morally perfect person would be an appealing ideal has become concrete for me. In working through that question, I have arrived at a distinction I have been leaning on, and I want it checked before I rely on it further.

I have been calling the two ideals "exhaustive sainthood" and "overriding sainthood." In exhaustive sainthood, the standard of maximal moral goodness ranges over every choice a person makes: the saint asks of each hour, each friendship, and each meal whether it is the morally best use of that hour, friendship, or meal. In overriding sainthood, most choices are not moral choices at all. The saint cooks, jokes, reads, or practices an instrument without first asking whether doing so maximizes the good; but whenever a choice is a moral one, moral considerations simply defeat all competing considerations, with nothing left over.

My suggestion is that nearly all the familiar objections to the saintly ideal—that such a person would be monomaniacal, humorless, unable to enjoy nonmoral excellence, and so on—are really objections to exhaustive sainthood. The overriding saint could in principle be an excellent dinner companion, because she does not regard the choice of what to cook as a moral question at all; her saintliness consists in her willingness to drop any of these pursuits the moment morality actually speaks. If that is right, then the anti-saint arguments prove less than they are usually taken to prove: they refute the ideal of moral maximality only when maximality is assumed to be exhaustive.

I can already see two ways this might collapse. "Overriding sainthood" may turn out to be ordinary decency plus strength of will, in which case the distinction contributes nothing. Or the act of sorting choices into moral and nonmoral may itself be governed by morality, in which case the overriding saint is no less caught up in moral bookkeeping than the exhaustive saint, only one level up. I would like an honest assessment: Is this a stable distinction, or does it already exist under another name? Does my central claim about which ideal the objections reach survive scrutiny? And what is left of the debate about moral sainthood once the exhaustive version is set aside?
