Sometime in the last year I watched two regulars on a rationalist forum argue about whether a person can ever rationally refuse to learn a freely available, decision-relevant fact. I have returned to the exchange several times because each side kept making points that seemed decisive and then dissolving under the other's reply, and I still cannot assign the moves. I'll reconstruct it as faithfully as memory allows.

Daniel opened with the familiar argument from expected value. Suppose you face a decision under uncertainty and someone offers you, for free, a piece of information relevant to that decision. You cannot be worse off in expectation for accepting it, because you can decide in advance to keep exactly the plan you would have followed had you never seen it. If the information does lead you to change plans, it does so only because you now judge the new plan to have higher expected value than the old one; otherwise you would simply not change. Therefore free information can never lower your expected utility.

Kiran replied that this assumes an agent who can precommit to act as if they did not know, and real people cannot un-read a result. She offered a person deciding whether to take a genetic test for a late-onset condition a month before a long-planned trip. The result will still be true after the trip. If he learns now, he may be unable to stop dwelling on it during the trip, and he knows this about himself. The plan "act as I would have acted without the information" is not available to him, because that plan includes the very mental state of not knowing, which he cannot reproduce once he knows.

Daniel answered that this is a cost of the act of learning, not a property of the information itself. The theorem does not say that receiving the result is pleasant or worth doing; it says that information, considered only as evidence, cannot worsen the expected value of the available outcomes. If anticipated distress is large enough, declining the test may be rational, but that is not a counterexample to the theorem. It is a case where acquiring the information carries real psychic costs, and those costs enter the decision as ordinary consequences.

Kiran pushed back that this move drains the practical claim. If every reason to avoid knowing gets reclassified as a cost, then "information can never hurt you" is preserved by definition, but "you should always want the data" is false for humans. Worse, more information can degrade decisions, not merely feelings. She gave a doctor who, from auditing her own behavior, knows that opening a section of the chart describing a patient's past disagreements with other clinicians makes her under-treat the patient's reported pain even when she resolves not to. The section is there to read, and she can decline to open it. The theorem seems to imply that she should be able to open it and then "not use" it, but she cannot selectively forget, and her attempts to correct for her own bias may overshoot or fail. A bounded agent can make better decisions with less data.

Daniel replied that this is the same point about the cost of processing and using information. If the doctor has reliable evidence about her own bias, the rational comparison includes "don't look" and "look and then try to compensate," and it may well tell her not to open the page. That is not a failure of the theorem; it is the theorem being applied correctly. What the theorem rules out is treating ignorance as valuable in itself. Deliberately staying uninformed is not an exception to the principle; it is an instance of it, because the agent has identified a cost that outweighs the expected gain.

Kiran answered that Daniel's fallback is now too weak to guide anyone. The formal result assumes fixed utilities, unlimited self-control, and costless computation; humans have none of these. So the correct response to someone who says "I'd rather not know" is not "the data can't hurt you." The data can hurt them badly. The correct response is "let's identify which limitation is doing the work, and whether the cost of working around it is smaller than the cost of remaining ignorant." On that point she thought they mostly agreed, though she doubted Daniel would admit how much of the original slogan had been given up.

Daniel ended by conceding that "always want the data" needs the qualifier "unless the act of acquiring, processing, or possessing it is itself costly," but he insisted that the qualifier is what makes the principle useful rather than empty.

I would like a point-by-point adjudication. Which specific claims is each party right about, and which are mistaken? At what point, if any, do they begin to talk past each other? Is Daniel's distinction between information itself and the act of learning it a sound way to preserve the theorem, or is it a motte-and-bailey? And does Kiran's doctor example defeat the view Daniel actually holds, or only a stronger view he has already abandoned?
