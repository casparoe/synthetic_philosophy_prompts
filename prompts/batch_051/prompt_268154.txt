After retiring from a career in the physical sciences, I have started reading philosophy in a more organized way, and one idea keeps surfacing in discussions of both personal conduct and constitutional design. The story, as I understand it, is that Ulysses had himself lashed to the mast so that he could hear the Sirens' song while his sailors, their ears stopped with wax, were under orders not to untie him no matter what he said. Philosophers often treat this as the model for a wider class of arrangements. A person with a recurrent illness signs an advance directive instructing physicians to disregard his later request for treatment. A recovering gambler arranges for his bank to refuse withdrawals above a fixed limit. A constitution entrenches certain rights or institutional rules so that they cannot be altered by a bare legislative majority but only by some deliberately more difficult procedure, such as supermajorities in separately elected bodies, approval by constituent units, or repeated votes separated by a waiting period. Ordinary contract law sits nearby: my earlier self may make my later self pay damages for walking away, though it cannot usually force me to perform.

These practices strike me as philosophically strange in the same way. Each lets an earlier stage of an agent, or an earlier generation of a polity, exercise authority over a later stage that is in some sense the same agent or the same people and in another sense not. The legal system appears to waver between treating them as the same and as different. A contract can be voided if the signer was incompetent or under duress, but not merely because the signer now regards the commitment as unwise. An advance directive may be overridden in some jurisdictions on evidence that the patient changed her mind, but much turns on just how settled the earlier wish was. A present majority is taken to be sovereign, yet it is not sovereign enough to undo a past majority's entrenchment by ordinary legislation. I would like you to lay out what view of the persistence of persons or peoples, of rational authority over time, and of democratic legitimacy these practices presuppose. Please distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with, and say where they leave the underlying philosophy genuinely underdetermined. In particular, how must one think about the standing of a later self, or a later public, to revoke the earlier decision—and when, if ever, is the claim that I am not the same person who made that commitment more than a metaphor?

Since I am new to this, I would also ask you to indicate how confident you are in the various claims you make and to single out the point on which you are least sure.
