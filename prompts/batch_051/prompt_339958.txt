You are a professional moral philosopher who works on deontic logic and the structure of moral conflict, and who regularly teaches advanced undergraduates. I want you to produce a handout for an upper-level ethics seminar: a classification instrument — a decision tree with a compact scoring key — that sorts someone into one of the live positions on whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible. The instrument must sort people by what they believe, not by what they call themselves.

Before writing the instrument, take a brief brainstorming section in which you lay out the logical space from first principles. Work at least through these elements:

- The standard quick argument against dilemmas: if an agent is required to do each of two things, and the two cannot both be done, then (a) obligations agglomerate into a single conjunctive requirement, while (b) no one can be required to do the impossible; therefore the two requirements cannot both hold. Say which premise a defender of dilemmas must reject, and what the resulting positions look like.
- The Rossian distinction between prima facie duties, which are genuinely binding but can be outweighed, and all-things-considered duty, and the thought that most apparent dilemmas are conflicts among prima facie duties that are always resolved into a single final duty.
- Whether the moral remainder of a hard choice — the agent's own sense of guilt or the duty to apologize or make amends — is evidence that a requirement really was violated, or merely an emotional residue.
- Whether obligations can conflict because the underlying values are incommensurable, so that no option is best and no ranking is available, rather than merely because the ranking is hard to compute.
- Whether every morally possible choice situation must contain at least one best or uniquely right act, and which positions are committed to that.

Then build the decision tree itself: about six to eight questions, no more than ten leaves. Each branch node must be a concrete case or a precise, self-contained claim, answerable by a respondent who does not already know which label the answer leads to. For every branch, state the question, the answer options, and where each option sends the respondent.

Use at least the following cases somewhere in the tree, modifying or compressing them only to isolate one distinction at a time: a parent forced to choose which of two children will survive (the Sophie's choice scenario); a student who must choose between leaving to join the resistance and remaining as the sole support of his dependent mother; and a promise case in which keeping an ordinary promise would cost a stranger's life (your own version).

At the leaves, give the position its standard name if it has one, or say it is unnamed if no one defends that exact combination, and add one sentence stating the position's most awkward commitment. Include at least these leaves, as well as any further coherent combinations the logical space yields: Kantian rationalism (no genuine dilemmas); act-consequentialism (no genuine dilemmas because one maximizing ranking always exists); Rossian prima facie pluralism (real conflicts below the all-things-considered level, but none at it); a remainder or dirty-hands view (dilemmas are real, justified wrongdoing is still wrongdoing, and the remainder is a sign of it); an incommensurability view (dilemmas arise because values do not share a single scale); and a deontic-logic view on which obligations do not agglomerate, blocking the standard argument.

Finally, provide (i) a compact scoring key that maps the answer patterns the tree actually permits to leaves, and (ii) a short note on unoccupied combinations: for each empty cell in the logical space, say whether no one is there because the combination is incoherent, because it would be unattractive for a stated reason, or because it is simply unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate, and explain what finer test would be needed.

Write the whole handout crisply and formally. Do not include references, reading lists, citations, or attributions to particular articles or books; the instrument must stand on the cases and the structure of the arguments alone.
