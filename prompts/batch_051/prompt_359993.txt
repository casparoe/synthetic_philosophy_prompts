I have no academic background, but I read a lot on my own and keep noticing the same pattern in my own reactions. In Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments he makes an observation I cannot get out of my head—I will not try to quote it exactly—that a person is more distressed by falling from a better to a worse situation than he is pleased by rising from a worse to a better one. That felt immediately true to me, but I have started to wonder whether it explains something larger: the fact that people regularly demand more to sell an object than they would pay to buy it, even when the object is trivial and they have owned it for only a few minutes. In both cases, a loss seems heavier than a matching gain.

My question is whether this asymmetry is a bias we should try to correct or a legitimate preference with a defensible rationale. Are Smith's point and the buying–selling gap the same underlying phenomenon, or merely similar-looking? Is there a good reason why losing should matter more than gaining, or why a thing should genuinely be worth more to someone just because it is hers?

If you think it is a bias, tell me what the corrected preferences would look like and whether a person could actually live by them. If you think it is legitimate, defend it against a hard case where possession carries no useful information: someone has just been handed an ordinary mug and is asked the smallest amount she would accept to give it up, while someone else—same resources, same tastes—has not been handed the mug and is asked the largest amount she would pay to get it. If the two numbers differ only because one person already owns the mug, is that difference justifiable, or is it an illusion created by the status quo? And if my framing is confused, say so plainly instead of pretending the question is well posed.
