For years I have been reading the exchange among economic historians, sinologists, and historians of science about the Great Divergence, and I have the impression the debate has effectively stalled. The main camps each retain their favored causes. One stresses material and ecological factors — accessible coal, and New World land and resources that relieved Europe's land constraint — and treats Europe's breakthrough as substantially contingent. Another stresses institutional and market conditions in Europe — secure property, financial sophistication, competitive state formation — and treats Chinese institutions as the binding constraint. Another stresses demography and factor prices, arguing that relatively high English wages made labor-saving machinery profitable while abundant, cheaper Chinese labor did not. Another stresses epistemic culture, arguing that a community of natural philosophers oriented toward useful knowledge made cumulative technical change possible. A revisionist strand adds a further complication: once the comparison is drawn between England and the Yangzi delta rather than between "England" and "China" as wholes, the two look roughly comparable in living standards, market integration, and technical competence as late as the mid-eighteenth century, with a decisive separation only after 1800. Meanwhile the Needham question — why China's earlier technical and scientific achievements did not lead to modern science — sits slightly askew of the divergence question, sometimes treated as the same problem and sometimes as a different one.

What frustrates me is that the debate no longer looks like a normal empirical disagreement. The same facts are recruited for different positions; each side accuses the others of comparing the wrong units or selecting the outcome of interest; and the counterfactual baseline — what should have been expected to happen in China, and by when — is left implicit. I have a hypothesis, but I want it tested rather than assumed: that this is not one stalled debate but several questions fused together — why Britain first; why Europe rather than China; why not the Yangzi delta; why China's earlier trajectory did not continue; why modern science arose where it did — and that the camps differ less about the facts than about what would count as an explanation, what the relevant unit of comparison is, what kind of evidence is admissible, and how much contingency versus structure a satisfactory answer must posit.

Diagnose this impasse. Is the question why industrialization began in Britain rather than China well posed? Is the disagreement substantive, verbal, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress — a sharper division of questions, an explicit counterfactual test, a formal model, a change in the unit of comparison, or the conclusion that the question should be retired in its current form.
