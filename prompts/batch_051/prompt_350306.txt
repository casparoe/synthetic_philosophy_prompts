I teach a seminar on the history of utilitarianism and well-being, and I want to pressure-test an old objection against a newer empirical finding.

Let me lay out the setup. In An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, Chapter IV, Bentham specifies the dimensions along which the value of any pleasure or pain is to be estimated:

II. To a person considered by himself, the value of a pleasure or pain considered by itself, will be greater or less, according to the four following circumstances:
- Its intensity.
- Its duration.
- Its certainty or uncertainty.
- Its propinquity or remoteness.

III. These are the circumstances which are to be considered in estimating a pleasure or a pain considered each of them by itself. But when the value of any pleasure or pain is considered for the purpose of estimating the tendency of any act by which it is produced, there are two other circumstances to be taken into the account; these are,
- Its fecundity, or the chance it has of being followed by sensations of the same kind: that is, pleasures, if it be a pleasure: pains, if it be a pain.
- Its purity, or the chance it has of not being followed by sensations of the opposite kind: that is, pains, if it be a pleasure: pleasures, if it be a pain.

IV. To a number of persons, with reference to each of whom to the value of a pleasure or a pain is considered, it will be greater or less, according to seven circumstances: to wit, the six preceding ones; viz.,
- Its intensity.
- Its duration.
- Its certainty or uncertainty.
- Its propinquity or remoteness.
- Its fecundity.
- Its purity.

And one other; to wit:
- Its extent; that is, the number of persons to whom it extends; or (in other words) who are affected by it.

This passage only makes sense if pleasures as different as the pleasure of eating, the pleasure of being vindicated, and the pleasure of grasping a proof can be placed on a single scale, scored for the same features, and summed. The heterogeneity problem for hedonism denies exactly that. Crudely put: pleasures have nothing in common except that English happens to call them all pleasures. The pleasure of a warm bath, the pleasure of Schadenfreude, the pleasure of understanding a proof, and the pleasure of scratching an itch do not share a single felt quality, and the project of aggregating them as quantities of one stuff called pleasure is a philosopher's fiction. So the objection goes.

Now add a development Bentham could not have anticipated. Over the last few decades, affective and decision neuroscience has produced evidence that a wide range of rewards — food, money, erotic images, social approval, music, even relief from pain — are processed through overlapping dopaminergic valuation circuitry, and that their subjective value appears to be encoded along a common scale in regions such as the orbitofrontal cortex. This is often called the common-currency hypothesis. Taken at face value, the brain seems to do something rather like what Bentham's calculus demands: it reduces heterogeneous goods to a single value signal.

My question is whether the heterogeneity problem survives this development. Specifically: does the neural common-currency finding show that there is, after all, something real that all pleasures have in common, so that the objection loses its force? Or does it merely show that the brain treats heterogeneous goods as if they were commensurable — which is the very move the heterogeneity objection says is a mistake, now relocated from the philosopher's page to the brain? Or is the right verdict that the objection needs repair, surviving only if restated as a claim about felt quality rather than as a claim about the absence of any shared underlying currency? I want to know which premise of the heterogeneity argument the neural evidence bears on, and whether the argument is dead, needs repair, or goes through unchanged.

One thing I ask: if the question as posed rests on a confused premise — for instance, if I am conflating a neural representation of value with the phenomenal character of a pleasure, or if the heterogeneity problem was never an empirical claim in the first place, or if the common-currency evidence is too contested to carry this weight — tell me that plainly and reframe the question, rather than politely answering the version I have set up.
