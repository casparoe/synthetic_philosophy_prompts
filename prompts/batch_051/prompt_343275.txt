I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on the central chapter of a comic novel — a funeral scene that's supposed to be sad and funny at the same time — so I've been trying to get my own sense of humour into some kind of order. I wrote down a bunch of cases and my honest gut reactions, and now I'm hoping you can do the philosophy I can't.

Act as a philosopher of humour who thinks like a working comedy writer, not a textbook. Start by brainstorming: run each of my cases through the four classic families — superiority, incongruity, relief, and benign violation — and note what each theory predicts before you commit to anything. Think carefully, and don't be polite with my intuitions; if my gut is inconsistent, I'd rather give up a verdict than end up with a tidy principle that dosen't fit.

Here are the cases.

1. In my draft, a pompous mayor is walking backwards through a council meeting, lecturing everyone about "civic-mindedness," when he backs into a skateboard and goes into the fountain. I laughed writing it, and I still think it's the funniest scene in the book. He's obnoxious, but I don't usually enjoy watching people get hurt.

2. Last week, through the café window, I watched a woman in a suit trip on the curb and spill coffee down her front. She just stood there looking defeated. I didn't laugh. It wasn't the same event at all, and it genuinely bugged me that my manuscript scene was, physically, nearly identical.

3. My nephew made up a pun on the spot about a clockmaker who, after a divorce, tells people he's living a second-hand life. I did the little exhale-laugh. It's clearly a different species from the fountain — there's nobody to look down on — but I can't say why it counts as funny at all.

4. I don't know whether this one belongs on the list, but when the nurse called to say my scan was clear, I laughed with no joke anywhere near it — just a laugh that had been sitting in my chest for a week. If humour and that laugh are related, I want the principle to say how.

5. A close friend, mid-treatment for lymphoma, said over dinner, "Well, at least I finally hit my goal weight." I laughed hard, the shocked way, and then felt strange about it. My brother, whose wife died of cancer, was at the table and didn't laugh. I can't tell whether the joke was funny and he just couldn't take it, or whether my laughing was the wrong response.

6. I watched a comic whose whole act was just increasingly specific fake memories of a childhood that couldn't have happened — a family dog that was also a municipal bylaw, that kind of thing. No punchlines. I couldn't breathe from laughing; my partner found it pointless. If it's funny, it's funny without a victim and without built-up tension, which seems to embarrass every theory at once.

Then there's the scene I'm torn about. In the novel, my kind but flustered protagonist gives the eulogy at his best friend's funeral. His mind blanks, and instead of the story he planned, he says that Mark was the sort of man who always finished your sentences — usually wrong, but he finished them. Half the characters snort, and I want the reader to have the half-laugh I had at my friend's lymphoma joke, not a cheap sitcom beat. I go back and forth on whether the line is funny, cruel, or just a pressure valve after a long sad chapter.

So: is there one principle — or a specific mix — that captures my verdicts better than the others? Which of my reactions do I have to give up if I want something coherent? And what does your best account say about the funeral line: should it play as comedy, and how should I write it to land that way?
