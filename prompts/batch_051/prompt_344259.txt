A friend has just read a popular introduction to ancient philosophy and sent me his summary of the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the person who dies. I keep feeling that the argument as he states it moves faster than it is entitled to, but I cannot locate the slide. This is what he wrote:

"The Epicureans held that death cannot harm the person who dies, because a person can be harmed only by something they are there to experience. At the moment death is present, the person is already gone, so there is no subject left to undergo any harm. Saying that death is bad for the dead is therefore not just false but close to incoherent, like asking what colour a number is. Your nonexistence after death is exactly like your nonexistence before birth; since nobody regards the aeons before their birth as an unlived misfortune, nobody can consistently regard the aeons after it as one. Any fear of death, and any grief over the dead, must then be the residue of imagining the dead person still stationed somewhere as a witness to their own absence."

I find the opening claim about there being no subject after death plausible as far as it goes, but the transition from that to the irrationality of fear and grief feels too quick, and I do not see why the two temporal directions of nonexistence must be on a par. Please go through the paragraph and separate what is a faithful statement of the Epicurean argument from what has been quietly added: where does the summary overstate a premise, conflate two distinct claims, or infer more than the accurate parts support? In particular, I would like to know how the weaker, defensible version of the argument fares against the view that death can be bad for the deceased because it deprives them of the goods they would otherwise have had, and whether the final claim about the irrationality of fear and grief survives the corrections.
