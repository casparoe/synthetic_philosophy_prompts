I am a doctoral student in comparative politics, working on federalism and local autonomy, and I keep running into the same difficulty: subsidiarity is invoked in EU law, German federalism, Swiss cantonal practice, and Catholic social thought as though it named a single criterion, but the moment I try to extract something testable from these uses I end up with at least two different principles sharing one name. One is a burden-of-proof rule: the smaller or more local unit is presumptively entitled to act, and a higher level may intervene only when it must. The other is an efficiency rule: authority should be assigned wherever a function is best handled, with spillovers, scale economies, administrative capacity, and local knowledge weighed as ordinary considerations, so central action can be the first-choice answer rather than an exception.

I would like a working definition of subsidiarity that lets me classify cases rather than retro-fit whatever conclusion I already hold. Here are the cases I want the definition to handle; for each, tell me what the definition implies—permitted, impermissible, or no determinate answer—and flag any verdict that surprises you or forces a revision:

1. A school district selecting the novels on its own literature syllabus.
2. A central bank setting the national policy rate.
3. A national ministry specifying, down to the centimeter, the maximum height of residential hedges, applied in every municipality.
4. A city setting a municipal minimum wage substantially above the national floor.
5. A national government preempting local zoning to require substantial upzoning around transit stations.
6. A national constitutional court striking down a provincial law on the ground that it violates a right secured in the national constitution.
7. A national government contracting with a church-affiliated charity to deliver social services that are nationally funded and nationally regulated.
8. A supranational body phasing out a category of single-use plastics across its member states.

After running the cases, address the underlying ambiguity directly: is subsidiarity best reconstructed as the burden-of-proof principle, the efficiency principle, or a combination of the two—and when the two readings conflict, which one takes precedence? Which of my cases separate the two readings, and which force a third reading I have not named? Finally, say whether any case is better handled by explicitly flagging that the definition you propose is vertical, concerning levels of government, and is therefore silent about the relation between public and private association. I would rather have a defensible criterion than an exhaustive taxonomy, so if you revise the definition partway through, say what the revision cost you and which classifications changed.
