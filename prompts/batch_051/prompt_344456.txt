I'm teaching an undergrad seminar on property and justice this term, and the slogan "is taxation theft?" keeps comming up in discussion. It's usually tossed out pretty glibly, but the more I think about it, the more the whole question seems to rest on what we mean by *theft*. I suspect the ordinary word does two jobs at once: it picks out a roughly descriptive kind of wrong (taking someone's property without their consent), and it also functions as a condemnatory label. If that's right, the slogan might come out true in one sense and false in another, or true but question-begging, and I'd like help untangling that.

Here's what I want. I'm looking for a working defintion of theft — or, if you share my suspicion that the word does two jobs, two clearly labeled definitions — that gets the following cases right:

- a pickpocket taking your wallet on the subway
- me buying a sandwich at the posted price
- federal income tax
- a parking ticket on a car left in a no-parking zone
- a warlord who demands half your crop "for protection of the village"
- an employer who pays you for 30 hours when you worked 40 (wage theft)
- the government printing money so that the value of your savings slowly erodes (inflation or seigniorage)
- a landlord doubling the rent on a tenant who has nowhere else to go
- civil asset forfeiture: police seize $20,000 in cash from your trunk and you're never charged with a crime
- a pharmaceutical company pricing a drug that costs $2 to make at $2,000 a dose, knowing patients need it to survive

I'm confident the first is theft and the second is not. I lean toward saying the income tax case isn't theft, but I can't explain why that doesn't let the warlord case off the hook too. And I genuinely have no idea about the landlord, inflation, and drug-pricing cases.

Act as a professional philosopher of law and property rights here. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming two or three candidate definitions before settling on one. Then run your definition through every case in order — no skipping. Flag anywhere it gives an answer you think is wrong, or where it gives no answer at all, and then either revise the definition or bite the bullet and defend the surprising verdict. I'd rather have a stable definition with one or two controversial consequences than a list of ad hoc verdicts.
