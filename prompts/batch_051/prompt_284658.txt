State your credence in the following proposition, then derive it from explicit components.

Proposition: Human valuing is irreducibly plural. That is, there is no single ultimate value — no common currency such as well-being, preference satisfaction, or autonomy — in terms of which every genuine terminal value can be measured and every apparent conflict among values can be resolved, even in principle, by an internal tradeoff. At least some of the conflicts people experience among the things they ultimately care about are conflicts between distinct, non-derivative values that would survive ideal reflection. Treat this as a claim about the actual structure of what human agents ultimately value, not about how we should model their choices for prediction. A "single value" may be complex and multi-dimensional; what makes it single is that its dimensions share a common scale.

Give me: (a) a single number for your credence; (b) the decomposition — the main sub-questions your credence turns on, a rough probability for each with one or two sentences of justification, and the rule by which they combine. Address at least these components:

(i) Conflict realism: the probability that some prima facie conflicts (honesty against kindness, loyalty to one person against loyalty to another) are genuine conflicts between terminal values rather than artifacts of limited attention, missing information, or context.
(ii) In-principle commensurability: the probability that for any two terminal values there exists some deeper standard by which they can be non-arbitrarily traded off.
(iii) Representational substantiveness: the probability that a scalar utility representation of a person's values, even when one can always be constructed, reflects the real structure of their valuing rather than the method of measuring it.
(iv) Verbal-dispute risk: the probability that monism and pluralism as usually argued are partly rival terminologies for the same phenomena rather than rival descriptions of them.

Then (c) identify the component your final number is most sensitive to, and (d) name one piece of evidence or argument — actual or merely possible — that would do the most to move that component. If you think the question splits into "there are many values" and "there is no common scale," say which one you are assigning the credence to and why, but still return a single number for the proposition as stated.

For calibration: I hold roughly 0.65 that the proposition is true; a colleague who accepts the same everyday cases holds 0.3. State which component most plausibly explains the gap between us, even if your own number differs from both.
