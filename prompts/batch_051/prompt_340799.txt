I am a novelist, not a social choice theorist, and I want an honest check on a concept I have been using while researching a novel. The book concerns a mid-sized city that, after a contested election, attempts to redesign its voting system so that ordinary citizens do not feel they must vote tactically. I have read far enough into voting theory to keep encountering the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, which says, in the form I have met it, that under mild conditions every non-dictatorial single-winner rule for three or more candidates is manipulable: there are preference profiles at which some voter obtains a better outcome by submitting an insincere ballot. The theorem treats manipulability as a binary property, and nearly every rule fails it. Yet in the world of the novel, and perhaps in the actual world, some manipulations seem harmless and easily ignored while others are corrosive, and the binary verdict cannot capture that difference.

The term I have been using is the "sincerity margin." I mean by it the set of preference profiles at which an ordinary, boundedly informed voter has an insincere ballot that would improve the outcome and would still improve it under small perturbations of the other voters' ballots; I exclude, or heavily discount, manipulations that pay off only when the voter's forecast of the others is accurate within a narrow band and that backfire or waste the vote outside that band. Burying a compromise candidate to protect one's favorite, for instance, counts as a serious strategic burden only when the maneuver remains profitable across a wide range of plausible tallies; if it works only when the pre-election forecast is nearly exact, it is fragile. My working claim is that Gibbard–Satterthwaite guarantees that no rule has a zero sincerity margin, but says nothing about how wide or narrow the margin is, and that a rule with a sufficiently thin margin is, for practical purposes, close enough to sincere. I would like candor rather than encouragement: is the concept coherent, or does its appeal to robustness smuggle in assumptions about voter belief that make it nearly as demanding as strategy-proofness itself? Does something like it already exist under another name? And where does it break?

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: first, the strongest charitable reconstruction of the sincerity margin; second, the nearest existing concepts and the respects in which mine is or is not distinct; third, what the concept genuinely gets right about voting that the binary theorem misses; fourth, the strongest objection to it as defined; and fifth, whether that objection is fatal and what, if anything, survives.
