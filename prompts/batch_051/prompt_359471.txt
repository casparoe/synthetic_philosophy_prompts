In a reading group on Parmenides, one member keeps cutting off objections with the Eleatic slogan that you cannot think or speak of what is not. He has used it to dismiss, in one gesture, four ideas: that we can have false beliefs (a false belief would be a thought about what is not the case); that we can talk about the past or future (what no longer is, or is not yet); that a name like "centaur" is meaningful though no centaur exists; and that there is genuine plurality (since distinguishing one thing from another seems to require the thought that the one is not the other). I cannot tell whether he is asserting something fairly innocent about reference, something radical about the impossibility of falsehood and change, or several different things at once. Lay out the distinct precise claims that Eleatic one-liner could be making, from the weakest to the strongest, as a menu of readings rather than a single interpretation. In doing so, separate the "is" of existence from the "is" of predication and the "is" of truth, and separate the act of thinking from the act of speaking and from the object thought or spoken about. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, whether it is trivial or substantive, and whether it would actually support dismissing the four ideas he used it against. Then say which readings Parmenides' own denial of coming-to-be and perishing actually needs, and which readings a contemporary philosopher could accept without following him that far. Be explicit about how confident you are in the taxonomy as a whole, and single out the point you are least sure of.
