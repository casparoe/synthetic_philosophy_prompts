Question: In the First Meditation, Descartes introduces the evil demon hypothesis. Explain the precise scope of the doubt this hypothesis is meant to produce. In particular, does it extend to the truth of 2+3=5, and does it extend to his own existence? Then contrast Descartes's way of escaping this doubt with Hume's claim that skeptical doubts are cured by nature rather than refuted by argument, and assess whether Hume's naturalism is an adequate reply to the evil demon.

Student's answer: Descartes introduces the evil demon at the end of the First Meditation after he has established that God might be a deceiver. The demon gives vivid form to the thought that an all-powerful being could make him go wrong about anything, so the doubt does extend even to simple arithmetic such as 2+3=5. It does not, however, extend to his own existence, because even a demon must have something that exists in order to deceive it, so whenever Descartes is thinking he cannot be mistaken that he exists. Descartes later escapes the demon by proving that God exists and is not a deceiver, and this proof is what allows him to trust his clear and distinct perceptions. Hume rejects that entire strategy. For him these doubts cannot be defeated by rational proof; they arise only in the study, and as soon as we return to ordinary life our natural belief in bodies and causal necessity reasserts itself and the doubt dissolves. Hume treats this as a sufficient answer to the skeptic, because a doubt that cannot be lived is not a threat to our claims to know. His account is therefore better than Descartes's, because it explains how we actually live without requiring a theological proof.
