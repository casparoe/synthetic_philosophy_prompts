I have been thinking about a parallel in Gödel, Escher, Bach that I cannot quite settle, and I would like help testing whether it is a genuine structural analogy or only a suggestive pun.

As I understand it, the technical engine of Gödel's proof is the diagonal lemma: for any formula with one free variable, a sufficiently strong formal system can construct a sentence that is provably equivalent to that formula applied to the sentence's own Gödel number. So the system needs no special introspective faculty to talk about itself; it only needs a coding scheme and enough arithmetic. Hofstadter then uses this as a bridge from formal self-reference to the claim that minds arise when a sufficiently rich symbolic system acquires the ability to represent itself. The analogy seems to be roughly this: a formal system's capacity to construct a self-referential fixed point is to Gödel's incompleteness results what a mind's capacity to model itself is to self-awareness, including the mind's apparent inability to survey its own mechanisms completely.

What bothers me is the following. The diagonal lemma is extraordinarily cheap: it applies to any predicate, including thoroughly trivial ones, and it yields a mere arithmetical sentence. A mind representing itself, by contrast, seems to involve something more substantive: a model that tracks at least some of the system's own states and processes and is actually used in guiding behaviour. If the parallel is deep, what exactly corresponds, on the mind side, to the special provability conditions that make the second incompleteness theorem work? And if the parallel is only superficial, what extra conditions would a self-model have to satisfy before the analogy to incompleteness becomes non-vacuous? Or is the analogy simply misleading from the start?
