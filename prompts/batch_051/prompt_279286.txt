I keep going back and forth on something and could use an adjudication from someone who can see both sides. I think the position is called bivalence: every statement is either true or false. And I'll be honest, I'm drawn to it. It's clean. It's basically how booleans work, and as a programmer that's a real aesthetic pull: two states, no undefined behavior, no NULL sneaking in where a value should be. I've also been burned enough by SQL's three-valued logic—where comparing NULL to something gives UNKNOWN—to appreciate how much weirdness a third value drags in. And there's a deeper version of the pull: a statement says that things are a certain way. Well, either things are that way or they aren't. That just feels like what it means to say something. Even 'This sentence is neither true nor false' sounds like a claim that's got to be true or false, so denying bivalence from inside language feels self-defeating.

But then I bump into the other side and it won't go away. 'The last bug in this codebase will be found on a Tuesday' doesn't just feel unknown; it feels unsettled, like the future hasn't made it true OR false, and treating it as already one or the other quietly commits me to the future being fixed. Same with vague cases: 'Marcus is tall' when Marcus is 5'10" and nothing in the context draws the line. I'm not saying I can't figure out whether it's true. I'm saying there's no fact there to be right or wrong about, so forcing a truth value feels like manufacturing a boundary that isn't in the world. And 'Sam's youngest child is a lawyer' when Sam has no children is another one—'true' feels wrong and 'false' feels wrong in a different way.

So here's what I actually want. I'm not asking you to pick a winner. I want an account of what would have to be true for each side to legitimately give way: what would have to be true for me to write off the intuition as a bug in my thinking; what would have to be true for bivalence to earn a patch or get scrapped entirely; and whether there's a version of 'statement' or 'true' where both can live together. Would bivalence holding for precise, already-settled statements while gaps only show up for vague, future-directed, or presupposition-loaded ones count as a real reconciliation—or just me carving out exceptions to protect a theory I'm already attached too?
