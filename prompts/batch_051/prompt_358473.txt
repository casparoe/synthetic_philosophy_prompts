I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy working on collective responsibility, and I keep returning to an argument that initially strikes me as sound but that seems to let the "small fry" in large-scale wrongs off the hook too cheaply. The argument runs:

(1) One is complicit in a wrongdoing only if one of one's actions is a causal contribution to that wrongdoing.

(2) An action is a causal contribution to a wrongdoing only if, had the action not been performed, the wrongdoing would not have occurred or would have occurred in some materially different way.

(3) For any single minor participant in a sufficiently large-scale collective wrongdoing — the filing clerk in a corrupt bureaucracy, the small investor in an exploitative firm, the ordinary taxpayer in an unjust war — that individual's particular action makes no such difference: had they not performed it, the wrongdoing would have occurred in just the same way.

(4) Therefore no individual minor participant in large-scale wrongdoing is complicit in it.

(5) So, whatever criticism these participants deserve, it cannot be the criticism of complicity; "you were part of it" fails as a charge of complicity against any one of them.

I suspect the argument is a trap, but I cannot locate the exact point of failure, or decide whether it is valid with a false premise. Is the argument valid as stated? If it is valid, which premise should be rejected or restricted, and what is the strongest defensible replacement that still lets complicity do critical work in large-scale cases? If it is not valid, identify exactly where the reasoning breaks. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
