I am a novelist preparing a book set in a city where gradual neuronal replacement is a mature, tightly regulated medical procedure rather than a thought experiment. In the society I am building, the protagonist works for the Registry of Continuous Persons, a public body whose rulings determine whether a person who has replaced most of their brain still counts as the same legal person. I want the institution to feel engineered rather than arbitrary, so I need you to stress-test the rule I have drafted.

The rule, as I have imagined it:

1. Any resident beginning the procedure must file a Continuity Declaration.

2. A person is certified as continuous — the same person before and after replacement — if and only if:
   a. they remain independently conscious and responsive through every session, with no general anaesthesia and no interruption of global function;
   b. each session replaces at most 3% of the brain by volume, and no session begins until the previous replacement unit has passed a 30-day integration review;
   c. total accumulated gap minutes during the entire procedure — minutes in which no functioning substrate is doing the work of a replaced region — never exceed 5.

3. If certification is granted, the person keeps their property, debts, marriage, contracts, voting registration, and criminal liability as if nothing had changed.

4. If certification is denied — because the subject died mid-procedure, was reconstructed from a scan, or accumulated more than 5 gap minutes — the resulting entity is legally a successor person, not the original. A successor may apply to inherit under a special succession statute, but cannot continue the original's marriage, is not liable for the original's unsecured debts or unserved sentences, and holds professional licences and copyrights only through new application.

5. A tribunal of continuity judges reviews disputed certifications, and a certification can be reopened if clinic logs or monitoring data later show that the legal thresholds were violated.

I need you to walk through how this would actually behave in a society that has lived with it for a generation. Do not litigate whether it is fair; I am looking for causal mechanics. Who tries to game it first, and how? What do clinics, insurers, spouses, heirs, creditors, employers, and the judges themselves do in response? Where does the 5-minute gap rule create perverse incentives, and how might people manipulate the definition of a gap or the timing of sessions? What second-order effects appear — for example, strategic interruption as a form of voluntary civil death, a market for continuity insurance, or a preference for dying mid-procedure rather than being certified?
