After a long argument with a friend last night, I am left unsure whether his position is even testable. He insists that certain coincidences—such as meeting the same stranger three times in different cities within a month—cannot be explained away by chance, and that our feeling of their improbability is itself evidence of some hidden structure. I replied that the birthday paradox shows that a naive probability judgment can be wrong by orders of magnitude, and that memory and social retelling select for the most striking cases. We kept circling around a deeper issue: is “this coincidence is too improbable to be chance” a claim about objective frequencies, or a claim about the salience of a pattern to a particular observer?

I want to know how one could actually test the claim that reported coincidences are probabilistically anomalous—not just a consequence of vague prior hypotheses, selection effects, or the elastic boundaries of what counts as “the same event.” Design the strongest practical test you can think of, concrete enough that someone could carry it out or simulate it. Say what pattern of results would support the friend’s hidden-structure view, what pattern would support the selection-and-misjudgment view, and where the test simply cannot reach: in particular, whether it can ever settle whether coincidences are real features of the world or an artifact of the categories and narratives we impose on experience.
