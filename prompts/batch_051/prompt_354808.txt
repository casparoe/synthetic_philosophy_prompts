After a session on decision theory, two members of my reading group — Daniel and Sofia, both long-time rationalist-forum regulars — started arguing about whether the embedded-agency problem is a genuinely new obstruction or just a relabeling of several older, local difficulties. By "embedded agency" they meant the situation of an agent whose own beliefs, utility function, and decision procedure are part of the world the agent is trying to model. They asked me to referee, and I could not. I want an adjudication that goes through the exchange move by move.

Daniel opened with what he called the usual line. Expected utility theory, he said, assumes an agent standing outside the world: a set of actions, a set of states, a probability distribution, and an expected value to maximize. But a real agent is inside the world — its beliefs, its utility function, and the computation that selects its action are all physical events. The Cartesian boundary between agent and environment is a modeling convenience, not a fact, and it stops being innocent once the agent reasons about its own decision procedure.

Sofia replied that this is an observation about diagrams, not about the mathematics. Decision theory requires only a well-defined action space and a well-defined probability measure over outcomes; it does not require an ontological split between mind and world. One can include the agent's own code, hardware, and even model-selection procedures inside the world model if needed. Self-reference may be computationally hard, but showing that the boundary assumption burdens a diagram does not show that the framework is broken.

Daniel said that this is precisely the move that fails. If the agent's decision procedure appears as a variable inside the model, then the action being evaluated is not independent of the beliefs doing the evaluating. A world in which the agent chooses A contains a different reasoner — one already disposed toward A — so comparing actions is really comparing across different reasoners rather than across a fixed reasoner's options. Expected utility theory gives no rule for assigning probabilities to outcomes under the counterfactual "were I to choose otherwise" when that counterfactual alters the reasoner itself. That, he said, is not a missing variable; it is a vicious loop.

Sofia answered that this is act-state dependence, and causal decision theory already handles it: evaluate an action as an intervention on the choice variable rather than as an observation about oneself. If one's choice is caused by one's computation, that is simply more causal structure, not a challenge in principle. The intervention leaves the rest of the graph fixed, and causal decision theory does the rest.

Daniel said causal decision theory works only by declaring the choice node exogenous — something set from outside the system. But in an embedded reasoner, the choice node is determined by the very computation that is trying to decide which intervention to make. If that computation can represent the intervention itself, then the causal graph is cyclic in the relevant sense, or the node's exogeneity is simply false. Pushing the boundary back does not remove it; it hides the Cartesian assumption inside the notion of a free intervention.

Sofia replied that every model must carve system from environment somewhere; that is a precondition of modeling, not a bug. Physicists are also parts of the world, and physics does fine by treating the observer as an abstraction. The fact that human beings can ask how they themselves are modeled does not invalidate the standard tools; it shows only that the common toy models are too coarse. On her view, Daniel was attacking a strawman: no one believes the action space is ontologically basic.

Daniel said the physicist's situation differs, because physics does not need the observer's possible choices to appear as variables in its laws, whereas decision theory exists to determine, from the inside, which possible choice gets made. When the abstraction of a freely settable choice node breaks, the thing decision theory is for disappears. And the breakdown is systematic precisely because a stronger reasoner is pushed toward modeling its own decision procedure: the better the agent, the worse the Cartesian boundary fits. That, he said, is the whole embedded-agency point — the problem grows with capability, so it is not a toy-model artifact.

Sofia said that may be true of ideal, logically omniscient agents that can fully represent themselves. But finite agents cannot fully model themselves; there are diagonalization-style limits, and real agents reason with simplified self-models and bounded resources. She accused Daniel of letting a failure mode of an impossible ideal dictate the research program for a possible one.

Daniel answered that full self-models are unnecessary for the pathology. An agent that can refer to its own beliefs only approximately already creates loops between model and modeling, and approximate self-reference becomes available long before full self-representation does. In any case, he said, ideal reasoning is the target that bounded procedures are supposed to approximate; if the ideal is incoherent, then "approximate ideal reasoning" has no well-defined target.

Sofia countered that Daniel was overstating the available results. What has been shown, at most, is that certain very strong systems trip over self-reference when combined with certain very strong consistency assumptions. She granted that there are real problems in logical uncertainty and in the semantics of self-referential belief, but said they are local. Calling them "embedded agency," she argued, makes a grab bag of problems look like a single phenomenon.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each move, say who is right about what and who is overclaiming; flag the places where a terminological disagreement is masquerading as a substantive one; and give a bottom line on whether "embedded agency" names a genuinely new problem, a relabeling, or a badly posed question.
