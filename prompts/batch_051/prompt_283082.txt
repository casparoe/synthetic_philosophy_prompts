In the Theodicy (the Essays on the Goodness of God, the Freedom of Man and the Origin of Evil), Leibniz appears to defend two claims that sit very uneasily together. In one strand he argues that God's wisdom and goodness make it impossible for him to create anything less than the best of all possible worlds. An infinitely wise being always acts on the strongest reason, an infinitely good being always prefers the greater to the lesser good, and so, when God surveys the possible worlds, his will is infallibly fixed on the most perfect one; the supposition that God could have created a worse world while a better one was available is, on this strand, a supposition unworthy of God. In another strand of the same book, Leibniz goes to considerable lengths to deny that this makes creation necessary: God's choice is free, the actual world is contingent, no absolute or metaphysical necessity attaches to this particular world, and freedom consists in acting spontaneously and intelligently rather than in a liberty of indifference in which the will chooses with no determining reason.

I cannot tell whether these two strands can genuinely be held together, or whether the second merely softens the first for theological appearances. Please work through the apparent conflict as follows. First, reconstruct as precisely as you can what Leibniz means by necessity and by freedom in each strand, and state the strongest version of the objection that the two claims contradict one another. Second, lay out and evaluate the main candidate reconciliations: a distinction between senses of necessity (for example, between the claim that the nonexistence of the best world is logically contradictory and the claim that God's not choosing it would be inconsistent with his wisdom and goodness); a distinction between senses of freedom (indifference versus spontaneity plus intelligence); a shift of position across the text; or some other reading. For each candidate, say what it would commit Leibniz to and what textual or philosophical costs it carries. Third, render a defended verdict: which reconciliation is most faithful to the text, and which candidates amount to special pleading? In particular, consider whether moral necessity ultimately leaves God with any ability to do otherwise worth having, and whether the view that survives the reconciliation is best described as compatibilism, as a necessitarianism that does not call itself by that name, or as something else.
