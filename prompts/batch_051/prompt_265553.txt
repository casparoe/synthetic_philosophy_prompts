For years I've been trying to explain why some games stay with me long after they're finished while others, however polished, vanish the moment I close them, and I've settled on a property I call counterfactual texture. I don't mean replay value or the mere presence of branching. A game has high counterfactual texture when the options not taken remain alive during and after play -- not as an abstract list of "paths I could have picked," but as felt alternatives with their own weight and atmosphere, so that the run that actually happened is always ghosted by the run that nearly did. A deckbuilder in which the card you passed over keeps defining the deck you didn't assemble; a tactics game in which the flank you refused keeps asserting itself as a shape in the negative space of the battle; a narrative game in which the conversation you avoided colors the one you had. Low counterfactual texture is the strange condition of a hundred-choice open world where each unchosen option is so interchangeable that choice never acquires any felt stakes at all, because nothing is lost when something is chosen.

My working claim is that this texture, rather than story, graphics, or even "fun," is a substantial part of what makes games artistically distinctive -- that games are unusually good at making counterfactuals themselves into objects of experience, and that much of their aesthetic achievement lies there. I know this overlaps with talk about agency as the medium of games and with the thought that interactivity itself can be expressive; I'm trying to get one step more specific.

I would like an honest assessment. Is counterfactual texture a genuinely coherent property, or is it a metaphor doing the work of several ordinary notions at once -- pacing, worldbuilding, difficulty, replayability? Is there an established concept that already captures it, and what would I find it called if I went looking? And where does it break down: what kind of game would satisfy my definition while still being aesthetically inert, and what unquestionably great game would score near zero on it?
