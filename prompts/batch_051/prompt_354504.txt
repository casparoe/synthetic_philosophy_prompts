Two colleagues of mine got into an argument at work, and I'd like you to referee it move by move. The background is this: our team is deciding whether to start consulting a free internal dashboard that predicts which customers are likely to churn. Access costs nothing, and consulting it doesn't by itself change any customer's behavior. Here is the exchange:

(1) Alan, a data scientist, said we should obviously consult it. There's a classic result in decision theory, Good's theorem (or the theorem on the value of information), showing that a rational agent never refuses cost-free information: the expected utility of choosing an action after seeing the information is always at least the expected utility of choosing without seeing it. Since querying the dashboard is free, looking can't make us worse off in expectation, and might make us better off.

(2) Maya, a product manager, replied that this can't settle whether we should actually look. The predictions are noisy, and if the team overreacts to them — say, someone sees a high risk score for a customer we're already committed to supporting and panics, redirecting resources — we could end up making worse decisions than if we'd never seen the number. For actual teams, she said, free information can hurt.

(3) Alan answered that this is not a counterexample to the theorem. The theorem doesn't require the information to be perfectly reliable; it requires the agent to update on it correctly. A team that misweights noisy evidence or panics is updating badly, and the theorem says nothing to license bad updating. It shows the information itself isn't the problem — our flawed response is.

(4) Maya said that's exactly the problem: the theorem applies to an ideal agent that doesn't make the mistakes she just described. We are not that agent, so a theorem about that agent gives us no reason to look at the dashboard. And she added that she could also refuse information for a different reason, one that isn't a mistake at all: she might simply prefer not to know certain things — like how likely a valued customer is to leave — regardless of whether it would change any decision.

(5) Alan said that if her preferences include not knowing itself, she has stepped outside one of the theorem's assumptions again: it assumes utility attaches to outcomes in the world, not to your state of knowledge. So she still hasn't refuted the theorem; she has only shown that agents with knowledge-averse preferences aren't covered by it. And in the clean case — an agent whose preferences are purely about outcomes — the information genuinely cannot make things worse in expectation.

(6) Maya said that this is precisely her point. The theorem turns out to be true for a kind of agent we don't resemble, and its "free information" condition is also rarely met in practice: seeing the dashboard could change the world by other routes — if we act on it, competitors might infer what we know, or the mere knowledge of a grim number could affect team morale and through morale affect outcomes. So she isn't refuting the theorem; she's saying it doesn't give us the advice Alan drew from it.
