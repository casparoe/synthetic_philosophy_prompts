Suppose we adopt the following criteria for saying that a learner has genuinely acquired a concept from a small number of examples. It must generalize (sort new instances correctly, including instances unlike any training example), compress (behave on the basis of something shorter and less ad hoc than a stored gallery of the examples), be systematic (combine the new concept with other concepts and draw inferences beyond the original task), and be corrigible (use the concept to answer counterfactual questions such as which alteration would move an instance across the boundary).

Now take a Bongard-style problem, in which a learner sees two panels of six simple line drawings and must infer the single rule separating them (for example, every figure on the left contains one closed loop and no figure on the right does). A retrieval-based system handles many unseen Bongard problems by storing the training drawings and classifying new ones by nearest-neighbor distance in a very large space of low-level image features. It performs well on a standard collection, including some rules we would describe as abstract; its errors cluster on rules that concern relations among figures rather than intrinsic features of individual figures. The system gives no justification, cannot identify which change would flip a classification, and succeeds only because its feature space was tuned to the kinds of rules the test set contains.

Does the retrieval-based system satisfy the criteria? Take them one by one, mark where they leave the case underdetermined, and then say whether the difficulty lies in the system or in the criteria — in particular, whether compression or corrigibility is doing real work in the account or asking for more than concept acquisition requires.
