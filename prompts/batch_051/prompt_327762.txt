Here's a passage from Nick Bostrom's paper "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?":

"The argument we shall present does not, however, depend on any very strong version of functionalism or computationalism. For example, we need not assume that the thesis of substrate-independence is necessarily true (either analytically or metaphysically) – just that, in fact, a computer running a suitable program would be conscious. Moreover, we need not assume that in order to create a mind on a computer it would be sufficient to program it in such a way that it behaves like a human in all situations, including passing the Turing test etc. We need only the weaker assumption that it would suffice for the generation of subjective experiences that the computational processes of a human brain are structurally replicated in suitably fine-grained detail, such as on the level of individual synapses. This attenuated version of substrate-independence is quite widely accepted."

I'm a philosophy professor and I keep hearing two grads talk past each other. One says the simulation argument should make us much less fanatical about astronomically large far-future consequences: if we are likely simulated, then the far future we think we're shaping is, in the relevant sense, not ours, and the expected-value sums built on it are built on sand. The other says this is a non sequitur: even if we're simulated, there's still a real far future, with real suffering and flourishing, and the simulation argument just adds another layer of ancestors while leaving the expected-value structure of longtermism basically untouched.

Write the joint statement these two would produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want exactly five numbered items, no intro or conclusion; each item should be one or two sentences. Don't give me a whole essay. The first item should be a shared formulation of what the simulation argument actually licenses, drawing on the Bostrom passage above; the second should be the residual disagreement stated so each side would accept the wording of their own view; the third should identify the central crux; the fourth should describe one concrete case or argument both sides agree would bear on that crux; the fifth should record what each side predicts that test would show. Use "the dampener" and "the invariantist" as their names. And don't try to do economics of it, I just want the conceptual structure.
