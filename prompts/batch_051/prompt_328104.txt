Ancient atomism supplies a sharp criterion for being matter, and I want to know whether it can be repaired rather than discarded. On the atomist picture, fundamental reality is exhausted by atoms and the void: atoms are solid, impenetrable, indivisible bodies with no void inside them, while the void is empty space and, in some sense, nothing at all. Material objects are composites of atoms, and their other features—color, liquidity, warmth—are, on this view, nothing over and above the arrangement and motion of the underlying solids. I still find the word matter doing real work in my thinking; I apply it unhesitatingly in ordinary cases and regard the contrast with empty space as indispensable, but I can no longer say what the word tracks. Modern physics seems to have pulled apart the features the atomist criterion fused: a granite block is, on the modern account, mostly empty space; a magnetic field fills a region of space and carries energy, yet two such fields can occupy the same region with their strengths simply adding; a neutrino passes through the Earth almost as though it were not there; and the elementary particles of contemporary physics are treated as point-like rather than as solid plena that hold a place by exclusion.

I would like a working definition of matter (or material object, if you prefer) that gets the following cases right:

- a granite block
- a Democritean atom, taken exactly as the atomists imagined it
- a volume of the void, with nothing in it
- a magnetic field filling a region of space
- a neutrino passing through the Earth
- a photon in transit
- a single atom of carbon
- an ordinary table, considered with the modern picture of its atoms in mind

I am confident that the granite block, the Democritean atom, and the table are matter, and that the void is not. The table is a constraint because it is an uncontroversially material object that nonetheless fails the atomist criterion if matter must be a continuous plenum with no internal gaps; the definition should not have to strain to admit it. For the other cases—the magnetic field, the neutrino, the photon, the carbon atom—I have no settled view, and I do not want the definition merely to legislate them from above. My suspicion is that the atomist criterion bundles three distinct features—impenetrability (excluding other matter from a place), solidity (being a continuous plenum with no internal gaps), and indivisibility (having no parts)—and that the ordinary concept of matter may not track any single one of them. Propose a definition, run it through all eight cases, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer at all, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. Please also say how confident you are in the resulting definition, and identify the point you are least sure of.
