I'm reworking the self-deception unit in my philosophy of mind course, and I've hit the usual wall: every account I assign starts by listing its favorite cases — the husband who can't see what everyone else can, the patient who insists he's fine — and then the theory gets built around those. I want to flip the order for once and ask what any adequate theory of self-deception actually has to deliver before it starts arguing. So give me the desiderata. The list should cover the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, and the paradoxes it has to dissolve rather than define away.

The obvious one is the standard paradox, how one person can be both deceiver and deceived, in the know and not; but I also want the less famous boundary work: self-deception versus wishful thinking, self-deception versus weakness of will, and self-deception about the world ("my spouse is faithful") versus self-deception about one's own states ("I'm not angry, I'm just disappointed"). Somewhere the list should force a stance on whether the self-deceived person literally holds contradictory beliefs or just manages her credences badly, because that changes what kind of theory you're even shopping for. And there's the hard case some people call twisted self-deception, where the person believes what he dreads rather than what he wants — the healthy man convinced he's dying the way his father did. Whatever the list says, its either got to cover that case or explain why it's a different phenomenon, and not just sweep it under the rug. Here's a case I use in class: a professor reads her end-of-term evaluations and tells everyone she only cares about the written comments, but her mood tracks the numerical averages and she can recite them from memory. Is that self-deception about what she values, or ordinary imperfect self-knowledge? Whatever the answer, the theory needs a principled line. For each requirement, give me the case or argument that forces it and say whether it's negotiable, and tell me which demands are genuine constraints rather than one camp's rhetoric. Then flag any two requirements that can't obviously be met together. Keep the focus on the list; I don't need a survey of which theory fails which item, though a line on which single requirement does the most to knock out the usual views would help me frame the lecture.
