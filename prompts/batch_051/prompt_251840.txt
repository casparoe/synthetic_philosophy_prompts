For a seminar I teach on the deep history of human welfare, we have spent several weeks on the dispute over whether the shift from foraging to farming should be judged a disaster for human wellbeing. The disagreement has settled into a familiar deadlock, and what I want is not another vote for one side but a diagnosis of the argument's structure.

One side treats the Neolithic transition as a decline for most people alive at the time: the skeletal record suggests episodes of nutritional stress, diets narrowed, settlements brought infectious disease, and labor became longer and more monotonous; on this view, mobile foragers had varied diets and something close to affluence, achieved by wanting little rather than producing much. The other side regards this as nostalgia dressed up as archaeology: the evidence is patchy and contested, forager life includes its own periodic hunger and violence, and by the only non-question-begging standard available agriculture won, since farming populations grew and displaced foragers over millennia.

My working hypothesis after watching the two sides talk past each other is that they are answering different questions under one heading. The first side is asking about the welfare of an average individual before and after the transition, and quietly imports a conception of human flourishing in which leisure, dietary variety, and rough equality count. The second side is asking about the success of a package of practices in a demographic and cultural competition, and treats that success as its own yardstick, so that a practice that spreads and out-reproduces its rivals cannot straightforwardly be called a mistake.

I would like you to assess, correct, or refine that hypothesis. Is this a verbal disagreement about the word mistake, a difference about values or about which question is worth asking, a clash over the empirical record, or some combination? Be concrete about what each side is actually treating as its decisive evidence, and say what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves the argument, a specific empirical finding that would move one side, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected. Throughout, I would like an honest account of your own confidence: indicate how confident you are in the diagnosis and identify the point about which you are least certain.
