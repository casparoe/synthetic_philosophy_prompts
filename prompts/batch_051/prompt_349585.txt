Question (short answer): In the Mu'tazila–Ash'arite debate over good and evil, one side held that acts are right or wrong because of what God commands; the other held that God commands what he commands because the acts are already right or wrong, and that human reason can discern this. Identify which school belongs to which horn, explain why the debate is sometimes called the 'Islamic Euthyphro', and give one consequence for whether God could command something unjust.

Student's answer:

The dilemma is the same one Plato poses: either good acts are good because God commands them, in which case 'good' is just a shadow of God's will, or God commands them because they are good, in which case God seems answerable to a standard outside himself. The Mu'tazila took the second horn. For them, acts have their moral character intrinsically and reason can grasp good and evil on its own; God commands justice because justice is good. The Ash'arites took the first horn: there is no right or wrong before God's command, an act is right because God commands it and wrong because God forbids it, and God can't be held to any standard he didn't create. So the debate gets called the Islamic Euthyphro because Euthyphro's definition made piety depend on what the gods love, which is the Ash'arite side, and the alternative—that the gods love it because it's pious—is the Mu'tazila side. On the consequence: the Ash'arite has to say God could command what we call cruelty, and that it would still be evil, so they're stuck saying God could command something evil; the Mu'tazila says God couldn't command it, because cruelty is evil by nature and his justice is bound up with the real character of the act. But there's a oddity in the Mu'tazila view: if reason alone can see good and evil, revelation adds nothing to morality, so their position makes the Qur'an's moral commands superfluous. And the Ash'arite view just empties the word 'just' of any content, since 'God is just' would reduce to 'God does what he does'. So both positions end up carrying the original dilemma with them rather than solving it.
