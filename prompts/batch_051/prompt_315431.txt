Two graduate students in my department, Mara and Eli, got into an argument after a colloquium in which the speaker defended the view that a left glove and a right glove lying together on a table are three things: the left glove, the right glove, and the pair. Mara and Eli disagreed about how to classify the dispute between the speaker's three-object view and the ordinary view that there are only two things. Their exchange, numbered for reference, went roughly as follows.

1. Mara argued that the dispute is substantive: either the pair is a further object or it is not, and the two sides cannot both be right about the inventory of what there is. Calling the dispute merely verbal, she said, is a way of refusing to say which ontology is true.

2. Eli replied that the dispute is merely verbal. The two sides agree on every fact about the gloves themselves -- their material, their arrangement, their behavior -- and disagree only about whether to apply words like object and there is to the pair. There is no further fact about the world separating them.

3. Mara answered that this test proves too much. Debates about whether numbers exist, or whether the average taxpayer exists, would come out verbal on that test, since the parties could accept all the mathematics and statistics while still disagreeing over existence. And a dispute about whether a figure seen in the hallway is a real person or a hallucination would also come out verbal, since both sides might accept all the sensory evidence. That latter dispute is plainly substantive, so Eli owes us a principled line, not just a label.

4. Eli said the principled line is downstream difference. In the hallway case, the parties disagree about observable and causal facts -- whether the figure answers when addressed, leaves footprints, appears in photographs -- so there is a test that could settle it, whereas in the glove case both parties would answer every empirical question identically. He added that the 2006 reclassification of Pluto shows what he means: nothing about the body changed, only the definition of planet changed, and the controversy was real even though it was definitional. That, he said, is what it is for a dispute to be verbal rather than substantive -- which is not to say it lacks stakes.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. Write your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, with no introduction or conclusion. Items one through four should rate the corresponding move above from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Item five should state which disputant is more right overall and identify the most important distinction or option that the two of them are missing.
