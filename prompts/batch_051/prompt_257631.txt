I'm a novelist and I've been watching friends do the whole Santa/tooth-fairy/elf-on-the-shelf thing, and I cant shake an asymmetry. A parent who spends years building an elaborate false world for a child -- forged notes in shaky handwriting, half-eaten carrots left out, a toy that supposedly patrols the house at night -- is doing something we call loving, even a little admirable, and we assume the child's trust usually survives the eventual reveal. But change the deceived party to an adult and keep nearly everything else fixed: the long con, the staged evidence, the benevolent intent, the gap in gullibility and power, the reveal that it was all a game played for their own delight. We'd call the adult version manipulative, creepy, maybe unforgivable. So the judgment flips almost entirely on the age of the person being lied to. What's the strongest candidate principle that would make that flip non-arbitrary? Test it against hard cases: a parent who keeps the fiction going well past thirteen, a parent who invents a bespoke little spying creature to make a kid behave, a cognitively impaired adult who would adore the same magic. If no principle survives, tell me that flat out. But give me a definite verdict and defend it, not a survey of the options.
