ok so ive been chewing on this old paper by Alan Turing, Computing Machinery and Intelligence, and theres one bit near the section on Lady Lovelaces objection that keeps messing with me. He takes her claim that a machine can never originate anything, that it can only do what we order it to do, and he says the mistake is thinking that once we know the rules and starting facts, all the consequences are already sitting in our heads. They arent. The consequences get worked out by the machine, so the machine can pull off results that surprise the people who built it. I wont pretend ive got his wording word for word, but the thing im stuck on is the priviledge we give to understanding: we assume that if something is doing a job competently, somebody somewhere must comprehend how its done. Turing seems to be saying no, those two can come apart, and the coming-apart is the part worth taking seriously.

So im asking for a satirical piece that actually hinges on that exact separation, not just the usual robots-take-over stuff. Write an Onion-style news article, maybe 650-750 words, with a headline and one or two quotes, about a parcel-delivery company whose sorting and routing system has been running itself so well for years that literally nobody in the building can explain how it makes its decisions anymore. The jokes should come from competence without comprehension being presented as a feature, not a bug. The CEO should defend the company in a way that accidentally reproduces Lady Lovelaces objection without realizing it: he says something like the system only does what we built it to do, so theres nothing to worry about, while the reporter points out that no one on staff can actually say what that was. Include a dispatcher who used to understand the old map-and-phone system and now describes himself as doing the machines typing. Avoid the lame joke where the system is secretly failing or has its own agenda; the unsettling part should be that it works fine and nobody comprehends it, and that the company is doing better than ever. A Turing person should be able to tell which part of his actual argument the piece is playing with, but a reader who never heard of Turing should still get the unease.

After the article, add a short note in the form of a fake editors correction. In that note, tell me how confident you are that the parody actually matches Turings point about Lady Lovelaces objection, and single out the place where you are least sure you rendered him fairly.
