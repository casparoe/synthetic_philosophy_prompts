I've been in a reading group on Darwin's *The Descent of Man*, and the discussion of the moral sense has stalled in a way that feels symptomatic of a deeper impasse. Darwin's argument, roughly as he gives it in chapter four, is that social animals would acquire a moral sense once their intellectual powers became sufficiently developed: the persistent social instincts, after conflict with more transient urges, leave behind a feeling of unease or regret that gets expressed as the word ought. Everyone in the group accepts this as a broadly accurate psychological account. The split is about what follows from it. One side argues that this shows moral judgments are really just the voice of the tribe made to feel categorical; once you see that conscience is an evolved social instinct, the claims it produces lose their authority, or at least their claim to objectivity. The other side insists this is a genetic fallacy: an account of how we came to make moral judgments says nothing about whether those judgments are true, any more than an account of how vision evolved tells us whether what we see is there.

We've gone back and forth for months, and I've started to wonder whether the disagreement is even substantive. Both sides agree on the evolutionary facts; both agree Darwin's story is basically right; both agree that ordinary moral experience feels categorical. So what kind of disagreement is this? A verbal dispute about what objective means, a hidden disagreement about the epistemology of moral judgment, a difference about whether evolutionary explanations carry debunking force, or just two groups answering different questions under the name Darwin's ethics? My own suspicion is that the two sides are really disagreeing about whether the causal history of a belief can count as a reason against it, and that this is a bedrock epistemological intuition rather than something either side can argue for. But I'm not sure. If you can diagnose the structure, what would actually move the debate—a distinction, a formal result, a change of question—or is it the kind of thing that no further argument can settle? And if I've misdescribed Darwin's argument or the two camps, or if the impasse is really an artifact of how I've posed it, tell me that plainly instead of trying to force a diagnosis.
