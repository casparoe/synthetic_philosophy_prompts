I want to subject a view of mine to honest adversarial scrutiny. The view is this: the apparent elegance of a scientific theory or a mathematical proof is not, in itself, evidence that the theory is true or that the proof will lead anywhere correct. Elegance is at best a heuristic for allocating attention. An elegant theory is easy to hold in the mind, easy to communicate, and therefore cheap to test or develop, so we are rational to prefer working on it; but that preference is not licensed by any correlation between elegance and truth. I also suspect that most judgments of elegance track features of our representation of a theory—our notation, our choice of axioms, our taste for certain idioms—rather than features of the theory's content, and that tracking features of our own representations is close to the definition of a heuristic. The impression that many celebrated results look elegant in retrospect I regard as largely an artifact of selection effects: successful theories are the ones we retell, and we redescribe them so that they look unified; in addition, the predicate elegant is loose enough to be fitted to almost any theory that survives.

Please tell me, systematically, what would count against this view. First, spell out what would have to be true about the psychology of aesthetic judgment, the structure of theories, or the historical record for elegance to constitute at least weak evidence of correctness rather than a merely useful instrument of inquiry. Second, mark the difference between findings that would show elegance is only an effective research heuristic and findings that would make it genuinely evidential; I care mainly about the second, but I want the boundary drawn explicitly. Third, rank the potential defeaters from those that would be decisive to those that would be merely awkward, and say which of them are live possibilities rather than bare logical escape hatches. Fourth, say what a defender of elegance-as-evidence would predict that my view does not predict, and which of those predictions would embarrass me most if they were borne out. Fifth, assess whether my view, as I have stated it, risks anything at all: is it insulated so thoroughly that no observation could move it? If it is, is that a defect in a claim of this kind, or is it the correct epistemic posture toward an alleged channel of evidence? For my part, the result I would find most decisive is not a curated list of beautiful theories that turned out true, but a study that assigns a pre-specified, independently scored measure of elegance to theories before their empirical fate is known and shows that this measure predicts subsequent success better than chance.
