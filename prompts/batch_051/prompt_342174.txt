I am writing a novel, and a central character—once a physicist, now a bitter teacher—defends the view that the scientific method is a piece of folk mythology. I want his argument to be strong enough that a thoughtful reader cannot dismiss it in a sentence, but I also do not want to smuggle in an obvious error. In Against Method Feyerabend argues that the history of science contains successful episodes in which any proposed methodological rule has been broken, and that these violations were often part of the achievement rather than mere mistakes. He concludes that the only principle left standing is that anything goes. My character adapts this into the following argument.

(1) If there is a scientific method, then it is a body of rules that distinguishes good scientific reasoning from bad.
(2) For any methodological rule that has been proposed as part of such a method, there is some episode in the history of successful science in which following that rule would have blocked the advance and violating it enabled the advance.
(3) Therefore, for any proposed methodological rule, it fails to distinguish reliably between good and bad scientific reasoning.
(4) If every proposed methodological rule fails in this way, then there is no body of rules that distinguishes good from bad scientific reasoning.
(5) Therefore, there is no scientific method.

I suspect the conclusion is too strong, but I cannot decide whether the flaw is in (2) or in the move from (2) to (3). Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what is the strongest way to state the revision that a defender of scientific method could accept?
