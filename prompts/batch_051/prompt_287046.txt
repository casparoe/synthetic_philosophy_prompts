Had a long argument with a friend last night about electoral systems and I'm bothered by where it landed. He wasn't exactly defending first-past-the-post; he said the standard fairness argument against it overreaches, and now I can't find the exit. He layed the argument out like this:

(1) The usual reason for thinking first-past-the-post is unfair is that a party can win a majority of seats on a minority of the vote, or get almost no seats on a large share of the vote.

(2) The principle underneath this is something like: groups of voters with a shared political preference deserve representation in proportion to their size.

(3) But there's nothing natural about taking parties as the relevant groups. A party is just one way of carving up the electorate. You could carve voters by region, by income, by age cohort, by preferred coalition, by whatever. And for absolutely any electoral system, there will be some way of carving the voters into "a group with a shared preference" that ends up underrepresented relative to its numbers.

(4) So if (2) is the principle, then every realistic electoral system is unfair, because for each one you can find such a group.

(5) But that can't be right. A pure list-PR system with a low threshold is not unfair in the same way, or to the same degree, as a first-past-the-post system that gives a party 5% of the seats for 40% of the vote. Some systems are just worse on the partisan dimension that matters.

(6) So (2) has to be wrong, or at least incomplete. And if (2) is incomplete, the standard proportionalist argument against first-past-the-post doesn't go through until you give a non-arbitrary reason to treat parties as the units that deserve proportional representation—which is exactly the thing in dispute. The obvious move, "proportionality is about parties because parties are what elections are about," feels circular, because under first-past-the-post you could just say the election is about choosing local representatives.

I have two instincts and they conflict. I want to keep (1) and something in the neighborhood of (2), but I also don't want to be stuck saying every conceivable electoral system is unfair. So: where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If I have to reject a step, which one should it be and what do I pay for rejecting it? If the mistake is in more than one place, tell me which step is doing the real work. Give me a committed diagnosis, not a menu—pick the step you'd give up and defend that choice.
