For the statehood and recognition course I'm taking, I'm writing a short position paper and I'd like a consistency check on my own views before I hand it in, because I genuinely can't see where the seams are comming apart. I've already got the declarativist/constitutivist distinction from the readings, so I don't need a survey — I need this specific set audited.

(1) I'm a declarativist about statehood: recognition doesn't create a state, it just acknowledges one that already exists. The existence question is answered by the four Montevideo criteria — defined territory, permanent population, a government, and capacity to enter into relations with other states.

(2) But I also think recognition matters enormously in practice. Unrecognized states can't easily borrow, sign treaties, join organizations, or get their passports accepted, and so on.

(3) In genuinely unclear cases — for instance, when it's contested whether a government has effective control — I'm happy to use widespread recognition as evidence. But it's only evidence; it doesn't do any constitutive work.

(4) Taiwan is a state. It meets the criteria, and the fact that most states don't formally recognize it is about political pressure, not about Taiwan's statehood.

(5) Somaliland is not a state, even though it seems to have the first three criteria nailed down. I can't fully articulate why — something about the fact that no member of the UN recognizes it.

(6) I also want my legal conclusions to track what states actually do and say — practice plus opinio juris — rather than falling out of some abstract definition.

Does this package hang together? Go through the relevant pairs, tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, rank the real tensions by severity, and say what the least costly revision would be for each. If something has to give, I'd rather know what it costs than be told I'm just inconsistent.
