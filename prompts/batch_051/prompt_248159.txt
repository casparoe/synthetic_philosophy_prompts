I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether the distinction between killing and letting die can carry any moral weight, and afterward he sent me a message presenting what he called the cleanest statement of the position he had been defending. I would like a careful account of how much of that message I should accept before I repeat it or concede the argument. His summary ran roughly as follows.

"The debate over active and passive euthanasia rests on a simple distinction: killing is an action, and letting die is an omission, and the received view is that this distinction matters because causing a death is intrinsically worse than merely allowing one. Rachels's two bathtub cases are meant to undermine that received view. In the first, Smith drowns his young cousin in the bath in order to inherit the child's money; in the second, Jones sees the same child slip under the water and stands by while the child drowns, also for the inheritance. Since nobody thinks Smith is worse than Jones, the bare difference between making a death happen and allowing it to happen turns out not to carry moral weight of its own. Rachels therefore showed that killing and letting die are morally equivalent whenever the surrounding features of the situation line up, and once that is granted the legal and medical distinction between murder and permissible withholding of treatment loses its foundation. A doctor who withdraws life support supposedly lets an underlying disease kill the patient, while a doctor who administers a lethal injection kills; but the two cases differ only by the discredited distinction, so active and passive euthanasia must be treated alike in every case. And in any event the so-called passive case is itself an active killing, because unplugging or withholding a ventilator is a deliberate bodily intervention, not a mere failure to act."

I can see that he is compressing several different stages into one continuous argument, and I no longer trust my own sense of where the compression becomes distortion. Could you go through the message sentence by sentence and tell me, for each stage, whether it is a fair statement of the position being reported or attacked, whether it is a genuinely contested claim presented as settled, or whether it is a conclusion that does not follow from what preceded it? In particular, I want to know: what exactly Rachels's two bathtub cases establish and what they leave open; whether the conclusions that killing and letting die are morally equivalent, that active and passive euthanasia must be treated alike in every case, and that withdrawing life support is really an active killing are licensed by the argument as summarized; and whether a defender of the doing/allowing distinction could accept the earlier parts of the summary and still consistently resist the later conclusions. Overall, is his message a fair representation of the argument he is drawing on, or does it depend on overstatements at the precise points where the force of the argument is being transferred to the euthanasia debate?
