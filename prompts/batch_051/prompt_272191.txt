Some friends and I keep returning to a fight about money in politics, and I've realized that we agree on all the empirical facts and disagree only about what should be called corruption. We all call the developer who hands a mayor a suitcase of cash in exchange for a zoning change corrupt, and none of us wants to call a fifty-dollar donation to a candidate whose platform the donor already accepts corrupt. But between these two poles we split on nearly everything: the lobbyist who writes a bill essentially word for word and gives it to a friendly committee chair; the industry PAC whose contributions preceded a member's vote on an industry-friendly amendment she probably would have supported anyway; the former senator who becomes a lobbyist within a year of leaving office; the foundation that pays a sitting official a six-figure speaking fee; the super PAC that runs ads indistinguishable from a candidate's own message but independent on paper; the job quietly found for a legislator's son-in-law at a regulated firm; the constituent whose permitting trouble gets resolved first because he is a generous donor. Some of my friends want to call almost all of these corruption; others reserve the word for explicit promises and treat the rest as the unlovely but licit traffic of representative democracy. I suspect the disagreement tracks two different jobs the word does: one aimed at discrete corrupt transactions between a giver and an officeholder, and one aimed at the condition of an institution that has become systematically responsive to funders rather than to the public, even where no promise was ever made.

Give me a working definition of corruption — or two, if you think the word really does split that way — and run it through each of the cases above, noting where a case shifts between the narrower definition and the broader one. I am not after an account of current campaign finance law and not after a policy proposal; I want the conceptual boundary drawn as honestly as possible. Where your definition returns a verdict that goes against what a reasonable person would expect, either revise it or stand by the verdict and say why the expectation is the thing that was wrong. At the end, tell me which of your classifications you would most want to retract if pressed, and whether what my friends were actually fighting about is the definition, the underlying wrong, or something else. Write this as continuous prose only: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
