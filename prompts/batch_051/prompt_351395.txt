I am a PhD student in philosophy with a background in decision theory, and I am to lead a fifty-minute seminar for advanced undergraduates who have taken only an introductory course. The topic is Quinn's self-torturer: a person is paid to move a dial one imperceptible increment at a time, rationally accepts each move because no single increment makes a noticeable difference, and ends at a setting he would have refused as a single move. I want the session to treat the case as a live tension in the theory of rational preference, and I particularly want the plan to keep two readings apart throughout: the agent is indifferent between adjacent settings, versus the agent merely cannot notice the difference between them.

Please give me a teaching plan, not an explanation of the case itself, in exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion:

1. the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what;
2. the concrete scenario that should carry the session and the features it must have and must lack for the paradox to stay live;
3. the misconceptions this audience is likely to bring or form and where in the sequence each should be defused;
4. the check for understanding that would show the session has landed;
5. what you would leave out at this level and say so.
