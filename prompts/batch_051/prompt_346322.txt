I am drafting a final assignment for a seminar on the problem of evil, and I want to build it around a legal-interpretation thought experiment, because the standard theodicies strike me as sharing a tacit assumption that the world was made for us and that the main question is whether historical evils are justified. I want to test whether that assumption can survive contact with facts its framers did not have. Here is the rule I would ask the class to work with, written as if it were a clause in a founding theological charter:

"Let no suffering be appointed in the order of the world unless that same suffering is necessary to a good which outweighs it; and where a lesser suffering would have served that good no less, the lesser suffering alone shall have been appointed."

Now consider cases the drafters of such a rule plainly did not foresee. The fossil record tells us that for hundreds of millions of years before any human being existed — before there were any rational creatures at all — animals were hunted, starved, injured, and killed; species were extinguished in events that had nothing to do with any creature's choices. A tradition that endorsed the rule while assuming creation was recent and organized around human beings must now decide whether the rule reaches these prehuman lives at all, and what it would take for their suffering to satisfy its two conditions.

Please argue the matter through the three familiar approaches to applying an old rule to unforeseen cases: the plain wording, the purpose of the clause, and what the drafters would have said had someone shown them the fossil record. Tell me what each approach yields, which you think should control, and why — and in particular whether any theodicy can take the rule seriously with respect to prehuman suffering without becoming a series of ad hoc rescues.
