Question: Are there any genuine moral dilemmas? In your answer you should (i) distinguish apparent from genuine dilemmas, (ii) set out the argument from ought-implies-can and the agglomeration principle against genuine dilemmas, (iii) discuss whether moral remainders (for instance, the appropriateness of regret) give us reason to think some dilemmas are genuine after all, and (iv) finish by saying what you think and what it implies for how we should evaluate an agent in a conflict case.

Student's answer:

An apparent dilemma is one where two moral requirements seem to conflict, but closer inspection shows there is an all-things-considered thing to do because one requirement is stronger. A genuine dilemma is one where two all-things-considered requirements really do conflict, so the agent is required to do each of two things and cannot do both; whatever she does, she will fail to do something she ought to do, and so acts wrongly. The question is whether such situations are really possible.

The standard argument against genuine dilemmas starts from ought-implies-can. Suppose an agent ought to do A and ought to do B, but cannot do both. By agglomeration, if she ought to do A and ought to do B, then she ought to do A and B. But ought-implies-can says one cannot be obligated to do what is impossible, and ex hypothesi she cannot do A and B. So the pair of all-things-considered obligations cannot both hold. If they cannot both hold, the situation is at most an apparent dilemma: one of the oughts was never all-things-considered after all, or it has been cancelled. This looks decisive, as long as one accepts agglomeration and ought-implies-can.

But that is too quick, and this is where moral remainders matter. Consider someone who must break a promise in order to help in an emergency. If she helps, the promise is not simply wiped out: she owes something afterwards, such as an apology, recompense, or at least a feeling of regret. This is what people mean by a 'remainder'. Williams brought this out: an overridden requirement does not just disappear. The important point is that the appropriateness of regret shows the overridden requirement was actually violated, not merely overridden. If it had really been cancelled, regret would make no sense; since regret does make sense, the agent did something wrong by breaking the promise even though she ought to have broken it. That means some conflicts are genuine dilemmas, because the agent does wrong either way even when one option is clearly what she ought to do.

I think a compromise is right. There are real conflicts in which the agent will act against a serious pro tanto duty no matter what she does. But in those cases there is always an all-things-considered thing she ought to do, even if she cannot always tell what it is. So genuine dilemmas, in the strict sense of two all-things-considered oughts that cannot both be satisfied, are impossible. Nevertheless, we should still treat the agent as having done something wrong when she fails to fulfil an overridden duty, and this should show in how we evaluate her — for example, we might withhold full praise without quite blaming her.
