I'm putting together a handout for a first-year epistemology seminar, and I want it to be a sorting quiz — the kind people take before they know any of the labels. It's on knowledge: what it takes for a true belief to count, and whether knowledge is closed under known entailment (if you know p, and you know p entails q, do you know q?). The questions should all be concrete cases, not definitions, and no question may name a position. I want the thing built from the logical space, not from a list of isms: work out which questions actually split people, then locate the real positions at the leaves and diagnose the empty answer patterns.

Use these five cases, in your own words, because they're the ones I'll discuss in class: (1) someone at the zoo looking at a zebra that could instead be a mule painted to look exactly like one; (2) the fake barn county with one real barn and dozens of convincing barn facades; (3) looking at your hands and whether you know you're not a brain in a vat being stimulated to have exactly these experiences; (4) the stopped clock that happens to read the correct time; (5) a fair lottery with a million tickets and the thought that my ticket won't win. For any case you can add one follow-up question if it earns its place by separating two positions that would otherwise collapse.

The scoring key should land people in at least these leaves: a Nozick-style tracking/sensitivity view (you'd roughly need to not believe p if p were false), a safety view (believing truly is fine, but the belief mustn't easily have been wrong), a Dretske-style relevant-alternatives view (you have to rule out the alternatives that are relevant, and relevance shifts with context), an infallibilist or skeptic, and a closure-friendly Moorean fallibilist (you keep both everyday knowledge and closure, so you say you know you're not a brain in a vat). If contextualism cant be sorted by one set of yes/no answers, say so and say what extra question would catch it — dont just give it a leaf to look complete.

Keep it to at most six questions and at most eight leaves. Every answer pattern nobody occupies gets a one-line diagnosis: incoherent, unattractive, or just uncharted. If any two leaves end up indistinguishable on the quiz, flag that instead of papering over it.

Then commit: pick the single question that does the most diagnostic work and defend it, and describe the nearest alternative scoring key you considered and why you rejected it. I dont want a menu of possible quizzes — build one, with a scoring key you're willing to stand behind.
