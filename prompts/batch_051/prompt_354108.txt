For the past few weeks I have been trying to work out how much of Hofstadter's account, in Gödel, Escher, Bach, of where a message's meaning lies I should actually accept. The account distinguishes three levels. The frame message announces that there is a message at all and that it is to be decoded. The outer message is carried by the choice and arrangement of the symbols themselves and implicitly tells the decoder how to construct the inner message. The inner message is the propositional content the message finally delivers. On this picture meaning is not deposited in any one of the three layers; it lives in the interplay among them, because the same marks can yield different inner messages depending on the frame and outer message a decoder brings to bear, and because much that we find meaningful—music, tone, style—never reduces to a single propositional payload.

I find the theory genuinely compelling. It seems to explain, better than anything else I have encountered, why the same sentence can be kind in one email thread and cutting in another without a single word changing, and why I can find a piece of music deeply meaningful without being able to state what it says. Yet I cannot shake a contrary intuition about ordinary cases. If I leave a note for my housemate that says the spare key is under the flowerpot, and someone asks what the note meant, I will answer with a proposition about a key and a flowerpot, not with a description of the note's frame or its outer message. The fact that it is written in English, in my handwriting, as a plain declarative sentence—these things seem to be the scaffolding by which the content is delivered, not ingredients of the content itself. If the outer message were really part of the meaning, then changing the wrapper while holding the content fixed ought to change what the note says. But translating it into another language, or typing it in a different font, or having someone else write it does not seem to change what it says at all.

So I am pulled in two directions: on one side, a unified theory with real explanatory power; on the other, a plain fact about how I individuate meaning in simple cases. I want a negotiated verdict rather than a one-sided resolution. Am I wrong to treat the note as a counterexample? Should I instead regard that intuition as an illusion produced by the transparency of a successfully shared frame, and what would have to be true for that debunking to be justified rather than an ad hoc rescue? Alternatively, should the theory be amended so that frame and outer messages are enabling conditions or channels for meaning rather than constituents of it? Or is there a reading on which the two never actually conflict because the word meaning is being used in two different senses? Tell me which move is best supported, and what considerations would justify each of the others.
