Write a sestina on the underdetermination of theory by evidence. The poem must make its philosophical point through the architecture of the form rather than through assertion. The sestina is your instrument for this: choose six recurring end-words that are theory-neutral items of evidence — observations that any of the rival theories would have to accommodate, and that do not implicitly favor one theory. Let the recurrence of those same six words in every stanza, in a different order each time, stand for the fact that the same evidence can be arranged into indefinitely many mutually incompatible theories, each fitting the data equally well. Each of the six six-line stanzas should quietly embody a different theoretical account of the evidence — a different causal story, geometry, taxonomy, or underlying mechanism — and at least one stanza should work the Duhem–Quine lever: keeping the observed facts fixed while altering which auxiliary assumptions are revised, so no observation by itself dictates what to give up. The form's restraint is itself part of the content: after the first stanza, no new evidence word may enter the end-word set, yet the poem keeps finding new ways to read the same six facts. The three-line envoi should gather all six end-words once more and leave the relation between evidence and theory one-to-many — not resolved by a final observation. Do not print the terms underdetermination, Duhem, or Quine in the poem, and do not have the poem announce its thesis; a reader who knows philosophy of science should be able to tell from the repeating, permuting evidence that this is a poem about underdetermination, and a reader who does not should still find it a coherent, controlled poem. Use a correct sestina end-word rotation, for example the standard sequence ABCDEF / FAEBDC / CFDABE / ECBFAD / DEACFB / BDFECA, followed by a three-line envoi that uses all six words.
