I have been defending what I call the triage taxonomy of truth-value gaps. When a sentence seems to be neither true nor false, I claim it is one of exactly three kinds of thing, and debates about bivalence go wrong by running the three together.

First, unfed gaps: the sentence itself is in order, but the world has not yet supplied enough for a verdict — future contingents, if genuinely unsettled, are the paradigm. Second, unsharp gaps: the trouble lies in the words, not the world — a predicate with no fixed boundary — and the fix is a stipulation, not a discovery. Third, unlicensed gaps: the question misfires, as with the present king of France being bald; here I claim there is no statement expressed at all, so bivalence is not even engaged.

The three can be separated by a repair test. Unfed gaps are cured by waiting or investigating; unsharp gaps are cured by drawing a line; unlicensed gaps cannot be repaired — you first have to change the subject. My core claim is that only the first two kinds are genuine threats to bivalence, and the third has been smuggling in apparent counterexamples that are not counterexamples to any thesis about statements.

Tell me: is this a real distinction or an artifact of my examples? Does it already exist under another name, and what would I have missed or mischaracterized? And where does it break — which standard cases land in the wrong bin, or why might the third kind still be a truth-value gap after all? Keep your answer to at most 250 words.
