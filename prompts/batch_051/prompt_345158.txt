Draft one essay question for a 90-minute, 25-mark final in an upper-level decision theory seminar. The question must be on the topic of whether a deliberating agent can assign probabilities to her own alternative actions. It must force students to engage the underlying tension between the first-personal, deliberative stance and the third-personal, predictive stance: if a choice is genuinely still open to me, can I simultaneously adopt a detached standpoint and attach a precise unconditional credence to the proposition that I will perform one of the actions under consideration? The question must not be answerable by reciting the definitions of evidential and causal decision theory; it should fail any student who merely reports that evidential decision theory values acts as evidence while causal decision theory values acts as causes.

Set the question inside a Newcomb-style case with this exact twist. The prediction has already been made and the boxes are sealed. A predictor is 90 percent reliable at anticipating the agent's choice, with the usual payoffs: the transparent box contains one thousand dollars, and the opaque box contains one million dollars if and only if the predictor predicted that the agent would take only the opaque box. Before the agent is permitted to choose, an experimenter who has not seen the boxes asks her to report a precise unconditional credence in the proposition that she herself will take both boxes. The experimenter truthfully assures her that the reported number will not affect the boxes, the prediction, or the payment. The exam question should ask whether a rational agent who is still genuinely deliberating can comply with this request; what, if anything, her ability or inability to comply commits her to; whether evidential and causal decision theory differ about whether such a self-credence has any role in determining what she should do; and whether the fact that both theories' verdicts about the boxes appear insensitive to the reported number supports or undermines the claim that deliberation crowds out prediction.

After the question, provide a model answer of roughly 800 words. The model answer must make each of the following moves: a clean separation of the agent's stance from the observer's stance, including the possibility that the request is ill-posed while deliberation is ongoing; a distinction between an unconditional credence in one's own act and the conditional probabilities of states given acts that appear in expected-utility calculations; a statement of what causal decision theory says about the case, including why its dominance argument is unaffected by the reported self-credence; a statement of what evidential decision theory says, including why it appeals to probabilities of states conditional on acts rather than to the agent's prior credence in the act itself; and a reconstruction of the strongest, appropriately restricted version of the thesis, restricted to a fully rational agent with stable preferences who is actively deliberating, ending with a verdict on whether the request for a precise self-credence is answerable, idle, or self-undermining. Do not name or cite any paper, and do not ask for any bibliography; argue from first principles throughout.

Finally, provide a marking scheme out of 25 marks. It should assign marks to the five moves listed above, and for each move add a one-line note on how students typically get it wrong, such as conflating an observer's prediction with an agent's conditional-on-act probability, treating the reported self-credence as if it could change what is already in the boxes, confusing self-prediction outside the deliberative stance with self-prediction while deliberating, or reducing the issue to a tedious rehash of the standard Newcomb debate.
