The void-for-vagueness argument, as I understand it, is that an offence is too vague to stand if it fails on either of two grounds: fair notice (a normal person cant tell in advance what's banned) and anti-arbitrariness (the law leaves too much room for officials to enforce it selectively or on the basis of prejudice). Both strands seem to assume a particular picture: a police officer or prosecutor reads an open-textured phrase and then decides, for reasons they could in principle give, whether to act on it.

Now here's the development I keep chewing on. Suppose a city has a perfectly ordinary public-order power, something like allowing the police to break up a gathering that's likely to cause serious annoyance. The drafting is no vaguer than plenty of laws that pass muster. But in practice, the decision about which gatherings get flagged is made by an opaque predictive model trained on data about the neighbourhood, the organisers, the time of day, and the officer simply rubber-stamps whatever the model outputs. The model's reasons can't be extracted or cross-examined.

Does the void-for-vagueness argument survive this? The fair-notice strand looks more or less untouched, but the anti-arbitrariness strand seems to be doing all the work — only now the arbitrariness lives in a system rather than in a officer's head. Which premise is actually under pressure, and is there a repaired version of the doctrine that still has bite? I'm asking what happens to the argument itself, not whether the policy is good or bad.
