I'm a doctoral candidate in history and philosophy of science, and in a few weeks I'm presenting a dissertation chapter to a joint workshop of our economic history and Chinese history groups, with two philosophers of science who work on causal explanation and counterfactual reasoning also attending. The thesis I'm defending is deflationary: the Needham question — why the scientific and industrial breakthrough happened in northwestern Europe rather than in China — is not a single well-posed contrastive question but an unstable bundle of several, and the appearance of one recalcitrant puzzle comes from an unexamined baseline that treats the West as the default and China as the deviation. I argue that once a contrast class and a counterfactual baseline are fixed, most specifications either dissolve into local and answerable questions, such as why the Yangzi Delta and England diverged over the eighteenth century after looking broadly similar a century earlier, or reveal themselves as questions we have no particular reason to ask, such as why China failed to have an industrial revolution rather than why northwestern Europe failed not to have one. I do not deny that a real divergence occurred and calls for explanation; I deny that the stock one-line question picks out a single explanatory demand worth answering.

The argument I'll give is roughly this: contrastive why-questions need a specified contrast class and a default or baseline; the Needham question as normally posed does not specify whether the contrast is with China's earlier technological precocity, with some projected natural trajectory, or specifically with the lower Yangzi; those different specifications set different explanatory targets and would be answered by different kinds of evidence; so the single question has no single answer and should be decomposed; and the pressure to keep it single comes from the very narrative — the West taken as the default and China as the deviation — that the question was supposed to probe. Now I need a Q&A prep sheet. The room will include cliometric economic historians who think hard counterfactual questions get posed and answered routinely; historians of late imperial China who are sympathetic to the anti-Eurocentric spirit of the argument but may think I've thinned a rich historiographical debate into a point about the logic of questions; and the two philosophers, who will press me on whether the argument overgeneralizes to every contrastive why-question in history, or whether it is ad hoc if restricted to this one. Give me the eight or so objections I should actually expect, in the order they are likely to come, phrased the way each group would put them; for each, a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and what to say if it comes anyway.
