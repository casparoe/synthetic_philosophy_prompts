I'm a PhD student in health policy and I sit on a small clinical guideline committee for our university hospital network. Every month or so we get a new trial or meta-analysis that seems to contradict a strong recomendation we already have — a drug we recommend for stroke prevention, a screening interval, something like that. The committee is smart about reading studies but nobody has any epistemology background, and we keep having the same argument. One side says the current recommendation is settled, so the new result is probably a fluke or a bad paper and we shouldn't touch the guideline. The other side says we should update on anything new, every time. I know there's a weird issue in formal epistemology under this — the dogmatism paradox, roughly: if you really know that p, then any evidence against p has to be misleading, so it looks like you can rationally ignore all counterevidence, and taken literally that would mean we never revise anything. Can you build me a reusable decision procedure for the committee? Something we could run in about 45 minutes at a meeting, in plain steps: the questions to ask, in order, when a new result appears to challenge a recommendation we currently grade as strong; for each question, what would count as a sufficient answer; at which steps the procedure says leave the guideline unchanged, at which steps it says reopen or downgrade, and at which steps it has to hand the decision to a person because the step is a value call rather than a technical one. It would also help if you handle the difference between a study that directly says the treatment effect is smaller than we thought and a study or reanalysis that says the original evidence was unreliable — those feel like they should trigger different kinds of review but I cant articulate why. Then run the procedure on three made-up cases: (1) a single small negative randomized trial against a large meta-analysis, (2) a big well-conducted trial that contradicts a strong recommendation that was mostly based on observational data, and (3) a reanalysis showing the original meta-analysis had a coding error in several included studies. Make sure at least one of the worked cases comes out "don't change the guideline even though the new result is statistically significant," since that's the outcome our committee will hate. And tell me where the procedure can't protect us — the failure modes where it would either let a false recommendation stand or make us chase noise.
