In debates over labor law and economic policy, opponents of collective bargaining often argue that a union is best understood as a cartel in the market for labor. On this view, the two institutions share the same structure: a cartel of firms withholds output to push prices above the competitive level, and a union withholds labor—through membership rules, collective bargaining, and strikes—to push wages above the competitive level. The analogy is offered not as a loose comparison but as an argument. If price-fixing among firms is condemned because it harms consumers, then consistency seems to require condemning collective bargaining because it harms employers, consumers, and the workers the union excludes, with the right to strike functioning as the cartel's mechanism for enforcing its restriction.

I would like a careful audit of this analogy rather than a verdict for or against unions. Begin by making the correspondence explicit: what plays the role of the firms, the product, the price, the cartel agreement, the withheld output, the consumers, and the antitrust enforcer? Then identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that merely limit the analogy from those that undermine the conclusion it is being used to support. Consider, among other things, whether labor is a commodity in the way a manufactured good is; whether an employer stands in the position of a consumer; what changes once the supplier of labor cannot detach herself from what is supplied; how the unemployed or excluded workers compare to the consumers a product-market cartel harms; and what the analogy quietly assumes about how labor markets would operate in the union's absence. Finally, say which conclusions the analogy is used to license—about economic efficiency, about fairness, and about the legal right to organize and strike—survive once these disanalogies are placed on the table, and whether some other analogy would do the argumentative work more honestly.
