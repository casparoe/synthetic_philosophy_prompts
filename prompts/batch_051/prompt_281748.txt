Me and a friend have been reading Benatar on our own — neither of us has any philosophy background, we just got interested in the idea that there's an asymmetry between pain and pleasure when it comes to bringing someone into existence. We give each other short essays to grade, and I'm suppossed to grade his answer to a question I wrote. I don't trust my own read of it, so I want you to be the grader. Give it a grade (out of 10 is fine) and then go through it point by point — what he gets right, what he gets wrong, and why. Not just an overall impression.

Here's the question I gave him:

"Explain, in your own words, the procreation asymmetry Benatar uses to argue that coming into existence is always a harm. Then apply it to this case: a couple is deciding whether to have a child who, as far as anyone can tell, would have a mostly good life — plenty of love and enjoyment, but also ordinary pain, dissapointment, and eventually death. What does the asymmetry say about whether they should have the child? Does it also say that a middle-aged person who is currently glad to be alive would have been better off never existing, or better off dead? Say what follows and what doesn't."

Here's his answer:

"Benatar's asymmetry is about how we weigh pain and pleasure in two states of affairs: one where a person comes into existence, and one where she never does. If she exists, her pain is bad and her pleasure is good. If she never exists, the pain she would have had is absent, and that absence is good — even though there's no one there to enjoy it. But the absence of her pleasure is not bad, because there's no one who is deprived of it. So in the existence column you have one bad entry, pain, and one good entry, pleasure. In the non-existence column you have one good entry, the absence of pain, and one neutral entry, the absence of pleasure. That's why he thinks non-existence beats existence for anyone whose life contains any pain at all — the pleasures of an existing life can't cancel out the extra good that non-existence gets.

Applying that to the couple: since the child would definitely have some pain, no matter how much pleasure the life also has, the asymmetry makes non-existence better for that child. And because it's better for the child never to exist, the couple has an obligation not to have the child.

For the middle-aged person, the same comparison says she would have been better off never existing — that part just falls out of the asymmetry, and it applies to her like anyone else. And by the same reasoning she would also be better off dead, since death just puts her back in the no-pain, no-pleasure situation. Benatar seems to want to resist that conclusion, but I don't see how he can avoid it."
