Say it's 2032 and on a roughly 150 km² forested island off New Zealand that's been overrun by invasive rats for most of a century, a conservation consortium—sick of failed poison campaigns—releases a homing-based suppression gene drive into the rat population, targeting female fertility with something like 90% inheritance bias. The drive spreads in about six generations and rats crash by >99% within three years; for the purposes of this question, grant that it never reaches the mainland and that feral cats are still on the island. Five years later the same group releases a founder population of maybe 40 birds of a de-extinct flightless rail that rats had originally extirpated. Give me a gears-level prediction of the next two decades of that island's ecology: what actually rebounds and what stays suppressed, whether the rail establishes or gets picked off by cats or re-invading rats, which second- and third-order effects (mesopredator release, invertebrate booms, seabird recovery, plant regrowth) dominate, and where human decisions swamp the biology. For each main link, put a rough confidence on it and say what single observation would move it most. Then give me your honest read: by 2057 is this system closer to its pre-rat baseline, or just a different novel state—and what would have to be true for the whole intervention to be net negative?
