A friend in a signaling seminar walked me into this argument and now I can't find the exit. It goes like this:

1. A signal is only honest if its hard for a cheater or liar to fake.
2. The only thing that makes a signal hard to fake, in humans and other animals, is the cost of producing it. If a bluffers can't afford it, we can trust it.
3. So every honest signal has to be a handicap: costly to the signaler in a way that trades off against the thing being signaled.
4. But huge amounts of ordinary human communication look cheap: saying "the restaurant is closed," answering "yes, this is the right train," apologizing when you bump into someone, telling your doctor "my pain is here."
5. By (3), those cheap signals must either not be honest, or be secretly costly, or we shouldn't trust them.
6. But conversation obviously works: we trust those signals constantly and we're usually right about them.
7. So either the handicap principle isn't an account of honest signaling in general, or one of the steps slipped.

Where exactly does this go wrong? I'd most like to keep (1) and (6). I keep staring at (2), but then I don't know what to say about the cases where cost obviously does seem to do the work, like a peacock's tail, stotting gazelles, or expensive engagement rings. If rejecting (2) or (3) is right, what does that commit me to about those cost-based cases? And if the mistake is elsewhere, tell me that too.
