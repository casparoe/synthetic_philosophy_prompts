Act as an expert in the epistemology of engineered and artificial systems, specifically the problem of inferring an optimizer's objective and search space from its behavior. In a workshop, I am working with the following frame. An observer sees an agent select a sequence of actions and wants to recover two things: the objective function being pursued and the search procedure by which actions get selected. These are jointly confounded in observed behavior. Any finite set of choices can be made consistent with many different objectives if one adjusts the assumed search space or constraints, and consistent with many different search procedures if one adjusts the assumed objective. A surprising action can therefore be read as revealing a new goal, as revealing a wider or differently structured search, or merely as forcing a revision of one's prior about the agent's competence. Which reading one adopts is not given by the behavior itself; it is imposed by prior assumptions about the agent's architecture, training distribution, capacities, and decision horizon.

Take as the real episode AlphaGo's move 37 in Game 2 of its March 2016 match against Lee Sedol. Treat the following facts as given. AlphaGo selected moves through a combination of a policy network and a value network under Monte Carlo tree search; the policy network was first trained on human games and later refined by self-play reinforcement learning. At move 37 of Game 2, AlphaGo played a move that professional human commentators first read as a probable error and later reframed as creative or intuitive. The move was reportedly assigned very low probability by the policy network as trained on human play. AlphaGo went on to win Game 2 and the match four games to one.
