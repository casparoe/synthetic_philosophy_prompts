I'm in a third-year seminar on transitional justice — we've worked through the South African TRC, Argentina's amnesty-then-trials arc, and a bit of the Rwanda/gacaca material — and I'm running next week's session on conditional amnesty. It's about 16 people, we've got 75 minutes, and I don't want another debate about whether impunity is worth it. I want them to actually feel something more specific: that a truth commission is a kind of exchange, not a values questionnaire. The only currency it can pay for truth with is impunity, and the people who know the most are exactly the ones with the strongest incentive to game whatever disclosure rule gets announced.

My rough idea is a fictional post-conflict state with three groups: commissioners, perpetrator-applicants, and survivor representatives. Each applicant draws a sealed card with a rank (foot soldier / commander / architect) and three hidden facts — one small thing that's already provable anyway, one damning thing only they know, and one bigger structural fact (a mass grave location, who authorized the disappearances). Survivors hold rumor versions of the same facts but can't turn hearsay into verified record by themselves; the commission's legitimacy score drops when it accepts unverified claims on faith. The commission has to adjust its amnesty rule as it goes, and the catch is the one we noticed with the 'full disclosure' condition in the South African case: you can only check a disclosure against what you already know, so the rule might select for the glibbest confessors rather than the most truthful. I want the rules to produce that discovery instead of me announcing it: blanket amnesty buys only what's already public, too-strict amnesty makes everyone deny and disappear, and the survivors end up arguing that a confession purchased with impunity isn't the truth they came for.

So can you turn this into something I can actually run? I need concrete rules, role cards and score sheets, a scoring system that works without real money, a 75-minute schedule for 16 people, and a debrief. Tell me honestly where it'll go wrong the first time — my bet is on applicants dumping the already-known fact as their 'full disclosure,' and on the survivir reps either refusing to trade at all or getting out-negotiated by the pre-law crowd. Also say what the game structurally can't reproduce (real fear, grief, the years-long spread of investigation, the fact that nobody in the room actualy killed anyone) and what the debrief needs to add so it doesn't come off as a weird marketplace for suffering. I can handle a bit of heaviness, but I don't want it to tip into grief tourism.
