I moved from our Lyon office to our Tokyo office six months ago, and I still can't get my head around how differently the same company treats the same job, depending on which side of the world you're sitting on. In Lyon the email server used to shut off at 7pm and HR enforced the 35-hour week like it was a secular commandment; if you showed up on a Sunday people assumed you were having some kind of personal crisis. Here in Tokyo the lights are on at 10pm, managers say things like "rest if for after the project," and when a colleague collapsed last spring the first question HR asked was whether his logged overtime met the karoshi threshold, as though overwork were a legal category to be managed rather than a scandal. Next quarter I have to propose a joint overtime policy for both offices, so I want to get past the labour-code level and understand what each arrangement actually presupposes about work, rest, and who owns a person's time. Does the French setup presuppose that leisure is a right and work is only instrumental? Does the Japanese setup presuppose that work is a calling or a duty that can override self-preservation? Where is each practice merely compatible with a theory rather than forced by it, and where are the two systems philosophically underdetermined? And is one of them closer to what Weber described as the Protestant work ethic, or is that ethic alive in both, just wearing different clothes?
