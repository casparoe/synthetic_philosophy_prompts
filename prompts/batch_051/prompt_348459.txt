I've been rereading Moore's Principia Ethica with a reading group, and we have found that the positions he actually holds are stranger and more interesting than the caricature. As I understand him, Moore thought the most valuable things we know are certain states of consciousness — roughly, the pleasures of human affection and the enjoyment of beauty — and he held that the value of a whole need not equal the sum of the values of its parts, his principle of organic unities. He also insisted that good is a simple, non-natural, indefinable property, so that whether something is good is never settled merely by what people happen to desire or by what causes pleasure.

We have been trying to work out what he would say about three developments that postdate his death in 1958, and we cannot agree. The first is art produced largely by generative models, where there is no traditional artist behind the work, though the finished pieces are often as striking as, or more striking than, human-made ones. The second is the close friendship conducted almost entirely through text and video calls, in which the parties are emotionally intimate but may never have been in the same room. The third is the parasocial relationship, in which one person develops a one-sided bond of affection toward someone who does not reciprocate, and who may not know they exist. For each case, I would like a prediction grounded in his actual commitments: would he place it among the most valuable states of consciousness, deny that it has intrinsic value, or give some third answer? And do his principles determine one of these outcomes in each case, or do they genuinely underdetermine it? Please identify the element of his view that does the most work in each case and say plainly where the extrapolation becomes indeterminate.
