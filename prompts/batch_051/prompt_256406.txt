Our school's activities policy contains this sentence: "The Principal may deny approval to any student event that, in the Principal's reasonable judgment, creates a significant risk of physical injury to persons or a significant risk of damage to school property." The clause was written two decades ago after a bonfire prank burned the gym floor; the handbook's examples around it all involve flame, power tools, or large structures.

My student group is planning a silent sit-in during fourth period: roughly four hundred students would leave class and sit in the main hallway for twenty minutes, with volunteer marshals keeping the lanes clear at each end. The administration says the event creates a significant risk of injury because it blocks a hallway that is also an emergency egress route and could produce crowding. We respond that those same corridors hold more students than that during every passing period, and sitting still removes much of the movement that causes collisions.

I care less about predicting the principal's decision than about what the clause actually reaches. Was a rule aimed at dangerous pranks and construction projects meant to govern a pre-planned, marshaled protest? Argue it from the wording (especially "significant risk of physical injury" and "reasonable judgment"), from the purpose of the clause and its surrounding examples, and from what the drafters would probably have said if asked. Which approach should control, and does it matter that the event is expressive rather than recreational? In your answer, please separate what is reasonably settled about interpreting safety rules such as this from what is genuinely contested among people who study risk regulation, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
