Analyze the July Crisis of 1914 — the sequence of decisions between the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand on June 28 and the outbreak of general war in early August — through the lens of a commitment race.

For this prompt, understand a commitment race as follows. In a standoff, each actor would prefer to keep its options open, but each also has an incentive to bind itself irreversibly to a course of action before its rivals do, because being the first to be visibly and irrevocably committed can make threats credible, force rivals to re-optimize around one's commitments, and shift the bargaining range in one's favor. When several actors face this incentive at once, they race to commit first, even though the result of everyone committing may be worse for all sides than the original standoff. The dangerous mechanism is the irreversibility of the commitments themselves.

Using this concept, give a disciplined mapping of the crisis:

1. Identify the players and their options at the main decision points: Austria-Hungary after Sarajevo and its ultimatum to Serbia; Germany's blank check; Russia's choice between partial and general mobilization; Germany's ultimatums to Russia and France; and Britain's decision to enter the war.
2. State precisely what counts as a commitment in this setting. Mobilization orders? Ultimatums? Declarations of war? Alliance guarantees? Say which were reversible in practice and which were not, and why that matters for whether a race was actually on.
3. Say what the commitment-race model would have each actor do at each point, and how the actual sequence compares. In doing so, distinguish a first-mover advantage — commit early to make a threat credible and shift the bargaining range — from a last-mover disadvantage — waiting becomes intolerable once a rival commits, so one commits defensively.
4. Then give an honest account of where the model breaks down. Consider at least: the Schlieffen Plan's timetable and the pressure it placed on Germany once Russia mobilized; the cult of the offensive; domestic political constraints; and the fact that several leaders treated mobilization as still negotiable bargaining rather than as an irrevocable step to war.
5. Finally, run the analysis in the other direction. Does the July Crisis confirm the commitment-race model, expose a gap in it — for example, ambiguity about what counts as commitment, or mutual misreading of how reversible one's own and others' moves were — or show that the race metaphor was a poor fit for 1914?

Keep the analysis organized around the model rather than retelling the narrative of the crisis.

End with a single-sentence summary on a line beginning "Verdict:" that states your bottom-line assessment of whether the July Crisis is best understood as a genuine commitment race, a miscalculation, a coordination failure, or some hybrid.
