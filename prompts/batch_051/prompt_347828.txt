I'm a longtime reader of ACX, LessWrong, and the adjacent history-blog ecosystem, and I keep noticing an argument move I don't have a name for, so I'm hoping someone can either give me the established term or carve it out cleanly.

The setting is always the same: an argument about whether some historical figure should be judged harshly—Jefferson and slavery, Churchill and empire, Columbus and the treatment of Indigenous people, the stock cases. And the defence is always some version of "he was a man of his time." But the thing I keep seeing is that the defence cycles through three different claims without anyone seeming to notice the swap.

First version: he didn't know better; the moral wrongness just wasn't visible to people of his era, so blaming him is presentism. Then someone points out that contemporaries of his did know and said so—this objection was available to him inside his own time. Second version: he couldn't have acted on it anyway, because he was embedded in the institution or economy and it would have cost him too much to do the right thing. Then someone points out that he took costly, unpopular, against-the-grain positions on other matters when he cared enough, so "no room for agency" is false in his case. Third version: we shouldn't be judging the dead by our standards at all; the whole project of condemning people across centuries is suspect. And then, weeks later, the same person is back to "everyone thought it was fine," as if the earlier refutations never happened.

Each of those claims has a very different logic—one is epistemic, one is about available action, one is a meta-normative refusal to judge—and they get swapped in and out as the argument requires. It's not quite motte-and-bailey, because there's no stable defensible core: the "safe" claim keeps changing too, and none of the three is obviously more defensible than the others. It's also not quite moving the goalposts, because the standard isn't just being raised; the defender is switching which kind of claim is doing the work.

So here's what I want to know: is there an established name for this specific ratchet—the man-of-his-time defence cycling between "he didn't know," "he couldn't act," and "we can't judge the past at all"? Or is it just an instance of something more general, and the historical-figure version is an unnamed subspecies? If there's no name, coin one and tell me exactly what qualifies and what doesn't. I'm not after a reading list; I want the carving.

Please structure your answer as:

- **Nearest existing things** (about 200 words): the closest named concepts—motte-and-bailey, moving the goalposts, equivocation, presentism, whatever they are—and precisely how the thing I'm describing differs from each.
- **The carve** (about 400 words): define the phenomenon, give it a name if it has one or coin one, and give boundary cases—where it starts and stops, and which similar-looking but different moves are excluded.
- **When it's legitimate** (about 200 words): the strongest version of the "man of his time" defence that is not this ratchet, and what line separates the honest use of it from the pattern I'm describing.
