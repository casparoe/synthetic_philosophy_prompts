In the past year I helped a friend incorporate a small business, and I was struck by how carefully the paperwork conferred a limited "personality" on the new entity -- it could own property, enter contracts, sue and be sued -- while the state's registration system seemed equally careful to stop short of treating that personality as anything like sovereignty. Shortly afterward I read about the East India Company, which for a long period raised armies, administered territory, coined money, and made treaties under a charter, yet was still called a company rather than a state. The juxtaposition has been bothering me: today we speak of "sovereign states" and "private corporations" as though they were different in kind, but the historical cases make the line between them look like a matter of degree, ambition, and historical accident.

The existing practice I want to understand is the modern legal order's categorical separation between the two. States are treated as the exclusive bearers of territorial integrity, the monopoly on legitimate coercion, treaty-making capacity, and the power to grant charters. Corporations, however large or long-lived, receive only a state-conferred legal personality for limited commercial purposes. Even when a corporation behaves like a ruler -- running private cities, operating arbitration systems, or, in the old chartered cases, governing territory -- we describe this as something the state has delegated, licensed, or failed to discipline, not as the corporation's having become a state. What view of the state, of sovereignty, or of collective agency does this practice presuppose? Does it require the thought that there is some non-delegable residue in the state -- original constituent power, an essentially public character, a territorial monopoly on force -- that a corporation cannot in principle possess? Or is the line merely a convenient legal convention, with the practice consistent with several quite different theories?

Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Then tell me what would have to change -- legally, institutionally, and conceptually -- if we instead held that the state is only a species of corporation, or, on the other side, that corporations can be genuine bearers of sovereignty. I am less interested in historical examples themselves than in the conceptual commitments: what one is committed to in drawing the line at all, and whether the bright line can survive the admission that the two categories have always bled into one another.
