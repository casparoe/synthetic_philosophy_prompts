I have been trying to think through Ian Hacking's claim that psychiatric classifications have looping effects. When a category such as ADHD or depression is introduced, the people classified under it learn the label, reinterpret their memories and behavior through it, and begin to change in response; this changes what the category picks out, which forces the category itself to shift, and the cycle starts again. Classifying people is therefore unlike classifying a chemical, because the thing classified is aware of the classification and acts on that awareness. Now suppose psychiatry managed to anchor diagnosis in biological markers — genetic variants, neural signatures, blood tests — so that someone could be classified without contributing the self-descriptions and symptom reports that used to feed the loop. Does Hacking's argument survive this development, or has its footing been removed? A biomarker is not aware of being classified, so the loop might seem to be cut at the source. But a person who is told they carry a marker may still change how they live, whether they seek treatment, and how they understand themselves, thereby altering the condition and even the meaning of the marker. Where does the looping effect actually do its work: in the category, in the act of classification, or in the subject's coming to know the label? Would a biomarker-based psychiatry escape Hacking's instability, or would it merely move the loop further downstream?
