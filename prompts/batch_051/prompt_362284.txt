In my philosophy of science seminar we've been doing a unit on underdetermination and scientific realism, mostly about whether our best theories actually license adding unobservable things to our ontology — dark matter, other minds, the very early universe, that kind of thing. Last week someone got frustrated in discussion and said "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence," and the room mostly nodded and the argument just ended. I've heard that line my whole life, but I've also seen it used to mean a bunch of different things: sometimes as a point about logic (a lack of proof doesn't entail nonexistence), sometimes as a point about how much we should update (don't lower your credence much just because we haven't found the thing yet), and sometimes almost as an excuse for not updating at all. In our discussion it was being used to defend the idea that we shouldn't be less confident in dark-matter candidates, or whatever, just because direct-detection experiments keep coming up empty.

Could you lay out the distinct claims that sentence could be making, and say which are trivial, which are genuinely controversial, and which are just false as stated? I also want to know which reading was actually doing the work in that argument, because it felt like the slogan was smuggling in the controversial version under the cover of the trivially true one. For context, someone else in the class pointed out that in a Bayesian setup, failing to find something is evidence of absence whenever the search would of had a decent chance of finding it if it were there — and that felt right to me, but then I couldn't tell whether the slogan was just denying something weaker than that or actually contradicting it.
