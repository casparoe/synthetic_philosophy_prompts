Question (20 points): State the principle of the identity of indiscernibles, explain Max Black's two-sphere thought experiment, and assess whether it shows the principle is false, considering at least one reply.

The student wrote:

The identity of indiscernibles, also known as Leibniz's law, says that if x and y have all the same properties, then x = y. Equivalently: no two distinct things can be exactly alike; numerical difference requires some qualitative difference.

Max Black asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two solid iron spheres, exactly alike in size, shape, color, temperature, chemical composition, and every other intrinsic feature, sitting two miles apart. Black says this universe is logically possible, and that in it the two spheres share every property but are still two things, not one. So if he's right, the identity of indiscernibles is false.

The obvious reply is that the spheres are not really indiscernible, because they differ in their spatial properties. One sphere is at one place and the other is at another place, and each stands in different relations to the other: one is two miles to the left of the other, and the other is two miles to the right. So the spheres do differ in properties after all, and the principle survives.

Black considers this reply and responds that in a universe with only two perfectly similar spheres there is no fact of the matter about which one is "on the left": the description is symmetric, and we could swap the two without changing anything. He also points out that the relation of being two miles apart is symmetric, so each sphere has the same relational property — namely, being two miles from the other sphere. So the relational reply does not give us any property that one sphere has and the other lacks.

A more recent response concedes Black's point about monadic properties but says the spheres are still weakly discernible, because they stand in an irreflexive symmetric relation: each sphere is two miles from the other, and neither is two miles from itself. Since weak discernibility is a kind of discernibility, there is a perfectly good sense in which the spheres can be told apart, and so the identity of indiscernibles is preserved.
