In my work as a software engineer I constantly lean on a rough division between two kinds of statements: those that hold because of the definitions, contracts, and conventions we chose when we built a system, and those that hold because the world outside the system happens to be a certain way. A field is required because we defined the schema that way; a query returns an empty result because that customer has no matching rows. Over time, though, I have come around to a stronger view: there is no principled boundary between these two kinds of truth. What gets treated as holding by definition rather than holding in virtue of the facts is always redrawn for convenience, usually when something breaks or needs to change, and any proposed boundary either smuggles in assumptions about the world or gives way when you push on it.

I want an honest map of what would count against that view. What would have to be true for the boundary to be principled after all? I am asking for defeaters, not a general survey: which arguments, formal results, or concrete cases should weigh most heavily against the no-principled-boundary position; which pressures from mathematical practice, programming-language semantics, scientific taxonomy, or ordinary lexicography most threaten it; and where a defender of the position would themselves concede that a proposed boundary is doing real work rather than being stipulated after the fact. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which are live possibilities rather than merely logical ones. I would find it especially persuasive if someone could exhibit a boundary that stays put when new empirical information arrives, without either changing the subject or turning into an empirical claim itself. Finally, tell me whether the position is at risk of being insulated from counterexample: if every proposed boundary can be dismissed as convenient, is the view still saying something substantive, and is that insulation a problem? Please answer in American English.
