Next Tuesday I'm presenting at our cognitive science brown bag, and I want a prep sheet for the Q&A, not a pep talk. The paper argues that the systematic errors students make in intro physics are better explained by a small number of fairly coherent, theory-like alternative frameworks (impetus-style motion, 'closer is stronger' for heat, things like that) than by either random mistakes or the knowledge-in-pieces view that intuitive physics is just a loose collection of small phenomenological primitives. My main evidence is transfer-task data: students who give an impetus-consistent answer on one problem tend to give impetus-consistent answers on structurally similar but superficially different problems, including novel ones, and the errors cluster in ways a pure knowledge-gap account struggles with.

The audience is the problem. There'll be two or three people from the diSessa-style knowledge-in-pieces camp who think calling these clusters 'theories' just reifies patterns in my test items; they'll say the apparent coherence is an artifact of the contexts I chose, and that the same students will flip to different answers under different framings. There's also a developmental person in the Vosniadou tradition who thinks 'theory' is too strong but that framework-like consistency is real, so she'll be annoyed if I overclaim coherence. And there's a psychometrician who will be all over my coding scheme and wether my predictions were registered before I saw the data.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way those people would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather then hope nobody notices. Also tell me the objection I'm probably hoping nobody asks.
