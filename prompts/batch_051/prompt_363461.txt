I keep noticing a pattern in debates about radical self-modification, and I don't have a word for it. The setup: someone wants to change their own mind in a deep way—not just learn a fact or pick up a habit, but change the very values or motivations they would use to judge wether the change was worth it. The usual worry is that you can't give informed consent, because the person after the change is the one evaluating the decision, and they might be perfectly satisfied with it precisely because the standard got swapped out. But there's an asymmetry that bugs me more. Before the change, you might look at the modified person as a stranger who has lost something essential, so the old you would refuse. After the change, the new you looks back and sees the old you as the one who was limited, and would happily ratify the decision. Yet only the old you ever gets to vote, since consent has to happen beforehand, which means 'no' gets a veto while 'yes' only ever shows up after the fact. I've seen this exact shape in arguments about moral bioenhancement and mind uploading, but also in ordinary cases like leaving a tight-knit religious community or moving somewhere that rewires your priorities.

Is there an established name for this configuration, and is the asymmetry real or just a framing effect? And can you distinguish it from adaptive preferences, from ordinary risk-taking under uncertainty, and from just slowly changing your mind over time? I'm not after a bare label—I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are.
