I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long piece about what chaos theory does and doesn't tell us about prediction, determinism, and the limits of scientific foresight. In the course of reporting, I've settled into a working set of commitments, and before I build the piece around them I want to know whether they actually hang together. Here they are:

(1) Setting quantum mechanics aside, I accept a classical, deterministic picture of the world: a complete state of a system at one time, together with the dynamical laws, fixes its state at every later time.

(2) Many real systems — weather, turbulent fluids, the long-term motion of the solar system — are chaotic in the technical sense: nearby initial conditions diverge exponentially over time.

(3) Because of that exponential divergence, the future of such systems is unpredictable in principle beyond a system-specific horizon (roughly a few Lyapunov times), not merely unpredictable in practice for observers with limited data.

(4) Laplace's demon — an ideal predictor in possession of the exact values of every relevant variable and able to compute without limit — could nevertheless compute any future state exactly from the laws.

(5) No predictor physically embedded in the universe can perfectly predict its own future states faster than those states occur, since it would have to contain and run a model as complex as itself.

(6) The unpredictability of chaotic systems is no evidence against determinism; a system can be fully deterministic and still resist prediction.

I can't tell from the inside which of these pull against each other and which only seem to, so I'd like an audit rather than a lecture. Please structure it in four sections, in this order:

1. Clarifications (about 150 words): flag any distinctions or readings on which the verdict depends, and note any commitments that need to be made more precise before they can be assessed.

2. Pairwise audit (about 450 words): go through the main pairs of commitments and say, for each, whether it is a genuine tension, a merely apparent tension, or an independent pair.

3. Tensions by severity (about 350 words): rank the genuine tensions from most to least severe, explain each one, and say which commitment would have to give and in what direction.

4. Cheapest repair (about 200 words): propose the single revision that restores the most coherence at the lowest cost, and be explicit about what that revision costs.
