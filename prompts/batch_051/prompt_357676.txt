I spent my career in physical chemistry, where a theoretical term lives or dies by the predictions it continues to earn, and I have lately been reading philosophy of mind with that background in mind. I now run a continuing-education discussion group — a dozen retirees, mostly with scientific or technical backgrounds — that meets for ninety minutes every other week. I would like to devote a session to eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires: the view that our ordinary talk of believing, wanting, hoping, and intending is best understood as a tacit theory with real predictive power, but a theory nonetheless, and therefore something that a mature neuroscience could in principle displace the way phlogiston talk was displaced. I want the group to feel why a reasonable person might accept such a view before anyone hears its name or its standard label; there should be no lecture up front.

Please design a runnable ninety-minute activity that accomplishes this. It should be a structured game or predictive task rather than a tour of the familiar thought experiments from the literature. I imagine something like this shape: participants must repeatedly predict the behavior of some agent, first using the familiar belief-and-desire idiom, then under a constraint or with an explicitly non-mental vocabulary, and then with a third method that proves noticeably better at the same predictions. The rules, scoring, and roles should be arranged so that the group discovers for itself that the belief-desire vocabulary behaves like a fallible instrument rather than a transparent report of what is going on inside. Do not make the third method a finished neuroscience; part of the lesson should be that a vocabulary can rationally lose its authority to a better-performing instrument even before a complete alternative theory exists.

Include a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the eliminativist argument, anticipates the standard objections (the self-refutation charge, the worry that the view must rely on the very intentional vocabulary it would discard, and the demand to specify a working replacement now), and is candid about what the exercise cannot reproduce — I suspect it cannot reproduce the felt immediacy of one's own occurrent thinking, which is a large part of why people resist the conclusion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it.
