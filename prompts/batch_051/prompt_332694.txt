I have recently moved into a bioinformatics group, and I keep finding myself in arguments about a debate I had previously encountered only through popular science: the gene's-eye view of evolution, with its claim that organisms are really just vehicles built by replicators for their own propagation, set against the critics who insist that organisms are cohesive units selected as wholes and that selection is legitimately described at several levels at once. My colleague presses the biological side of this, but I am a software engineer and I grasp the arguments far better when I translate them into distinctions I handle every day: a version-controlled codebase versus a running process, a library function that gets copied into many processes versus the overall behaviour of the whole program, differential propagation of one version of a function through a population of deployments versus the success or failure of the program as such, modularity and refactoring, shared mutable state, and the difference between a function's local effects and its effects on resources outside the process.

Please restate the gene's-eye view and its critics' main objections in that vocabulary, as precisely as you can. Let genes correspond to the replicating code (the functions or source lines that are copied), organisms to the vehicles or runtime processes, phenotypes to the observable behaviour of a run, and natural selection to a population-level process in which some code spreads because processes running it leave more copies of it than competing variants do. In particular, translate these claims: that the organism is a mere vehicle or survival machine; that the gene is the primary unit of selection in a causal sense, not merely one useful way of keeping the books; that selection can also be described at the level of the whole organism or the group, with the levels representing different accounting conventions rather than mutually exclusive causal stories; and that gene-level bookkeeping obscures important interactions such as a single gene with multiple effects (pleiotropy) and genes whose effects depend on other genes (epistasis), for which code reuse, tightly coupled modules, and shared state are my admittedly imperfect home turf.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the biological debate map cleanly onto the software frame, and which did you have to fudge or load with tendentious assumptions to make fit? Which parts have no real counterpart in how programs work — for example, that software is copied with exact fidelity by design whereas biological copying introduces mutation; that programs have authors, specs, and release cycles whereas the gene's-eye view is filled with design talk in the absence of any designer; or that my vocabulary may smuggle in modularity and intentionality that the biology does not contain? Conclude with your honest view: are the places where the translation breaks down merely defects of my target vocabulary, or do they reveal something genuinely misleading, or genuinely unclear, in the gene's-eye view itself? Please write your answer in British English.
