so ive been chewing on Carroll's tortoise for a course on the norms of reasoning, and I've ended up with a position I want someone to attack properly. The position is this. The tortoise accepts P and if-P-then-Q but refuses to accept Q; Achilles adds a further premise, something like if P and if-P-then-Q then Q, and she accepts that too but still won't move to Q, and off it goes. The usual moral is supposed to be that deduction needs a justification it can't get, or that the inference rule has to be added as a premise forever. I've come to think that's exactly backwards. My view is that the real lesson is that rules of inference are not the kind of thing that can be premises at all, so the demand for a further premise to justify modus ponens is a category mistake. Inferring is something you do — a capacity or a commitment you exercise — not an extra belief you hold for reasons; the regress is just what you hear when you ask a practice to justify itself propositionally.

So what I want is a map of what would count against this view, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. For example: what would have to be true for the premise/rule distinction I'm resting on to collapse, or for somebody to have actually produced a non-circular justification of modus ponens after all? What should my view predict that the rival premise-view doesn't — about whether the tortoise is coherent, about whether a logical error is a false belief or a failure of performance — and where do those predictions get tested? If the view pushes me toward psychologism about logic (validity becomes a description of how we happen to go on, and the tortoise simply has different dispositions), is that a refutation or a price, and what do versions of my position that avoid it have to give up? I'd also like to know what defenders of my kind of view themselves concede counts against it, not only what critics say — for instance, does it just relocate the problem of normativity to the metalanguage as soon as I say a rule was applied correctly, and if so how bad is that? Finally, I'm aware the position might be insulated: every objection can be met by saying the objector still doesn't get that rules aren't premises. Is that a problem for a view of this kind, or exactly what it should predict? And be willing to tell me if the view is so well insulated that it no longer risks anything, and whether that's a bug or a feature here.
