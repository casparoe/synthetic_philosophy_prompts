I've been trying to pin down the value of emotions, and I've ended up on the romantic side: emotions aren't just noise or raw material that reason should sculpt, they're partly constitutive of the good life. Real love, real grief, real anger at injustice feel like they are themselves part of what we're after, not just signals that something else is going well or badly.

What keeps nagging at me is a passage in Epictetus's Enchiridion. He describes seeing someone weeping in grief, and he says, roughly, that the thing that distresses the person is not the actual event but their opinion about it. The grief, on his account, is produced by a judgement that something bad has happened; change the judgement and the grief goes. He seems to take this as reason to treat the grief itself as a kind of cognitive error, and he advises not being carried away by the impression that the person is suffering from external ills.

I want to know what would genuinely count against my romantic view, not just what Epictetus would say. What evidence, arguments, or cases should actually move me? I'd like them sorted from fatal to merely awkward. In particular, where should I draw the line between the Stoic point that emotions have a judgement-like structure — which I might accept — and the further claim that because they do, they are not part of the good life? Does the romantic view have to deny the judgement-like structure, or can it accept it and still insist that the felt quality of emotion is itself a good? And is my position at risk of being insulated by qualifications, or does it genuinely make predictions about what a life without such emotions would be like, predictions that could turn out false?

I want an honest map of the view's exposure. What would the world have to look like for my romanticism to be wrong? Are there empirical findings about emotional memory, decision-making, or subjective wellbeing that should shift me, or is this mostly a conceptual battle?
