My older brother is weirdly into commitment puzzles, so ive been writing down his rulings. He says Ulysses tying himself to the mast before the sirens is obviously rational. He also says that in Parfit's hitchhiker case the stranded guy is rational to make himself into someone who would actually pay the driver once hes been dropped off, not just someone who promises to, because thats the only way the driver will pick him up. But on Kavka's toxin case — you get a million dollars if, at midnight, you genuinely intend to drink some nasty but non-lethal stuff tomorrow, and you don't actually have to drink it to get the money — he says no, trying to force yourself into the intention is just self-fooling and you can't rationally do it. He calls this whole corner of decision theory rational irrationality.

Next week he's ruling on this one: a negotiator pays a hypnotist to give him a real explosive temper, not an act, because people cave to someone who might blow up. The temper is genuinely involuntary once it's installed; he knows he'll sometimes storm out of good deals or get himself fired, but he expects the overall deal outcomes to make up for it. What will my brother say — rational or not? I mainly want the rule he's implicitly running, the other rules that fit his three verdicts just as well, and one extra case that would best separate them.
