Last week two of my friends got into it over the counterfactual mugging problem and the argument went in circles. The setup: Omega, a perfect predictor, flips a coin. If it's heads, Omega gives you $10,000 if it predicted you would have paid $100 if the coin had landed tails. If it's tails, Omega asks you for $100. You're told it landed tails. Should you pay the $100?

Ben said no. The coin already landed tails; handing over $100 cannot change that. The $10,000 is gone, so paying is just giving money to an algorithm that exploits your soft spot for parallel worlds.

Ari said that's exactly how you never get the $10,000. If you wouldn't pay in the tails case, then Omega knew that, and in heads cases you don't get paid. The only way to be the kind of person who gets the reward is to actually pay when the moment comes.

Ben came back: that's sunk cost reasoning with extra steps. A policy can be sensible before you know the outcome and still be dumb after the uncertainty is resolved. Once I know it's tails, the heads world isn't waiting for me to make it real.

Ari said it's more like keeping a promise. If you'd precommitted before the flip, you'd pay because that's what makes the precommitment worth it. Omega doesn't need the literal precommitment; it can see what you'd do. Your decision now is a fact about you, and that fact has effects in heads branches even if you never see them.

Ben replied: so now the argument is that I should pay for personality maintenance. A normal mugger could say "I'll give your counterpart in another branch $10,000 if I predict you'll give me $100 now." Would you pay? At some point you're just paying for a nice story about yourself.

Ari said the normal mugger isn't a perfect predictor, so the analogy fails. Perfect prediction isn't a metaphor; it's the whole reason you can't treat the heads branch as already settled.

Neither would budge. Could you adjudicate move-by-move: who's right about which point, where each of them missteps, and what's the more defensible overall position? Also, please separate what's settled among people who work on decision theory from what's still genuinely contested.
