I've been trying to sort out where manipulation ends and legitimate persuasion begins, and my verdicts on a batch of cases don't yet add up to a principle I can defend. Here are the cases.

1. A colleague gives me an even-handed rundown of the costs and benefits of switching project-management software, including drawbacks she has personally hit. I end up convinced. This is persuasion, full stop.

2. A grocery store places apples and bananas at eye level and chips on the bottom shelf, and shoppers buy more fruit. I don't want to call this manipulation, even though it changes what people reach for without engaging their deliberation.

3. A social app keeps a "streak" counter on a shared activity feed, so missing a day visibly breaks the streak with a friend; users who had decided to cut back keep logging in. I do want to call this manipulation, though nothing is hidden.

4. A political ad uses entirely true, sourced claims about the opponent's voting record but sets them in drained color, slow-motion footage, and a minor-key drone. I call it manipulation: the presentation makes me feel a verdict the stated facts don't license.

5. A friend says, "I guess I'll just go to the recital alone, then." I count it as manipulation by guilt rather than persuasion, though no reason has been offered and no threat made.

6. A fundraising letter centers on one named child with a photo rather than statistics about a million children; I give more than the statistics would have moved me to give, and I know about the identifiable-victim effect while I'm doing it. I don't want to call this manipulation — I'm not even sure it's wrong — but I can't say why it differs from the ad in case 4.

7. A car salesman truthfully tells me another buyer is coming back in an hour prepared to pay the asking price, and I close the deal. I say this is not manipulation: the information is true, relevant, and I act with open eyes.

8. A subscription page makes renewing a one-click button but cancellation a four-step, gray-on-gray process. I say manipulation.

My working principle is roughly: influence is manipulation when it bypasses or undercuts my capacity to act on my own assessment of the reasons — by hiding what it is doing, by exploiting a weakness I can't correct for, or by making me feel something the facts in view don't license. But that principle doesn't fit the streak counter cleanly, since nothing is hidden; and if I simplify it to "any influence that works through channels other than reasons," then the eye-level fruit and the charity photograph have to count as manipulation too, which I'm reluctant to accept.

What principle best captures these verdicts? State it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and test it on two cases I haven't considered: a doctor who tells a nervous patient that her test results show damage that will very likely progress if she keeps smoking, and a fitness app whose cartoon character looks sad and begins to cry when you skip a day. Are the grocery shelf and the streak counter really different, or does one of those verdicts have to go?

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the principle; (2) how it handles the grocery shelf and the streak counter; (3) how it handles the political ad and the charity appeal; (4) its verdicts on the doctor and the fitness app; (5) which of my verdicts, if any, I should give up or revise.
