I've been trying to get clear on what separates a Lewis-style convention from a norm, and I keep discovering my gut verdicts dont obey the definition I thought I held. I want to know what principle, if any, captures my actual case judgments.

My working definition, roughly: a regularity R is a convention when everyone conforms, everyone prefers to conform given that everyone else does, there is some clear alternative R' the population could have followed instead, and all of this is common knowledge. And I had thought a norm is just a convention plus sanctions. But the cases keep wiggling out from under that.

(a) Driving on the right. Pure convention. If I woke up in a world where everyone drove on the left, I would too, with no sense that anything had been lost.

(b) Calling dogs "dogs." Also convention, but it doesn't feel arbitrary in the way driving-side does, and I'd be readier to correct a child on it than my definition predicts. Maybe that's just because language is near the center of my machinery, but I can't tell.

(c) Queueing. I get genuinely angry at line-cutters, even when they cost me five seconds and even when no one else is watching. But "everyone pushes to the front" would still get people served, and "last arrival goes first" would be a stable alternative ordering. So by my definition queueing is just a convention. Then why do I blame someone for not taking part in an arbitrary equilibrium?

(d) Tipping in restaurants. I feel real obligation, but I think the obligation is hostage to the wage structure: if menu prices rose and servers got full pay, I'd definately stop tipping and regard stopping as correct. So the ought seems to track a non-arbitrary fact, not the common-knowledge regularity.

(e) Recycling on Tuesday. The first year it's a pure convention; ten years later neighbors glare if you put the bin out on Sunday. Same payoffs, same regularity, but I want to say it started as a convention and became a norm. What changed, on my definition, is just the sanctions. But then "becoming a norm" is a fact about the observers, not the regularities, and I'm not sure that's right.

(f) Dueling among eighteenth-century gentlemen. I feel no obligation at all, yet I have no trouble saying it was a norm for them, with real costs for refusing a challenge. So a norm can be fully real in a group without binding me. Then what is my own feeling of obligation in the tipping case tracking?

(g) Saying "bless you" after a sneeze. Tiny residual obligation, no sanction worth speaking of, and it doesn't coordinate anything with anyone. I'd rather call it a ritual and set it aside. Fine, but that's me gerrymandering the dataset unless there's a principled reason.

(h) My friend group's way of saying goodbye. It's a convention, not using it has a cost (you mark yourself as outside the group), but I wouldn't call it a norm, though a bystander who saw it would probably say it is. Is it just a norm with a much smaller audience?

The pair I keep getting stuck on is (a) vs. (c): they look identical to my working definition, but one has no blame on it and the other does. And (d) vs. (f) pull in opposite directions about whether obligation is inside or outside the person.

I have a vague idea that the missing ingredient is something like expectations becoming reasons, or the regularity solving a problem each party already has independently, or third-party enforcement, but each of those breaks one of the cases when I try to make it precise.

So: give me a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that gets as many of these verdicts as possible, and say honestly which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which it forces me to drop. Then tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't settled: boarding a bus in a culture that always forms a line, versus a culture that always piles in — same buses, same schedules, both regimes stable. Is piling-in a convention that never became a norm, a norm, or neither, and does the principle say that without hand-waving?

Also, please separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
