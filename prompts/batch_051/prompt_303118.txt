I want to know whether the philosophical idea of humility — intellectual, moral, and social — is anything more than well-tuned confidence.

Treat it as an intellectual virtue first: translate its claims into the language of calibration, epistemic uncertainty, and abstention on out-of-distribution or distribution-shifted cases. Then attempt the same for moral modesty — the reluctance to assume one is better than others, or to take oneself as qualified to judge how they live — and for social modesty — the norms against self-promotion, status-claiming, and making oneself the center of attention. Then give me the residue report. Are the untranslatable pieces pointing to something real that calibration-talk misses — something about the direction of attention, the standing one grants another person, or a social relation rather than a property of one's own beliefs — or are they places where virtue vocabulary was doing less work than it appeared? And after the translation, decide whether intellectual, moral, and social humility still look like one virtue with three expressions or like three distinct phenomena sharing a name. Be willing to conclude that my vocabulary needs to be enlarged, that the traditional account was inflated, or some of each.
