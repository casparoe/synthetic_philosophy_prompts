Two students in my probability theory course recently locked horns over the two-envelope problem, and neither would give ground. A host sets out two sealed envelopes, one containing exactly twice as much money as the other. You choose one, and before opening it you are offered the chance to switch. Here is the exchange as I remember it.

Elena began with the familiar calculation. Suppose your envelope contains an amount x. Then the other envelope contains either 2x or x/2. Since nothing in the stated setup distinguishes those two possibilities, each should be assigned probability one-half. The expected value of switching is therefore (1/2)(2x) + (1/2)(x/2) = 5x/4, which is greater than x. And because the argument does not depend on the particular value of x, she concluded that you should switch even without opening your envelope.

Simon objected that the same reasoning runs in the opposite direction. If the other envelope contains y, your envelope has expected value 5y/4, so the argument would also instruct you to switch back. But before anything is opened, the two envelopes are perfectly symmetric; there cannot be a rational preference for either one. He concluded that the 5x/4 argument must be misapplying the probabilities somewhere.

Elena responded that choosing an envelope already breaks the symmetry: once your envelope is fixed, it has a definite amount x, and the relevant probabilities are the conditional probabilities that the other envelope contains 2x or x/2 given that amount. The reverse calculation, she said, is not a genuine converse of the first decision but a new problem that re-fixes the variable on the other envelope. She allowed that the one-half probabilities are not forced by symmetry alone, but insisted that the story is incomplete until one specifies how the host selects the pair of amounts.

Simon replied that in the decision as posed, you never learn x. Conditioning on an unobserved amount, he argued, treats a variable as if it were evidence you actually have. The only clean calculation starts from the pair itself: the host has chosen an unordered pair of amounts, call them a and 2a. If your envelope is the one with a, switching gains a; if it is the one with 2a, switching loses a. A chooser with no way to tell the cases apart has no more reason to be in one than the other, so the expected gain is zero. He added that the 5x/4 calculation uses the same symbol x for the smaller amount in one branch and the larger amount in the other, as though a single envelope could be both.

At this point Elena narrowed her claim. She granted that for an unopened envelope, the pair-level symmetry makes switching neutral. But she argued that once an amount is observed, the rational choice depends on the host's distribution over pairs: some specified priors make certain observed amounts favor switching, while other amounts favor staying, even though these conditional advantages average out to zero across all amounts. So she concluded that the problem is genuinely underdescribed, and that the real defect in the original 5x/4 reasoning is running the calculation with no distribution at all.

Simon responded that this was true but beside the point: the version people actually face asks whether to switch before opening, and there his zero-gain calculation settles it. Elena answered that if the zero-gain calculation only works by refusing to condition on anything, it has not explained why the 5x/4 calculation feels forced; it has merely changed the subject.

They ended there. I would like a rigorous, point-by-point adjudication of this exchange. Which of Elena's and Simon's specific claims are correct, which are incorrect or overstated, and what would a complete resolution of their disagreement say? In particular, are the unopened and opened versions of the problem genuinely different, and does either party account for that difference properly?
