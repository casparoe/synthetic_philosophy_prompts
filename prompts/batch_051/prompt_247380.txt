Two students in my school's philosophy club got into a long argument about whether deplatforming and other organized social sanctions should be understood as punishment. I was present but not involved, and I have reconstructed their exchange below as accurately as I can. I would like you to adjudicate it point by point: go through the exchange move by move and say who is right at each step, where each argument overstates or understates something, and what both parties are missing. I care less about which side wins overall than about which precise claims are sound.

First student: When a platform removes someone's account in response to offensive speech, that is punishment. It is a deliberate, harmful response to perceived wrongdoing, and the people pushing for it often want the target to suffer and to be made an example. If it is punishment, it should have to meet the standards we normally demand of punishment: a specified offense, actual evidence, proportionality between the offense and the harm inflicted, and some opportunity for the person to answer before their livelihood is destroyed.

Second student: That is too broad. Punishment is not just any harm inflicted in response to wrongdoing; it is a burden imposed by an authority with standing over the offender. A platform is a private actor choosing whom to associate with, and removing an account is the withdrawal of a benefit, not the imposition of a burden. A bookstore that declines to stock an author is not punishing the author, and neither is a venue that declines to book a speaker, even when the decision is made out of disapproval.

First student: A bookstore is a bad analogy. Major platforms are closer to public infrastructure, so being removed from them is closer to losing access to the public square than to failing to get a book deal. And the intent matters. Campaigns for deplatforming often openly aim at retribution and deterrence, which are the standard aims of punishment, not merely a shopkeeper's preference about whom to serve.

Second student: Intent still does not settle the category. A store owner who refuses service to a rude customer may want the customer to regret it and to deter others, but that is not punishment; it is the owner's own liberty. The same is true of declining to date someone or uninviting a guest. If every withdrawal of cooperation that is meant to hurt counts as punishment, then ordinary social life is full of punishment, and the category stops doing useful work.

First student: That only shows there is a continuum, not that deplatforming sits at the harmless end of it. We already distinguish a friend ending a friendship from an organized blacklist by the scale of the harm and the degree of coordination. The question is where on that continuum fairness requirements begin to apply. When a coordinated campaign gets someone removed from every major platform and the result is the loss of their career, the effect is at least as severe as many formal penalties, and it seems perverse to treat it as if no standards apply.

Second student: But the safeguards you are reaching for, a hearing, a proportionality review, a chance to respond, all presuppose a single decision-maker with the authority to conduct them. A decentralized campaign has no such agent. If a hundred thousand people each independently decide to stop reading someone, no one is in a position to hold a hearing or calibrate the response. Calling the aggregate result punishment imports an institutional model that does not fit the phenomenon, and then blames ordinary people for failing to provide a process they structurally cannot provide.

First student: Then the absence of a decision-maker is the problem, not a defense. We would have found a way to impose career-ending harm through pure aggregation while remaining unable to apply any standards at all. If the outcome is as severe as a formal sentence, saying that this is not punishment merely names the accountability gap; it does not answer the question of whether some standards, proportionality at minimum, should govern it.

Second student: The outcome can be severe, but fairness and proportionality can be applied as standards for criticism and association rather than as standards for sentencing. We can ask whether the speech was truthfully characterized, whether the response was proportionate to what was actually done, and whether the person has a path back. Those are real standards, but they are standards for responsible speech, not for punishment. If we treat every boycott as a sentence, we end up requiring ordinary people to justify their criticism before expressing it, which protects the powerful more than the vulnerable.

Please rule on this exchange step by step. For each move, identify who is right, where a claim is partly right but overstated, and what the two of them are jointly failing to consider.
