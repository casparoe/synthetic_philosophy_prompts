I'm taking a course that's half welfare economics and half ethics, and after a late-night argument with my roommate I've ended up defending a position I didn't expect too hold. The position is: interpersonal comparisons of utility are not just hard to make, they're strictly meaningless. Since each person's utility function is only defined up to its own positive affine transformation, there's no common unit and no common zero across people. So a sentence like "the tax costs the rich less in utility than it benefits the poor" isn't a claim we happen to be unsure about; it's more like saying one person's temperature is twice another person's length. It looks to me like a category mistake, not an empirical question.

But I keep running into things that make me nervous, and I want someone to actually push on the position rather than just list alternatives. For one, we all talk as if these comparisons make sense all the time — "you'll be better off with the surgery than without it," "that policy hurt them more than it helped us." Social welfare functions seem to need some way of adding or comparing utilities across people, and there are arguments where you imagine having an equal chance of being each person and then choosing from behind a veil, which looks like it smuggles in a common scale. So here's what I want: what would have to be true for my position to be false? I want a map of what actually counts against it — which results, arguments, or possible findings would be decisive, which would merely force me to modify it, and which a committed defender could absorb as "as-if" comparisons or as moral judgments dressed up as measurement. Distinguish the fatal from the merely awkward, and say which defeaters a no-comparisons person would themselves concede are genuine problems rather than just critics' talking points. Then take a definite stand: does the position survive, and if it survives only by qualifying itself, is that a problem? I'd rather have a committed verdict with reasons than a survey of options.
