I teach Rawls on religion every couple of years, and my students always want to fight about the proviso on public reason. The way I present it, someone arguing in a public political forum may introduce reasons from their own comprehensive doctrine—religious or otherwise—but only if, in due course, they also give reasons that citizens who don't share that doctrine can accept, and those public reasons have to actually support the conclusion rather than just gesture at it. I used to think the interesting question was whether that's too demanding on believers. Lately I've been wondering whether it's too easy to game in the other direction.

Take a city council member who's pushing a zoning change so a church can run a large homeless shelter in a residential area. At the hearing he gives two reasons. First, his church teaches that sheltering the stranger is a divine command. Second, the shelter serves the political value of securing the basic needs of the least advantaged, which no reasonable citizen could reject. On its face, the proviso is satisfied: a religious reason, plus a public one. But when his colleagues press him, it emerges that his picture of what destitution is—and why a church-run shelter with a particular religious ethos is the right response to it—is inseparable from a theology in which homelessness is a spiritual condition with material symptoms. Strip out the theology, and the jump from the public value (meeting basic needs) to this particular proposal (a church-run shelter) isn't supported; cash transfers or a secular facility would seem to fit the public value just as well. He insists he followed the proviso exactly, because he offered a secular-sounding reason too.

So here's what I want to know. Has he actually satisfied the proviso, or is his second reason a fake public reason, with the doctrine doing all the justificatory work while the public vocabulary does the window dressing? What makes a reason count as genuinely public in the sense the proviso needs, and does this case show that the distinction between public and comprehensive reasons can't be policed in practice? If the proviso is too weak in this direction, is there a way of strengthening it that wouldn't end up excluding ordinary religious citizens who are reasoning in good faith?
