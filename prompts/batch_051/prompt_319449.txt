Before our AI-governance seminar spends another week waving at Fearon, I want the actual spec on the table. Write me the desiderata for an adequate rational-choice account of an AI race: what any theory in this area has to get right, with the case behind each requirement, and a mark on which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preferences. It has to handle the lab whose researchers publicly sign serious risk statements and then go back to scaling; the unilateral-pause problem, where each actor would slow down only if it believed the others would too; the winner-take-all prize structure, where being first plausibly buys a decisive and lasting advantage; and the awkward fact that much of the danger is common knowledge and the race still doesn't stop. It can't collapse racing into ordinary market competition, and it can't treat ex ante inefficiency (a prisoner's dilemma) as if it were ex post bargaining failure; those are different animals. Then briefly score Fearon's three mechanisms — private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and issue indivisibility — against the list. The part I most want pinned down is which single requirement does the most to knock out popular stories, and whether any two requirements on the list are in tension.
