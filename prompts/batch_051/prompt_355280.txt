I serve on the standards committee of a professional association of consulting statisticians. Our Code of Professional Conduct, last revised in 1987, contains a clause — call it Article 4 — that has never been amended:

"In communicating findings to any person or body that does not share the member's technical training, a member shall not knowingly omit any material fact, and shall take reasonable care to ensure that the presentation does not create a false or misleading impression."

A complaint has now reached us. A member was retained by a tenants' association in a dispute with a landlord over an algorithm used to set rents. The association's members have no background in data analysis, and the member knew this. His final report is by all accounts scrupulously accurate: he supplied the complete raw data, the model specification, his testing scripts, and a lengthy technical appendix. He offered no plain-language summary, and the few non-technical paragraphs in the report say only that the results are conditional on a number of modeling choices. The landlord's own expert had no trouble reading the report and quoted from it at the hearing; the tenants, by contrast, could not tell what the member had concluded or which parts of the analysis mattered, and their lawyer now argues that they were effectively left unrepresented. The member's defense is that Article 4 prohibits omissions and misleading presentations, and he omitted nothing and said only true things. Whether the tenants could understand the report, he says, is an accessibility problem rather than an honesty problem, and nothing in Article 4 requires findings to be made intelligible.

The committee is divided, and I would be grateful for a careful analysis. Please work through what the words of Article 4 require in this case, what the purpose of the clause would suggest, what the members who drafted it in 1987 would probably say if asked, and what a reasonable reader of the clause at the time would have understood it to demand. Then say which of these approaches should govern in our decision, and what that would commit us to about the underlying question: when someone has disclosed everything, is that enough, or does sincerity toward a non-specialist audience include a duty to make oneself legible?
