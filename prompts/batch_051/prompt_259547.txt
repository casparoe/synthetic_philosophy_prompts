I run a five-person model-risk oversight team inside a company that operates several machine-learning systems, including a resume screener, a customer-support chatbot, and a pricing model. Every six months we decide how each system will be supervised for the next period. Leadership has accepted in principle that we cannot supervise every system the same way: some should be supervised by process, meaning training-data controls, pre-deployment testing and red-teaming, documentation requirements, and human review of changes; others should be supervised by outcome, meaning continuous monitoring of error rates, complaint logs, and downstream harms. Our audit budget is too small to do both intensively for every system. Leadership has asked my team to write a standing decision procedure that assigns any given system or major update to process-based supervision, outcome-based supervision, or some explicit mix of the two, and that allocates our scarce monitoring hours accordingly.

The procedure must be runnable in under two days by an analyst who has no background in the theoretical dispute, and each assignment must be defensible in a one-page note to product and legal. It should specify the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a definite assignment, and where it must escalate to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. It should explicitly handle at least these considerations: how predictable the failure mode is before deployment; how long a lag there is between a bad decision and our ability to observe it; how reversible the resulting harm is; how much outcome data already exists and how noisy it is; and the risk that process supervision decays into box-ticking while the system continues to cause harm in ways nobody is checking.

Then run the procedure on three cases we actually face. First, a new customer-facing chatbot that can recommend financial products, where most bad answers are subtle and we learn about them only through complaints arriving weeks later. Second, an internal resume-screening model retrained quarterly on the company's own past hiring decisions, where outcomes are abundant and measurable but may simply reproduce past bias. Third, a low-stakes internal summarization tool whose main risk is quietly inserting errors into documents that nobody checks. I expect the procedure to force at least one answer we will dislike, perhaps requiring process controls on a system we wanted to deploy quietly, or requiring us to let a high-profile system rely on outcome monitoring alone when leadership prefers the visible discipline of process. Tell me where the procedure draws its boundary between the two modes, how it handles sparse or lagging outcome data, and which steps are ineliminably judgment calls. The deliverable is the procedure and its worked applications, not a survey of the field.
