I have been taking part in a long-running argument about whether the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki were justified, and it has stalled in a way that now strikes me as philosophically significant. The parties agree on the chronology, the approximate casualties, the state of the Japanese government's deliberations after the first bomb, and the fact that the Soviet Union was about to enter the war against Japan; yet the defenders and the critics have not shifted their positions in years. I suspect the appearance of a single debate conceals several different disagreements. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? A hidden empirical disagreement about a counterfactual — whether Japan would have surrendered before an invasion, and how decisive the Soviet entry really was? A disagreement about which consideration just-war reasoning treats as decisive — for instance, whether non-combatant immunity rules the bombings out categorically, or whether necessity and proportionality may override it in view of the lives an invasion would have cost, with the long-run erosion of the nuclear taboo either inside or outside the proportionality calculation? A disagreement about the standpoint of judgement — whether the decision should be assessed as a gamble under uncertainty, by what could reasonably have been foreseen in August 1945, or as an accomplished act whose consequences are now known? Is part of the trouble that the two bombs are being treated as one act when their circumstances were different and they may warrant separate evaluation? Or is it a deeper clash of intuitions about whether intentionally killing civilians differs categorically from harming them as a foreseen side effect, and about whether a nuclear attack was different in kind or merely in degree from the firebombing of Tokyo? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, supported by what each side is actually committed to, rather than a verdict on the bombings or a recitation of the historical evidence. Given your diagnosis, what, if anything, could move the debate — a conceptual distinction, a piece of historical evidence, a reframing of the question — or is stalemate to be expected?
