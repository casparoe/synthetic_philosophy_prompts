Place yourself in the role of an expert philosopher of cognitive science, familiar with both the empirical literature on visual attention and memory and the debate over access versus phenomenal consciousness. I want you to work through a reconstruction of the overflow argument associated with Ned Block against global workspace theories of consciousness, and then commit to a verdict.

Here is the argument as I understand it, in numbered steps.

(1) According to global workspace theory, a perceptual content is phenomenally conscious only if it is globally broadcast—that is, made available to a wide range of consumer systems such as working memory, decision, and verbal report.

(2) In Sperling-style partial-report experiments, subjects briefly see an array of letters. When cued immediately after the array disappears, they can report any single row with high accuracy, but they cannot report all the items from the whole array.

(3) Because they could, at the moment immediately after the display, report any one of the rows, information from all the rows was available in a high-capacity store at that time.

(4) Subjects also report, and the standard interpretation has it, that they consciously saw the whole array, not merely the cued row.

(5) The best explanation of (3) and (4) is that the whole array was phenomenally conscious at the time of presentation, while only the cued row was globally broadcast.

(6) Therefore, there is phenomenal consciousness without global broadcasting, which contradicts the global workspace claim in (1).

(7) So global workspace theory is either false or must give up the claim that global broadcasting is necessary for phenomenal consciousness.

I want a committed answer to two questions. First, is this argument valid as written, or does one step fail? Second, if it is not valid, which exact step should a global workspace theorist reject, and what is the strongest version of that step they could put in its place? If it is valid, which premise would a defender resist, and why? Think carefully before answering; my suspicion is that the argument trades on an ambiguity between availability in a sensory buffer and availability to the global workspace, but I am not sure whether that distinction is ultimately defensible.
