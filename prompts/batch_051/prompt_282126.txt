A friend of mine summarized the causal exclusion argument for me from something he read, and I want to know how much of it I should trust before I go repeating it. His version goes like this. The physical world is causally closed, meaning every event has a complete physical cause. So when I decide to raise my arm, the neural state of my brain is by itself sufficient to make my arm go up. If I still want to say that my decision, as a mental event, also caused my arm to rise, then the arm movement has two sufficient causes, one neural and one mental, and that's overdetermination. Overdetermination is supposed to be like two bullets hitting a victim at the same time, each fatal on its own: possible in a weird technical sense, but nobody should accept it as a serious picture of how anything actually happens. So Kim's conclusion, he said, is that non-reductive physicalism is false, the mind does no real causal work, consciousness is epiphenomenal, and our ordinary idea that our decisions cause what we do is an illusion. The book he read apparently took this to show that free will is impossible too.

That last move feels too fast to me, and I also don't see why a neural cause and a mental cause have to be treated as competitors; but I don't know enough about the argument to say whether my friend mangled it or where.
