I have been turning over an argument that seems to prove far more than it should, and I cannot locate the step that does the illicit work. It starts from the standard simulation trilemma. Say a civilization is posthuman once it can run vast numbers of high-fidelity ancestor simulations. The trilemma runs as follows: if a non-negligible fraction of civilizations at our present stage reach the posthuman stage, and a non-negligible fraction of posthuman civilizations run many ancestor simulations, then observers with experiences like ours are far more numerous inside simulations than outside, and we are very probably simulated. So at least one of these must be true: (i) civilizations at our stage almost never reach the posthuman stage; (ii) posthuman civilizations almost never run ancestor simulations; or (iii) we are very probably living in a simulation.

Now take far-future fanaticism to be the view that our dominant priority should be to shape the long-run future, because of the astronomical number of future lives potentially at stake. The argument proceeds:

(1) Far-future fanaticism is warranted for us only if our present choices can be expected to have large, durable effects on how much value the far future contains.

(2) The trilemma above is sound.

(3) On horn (iii), we are very probably simulated. The world we inhabit is then an ancestor simulation whose continuation and parameters are controlled from outside: our simulators can terminate, alter, suspend, or rerun it. Whatever our choices change locally within the simulation, they cannot be expected to give us durable leverage over the far future of the base-level, non-simulated world. So fanaticism lacks the leverage it needs.

(4) Suppose instead that horn (iii) fails because we are not simulated. Then either (i) or (ii) holds. On (i), our lineage almost certainly never reaches posthumanity, so the base-level far future contains no astronomical population of our descendants for us to influence. On (ii), posthuman civilizations almost never run ancestor simulations, so the astronomical populations the trilemma itself makes salient—vast numbers of simulated minds—will not come about through our descendants either.

(5) Therefore, on every horn, our choices lack the durable leverage over an astronomical far future that fanaticism requires, and far-future fanaticism collapses for us.

(6) The reasoning in (1)–(5) is perfectly general. Any beings with an epistemic situation like ours, at any stage of their civilization or at any level of a simulation hierarchy, can run the same argument and should reach the same deflationary conclusion about their own far-future fanaticism.

(7) But that conclusion is absurd. Suppose, as the trilemma itself contemplates, that some base-level civilization reaches the posthuman stage and is deciding whether to run billions of ancestor simulations. Those beings would be deciding whether to create billions of minds—plainly a decision with astronomical far-future stakes, exactly the kind of situation far-future fanaticism is designed for. If (6) is right, even they should conclude that they lack the relevant leverage, which cannot be correct given their position.

Where does this go wrong? Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost for the rest of the view? I would most like to keep (1) and (2), since (1) merely states the standard fanatical criterion and (2) is the trilemma itself, but I am prepared to pay more than I would like.
