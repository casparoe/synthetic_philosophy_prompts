I am a novelist rather than a philosopher, and I am drafting a novel whose plot turns on the afterlife. In the course of the work I have had to settle a question I had previously treated as closed, and I want my position tested rather than defended. The position I have come to hold is this: the traditional doctrine of eternal conscious torment cannot be reconciled with any workable principle of proportionality, because sins committed in a finite life cannot warrant a punishment infinite in duration and severity; the only live alternatives are therefore annihilation and universal reconciliation. I do not want a case for this position. I want a map of what could be true that would make it false: which theological, ethical, and metaphysical considerations count against it, which are merely awkward and which would be fatal, and which a defender of the position ought to concede as genuine threats rather than dismiss. In particular I would ask you to address the claim that sin against an infinite being carries infinite demerit; the claim that the damned continue to sin and so continue to deserve punishment; the claim that hell is self-chosen separation rather than inflicted punishment, so that proportionality never gets a grip; the claim that annihilation is not the moderate option but the total loss of the greatest good; and the worry that universal reconciliation can be had only by coercing or dissolving the person who refuses it. I would personally find most persuasive those considerations showing that the position has misdescribed something about the nature of sin, of persons, or of what punishment is for, rather than considerations that merely make the alternatives look unappealing. Please end with an honest assessment of how exposed or insulated the position actually is, and whether that insulation would itself be a defect in a view of this kind.
