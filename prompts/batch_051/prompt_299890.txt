I've been going back and forth with freinds about whether a tourist in the US is morally required to tip, and whether cutting a queue in a country where queuing is sacred is morally wrong or just extremely rude. Somewhere in that argument I ended up with a little homemade theory I'd like someone to pressure-test properly instead of being polite about it.

I've started sorting everyday conventions into three boxes. (1) Distribution conventions: the total burden is fixed, and noncompliance just rearranges who bears it. Queueing is my cleanest case — if I cut a line, the total waiting time doesn't change, I just moved my share of it onto someone else. Rules about which side of the walkway to use fit here too. (2) Reliance conventions: people have arranged something material around the convention, so noncompliance creates a new loss instead of redistributing an existing one. Tipping in the US is the clear case, since a server's wage is structured on the assumption of tips; if a tourist stiffs them, the server ends up short actual money they had planned around. (3) Risk conventions: noncompliance creates a risk of harm that wasn't there before — jaywalking across a busy road in a city where drivers aren't anticipating pedestrians, even though it'd be harmless back home.

My claim is that only boxes 2 and 3 create genuine moral obligations, especially for visitors; box 1 things are etiquette, so a tourist who cuts a line is being a jerk but isn't wronging anyone. But I also think a distribution convention can slide into a reliance convention when the people it benefits are identifiable and have no reasonable alternative, like a queue for a water tap during a shortage. Is this a coherent distinction, or am I just relabeling the standard convention-versus-moral-norm split? Is the central claim too strong (queue-cutting sure feels like wronging the person behind me), too weak, or does it break in cases I'm not seeing? Think through the strongest version of my view and the nearest existing ideas, then give me a candid assessment that says exactly where the proposal fails or stops being distinctive.
