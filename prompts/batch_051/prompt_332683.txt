In my current book there's a scene where a historian has just published a monograph and is cornered at the launch party by an epistemologist who works on the lottery paradox. He insists her preface is the same disease in a different body, and he runs this argument at her. In my draft she gets stuck at step (4) but can't articulate why, and I want to be sure I've written her into the right hole. Can you tell me whether the argument is valid, and if not, tell me excatly which numbered step is the first one that fails?

(1) The author is rationally justified in believing each of the 300 factual claims individually, since she carefully checked each one and the evidence for each is strong.

(2) The author is also rationally justified in believing the preface proposition: that at least one of the 300 factual claims is false. No one produces 300 factual claims without at least one error, and she knows this about herself.

(3) Believing that at least one claim is false just is believing the disjunction: claim 1 is false, or claim 2 is false, or ... or claim 300 is false.

(4) If she is rationally justified in believing that disjunction, then there must be at least one particular claim — a specific claim among the 300 — that she is rationally justified in believing to be false. A rational belief can't be grounded in a vague 'somewhere or other'; it has to be grounded in some particular possibility.

(5) So there is a specific claim, claim k, such that she is rationally justified in believing claim k is true (by (1)) and rationally justified in believing claim k is false (by (4)).

(6) No one can be rationally justified in believing both a claim and its negation.

(7) Therefore a careful author cannot rationally believe each individual claim in her book while also believing that the book contains at least one false claim; one of those two beliefs has to go.

I've a suspicion the rot is in step (4), but I can't put my finger on it without making the character sound like she's just hand-waving. Could you answer in British English, please. And at the very end, after the explanation, give the verdict in a machine-readable line: \answer{...} where the brackets contain either the number of the first step that fails (for example \answer{4}) or the word valid (for example \answer{valid}).
