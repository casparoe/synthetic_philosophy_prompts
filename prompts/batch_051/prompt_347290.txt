I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, so I'm not a specialist in political philosophy, but a story on the 400th anniversary of a colonial charter company (the Dutch West India Company, if you want the specific rabbit hole) has landed me in the middle of a problem I can't shake. I keep returning to the opening of Hobbes's Leviathan, in the Introduction, where he writes:

"Nature (the art whereby God hath made and governes the world) is by the art of man, as in many other things, so in this also imitated, that it can make an Artificial Animal."

He then builds the commonwealth up as an artificial person, with sovereignty as its soul, and it strikes me how much this sounds like what we'd now call corporate personhood: a group of people, through a set of rules, becomes one "person" that can own, contract, and act. And the historical examples that send me back to Hobbes are charter companies like the East India Company or the Virginia Company, which really did govern territory, levy taxes, wage war, and administer justice while being formally profit-making joint-stock corporations. So the systematic view I'm attracted to is roughly this: sovereign states are not different in kind from chartered corporations; they are just the ones that swallowed the others or got swallowed themselves. But I also have a firm, almost physical intuition that something is being flattened out there. If the United Kingdom is just an East India Company that won, why does it feel like an abuse of language to say Parliament and a company's court of directors were doing the same sort of thing? Why does the state's claim to authority feel non-derivative in a way a charter company's claim never quite does—even when the company was, in practice, exercising powers we'd now call sovereign? Maybe my intuition is just confusing familiarity with naturalness. Present both sides at their strongest and tell me whether the theory or the intuition should yield, or whether there's a reading on which they're compatible.
