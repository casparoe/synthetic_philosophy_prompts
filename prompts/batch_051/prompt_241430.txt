I'm a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas, and I've been working on a piece about why the victors' justice objection keeps coming back in debates over international criminal law — it was thrown at the Tokyo trials, and it still shows up whenever a court tries a state's own former officials after a conflict. While reporting it I rewatched Stanley Kramer's Judgment at Nuremberg, which I hadnt seen since college, and I keep wanting to make the film the spine of the essay. But I'm worried I'm reading my thesis into it, and I'd like a careful philosophical reading before I commit.

The film is built around the retroactivity question: the defense argues that these German judges are being tried under law invented after the fact by the winners, and that the defendants were just applying the positive law of the German state, which is what judges do. The prosecution answers that some acts violate a higher law that no statute can make permissible. I know the standard legal-philosophy moves here — legality versus legitimacy, positivism versus natural law, the tu quoque problem of the Allies' own conduct — but I want the reading to start from the film rather than from doctrine. Does the movie actually take a side, or does it mostly stage the arguments and let the atrocity footage carry the persuasion? The part I can't settle is Ernst Janning. The film goes out of its way to make him a serious jurist rather than a caricature, and his late self-indictment seems to both concede the prosecution's point and subtly resist the tidy story the court wants. Where do you think the film genuinely illuminates the philosophy — for example the question of whether a court set up by the victors can still be legitimate, or how a judge's responsibility relates to a law's formal validity — and where does it fudge things or lean on sentiment instead of argument? I'd like an honest assessment of where the movie is doing real philosophy and where it's just very good courtroom drama, because I don't want the piece to claim the film settles something it only dramatizes.
