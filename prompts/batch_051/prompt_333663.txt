I'm part of a reading group that spent this term on scientific discovery, and our last session turned into a debate about "multiples" — cases where the same result is reached independently at nearly the same time, as with Newton and Leibniz on the calculus and Darwin and Wallace on natural selection. One member of the group is a committed inevitabilist (someone who thinks these discoveries were bound to happen sooner or later), and she compressed her view into the following argument. I find the conclusion hard to accept, but I can't locate exactly where it goes wrong, and I half-suspect that on some readings it doesn't go wrong at all.

(1) In both the Newton–Leibniz and Darwin–Wallace cases, a major theoretical breakthrough was made by two people working independently and almost simultaneously. (2) The best explanation of this pattern is not isolated genius or mere coincidence but the state of the field: the accumulated results, available techniques, and shared questions that made the breakthrough possible for more than one competent investigator. (3) If the state of a field makes a discovery possible for many competent investigators, then it was only a matter of time before one of them made it. (4) So in each case, the discovery would have been made even if the actual discoverer had never lived — by someone else, sooner or later. (5) If a discovery would have been made without the actual discoverer, then the actual discoverer deserves no special credit for making it. (6) Therefore Newton, Leibniz, Darwin, and Wallace deserve no special credit for the calculus or for natural selection, and the priority disputes between them are fights over a kind of accident.

Please work through the argument step by step. Is it valid as stated? If not, which step fails, and is the failure a matter of a false premise, an ambiguity in a term like "inevitable" or "would have been made," a scope shift, or something else? If the argument can be repaired by distinguishing two readings of inevitability, give the strongest reading under which the argument is valid and say what the conclusion amounts to on that reading. Throughout, please separate what is historically well established (for example, that the two pairs were independent and roughly simultaneous) from what is genuinely contested among historians and philosophers of science — including how much the state of a field by itself explains, and whether the fact that someone else would eventually have made the discovery removes the actual discoverer's credit — and do not present a contested view as the consensus. American English, please.
