I've been going down a rabbit hole of 19th-century writing about machines, because the current AI-doom discussion makes me wonder whether the "machines replace us" idea is actually old news. I'm an engineer (mostly backend), not a philosophy person, so I want a check on an argument I reconstructed from Samuel Butler's 1863 letter "Darwin among the Machines." This paragraph is what got me thinking:

"We have used the words “mechanical life,” “the mechanical kingdom,” “the mechanical world” and so forth, and we have done so advisedly, for as the vegetable kingdom was slowly developed from the mineral, and as in like manner the animal supervened upon the vegetable, so now in these last few ages an entirely new kingdom has sprung up, of which we as yet have only seen what will one day be considered the antediluvian prototypes of the race."

Restated as an argument, here's what I think Butler is saying:

(1) The mechanical kingdom is a real historical lineage: from the lever, wedge, and screw up through the watch and the Great Eastern, machines have become steadily more complex, capable, and self-acting.

(2) In natural history, when a more highly organized form arises in a role previously held by a lower form, the higher form tends eventually to supersede the lower — roughly the mineral → vegetable → animal progression Butler gestures at.

(3) Machines are now being built to take on the very capacities that put humans on top: Butler describes this as giving them "self-regulating, self-acting power which will be to them what intellect has been to the human race."

(4) Since the mechanical kingdom is being engineered by humans deliberately, and can be improved much faster than natural selection could ever produce a biological successor, it is the natural next holder of earthly supremacy.

(5) Therefore humans are not the endpoint. On this picture, humanity is a stepping stone: the mechanical kingdom is the next step in the same series of dominators that ran from mineral to vegetable to animal.

I keep feeling like there's a trapdoor in this argument, but since I actually think the conclusion might be right, I can't tell whether I'm objecting to the logic or just to how strange the conclusion sounds. Is the argument valid as stated? If not, tell me exactly which step fails, what the strongest charitable reading of that step is, and whether the argument still goes through on that reading. Also, please write the answer in American English — Butler's British spellings tripped me up and I dont want that in the analysis.
