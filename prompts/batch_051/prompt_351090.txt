I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether printing money actually creates wealth, and I keep noticing that the word "wealth" is doing most of the work, so I want to taboo it and force the argument to be about real stuff. His line is roughly MMT-adjacent: money is just claims on real goods, a sovereign that borrows in its own currency can always print, so inflation is the only binding constraint. My line is that printing mostly changes who gets the real goods and, in a slump, maybe mobilizes idle resources, but it can't conjure real output out of nominal claims — so the interesting question is whether the idle-resource channel is big and durable enough to count as "creating wealth" rather than just moving it around or pulling consumption forward from later years. We've both read the usual blog takes, so this is more about locating the crux than learning the positions.

The proposition I want priced: in a developed economy starting with unused capacity, a permanent money-financed fiscal expansion (say the treasury runs a deficit of about 10% of one year's GDP and the central bank credits the spending directly, and nobody ever "unwinds" it) leaves a representative household's lifetime real consumption higher than the same real transfer financed by debt, after accounting for the inflation, distortions, and whatever future taxes both regimes eventually imply. If that feels ill-posed in a way that matters, say what you're substituting, but still give a number to the nearest reading you can make precise. I'm around 0.35 that the claim is true on that reading; my friend is around 0.8.

So give me your credence and decompose the reasoning: the prior that real resources are actually idle and demand-mobilizable, the chance any output gain isn't just pulled forward from later years, the chance that permanent money finance really changes private behavior relative to future taxes, the chance the net gain survives the inflation and reletive-price distortions it sets off, and any other term you think carries weight. Give each term a number and a one-line justification, show how you combined them, say which term the final number is most sensitive to, and say where the 0.35-vs-0.8 disagreement most likely lives. Also tell me what single observation or argument would move your number most. Lead with your number in the first sentence, no preamble, no restating the question.
