I teach a seminar on longtermism and the ethics of the far future, and there's a distinction I keep making in discussion that I want stress-tested before I let it into a paper. I've been calling value change either "revision" or "rupture." Revision is when an old value remains on the books as a live reason long enough to be weighed and knowingly overridden. A former vegan who went through a real struggle, still feels the pull of the old arguments, and can explain which ones stopped winning—that's revision. Rupture is when the old value just goes offline without ever being faced down: a former vegan who drifted out of the practice, now finds it faintly embarrassing, and can only reconstruct his former self in the third person as someone who happened to have a set of beliefs. The difference isn't in how much the person changed; it's in whether the old value had standing during the transition.

My claim is that rupture is the bad kind of value drift and revision isn't drift at all; it's just moral development. If that's right, the standard far-future worry is aimed at the wrong thing. We shouldn't be terrified of descendants who reject our values—a descendant who can still feel the pull of "reduce suffering" and has reasons for setting it aside is a legitimate heir to the moral project, even if we'd hate the outcome. The terrifying possibility is descendants for whom our values have no pull whatsoever, so that "respect autonomy" is merely an anthropological fact about their ancestors rather than a reason they are overriding. So what matters for a good value future is continuity of normative uptake, not continuity of content. It might also mean a lot of what we call moral progress is actually rupture wearing a revision mask, and genuine moral progress may require making sure you could have been talked out of it.

I'm aware the line depends on an agent's capacity to tell a first-person story, and stories can be confabulated, so maybe the distinction is less objective then I want. Still, I'm attached. So: is the distinction real or just a narrative gloss? Has someone worked this out under another name (authenticity, dynamic consistency, transformative experience, preference purification)? What are the three strongest objections, where does the distinction break down, and is any of that fatal rather than just uncomfortable?
