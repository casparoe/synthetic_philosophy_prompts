I have an epistemic policy about reports of miracles, which I think of as my application of Hume's argument about testimony. It is this: when someone reports an event that would violate a well-established law of nature, I assign the hypothesis that the report is false—through error, fraud, or some natural cause I cannot yet identify—a higher prior probability than the hypothesis that the law was actually violated, and I do this before considering the particular witness's reliability. I also hold that no amount of testimonial evidence could in principle make the miracle hypothesis the better explanation, because the uniform experience supporting the law always counts for more than any human report. My friend says I have turned a sensible caution about extraordinary testimony into an unfalsifiable bias: a genuine anomaly would, by definition, initially look like this, and my policy guarantees I can never discover one. Is my policy defensible as stated, or is it a shield for my background picture of the world? If it is overbroad, what is the strongest restatement that keeps the protection against credulity while leaving me able to update on genuine anomalies? And how would I tell, from the inside, which version I am actually following?
