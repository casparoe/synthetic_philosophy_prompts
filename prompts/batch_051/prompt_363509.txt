Okay, a guy in my polisci group chat keeps pitching this and I want the mechanism stress-tested rather than just called a company town and dismissed. Suppose a new city-state is chartered as a for-profit joint-stock corporation. The city owns all the land; its revenue is the ground-rents on long leases plus user fees, and it spends on courts, police, public health, roads, and nothing else. Any budget surplus is paid out each year as an equal dividend per share. There is exactly one share per adult resident: a share is simultaneosly a vote, the right to reside, and a claim on the dividend. You can't hold more than one share, you can't split a share, shares can be bought and sold freely, and to reside in the city more than half the year you have to own a share. Each year shareholders elect a nine-member board that sets rents and fees and runs the services; the founding charter, including the one-share cap, the dividend formula, and the residency rule, can be amended by a two-thirds vote of shares. The board can also issue new shares to raise capital, diluting everyone equally.

Walk me through the first two or three decades. What does the share price end up tracking, and what does that imply about who sells to whom? Do shareholders vote to keep services lean so the dividend stays high, or to fund things that raise ground-rents, and what decides the crossover? Where does the system get gamed first: trusts or nominee residents collecting votes through other people, shareholders borrowing against future dividends and becoming forced sellers, the two-thirds clause being used to abolish the one-share cap, or something earlier than all of that? How long until there's a permanent non-voting class inside the city, and what form does it take? Then tell me which empirical or conceptual assumption in your account, if it went the other way, would most change your verdict about how the thing plays out.
