My partner L and I moved into a forty-unit housing cooperative last spring, and I have spent the year since then keeping a private list of her moral verdicts. She has no formal philosophy beyond a single undergraduate course years ago, but every dispute eventually meets her standard question — "what if everyone did that?" — and her answers are far more consistent than mine. I want to understand what principle is generating them, because the question is about to stop being theoretical for me.

Here is the record I have compiled so far.

First, a month after we moved in, I backed out of helping a neighbor move because a better invitation came up. L said it was wrong, and not because the neighbor was especially harmed: she said the maxim "I may break a promise whenever something better comes along" could not be willed as a universal law, since if everyone did that, promising itself would come apart.

Second, at the co-op's pay-what-you-can film nights, a well-paid couple in our building attends almost every week, eats from the shared food, and pays nothing. L says this is wrong. If everyone with their income did the same, the film night would no longer exist, and they are free-riding on the contributions of people who do pay.

Third, when a neighbor skipped his assigned turn on the compost roster because it was raining, L said it was wrong. If everyone skipped whenever the task was unpleasant, the roster would collapse and the work would fall on whoever felt most guilty.

Fourth, when a visitor parked in a guest spot without a permit, saying he would be gone "in ten minutes," L said it was wrong. If everyone helped themselves to guest parking for "just ten minutes," the distinction between guest and resident parking would lose its meaning.

Fifth, during the annual safety-clinic season, she said it was wrong for someone to mark the mandatory fire-safety module as completed without watching it. If everyone certified things they had not done, certification would cease to certify.

Sixth, last week she said it was wrong for a member to leave the communal wheelbarrow chained to her own plot overnight because she would need it early the next morning. If everyone kept shared tools where it suited them, the tool shed would no longer be a shared resource.

Now the part that matters to me. Next month I want to propose to L that, for the coming year, I not attend the co-op's monthly general meetings or vote in its elections. I will still pay my dues and do my chore shifts; I simply want to opt out of the governance side. My reason is honest: I have checked the meeting minutes, and in the last two years my single vote has never come close to deciding any question. L will almost certainly ask, "But what if everyone did that?" — and I genuinely cannot predict where she will land.

So my question is this. What is L likely to say about my proposal? I want more than a guess. I would like you to identify which principle or principles fit the six verdicts above — Kant's formula of universal law, R. M. Hare-style universal prescriptivism, Marcus Singer's generalization argument about the consequences of everyone's doing the same, a fair-play principle, or something else — and then say what each live candidate would predict about my abstention. After that, give me your best guess at her verdict, the reasons for preferring it, and one further case I could put to her that would best tell the surviving rules apart.
