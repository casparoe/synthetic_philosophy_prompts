I just had a long argument with a friend about Buchak-style risk-weighted expected utility, where the agent is represented by a risk function r(p) that weights probabilities without collapsing into expected utility, and the Allais preferences that are supposed to motivate it. His point was that the whole defense of REU as rational seems built for a single choice, or at least for an agent who can always revisit their own risk function. He asked me to consider instead an agent whose r(p) gets fitted once, from a batch of their choices, by some automated system that then acts on their behalf across a long sequence of independent gambles. The system can't keep asking how they feel, so it just maximizes REU with the stored r(p). By the end, the accumulated set of choices is worse by expected value, and also worse by the agent's own after-the-fact lights, than following a crude rule like "take the positive-expected-value gamble every time." I tried to say this is just the old dynamic consistency criticism in new clothes, but he pushed back: the new part is that the risk function stops being a living preference and becomes a fixed input to an optimization process, and the agent doesn't get to renegotiate it as the sequence unfolds. Does Buchak's argument that these risk-averse preferences are rationally permissible still survive that setup, or does the permissibility claim crack once the preferences are asked to be the stable operating manual for someone or something over time? I'm less interested in whether you'd personally accept REU and more interested in where exactly the argument gives out if it does, and what premise has to be strengthened to save it.
