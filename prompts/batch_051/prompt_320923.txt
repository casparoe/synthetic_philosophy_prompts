In my decision theory module on the value of information, we proved a result I later learned is called Good's theorem: for an agent who maximizes expected utility and updates by conditionalization, the expected value of acquiring costless information is never negative; roughly, it is strictly positive just when there is a possible signal that would lead the agent to act differently. The theorem has worked its way into my everyday intellectual habits, and I want to know whether the habit it inspired is defensible or whether I have dressed up closed-mindedness as a proof. My rule is this: before I spend time on a new study, argument, or piece of evidence about a question on which I already hold a settled view, I ask myself whether I can write down in advance at least one thing I might plausibly learn that would change my mind or change some concrete plan. If I cannot specify one, I refuse to engage with the material, even when it is free and easy to obtain. I justify this by saying that information that could not change anything is worth nothing, so skipping it saves time at no epistemic cost.

A friend has objected that Good's theorem concerns acts, not beliefs: my inability to state in advance what would move me may reflect a failure of imagination rather than the absence of any moving evidence, and refusing to look for that reason amounts to protecting a belief from possible correction. She also says beliefs can matter even when they do not currently feed into any decision, so the theorem cannot license a stopping rule for inquiry, but only for information-gathering tied to an action. I think she is at least partly right, but I also think that no stopping rule at all is unworkable for a student who cannot read everything. So I have two questions. Is my current rule rationally defensible as it stands? If not, what is the strongest defensible version of it, and should the criterion be formulated in terms of actions, credences, expected value, or something else? Please keep your answer to at most 600 words.
