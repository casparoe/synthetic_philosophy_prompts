I am a graduate student in an environmental-history seminar, and I am trying to predict how my professor will rule in our next session. She has spent the term training us to ask of some natural event — an epidemic, a climatic episode, or a volcanic eruption — whether it was a mere background condition, an intensifier of trends already set by human institutions, or a historical actor in its own right. She is not an environmental determinist, and she insists that an event can be massively destructive without being a historical actor if its effects were absorbed by existing structures, while a smaller event can qualify if it reorganized a society's norms or institutions.

Her rulings so far. (1) The Plague of Athens, 430–426 BCE: historical actor. Her touchstone is Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War, Book 2, section 53:

"Nor was this the only form of lawless extravagance which owed its origin to the plague. Men now coolly ventured on what they had formerly done in a corner, and not just as they pleased, seeing the rapid transitions produced by persons in prosperity suddenly dying and those who before had nothing succeeding to their property. So they resolved to spend quickly and enjoy themselves, regarding their lives and riches as alike things of a day. Perseverance in what men called honor was popular with none, it was so uncertain whether they would be spared to attain the object; but it was settled that present enjoyment, and all that contributed to it, was both honorable and useful. Fear of gods or law of man there was none to restrain them."

On her reading, the epidemic did not merely kill Athenians; it dissolved the fear of gods and law and rearranged what Athenians counted as honorable. (2) The 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora and the "Year Without a Summer" of 1816: historical actor, because the volcanic cooling caused multi-year harvest failures, food riots, and migration, and pressured governments into new famine-relief and grain policies. (3) The ash cloud from the 2010 Eyjafjallajökull eruption: not a historical actor; it grounded flights and cost airlines billions, but it was a short, sectoral disruption that transformed no norms, laws, or institutions and did not meaningfully constrain the material reproduction of any society.

The new case is the volcanic dust veil that began around 536 CE. Ice cores and tree rings indicate that one or more major eruptions dimmed the sun for well over a year and caused severe cooling across much of Eurasia; contemporary observers reported crop failure and unusual cold from Constantinople to Ireland to China. The physical shock was large, exogenous, and geographically vast, yet there is little evidence that it changed long-run political, religious, or institutional trajectories — societies seem mostly to have persisted through it.

Act as an expert in historical methodology. Think carefully before you answer. Finally, name one further case, real or invented, whose ruling would cleanly separate the competing rules. Do not end in pure agnosticism: commit to one predicted verdict.
