I'm giving a paper at a law school faculty workshop to an audience of roughly fifteen: mostly constitutional lawyers and doctrinal legal theorists, plus two public choice economists and one democratic theorist who works on the boundary problem of the demos. The thesis I'm defending is that constitutional entrenchment—supermajority amendment rules, justiciable rights, entrenched fiscal rules—should not be justified with the familiar Ulysses analogy, in which a sober present self binds a future self against predictable weakness of will. I argue that the analogy is positively misleading: a polity is not a single continuous agent whose earlier and later stages share one will, and entrenchment is better understood as a temporally extended planning practice by which a series of legislative cohorts coordinates on long-horizon commitments it could not sustain through ordinary, endlessly revisable legislation. The constitutional lawyers are comfortable with judicial review as doctrine but suspicious of abstract foundations; the public choice economists will treat every constitutional clause as delayed rent-seeking unless I give them a mechanism, not a metaphor; the democratic theorist will push on whether later citizens who never consented are bound by the plan.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet for the question period: the seven or eight objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they would do if I mishandled them, each phrased the way a particular member of this audience would phrase it (not how a philosopher or a general reader would), with a two-minute reply and a precise note on where that reply is soft. Tell me where I should concede and where holding firm will read as evasive. The room will split: some think the Ulysses analogy was doing real work and I have thrown it away, while others think no precommitment story can justify entrenchment at all. Cover both directions, and include the question I should be most afraid of—the one that will let a smart questioner separate the planning view from the old autonomy-based view in a way I may not be able to answer cleanly.
