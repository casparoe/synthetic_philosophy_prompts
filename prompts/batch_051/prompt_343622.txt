Student answer:

Searle's objection is that a bunch of individual intentions plus common knowledge is compatible with everyone merely acting in parallel, so the group level drops out. He therefore introduces a primitive we-intention: when we do something together, I intend to do my part as part of our doing that thing. Bratman avoids primitive we-intentions by analyzing shared intention as a web of ordinary individual attitudes. Each participant intends that we J, and each intends to do her own part in a way that meshes with everyone else's subplans, and all of this is common knowledge between them. Because the attitudes are all just I-intentions, his account is a genuine reduction of the we to the I. Gilbert goes the opposite direction. For her, shared intention is a joint commitment by the parties as a body, and once it exists each person owes the others her part and cannot unilaterally back out without their say-so. So the tradeoff is that Bratman captures the planning structure of joint action but leaves shared intentions normatively inert, while Gilbert captures the obligations but only by positing a joint commitment that does not reduce to the individual attitudes.
