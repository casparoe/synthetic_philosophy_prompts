Two friends of mine, Emma and Dev, got into it at a party about a shirt Emma bought from a brand whose supplier factory in Bangladesh pays workers about two dollars a day and has locked fire exits. She said she had looked into it and still bought it. The argument went something like this (maybe not verbatim, but close):

(1) Emma: The workers chose the job. It's lousy by our standards, but it beats their alternatives—scrubbing for less, or no income at all. If people like me stop buying, orders drop and they lose hours or get laid off. Me putting the shirt back on the rack doesn't make anyone better off.

(2) Dev: That's a false choice. The question isn't only "buy or don't buy"; it's whether the terms are fair. The factory could nearly double wages and the shirt would cost maybe thirty cents more. You're fine getting a cheap shirt because someone else is massively underpaid, and you're dressing it up as concern for the workers.

(3) Emma: Even if the wage is unfair, that's set by the market and the government, not by my purchase. And if a transaction leaves both sides better off than they'd otherwise be, I don't see how it can be wrong to do it. Refusing a deal that helps someone because the terms could be better seems perverse.

(4) Dev: "Better off than before" doesn't settle fairness. If someone's drowning and I offer to throw them a rope only if they sign over half their savings, the deal is mutually beneficial in exactly that sense—and it's still taking advantage of them. What makes it exploitation is that I'm using how bad their baseline is to extract terms I couldn't otherwise get.

(5) Emma: The drowning case is different because there's no third option—walking away doesn't affect the drowning person's baseline. Here the worker's alternatives are fixed by a huge economy. If I walk away, the worker might end up in something worse. So the real options are: transact on current terms while pushing for better regulation, or refuse and risk making them worse off. Calling the first one exploitation punishes workers for being poor.

(6) Dev: But "take this deal or you'll get something worse" is exactly the structure of extortion, except the background economy is doing the threatening instead of me. The employer's power comes from the same desperation you're pointing at. And if one shirt purchase changes nothing, then "I'll buy and push for regulation" lets every individual use the same excuse while nothing changes. The non-exploitative response might not be to stop buying, but you can't act like the transaction is clean.
