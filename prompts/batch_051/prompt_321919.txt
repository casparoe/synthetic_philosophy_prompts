Here is an asymmetry I keep running into, and I am unsure whether it is a real distinction or a conceptual dodge. A human assistant who confidently gives a client a plausible-sounding but false answer — say, a date, a legal detail, or a drug's side effects — is normally taken to have done one of three things: if they knew it was false, they lied; if they did not care enough to check, they bullshitted; if they checked and were mistaken, they made an error. Which one it was matters for blame, trust, and whether they should be retrained, disciplined, or fired.

A language model that does the same thing is almost always described as having "hallucinated," and the lie/bullshit/error question is dropped. We treat it as an engineering defect: the output is bad, but we do not ask what the output shows about the speaker's relation to the truth.

I want to know whether anything justifies this asymmetry. Is the difference between the two cases enough to make the lie/bullshit/error framework inapplicable to language models, or is "hallucination" doing work that the older vocabulary would do more honestly? In particular, Frankfurt's distinction between lying and bullshitting turns on the speaker's orientation toward truth: the liar is tracking the truth in order to conceal it, while the bullshitter is indifferent to how things stand. Does a language model — or the practice of presenting its outputs as answers in an assertive, testimonial register — have enough of an orientation toward truth for that distinction to apply? Or are confident LLM falsehoods a fourth category that the framework obscures?

Argue rather than survey. Find the strongest justification for the asymmetry, then test it against nearby cases: a tired human assistant pattern-matching without checking; a human trained always to produce a helpful-sounding answer and punished for saying "I don't know"; a corporate spokesperson reading a script they only half understand; a deliberately deceptive system that outputs known falsehoods to manipulate; a retrieval system that faithfully passes along a bad source. Say which cases the justification handles and which it does not, and then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned. Be explicit about what hangs on the answer — for instance, how an incident report should characterize the failure, what a user should be told about why the output was wrong, and where blame or liability should fall.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this material, and do not present a contested reading of Frankfurt or of LLM behavior as the consensus.
