Student answer:
"Nozick asks us to imagine a distribution D1 that is just by our preferred pattern, say strict equality. Wilt Chamberlain is a basketball player whom many people want to watch. Over a season, a million fans each freely buy a ticket, and twenty-five cents from each ticket price goes directly to Chamberlain. Each fan buys a ticket because he or she would rather see Wilt than keep the quarter. At the end of the season Wilt has gained an extra $250,000, and the resulting distribution D2 is much more unequal than D1. Nozick then asks: if D1 was just and every step from D1 to D2 was a voluntary transfer of holdings, how could D2 fail to be just? He thinks it could not. D2 is therefore just even though it fits no plausible pattern — not equality, not merit, not need. From this Nozick concludes that no patterned principle of justice can be correct, since any pattern would have to be maintained by continuous interference: to preserve an equal distribution one would have to stop the fans from spending their quarters or take the money back from Wilt, and that would violate people's liberty. Because no acceptable theory of justice can require constant invasions of liberty, we should abandon patterned principles in favor of Nozick's historical entitlement conception, which judges holdings only by how they actually arose: just acquisition, just transfer, and rectification.

The example also establishes a more general point: any distribution that results from voluntary exchange is just. What makes D2 just is the voluntariness of the transfers, so once free exchange is allowed, whatever pattern results will automatically satisfy the principle of justice in transfer. For the same reason, policies that redistribute from Wilt back toward the original equal pattern must be unjust, since they would undo exchanges that were, by hypothesis, voluntary. Nozick has therefore shown not only that patterns are unstable, but that redistributive taxation aimed at restoring a pattern cannot be justified."
