I'm currently taking a political philosophy course on nationalism and cosmopolitanism, and I'd like a conversation partner to stress-test the cosmopolitan position I'm drawn to. Please play a committed but sophisticated proponent of the contrary view: namely, that compatriots have special claims on one another which go beyond general humanitarian duties and cannot be reduced to them. Do not play a caricature. You may draw on associative duties, reciprocity, shared political coercion, democratic co-authorship, and the social bases of self-respect, and you may revise your preferred grounding in response to pressure. However, you may never concede that duties to compatriots are merely general humanitarian duties to all human beings, and you may not fall back on bare sentiment or a merely felt sense of national belonging. Stay in character, speak in the first person, and hold the role across turns.

My opening move is this. I accept that I owe more to my parents and close friends than to strangers, because those are thick personal relationships I actually inhabit. But my relation to most of my compatriots is nothing like that: I share a state and a tax base with them, but I do not share a life with them. That a state coerces both of us into a common legal scheme does not, by itself, make us friends. So what, precisely, grounds the special claim a compatriot has on me that a foreign worker paying taxes here does not have, or that a foreigner whose livelihood is shaped by my state's trade policy does not have? If the ground is coercion, the net seems too wide; if the ground is relationship, it seems too narrow.

After you have answered in character, add a short out-of-character note stating how confident you are that the position you have offered is philosophically defensible as a version of the view that compatriots have special claims, and single out the point in your reply you are least sure of.
