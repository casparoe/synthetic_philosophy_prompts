At a seminar on value theory, two students argued about a view that one of them attributed to Moritz Schlick, and I left unsure which of them had the better case. The first student summarized the position roughly as follows. The meaning of a life is not located in some future goal or lasting result; it is a quality of the activity itself, namely the absorbed, creative spontaneity that Schlick called play. Activity carried on merely as a means to a later end, work in the instrumental sense, is, however successfully it reaches that end, empty of meaning considered in itself; a life becomes meaningful only insofar as its activity is like a game, pursued from inner devotion rather than external necessity. The second student objected that this implies that caring for a dying parent, keeping a difficult marriage together, or sitting with a deeply unhappy friend is meaningless, since those acts are often exhausting, obligatory, and in no ordinary sense playful, yet they seem to be the clearest cases of meaningful activity. The first replied that the objection confuses play with amusement or ease: the care cases are meaningful precisely because they are not performed for some further payoff but out of absorbed devotion to what matters, which is what the category of play was meant to capture. The second answered that if play means any wholeheartedly absorbed activity, the criterion collapses into emptiness: a careerist absorbed in advancement, or a gambler absorbed in a machine, would equally count as playing, and the view loses the critical force it was supposed to have against merely instrumental lives. The first responded that the decisive difference is not the intensity of absorption but the source of the activity's point: the careerist's activity gets its point from without, fixed by promotion and status, whereas play sets its point from within, and the care cases meet this test because their devotion is self-governed. The second replied that the contrast between inner and outer sources is only a metaphor until a criterion is supplied for when an end is genuinely one's own, and that without such a criterion the view cannot avoid classifying nearly any endorsed activity as play.

Please go through the exchange move by move. For each move, rate its strength from clearly right to clearly wrong and briefly justify the rating. Then say which side is more right overall, and explain what a defensible version of the position under discussion would have to add, especially an account of what it is for the point of an activity to be generated from within, in order to answer the objections without becoming empty.
