For an upper-level undergraduate seminar on decision theory (about 15 students, 75 minutes), design a staged activity around Quinn's self-torturer in which students make a sequence of individually acceptable choices—each offering a small gain in exchange for an imperceptibly worse condition—until they reach a condition none of them would have accepted as a single leap. The activity should be arranged so that the idea arises from what students do before it is named: they should actually lock themselves into the pattern, and the debrief should map what happened onto the formal options (genuine intransitive preferences, an unnoticed threshold or scale-insensitivity, incomplete pairwise comparisons) and onto the money-pump argument. The intended takeaway is a felt grip on why the puzzle resists easy dissolution—why the advice to stop at some earlier point needs a justification the student cannot give without breaking the symmetry of adjacent steps—rather than a settled verdict. Specify the rules or roles, materials, a timeline, the debrief, an honest note on which feature of Quinn's original case the classroom version cannot reproduce, what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run and how to handle it, and a closing assessment of which consideration would change your design or your judgment that the activity succeeds.
