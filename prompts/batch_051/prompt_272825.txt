I am a doctoral student in the philosophy of language, and in thinking about how a singular term can refer to the same object across time I have begun to suspect that several familiar examples are being run together, and I want to know whether the running-together is itself a recognized position.

Consider three cases. A poker is cold at noon and hot at midnight; throughout, it is called the same poker, and yet the same object is said to have two incompatible temperatures. A ship has every plank replaced over a period of years, and the community continues to call it the same ship, despite a complete change of material parts. A statue and the lump of clay from which it is made occupy the same place at the same time, but they are distinguished because the clay would survive being flattened and the statue would not. In discussions of how things persist, I keep finding these three cases treated as if they were one problem with two candidate solutions: on one solution an object is wholly present at each time at which it exists, and on the other the object is a four-dimensional whole composed of distinct instantaneous parts.

My question is not merely for labels. Is there an established name for the thesis that these three cases are one underlying problem, or for the broader configuration of which all three are instances? If not, what would you name it, and how should the cases be carved relative to one another? And on the second question: how exactly does each of the two candidate solutions handle the statue-and-clay case, and what does each have to give up in doing so? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
