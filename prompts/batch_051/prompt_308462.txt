I am writing a short position statement on how value aggregates, both within a life and across lives, and I would like a consistency check before I circulate it. The question stopped being abstract for me this year, when I began helping a small foundation evaluate proposals: one intervention would spare a small number of nonhuman animals an intense but brief pain, while another would spread a mild improvement in comfort across an enormous number of animals over a long period, and I found I had no stable rule for trading those off against each other. In trying to write the rule down, I have settled on six commitments, each of which feels right to me on its own:

(1) Additivity across lives. If two lives go equally well, two such lives are exactly twice as good as one, and in general the value of a population is the sum of the values of the lives in it.

(2) Multiplication within a life. The value contributed by a stretch of experience is, very roughly, the product of two factors: how richly conscious the subject is during it, and how happy or unhappy it is. A barely experienced state contributes almost nothing even if it is mildly positive, because near-zero richness times modest happiness is near-zero.

(3) Lexical priority of extreme suffering. No amount of mild happiness, however widely distributed, can compensate for one life of unrelenting agony. Faced with a choice between preventing that single agonized life and producing a very large amount of mild happiness, I would prevent the agony.

(4) Smooth trade-offs among mild states. Apart from the extreme case in (3), mild goods and bads trade off in the ordinary additive way: enough mild happiness, spread across enough individuals, does outweigh a given amount of mild discomfort.

(5) Full moral weight for cognitively atypical humans. A person with advanced dementia or profound intellectual disability, whose experience is far less richly structured than a typical adult's, matters exactly as much as anyone else; I would not discount that person's happiness by a richness-of-consciousness factor.

(6) Resistance to the Repugnant Conclusion. I would not trade a world of several billion flourishing, fully conscious lives for any number of lives that are only barely worth living, and I regard the idea that quantity could swamp quality in that way as a reason to distrust whichever aggregative assumptions generate it.

Some of these feel as though they might conflict, but I cannot tell which conflicts are genuine, which are only apparent, and which single revision would cost me the least while preserving as much of the rest as possible. Please audit the set: identify the tensions, rank them by severity, say what would have to give in each case, and flag any pair that looks contradictory but, properly interpreted, is not.
