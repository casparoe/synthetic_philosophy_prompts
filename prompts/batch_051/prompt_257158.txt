I've slid into a Braudelian way of looking at history almost by accident — I was trying to explain why a small coastal town near me had declined, and kept getting pulled toward siltation, shifting trade routes, and the slow swings of the Medieval Warm Period, until the decisions of individual mayors and merchants started to look like noise. The position I'm now tempted by is something like this: the main movers of history are slow structures — geography, climate, population, the layout of seas and rivers, agricultural technique, trade corridors — and what historians call 'events' (battles, treaties, revolutions, charismatic leaders) are mostly foam on top of currents that were already running more or less where they went. Up close, individuals look decisive; from far enough out, even Napoleon starts to look like a wave on a tide.

Trouble is, I can't tell whether I've actually got a thesis or just a seductive zoom-out. Here's what I want: suppose you had to make the strongest case that my structural view is false, or at least over-extended. Don't give me a reading list. Give me the considerations that would count against it, ranked from frankly lethal down to merely awkward or patchable. Include at least a couple of things a committed advocate of the structural view would themselves admit as a serious internal problem, not just what the view's critics say. And be honest about whether the position, as I've stated it, risks anything at all — or whether it's been padded into something no historical outcome could discomfort — and whether that would be a problem or just the price of doing structural history. For what its worth, the thing that would shake me most is a single clean example where a short-term decision or accident (an assassination, a storm, a technological gamble) visibly bent a multi-century outcome away from where the deep structures were pointing.
