I recently came across the lottery paradox and the preface paradox while reading around, and I have been trying to get clear on them on my own. A friend with a background in philosophy offered me the following summary, which I wrote down and have been mulling over: "In a large, fair lottery, it is rational to believe of each individual ticket that it will lose, since each ticket is almost certainly a loser; but it is also rational to believe that some ticket will win, since the draw is guaranteed to produce a winner. The puzzle is that these two rational beliefs are flatly incompatible. The preface paradox is the same structure applied to a careful book: the author rationally believes each sentence she wrote, yet also rationally believes that at least one sentence in the book is false. What both paradoxes show is that a rational person can hold a straightforwardly contradictory set of beliefs, which means logic is at best a loose idealization in ordinary reasoning; and since high probability is not certainty, neither case leaves us justified in believing anything on merely probabilistic grounds."

I have a nagging feeling that this summary slides over some important distinctions, but I do not have the training to say exactly where. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a careful philosopher would accept, which parts are subtly off or overreaching, and whether the two final lessons about logic and probabilistic belief actually follow from the paradoxes? In particular, I would like to know whether the two paradoxes really do have the same structure and whether they really force the conclusion that rational people can hold contradictory beliefs.
