ok so my friend and I went back and forth about this for like three hours last night and I still can't decide. I'm in a small contemplative order, and our constitutions—mostly drafted in the 1600s but still binding—contain this rule:

"Let no brother make known to another, by speech, by writing, or by any signe or likenesse whatsoever, the things which it hath pleased God to shew unto him in secret prayer; for such things are a private gift, and not for the edifying of others."

One of our brothers is a former sound engineer, and last year he released a meditation app under a pseudonym. No words at all—no scripture, no guidance, just a slow sequence of tones, breath-pacing pulses, and haptic vibrations. He told me (and a couple of others) in private that every pattern in the app is a direct transposition of what he was shown in prayer, and that he made it so people could be led to the edge of the same unsayable thing without him having to turn it into a concept. The app is popular; users say they fall into a state they can't describe. It never mentions God, and nobody knows a monk made it.

So has he broken the rule? What does the plain text give here—does compiled code count as "writing," are the sound patterns a "likenesse" of something that has no sensory form? What does the purpose of the rule give? What would the 17th-century drafters have said if you showed them a glass box that hummed out the shape of a private revelation to strangers? Which approach should win, and does it matter that the app is anonymous and that no user could reconstruct what he actually saw? My friend kept insisting the ineffable can't be "made known" by nonconceptual means anyway, and i kept saying that's exactly what the tones are doing. I'm less interested in the practical verdict than in what the rule can reach here.
