so i've got this rule and i want to know whether it's actually defensible. whenever someone in an argument says "it's possible that X" but won't give me even a rough probability, i translate that in my head to "X is not ruled out," and then i treat it as carrying no information at all, because almost nothing is ruled out if you're creative enough. for purposes of what i'll actually do, i round that possibility down to zero. a friend says this is confused: there's a difference between a bare logical possibility (a meteor made of cheese hits my house) and a possibility that has some mechanism or base rate behind it even if no one can put a number on it. he says my rule makes me systematically blind to tail risks, since the scariest failures are usually exactly the ones where you can't give a probablity, and if you round those to zero you'll never prepare for anything genuinely new. i reply that without a number there's no way to compare two "possible" outcomes or decide which one deserves more weight, and almost any policy has some terrifying possible consequence, so taking all bare possibilities seriously means getting paralyzed or tricked. i apply the rule in everyday stuff—whether to move out of a flood zone, whether a new medication could have some weird reaction, whether a piece of software could fail in an unusual way—but i make an exception when the downside is death or ruin.

is this rule defensible, or is it just "i don't want to think about low-probability events" dressed up as a principle? is there a version of it that keeps the good parts without the blind spot? and should the death-or-ruin exception actually swallow the whole rule, since those are the cases people most need to think about?
