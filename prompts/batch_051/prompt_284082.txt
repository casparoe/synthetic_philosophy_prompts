Ok so I just spent like two hours arguing with a friend about conceptual engineering, and it left me with the skeleton of an essay I want to write. The essay is tentatively called "The Repair Metaphor in Conceptual Engineering" or something like that. I'd love comments on it before I write the real version—specifically on whether the argument actually works, not line-level stuff. I'll paste the notes below.

The idea: when people say we should "fix" a concept (race, marriage, mental disorder, whatever), the word does more than one thing. On one reading, "fix" just means alter or modify: we change the concept so it does what we want it to, the way you fix a recipe by adding salt. On another reading, "fix" means repair, like fixing a broken tool. I think the second reading is the interesting one, becuase it imports a whole functionalist picture: you repair a tool to fit its function, and the function determines whether you succeeded.

But tools don't have a single function. A hammer drives nails, cracks nuts, and makes a perfectly good paperweight. You can only say a hammer is broken once you've already decided what it's for. And with concepts it's worse, because what the concept is for is usually exactly what's in dispute. Take "woman" (I know, overused, but it's the cleanest case). The concept plausibly does classification work, explanatory work in the social sciences, and political work in organizing people around shared interests. An ameliorative analysis that says "we should define it to serve the political work" isn't reporting that the concept is broken—it's endorsing one of the functions over the others. The brokenness was not discovered, it was selected.

So the repair metaphor gives conceptual engineering a false air of objectivity. It lets someone say "I'm not changing the meaning because I prefer it; I'm fixing it becuase it's broken", while the standard of brokenness is the very thing being fought over.

My friend's counter was: if a concept is genuinely used for a purpose—like the state uses "marriage" to allocate legal benefits—then we can objectively say it's broken relative to that purpose. I think my reply is: fine, relative to a stipulated purpose, sure. But the interesting cases are exactly the ones where there's no shared purpose, and the metaphor does nothing to settle those. Relative-to-a-purpose brokenness is cheap; the metaphor only does work when it pretends the purpose isn't a choice.

Where I want the essay to go: maybe the metaphor isn't just a rhetorical trick. Maybe it captures something real, namely that many concepts have a point that their users already implicitly accept, and conceptual engineering is just making that latent point explicit before fixing the concept to match it. But then the hard question is how we identify the "real" function: by patterns of use, by what people say when pressed, by how the concept evolved? Each option smuggles in a different methodology.

So, comments I'd actually find useful: is the two-sense distinction (modify vs. repair) stable, or does every modify case turn out to be a repair case under some function? Does the hammer/multi-function argument actually damage the repair talk, or is it just a point about underdetermination? Is my reply to my friend's counter convincing, or does the "relative to a purpose" move salvage the metaphor? And does the ending work—can the "latent function" defense be made rigorous, or should I cut it?
