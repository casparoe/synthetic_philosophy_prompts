You are a political philosopher specializing in theories of collective responsibility and transitional justice. I want you to work through a careful case analysis rather than produce a general survey.

Karl Jaspers, writing in the immediate aftermath of the Second World War, distinguished several registers of guilt in connection with the crimes of the Nazi period. One common but simplified reconstruction goes something like this: criminal guilt attaches to individuals for acts that violate law and is the proper business of courts; political guilt attaches to citizens of a state for the deeds of that state and is the responsibility of the political community, sometimes judged by victors or outside powers; moral guilt attaches to individuals for wrongs they personally commit or fail to resist and is a matter for conscience; and metaphysical guilt attaches to anyone who remained alive while others were murdered even though they did not participate, and it has no proper human tribunal. The last category in particular has always been controversial.

I want you to analyze the post-1945 treatment of ordinary German citizens by the Allied occupation authorities and the early West German state through the lens of this framework: the denazification questionnaires, the early war crimes trials, the widespread postwar attitude that many Germans saw themselves as victims, and the gradual shift in public language from collective guilt toward collective shame. Using Jaspers’s distinctions, give me an analysis that runs in both directions. First, what would the framework prescribe or predict about who was entitled to judge whom, which register of guilt applied to different groups of Germans, and why the boundary between political and moral guilt mattered for assessing denazification? Second, where does the episode fit the framework naturally, and where does it strain it—for example, in the position of Germans who were children at the time, ethnic German refugees, or people who benefited from confiscated property without being direct perpetrators, or in the way public discourse slid from guilt to shame? Finally, what does this episode in turn reveal about the theory: does it expose a missing category, an unstable boundary between registers, or a practical advantage in keeping the registers apart?

Please think carefully and begin with a brief brainstorming sketch of possible mappings before committing to your full analysis. Do not treat my reconstruction of Jaspers as authoritative if you know a more precise version of his distinctions, but keep the emphasis on the conceptual mapping rather than on exegetical correctness. And be explicit about where you think the framework is silent rather than forcing it to answer.
