I've been going round in circles on an arguement about what, if anything, adult children owe their parents, and I'd like a second pair of eyes on it. The reasoning runs like this, more or less as I've assembled it.

(1) Whatever I now owe my parents, as an adult, must be grounded in one of three places: repayment of what they gave me, gratitude for it, or the relationship we actually have now.

(2) The repayment story seems hopeless. A debt is something you incur by accepting a benefit on terms, but part of the point is that I never accepted terms: nobody asked me wether I wanted the packed lunches, the lifts, the school fees or the years of worry. You also cannot price any of it, so the word owing starts to do metaphorical work and then gets treated as if it were literal.

(3) Gratitude does no better. Gratitude may be owed, but it is an attitude, not a service schedule: it doesn't settle which act, how much, or for how long. Worse, in the cases of genuinely cold or selfish parents, gratitude seems to lapse, yet many people still hold that the child owes something there too. So gratitude explains neither the precision nor the stubbornness of what is claimed.

(4) By elimination, then, we are pushed to the third option: whatever is owed floats on the quality of the present relationship. The friendship model, roughly.

(5) But on that model the duties are the duties of a friendship you would freely be in: mutual, ongoing, sustained by affection, allowed to lapse when the affection lapses.

(6) The trouble is that the contested cases are exactly the ones where there is no present adult bond the child would voluntarily sustain. A parent can be non-abusive, can have done a perfectly ordinary, decent job, and still now feel like an emotional tax: daily phone calls, little criticisms, the same stories, the same demands. I would not choose this person as a friend today. Yet the stubborn intuition is that I owe them something—the occasional visit, not cutting them off, help when they're ill.

(7) On friendship-model duties I owe no such thing, because the current relationship doesn't warrant it. So in precisely the cases people argue about, all three candidate grounds fail, and the conclusion is that a decent-but-difficult parent has no more claim on me than a stranger. That can't be right.

So: where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep (2) and the closing thought that a decent parent is owed something more than a stranger. I don't know which premise to sacrifice, and I want to know what each possible fix costs. Could you respond in British English, with four sections in this order: Diagnosis (about 250 words), Why the tempting steps tempt (about 150 words), What each fix costs (about 250 words), and Bottom line (about 100 words).
