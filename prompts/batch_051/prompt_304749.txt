Consider this argument against expressivism:

(1) Expressivism says that the meaning of a moral sentence is the attitude it serves to express; "Lying is wrong" expresses a negative attitude toward lying.

(2) But in the conditional "If lying is wrong, then getting one's little brother to lie is wrong," the embedded clause "lying is wrong" does not express a negative attitude toward lying: a speaker can sincerely accept the entire conditional while neither disapproving of lying nor feeling any inclination to disapprove of it.

(3) Therefore "lying is wrong" differs in meaning between its embedded occurrence in the conditional and its free-standing occurrence as a premise.

(4) A modus ponens inference—"If lying is wrong, then getting one's little brother to lie is wrong; lying is wrong; therefore getting one's little brother to lie is wrong"—is valid only if the antecedent of the conditional and the minor premise have the same content. By (3), they do not.

(5) So expressivism cannot account for the validity of ordinary moral modus ponens.

Is this argument valid? If not, identify the exact step or transition at which it goes wrong, and explain why. If it is valid, say whether it is sound; if it is unsound, identify the false premise and give the strongest expressivist reply to it.
