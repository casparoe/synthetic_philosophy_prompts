I work as a software engineer, with no formal background in philosophy or the history of science. I've recently become interested in how credit gets assigned for ideas, partly because I see the same pattern in open-source software: the person who opens the GitHub issue often gets no credit, while the person who writes the merge request becomes the name everyone remembers. A colleague pointed me to Stephen Stigler's 1980 paper "Stigler's Law of Eponymy" in *Transactions of the New York Academy of Sciences*, and I found this passage in it:

I have chosen as a title for this paper, and for the thesis I wish to present and discuss, "Stigler's law of eponymy". At first glance this may appear to be a flagrant violation of the "Institutional Norm of Humility", and since statisticians are even more aware of the importance of norms than are members of other disciplines, I hasten to add a humble disclaimer. If there is an idea in this paper that is not at least implicit in Merton's *The Sociology of Science*, it is either a happy accident or a likely error. Rather I have, in the Mertonian tradition of the self-confirming hypothesis, attempted to frame the self-proving theorem. For "Stigler's Law of Eponymy" in its simplest form is this: "No scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer."

After reading that, I found a blog post that summarized the paper like this:

Stigler's law of eponymy says that every scientific discovery is named after someone who didn't actually discover it. Stigler argues that this happens because scientists systematically award eponyms to the person with the highest status in the field, regardless of who actually did the work. He proves this by compiling a long list of examples—Pythagoras did not first state the Pythagorean theorem, Halley did not first observe Halley's comet, Hubble did not first infer the expanding universe—so the pattern is not merely anecdotal; it is a genuine empirical regularity. The moral is clear: eponymy is a broken credit system. When a result is named after someone, that person usually deserves little or no credit for the underlying discovery. Stigler himself, with unusual honesty, admitted that his own law had actually been stated before him by Robert K. Merton. This self-referential twist is often taken as the clearest proof that the law is true: even the law about misattribution is itself misattributed.
