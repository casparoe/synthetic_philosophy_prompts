Retired statistical physicist here, so I've spent decades treating ensemble averages and time averages as interchangeble. Since getting into philosophy I've been reading the ergodicity-economics crowd — the ones who say that in multiplicative repeated gambles you should maximize time-average growth (a geometric sort of quantity) rather than expected value, because once a process is non-ergodic the two come apart. I keep meeting people who cite one toy gamble — a fair coin that multiplies your wealth by 1.5 or 0.6, say — and act like it settles things either way. Before I go further, write me the spec sheet: what does an adequate theory of choice under repeated risk have to deliver, once you grant that ensemble averages and time averages can diverge? For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it — multiplicative vs additive gambles, absorbing boundaries like ruin, finite vs infinite horizon, the difference between one agent over time and many parallel copies at one time, the cases where the two averages still coincide, whatever else matters. Then tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just a camp's taste, whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect expected-value maximization and time-average maximization can't both be mandatory — and which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the standard expected-utility story. No scorecard of named theories; I want the criteria themselves. At the end, say how confident you are in the list as a whole and single out the item you're least sure of.
