I have been going back and forth with a colleague about the absent-minded driver problem, and we have reached the point where it would help to have someone force the issue by assigning probabilities rather than restating the paradox. The case, as we are using it, is this. An agent is driving home on a road with two exits. He cannot distinguish the exits while driving, though he knows the structure of the road and the payoffs. If he takes the first exit, he gets 0. If he takes the second, he gets 4. If he drives past both, he gets 1. Before setting out, he can choose a randomized plan, and the plan that maximizes expected payoff from the planning perspective is to take each indistinguishable exit with probability one third, for an expected payoff of four thirds. But once he is at an exit, he can reason differently: taking this exit now yields 0 with certainty, whereas continuing yields some conditional expected value that depends on the probability he assigns to being at the first rather than the second exit, given his own planned strategy. My colleague takes this to show that the ex ante optimal plan is not authoritative at the time of action. I am not convinced.

I want your actual credence that the planning-stage optimum — exit at each indistinguishable exit with probability one third — is the correct rational strategy for the agent at the driving stage, and that the action-stage reasoning that seems to undermine it rests on a mistake. Please give me a number, but more importantly decompose the question. I would like at least these components: the probability that each major decision-theoretic framework you take seriously — causal, evidential, functional, or whatever else — is the correct one for this case; the probability, conditional on each such framework being correct, that it yields the one-third planning strategy rather than some other strategy once self-locating uncertainty is included; the probability that dynamic consistency is a genuine normative principle that binds an agent to his earlier plan even when his later self faces indistinguishable choices; and the probability that the payoff structure here really creates a self-locating belief problem rather than a merely formal indeterminacy. Assign a number to each component with a one-line justification, show how they combine to your overall credence, and then tell me which component you are least confident in and what evidence or argument would shift it most.
