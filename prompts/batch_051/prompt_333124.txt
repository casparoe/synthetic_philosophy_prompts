I keep having the same arguement with a friend who works in tech ethics. She says that when a large language model invents a case name or a fake reference, we should call it bullshit rather than hallucination or error, and she defends this with an analogy: the model is like a student who hasn't read the book, sat in the exam, and is blagging an essay in plausible-sounding paragraphs, completely indifferent to whether any particular sentence is true. The student isn't lying, because a liar has to know the truth well enough to steer around it; and they aren't exactly making a mistake either, because a mistake is trying and failing at truthfulness. They've simply stopped caring which way the facts point. She thinks the model is in basically the same situation, and that the analogy does real work: it pushes us away from "hallucination" (too medical, lets developers off), away from "lying" (too agent-y), and toward the attitude we should actually have about the output. I half buy it.

Can you audit the analogy properly? I'd like a correspondence table first — the student, the unread book, the essay, the examiner, the desire to impress, the indifference to truth — mapped onto the model, the training data, the generation process, the user, RLHF, and whatever the closest analogue of the student's indifference to truth is in a system that may not have beliefs at all. Then I want the disanalogies, especialy those around the fact that the student has a concept of truth they are setting aside while the model doesn't, and around what each is actually optimising for. Finally tell me whether her conclusion — that bullshit is the right category for this behaviour, and that using it would meaningfully change how we talk about model output and responsibility — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, no preamble, no restating of the question.
