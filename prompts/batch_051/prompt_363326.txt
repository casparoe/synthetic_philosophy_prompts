I am a novelist, and in both my own prose and the fiction I read I keep noticing a peculiar feature of certain sentences, though I have never known the technical vocabulary for it. The very same words—"I promise," "I bet you fifty dollars," "I hereby open this meeting"—sometimes appear to do something in the world: they create a promise, a wager, an opened proceeding. At other times the identical words, clearly understood and not at all false, accomplish nothing; they simply fail. The difference seems to lie not in the content but in the circumstances: whether the speaker is in a position to do that act, whether the occasion is of the right kind, whether the addressee is present and accepting, whether the words are merely quoted or rehearsed, whether they are spoken in a play, played back in a recording after the speaker's death, or uttered by a character who is himself fictional. I can point at each of these failures individually, but I lack an account of what unites them. Is there an established term for this class of utterance, and for the specific ways it can fail or be hollowed out? A bare label would not satisfy me; what I need is the anatomy—what must be in place for such a sentence to take effect, how the different failures differ in kind, and what to say about the boundary cases in which it seems to work only in a shifted sense: a promise made inside a story, a recorded vow played after the one who made it is gone, an oath sworn on the wrong authority and yet accepted by everyone assembled. Are those failures, fictions, or something further?
