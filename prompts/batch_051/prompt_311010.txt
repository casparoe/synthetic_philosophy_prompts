Question: Explain Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge, how it responds to the brain-in-a-vat skeptic, and whether the theory has to give up closure under known entailment. If it does, is that a serious cost?

Student answer: Nozick says S knows that p iff p is true, S believes that p, and two counterfactual conditions hold: if p were false, S would not believe that p, and if p were true, S would believe that p. The first condition is sensitivity and the second is adherence. Sensitivity is not closed under known entailment. For example, I can know I have hands, because in the nearest worlds where I don't have hands, roughly the ones where I've lost them in an accident, I wouldn't still believe I have them. But I cannot know I'm not a brain in a vat being fed experiences of having hands, because in the nearest worlds where I am such a brain in a vat, I would still believe I'm not one. So the tracking theory denies closure under known entailment, and this is exactly how it answers the skeptic: the skeptic's argument assumes closure, and Nozick says closure fails. The weird result is that I can know I have hands while not knowing I'm not a BIV, even though I know the first entails the second. Nozick just accepts that bullet. I think the cost is real but maybe worth paying if you want an externalist theory. The reason tracking theory is often classified as a safety theory is that it says that in every nearby world where S believes p, p is true. So the real debate is whether giving up closure is worth it to keep that safety requirement.
