I organize a monthly lunchtime discussion group at my company for software engineers who want to think through the conceptual questions underneath their work. I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. The next session has 14 people signed up, all working engineers, with attendance typically running between 10 and 18, and we have 75 minutes. I don't want a lecture; I want a runnable activity that makes the group feel one specific idea before it is named. The idea is this: for some well-defined problems there is a structural asymmetry between how hard it is to find a solution and how easy it is to check one, and that asymmetry puts pressure on idealized pictures of reasoning and decision-making in which an agent simply derives the right answer or picks the action that maximizes some objective. I want the debrief to land on a sharper claim than the usual slogan that P versus NP is somehow about creativity: the cost of search is not an engineering detail that a model of an ideal reasoner may ignore, and once you idealize it away you have stopped talking about the property that makes finding different from checking. A reassurance that a problem is solvable in principle can turn out to be nearly empty, and I want the group to feel why rather than be told why.

Please design the session around the subset sum problem. Give me the specific small lists of integers and targets to print, with the instances chosen so that verifying a claimed subset is genuinely trivial while finding a subset by search is genuinely frustrating. Structure it as a game with two roles or two phases: some participants try to find a subset that sums to a target, while others are handed a claimed subset and asked to verify it. The terms subset sum, P, NP, and NP-complete should not appear until the debrief. Include clear rules, printable materials, a token or points scheme that does not use real money, and a full 75-minute timeline. The debrief should first draw out what the participants noticed in the room, then map specific moments onto the idea: the stalled search, the instant verification, the false impression that the problem should be easy because all the information is sitting on the page, and the way the effect changes with scale. End the debrief on the sharp claim above and explain how it goes beyond the familiar point that hard problems are hard for people.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when this is run, such as someone finding the subset immediately, verifiers finishing early and drifting into the searchers' task, or participants concluding that the exercise is merely about human slowness. For each failure mode, explain how to handle it. Then be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: I suspect the small instances that work in a room are all brute-forceable with enough time, so the activity cannot literally demonstrate intractability and can only make the scaling claim vivid. If I'm right about that, say what the facilitator must supply in the debrief to close the gap; if you think a classroom-scale instance can be made genuinely hard, say that instead.

Finally, wherever the design or debrief asserts something as fact, whether about the complexity-theoretic status of subset sum, about what the exercise will make participants feel, or about how the debrief will land, say how confident you are in that assertion and single out the one point you are least sure of.
