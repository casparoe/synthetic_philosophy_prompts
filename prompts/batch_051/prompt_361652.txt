Here is an argument I constructed for a discussion group on temperance, and it ends somewhere I refuse to accept.

(1) Self-control is exercised only where considered judgment conflicts with a recalcitrant desire and judgment wins.
(2) The more powerful the recalcitrant desire successfully resisted, the more self-control is displayed.
(3) So becoming more self-controlled requires having, or actively producing, strong recalcitrant desires for self-control to work on.
(4) Temperance, by contrast, just is the condition in which desire has been so trained by habit that it no longer conflicts with judgment; the fully temperate person has nothing recalcitrant left to resist.
(5) Therefore progress in temperance progressively eliminates the conditions under which self-control can be exercised, and pursuing one ideal systematically undercuts the other.
(6) So self-control and temperance cannot be joint ideals of a single character.

I want to keep (1) and (2), since they seem to be what distinguishes winning against a severe craving from simply never having the craving. But then (3) through (6) follow, and (6) is absurd. Which step is the culprit, and what does rejecting it cost? If the chain is valid, which premise is cheapest to give up, and how must the retained concepts then be redrawn so that self-control does not collapse into mere inner conflict and temperance does not collapse into mere absence of temptation?
