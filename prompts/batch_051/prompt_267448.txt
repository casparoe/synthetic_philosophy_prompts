You are a philosopher of mathematics with a specialty in the foundations of arithmetic and ancient Greek mathematics. Work slowly; begin by brainstorming at least three candidate definitions of “unit,” note their failures, and then refine the most promising one.

I work on the item master and inventory schemas for a pharmaceutical distributor, and I have hit a practical problem. The field “unit” means several incompatible things across our systems: sometimes a count, sometimes a standard of measure, sometimes a package quantity, sometimes a synonym for “one.” Recently I started reading Euclid’s treatment of number in Book VII of the *Elements* to see whether the historical source of the concept gives me a usable criterion. In the translation at euclids-elements.org, Euclid, *Elements*, Book VII, Proposition 1, begins:

“When two unequal numbers are set out, and the less is continually subtracted in turn from the greater, if the number which is left never measures the one before it until a unit is left, then the original numbers are relatively prime.”

The passage presupposes that the reader knows when a “unit” is left. But what exactly is a unit? In the definitions of that same book Euclid contrasts a unit with number: a unit is, roughly, that by which something is called one, while a number is a multitude of units. The unit seems to be the indivisible stopping point of subtraction, not itself a number, yet modern usage constantly treats composite things as units.

I would like a working definition of “unit” that handles the following cases, or at least gives a definite and defensible verdict for each:

- one apple, as a whole fruit
- one bite of an apple
- one kilogram, as a standard of mass
- a 0.5 kg portion of a kilogram standard
- the number 1 in a system that treats it as a number
- one mole of a chemical substance
- a package containing six bottles sold as one shipping “unit”
- a continuous liter of water, with no discrete container
- a point in Euclidean geometry
- the empty set, considered as zero
- one person in a census record
- a single bit in a digital transmission

Then test your definition against the Euclid passage above: in the subtraction algorithm, is the leftover unit the same kind of thing as the numbers being subtracted, or something different? If your definition classifies the unit as a number, explain how you would deal with Euclid’s apparent exclusion of the unit from number. Finally, identify which of your verdicts you are least confident about, and say whether the concept “unit” is doing one job or two.
