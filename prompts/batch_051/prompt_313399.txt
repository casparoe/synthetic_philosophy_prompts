I'm prepping for a criminal law exam and I keep getting stuck on the void-for-vagueness doctrine instead of just memorizing it. The standard story is that a statute can be struck down as vague because it fails to give ordinary people fair notice of what's prohibited, and because vague language hands too much arbitrary power to cops and prosecutors. Both of those arguments seem to assume a pretty low-tech picture: a person reading the statute text (or hiring a lawyer if they're rich enough), and a human officer deciding on the spot whether the conduct counts.

So here's what's bugging me. Imagine, ten years out, that near-universal, cheap LLM legal assistants are good enough that ordinary people routinely ask "is this illegal?" before doing anything borderline and get a reasonable read of how courts actually apply the statute. And at the same time, suppose more policing gets automated: a department takes a vague ordinance and turns it into fixed code, so that enforcement is perfectly uniform across officers, in the sense that everyone who gets flagged gets flagged by the same algorithm. In that world, does the void-for-vagueness argument survive? Which of its two rationales is actually under pressure, and is there a repaired version that does the same work—maybe worrying less about whether the statute text is clear and more about whether the interpretive layer (the model, its training data, the code) is transparent and can be challenged? I'm less interested in what current doctrine would say than in whether the underlying argument still holds up or quietly dies.
