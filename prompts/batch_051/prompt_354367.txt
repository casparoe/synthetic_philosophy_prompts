I am a novelist, and for the past year I have been experimenting with a writing process meant to extend my own judgment across a long manuscript. I write a chapter myself, then give that chapter and my outline to a careful assistant, who expands the outline into a dozen candidate scenes. I annotate and rank the candidates, then condense my annotations into a one-page memo about the novel's aims, voice, and prohibitions. I hand only that memo — never my draft, never the annotations — to a faster, cheaper assistant, who writes the next chapter from it. Each new chapter begins the cycle again: candidates, annotations, a tighter memo, a faster assistant. By chapter ten, the prose is polished and follows the memo almost perfectly, but it no longer seems to be doing what the first two chapters were doing. It is competent and hollow in a way I find hard to locate. My question is whether the drift is produced by the structure itself rather than by my execution: does compressing my reactions into a memo that a new assistant must follow necessarily discard the very judgment the memo is meant to carry forward, and can a sufficiently careful memo preserve both the quality and the fidelity of the work over an indefinite series of such cycles — or should I abandon the method before the manuscript moves further from the book I intended to write?
