Hey, I'm a PhD student working on the interpretability side of AI alignment — specifically on what you can and can't infer about a learned optimizer from its behavior — and there's a tangle I'd like to get clearer on before I try to teach it to my reading group. Everyone talks about inferring the objective or reward function (inverse RL, revealed preferences, the usual stuff). But I've started to think the more interesting underdetermination is about the search space, not just the objective. If all you get are trajectories — states, actions, maybe losses — you're seeing the realized behavior, not the set of alternatives the system was actually searching over. A lookahead planner and a cached reactive policy could emit exactly the same action sequence on a particular distribution, but what they are optimizing over differs enormously (plans vs. low-level actions vs. latent options vs. weight updates). And even the objective part is only "solved" in a deflationary way: for a finite set of behavior there's always some boring reward function that makes it look optimal, so what would license saying you recovered the real objective rather than one rationalization among many?

What I want is a handout laying out the distinct conceptual landmines someone should have in mind when trying to infer both the objective and the search space from behavior alone. I'm less interested in the math — we already know the standard ambiguity results — and more in the concepts: what gets conflated, where the underdetermination bites hardest, and why each point matters if you want to say something true about the system rather than something merely consistent with it's behavior. Could you give me exactly five such issues or distinctions? Make it a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion.
