Before I read any more proposals about the welfare of farmed fish and crustaceans, I want the specification that any adequate theory of their welfare must meet. By a theory of welfare I mean either an account of what their welfare consists in — experiential, preference-based, capability-based, or otherwise — or an account of how welfare should be measured and traded off in husbandry, transport, and slaughter decisions. The purpose is practical: the theory should be usable in cage systems, ponds, hatcheries, and processing, under real commercial constraints, not only in ideal theory. Do not spend space on citations, named studies, or bibliographies; the substance is in the reasoning for each requirement.

Organize the reply in the following sections, in this order.

1. The cases it must handle (about 400 words). Set out at least the following cases and say what each forces a theory to say or deny: the intensively stocked grow-out unit (a salmon net pen or a shrimp pond at commercial density); slaughter with and without prior stunning, which raises the question whether a humane death and a humane life are separate requirements or can substitute for one another; the transported young salmon moved from freshwater hatchery to a seawater net pen, or the transported juvenile shrimp stocked into a pond, where handling, confinement, and physiological stress overlap; eyestalk ablation in shrimp hatcheries, where a female's eyestalk is removed to induce reproduction; a disease outbreak in a pen in which feeding and growth continue roughly as usual; and the wild-baseline comparison, which contrasts a farmed life with the life the animal might have had in the wild against contrasting it with a feasible better farmed life. For each case, state what a theory that stayed silent about it would have failed to do.

2. Distinctions it cannot collapse (about 350 words). Give the distinctions an adequate theory must respect, with the practical error that follows from collapsing each. At minimum: nociception versus affective pain — registering and avoiding a noxious stimulus does not by itself establish an aversive state; welfare versus productivity — growth, feed conversion, and survival can all be acceptable while welfare is poor; individual-level versus population-level welfare — the aggregation problem raised by shrimp, in which very large numbers of small, short-lived crustaceans cannot simply be run through a metric calibrated on larger, longer-lived fish; sentience versus welfare — the question whether a being can have welfare-relevant interests without conscious feeling, and why a theory may not simply assume the two terms pick out the same thing; and acute versus chronic welfare, with death treated as a separate item rather than as the extreme end of a single scale of suffering.

3. Non-negotiable versus negotiable requirements (about 300 words). Mark which requirements any adequate theory must satisfy and which reflect the preferences of particular camps. I take the non-negotiable core to include at least the following, but argue for or revise it: it must not define welfare as growth rate or production; it must specify at least one operational indicator of welfare that could in principle conflict with a producer's output data; it must cover both farmed fish and farmed crustaceans or give a principled reason for drawing the line elsewhere; and it must say how the theory would track the evidence if findings on nociception, avoidance learning, and responses to pain-relieving drugs in these taxa went one way rather than the other. Treat as negotiable, and say why: whether welfare is ultimately experiential, preference-based, or capability-based; how much weight to give death versus chronic deprivation; whether the wild life is the right comparison; and how much weight to give commercial feasibility.

4. Tensions and possible joint unsatisfiability (about 250 words). Identify the pairs or subsets of desiderata that may not be jointly satisfiable. Consider at least these candidates: a single uniform welfare metric across a salmon and a juvenile shrimp versus respect for neurobiological and life-history differences; operational measurability versus defining welfare partly in terms of unobservable affective states; and species-typical behavioral opportunity at scale versus the stocking densities that current production systems can afford. For each tension, say which requirement should give way in a practical setting and what the theory loses when it does.

5. Scorecard (about 250 words). Briefly score four families of view against the list: experiential or hedonistic theories; preference or aversion-based theories; capability or natural-behavior theories; and production-metrics views that treat health, growth, and reproduction as the content of welfare. State which single requirement eliminates the production-metrics view fastest, which family comes closest to satisfying the list, and where it still falls short. Do not let the scorecard take over; the criteria should remain the center of the reply.
