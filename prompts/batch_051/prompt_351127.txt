Analyze China's one-child policy through the lens of population axiology, with the repugnant conclusion as the organizing problem. In force from roughly 1980 until its relaxation in the mid-2010s, the policy legally restricted most couples to one child, was officially defended on the ground that slower population growth would raise per-capita resources and welfare, and was enforced with considerable coercion; it is now associated with a skewed sex ratio and rapid population ageing. Model the policy as a choice among population states of different sizes and average welfare levels. What would total utilitarianism, average utilitarianism, and a person-affecting view each have recommended, and how much do those recommendations shift as the boundary of a life worth living is drawn more or less conservatively? Then run the analysis in reverse: which axiology, if any, was actually doing the work in the official reasoning, and which considerations — coercive enforcement, individual rights, the distribution of burdens, the gendered consequences — does population axiology have to import from outside itself even to represent? Does the episode make the repugnant conclusion look more like a live practical hazard or more like a theoretical curiosity? Be explicit about where the theory explains the policy and where it merely redescribes it.
