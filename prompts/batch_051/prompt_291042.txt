ive been kicking around a distinction-slash-theory i call 'borrowed sacrilege,' or sometimes 'the third-party sacred.' the usual line is that blasphemy is insulting the divine in speech, and sacrilege is mistreating a holy object or place. i think a huge amount of what gets called offence against the sacred, especially in secular societies, is neither. the pattern is: person A does something to an object that person B holds sacred, and person C—who does not hold it sacred—is the one who feels violated. and C isn't just feeling bad on B's behalf. C is offended on their own behalf, at the treatment of the thing itself, even if no believer is around to be hurt.

my go-to example: an atheist friend of mine was genuinely shaken by a video of someone destroying a Qur'an, and when i pressed him, he said it wasn't that he was worried about Muslims' feelings and it wasn't that he thought the book had any special properties. something about the act itself felt like a violation. i think the book had gone 'borrowed-sacred' for him: he was running the emotional grammer of the sacred—set apart, not to be used as raw material, damage marks you as low—without the metaphysics underneath. the same shape shows up when the Taliban blew up giant carved Buddhas, or when people who never set foot in a church are genuinely disturbed by a porn shoot in a cathedral.

the actual claim is threefold. (1) borrowed sacrality is a real and stable state, not just social performance or virtue-signalling. (2) it can't be reduced to empathy with believers or to ordinary disgust at cruelty, because it tracks the object rather than the audience. (3) most of what modern law and culture-war discourse call blasphemy is really this third thing, which is why the old blasphemy/sacrilege distinction keeps misfiring—neither term has a slot for an offence committed with no believing victim in the room.

so: is this a real thing or am i just renaming 'respect for other cultures'? has someone already worked it out under another name? and where does it break—what does it wrongly count as a wrong, or what genuine differences between cases does it flatten? i can already feel the obvious objection, that if nearly anything can go borrowed-sacred then ive just invented a fancier word for 'things people care about a lot.' i dont think that's right, but i keep using the category and it keeps feeling true, which is exactly when i stop trusting myself.
