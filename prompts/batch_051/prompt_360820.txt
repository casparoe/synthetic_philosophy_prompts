Suppose a common-law country writes a formal version of the old desuetude principle into its constitution: every five years, the justice ministry publishes a registry of criminal offences that have gone a full decade without a single charge or conviction, and twelve months after an offence appears on the registry it lapses and can no longer be prosecuted unless the legislature votes by simple majority, on the record, to re-authorise it for another decade. Walk me through how this actually behaves. What does it incentivise for prosecutors, police, legislators, and defence lawyers? Where does it get gamed first — token prosecutions to keep a dormant law alive, quiet re-authorisation of nasty old laws, manufactured charges just before the clock runs out? And what does it do to the usual rule-of-law worry about laws that are never enforced except when convenient: does it reduce the arbitrariness or just move it somewhere else? Once you've reached a verdict on whether the scheme is a good idea, tell me what would change your mind — which argument would push you the other way, and where your least sure your analysis might be wrong.
