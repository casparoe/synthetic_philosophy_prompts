I am an undergraduate in a philosophy seminar on forgiveness this term, and I have to write a paper taking a position on two questions we keep circling: whether forgiveness can ever be obligatory, and whether it can properly be unconditional. In our discussions, one analogy has been doing a great deal of work, and I want to know how much weight it can actually bear before I either rely on it or reject it. The analogy is that forgiving a wrongdoer is in all essential respects like a creditor cancelling a debt owed to them.

The seminar participants who press this analogy use it to support three conclusions. First, only the wronged party can forgive, because only the creditor is in a position to release the debtor. Second, forgiveness cannot be obligatory, because no one can be morally required to cancel a debt owed to them. Third, forgiveness can be unconditional, because a creditor may simply tear up the note without waiting for repayment, apology, or any other condition.

I would like you to audit this analogy rather than simply endorse or dismiss it. Begin with an explicit correspondence table: what is the creditor, the debtor, the debt itself, the repayment, and the act of cancellation in the case of forgiveness? Then identify the respects in which forgiveness differs from debt cancellation, and distinguish the disanalogies that threaten the three conclusions above from those that are merely incidental. In particular, address what the economic picture quietly imports: that wrongdoing creates a private bilateral liability, that the wronged party owns the claim and can extinguish it unilaterally, that what the wrongdoer owes is something whose release costs the victim something burdensome they are entitled to keep, and that no third party has a legitimate stake in whether the debt is enforced. Finally, say whether each of the three conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and indicate what a better analogy would need to capture that the debt picture obscures.
