Picture two editors assembling a volume on George Eliot's relation to religious belief. They accept the same biographical background: Eliot was shaped by an earnest evangelical youth, translated Strauss and Feuerbach in early adulthood, abandoned supernatural faith, and yet spent her fiction and essays insisting on the moral seriousness of sympathy, duty, and renunciation—on what she took to be the enduring core of Christianity once its theology is set aside. They also grant, for the sake of argument, the Feuerbachian premise that the attributes believers ascribe to God are idealized human attributes, so that piety is really devotion to humanity in projected or alienated form.

The first editor argues that this makes Eliot's secular religion of humanity a genuine preservation rather than a dilution: reverence, gratitude, self-surrender, and the desire to amend one's life can be retargeted from a supernatural being to the human community without loss of content, because their content never depended on the supernatural referent. The second editor replies that these attitudes are partly constituted by their objects, so retargeting changes what kind of state they are: reverence for an unbounded and perfect being is not the same psychological act as reverence for finite, morally uneven humanity, even where the practical consequences overlap. On this view, what survives in Eliot is less religion than the emotional atmosphere of religion.

Write a dialogue between the two editors in which each character first states the strongest version of the opponent's position and then responds to it. Neither should be a strawman; the exchange should contain real back-and-forth rather than set speeches. At some point they should consider whether the first editor can avoid the second's objection by insisting that the object of devotion is humanity in its ideal possibilities rather than humanity as it currently behaves, and whether that move costs the first editor anything. By the end, the dialogue should locate the disagreement precisely—ideally clarifying whether it concerns the nature of the attitudes, the nature of their objects, or what is required for something to count as religion.
