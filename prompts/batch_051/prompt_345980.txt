I'm on the compliance team at a seafood company that buys finfish, shellfish, and insects for feed. Our current welfare policy is one line: anything with a backbone gets our handling standard, anything without one gets nothing, on the theory that it isn't conscious. That line is starting to look less defensible—octopuses do things that sure look smart, lobsters rub and guard injuries, bees learn from watching, and oyster probably don't feel anything, but nobody here can say where the line goes. My own view is that consciousness is graded and patchily distributed across animals, not a clean yes/no, but a procedure still has to output something we can act on. Corporate asked me to write a reusable decision procedure we run whenever a new species or new supplier practice comes up, because we can't afford to re-litigate each case from scratch. I don't have animal science training, the ops director has to sign off and won't read more than one page, and the budget for equipment like stunners is limited. Give me the procedure: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where it stops with a no, where it has to kick the decision up to a person because it's a values call, and where it says the evidence is too thin to justify acting. Then run it on three of our cases: requiring electrical stunning for prawns before chilling; adding physical enrichment for octopus we hold a day or two; and doing nothing for the black soldier fly larvae we grind into feed. Be honest about where the procedure would force a call my team won't like.
