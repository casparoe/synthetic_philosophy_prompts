Hey, I'm a novelist. I'm working on a book set in Berlin during the Deutsche Wohnen & Co enteignen campaign and referendum, and I've realized that I've been letting my characters talk past each other the way people do at actual housing meetings. So I want to actually figure out the arguments before I put words in their mouths.

Heres the episode as I understand it. In the fall of 2021 Berlin voted on a non-binding referendum, pushed by the Deutsche Wohnen & Co enteignen campaign, to force the city to socialize — that is, expropriate with compensation — the apartments of any private landlord owning more than 3,000 units in Berlin. That's roughly 240,000 flats, mostly held by big landlords like Deutsche Wohnen and Vonovia. It passed with something like 56 or 57 percent of the vote, but it's non-binding, and the Senate has mostly stalled, set up commissions, looked for softer paths. There's also the recent rent cap, the Mietendeckel, which was struck down by Germany's constitutional court in 2021 because the state of Berlin didn't have the legal authority to impose one.

What I want is to analyze that episode through Lefebvre's right to the city, using the standard supply-side / YIMBY view of housing as the foil. So for a start: who actually counts as the inhabitant in Lefebvre's sense in a city where most people rent, and does the referendum empower that inhabitant or just transfer the rent-extraction from a private landlord to a public one? Where, concretely, do use value and exchange value show up in the conflict, and does the left's instinct to treat housing as use value actually handle the supply-side point that Berlin's real problem is scarcity and that only building lots more housing brings rents down? Does the YIMBY view have its own hidden notion of who the city is for, and does that conflict with Lefebvre's idea of the city as something collectively produced rather than allocated?

And then flip it: what does the case do to the theory? Is Berlin — a vote for expropriation that the state is quietly not implementing — a vindication of the right to the city, a counterexample to it, or a case the theory was never built to handle? Which parts of the episode does Lefebvre's framework explain well, and which parts does it have to squint at or stay silent about, like the legality, the compensation, the actual construction pipeline, or the suburbs? I'd rather you say the framework has nothing to say here than force it to speak.

Seperate what's settled from what's contested: the referendum happened, those numbers are roughly right, the Mietendeckel was struck down, and the implementation has stalled — that's all settled enough. But whether expropriation would actually improve things for tenants, whether the vote was a real mandate for decommodification or just a protest about rents, and what Lefebvre's concept actually commits one to — those are genuinely contested, and I don't want you to present a contested view as the consensus.
