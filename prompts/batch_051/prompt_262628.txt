I have been developing a definition of "avoiding side effects" that is meant to be usable without a full specification of value, and I want an honest assessment of the idea. I call it the Task-Relative Baseline Principle.

Fix an artificial agent and a task specification, where the task is given only by success conditions or an objective function — for example, "deliver the package to the top floor" or "remove the weeds from this plot." The task-relative baseline is the class of outcomes in which (a) the success conditions are met and (b) every variable not mentioned in the task specification is held as close as possible to its value in the no-intervention world, under a generic, value-free notion of closeness such as "fewest variables altered" or "least overall physical change." A side effect, relative to the task, is then a departure from this baseline in any unmentioned variable. To build a low-impact agent, one trains or constrains the agent to satisfy the success conditions while minimizing departures from the task-relative baseline, with no reference to whether those departures are good or bad.

The principle is meant to capture ordinary judgments about side effects: if an agent delivers the package but breaks three vases on the way, the vases count as a side effect because they are unmentioned variables that could have been left at their pre-intervention values while still delivering the package. And it is meant to keep goal-directed effects from being misclassified as side effects: a rescue drone that must push open a door to reach a patient has not caused a "side effect" with respect to the door, because no outcome meets the success conditions while holding the door at its pre-intervention value.

I realize this is adjacent to existing work on impact measures, but I have not seen the notion of a baseline specified in quite this way. Please give me a candid assessment. Specifically:

1. Does the definition genuinely avoid a hidden appeal to value, or does value re-enter through the choice of baseline, the closeness metric, the variable partition, or something else?
2. Where does the definition go wrong as a classifier? Construct at least one case in which it counts something as a side effect that should not count, and at least one case in which it fails to count something that plainly is a side effect.
3. Has this been worked out under another name already, and if so, where exactly does my version stop being distinctive?
4. What is the strongest objection to using this principle as an engineering target for a real AI system, and is that objection fatal or repairable?
