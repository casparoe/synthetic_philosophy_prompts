Me and my friend spent last night arguing about wether companies can really bind themselves against persuasive design, or whether any rule with words like "primary" or "necessary" just becomes a game. He works at a company whose design code, written in 2019 before they bought the predictive-timing startup, says: "No feature shall be shipped if its reasonably foreseeable primary effect is to cause users to spend more time engaged with the product than is required to accomplish the task the user opened the product to perform." Now they want to ship a meditation app update where an algorithm predicts, from in-app behavior and phone sensor data, the moment you're probably doomscrolling on social media, and fires a push notification inviting you to a one-minute breathing exercise in their app instead. On average users open the app more often and total daily time in the app triples, but each session is short and does exactly what the notification said it would. Does this violate the clause? Give me the arguments from the words, from the purpose of the clause, and from what the drafters would have said if you'd explained intent prediction to them in 2019, and tell me which approach should win here. Also, does it matter that the extra time is spread across more visits rather than longer ones? I care less about the practical outcome than about what the clause was ever actually able to protect.
