I'm grading midterms for a mathematical reasoning course and want a second opinion on one answer before I write comments. The exam question was:

"Let f(x) = x² + 1, which over C has exactly two roots, usually written i and −i. (a) Could someone define 'the positive square root of −1' in the spirit of 'the positive square root of 2'? Why or why not? (b) Complex conjugation, a + bi ↦ a − bi, is a field automorphism that fixes every real number and swaps the two roots. Does this show that the symbol i is not well-defined? Why or why not? (c) Give an example of a polynomial over R with two distinct real roots whose roots can be singled out from each other without any arbitrary convention, and say what singles them out."

The parts were worth 3, 4, and 3 points, respectively.

Here is what the student wrote:

(a) No. The positive square root of 2 is picked out by the usual order on R: one root is greater then zero and the other less than zero. There is no total order on C compatible with its field operations, so you can't call one of the roots of x² + 1 positive or negative. That's why we introduce i as a name instead of pretending one of the two roots is preferred.

(b) Yes. Since conjugation is an automorphism of C fixing R and sending i to −i, the two roots are completely symmetric. Any statement built from +, ×, 0, and 1 that's true of one is just as true of the other, so there's no structural difference between them. So when we write 'i' we aren't referring to one specific object as opposed to the other; it's just a convention, and strictly speaking 'i' has no determinate reference. It's defined only up to conjugation, which is exactly the sort of symmetry that 'well-defined' is meant to rule out.

(c) For x² − 1, the roots are 1 and −1. These are distinguished by the field structure itself: 1 is the multiplicative identity and −1 is its additive inverse. Positivity in R is even definable from the field operations as the nonzero squares, so no arbitrary choice is needed there.
