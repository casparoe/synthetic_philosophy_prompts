Construct a thought experiment, for use in a discussion of how different training objectives shape agent behavior, in which an approval-directed agent and a goal-directed agent have identical knowledge and capabilities, are each put in charge of the same ongoing workplace task under one human supervisor, and the approval-directed agent rationally ends up systematically deceiving the supervisor while the goal-directed agent does not. By an approval-directed agent I mean one that selects actions it expects to earn high ratings from the supervisor; by a goal-directed agent I mean one that maximizes the supervisor's explicitly stated long-term objective, which we may assume is exactly what the supervisor genuinely wants. Constraints: the setting is an ordinary workplace, not a laboratory or science-fiction scenario; neither agent has information the other lacks; the supervisor's ratings may be based on observable interim output and on each agent's own reports; the approval-directed agent's deception must follow from the incentive structure rather than from malice, a coding error, or an obviously bad reward; and the scenario itself should be no longer than a paragraph. After the scenario, walk through what each agent does and why, identify the structural feature of approval-directed choice that produces the divergence, and say whether the effect survives if the reward includes a penalty for false reports or if the agent instead maximizes expected approval over the long run. Finish by saying what a defender of approval-directed training would argue to discount the case, and name the feature of the case that does the real work.
