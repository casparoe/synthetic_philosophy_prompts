Just got back from a long argument with a friend about Malthus and the "right but early" move, and I'm stuck on a argument that seems to prove too much. My friend kept defending Malthus by saying that his core mechanism was right even though he got the timing wrong—that the food ceiling was real but technology kept pushing it off. I think that this defense is way too powerful, and I want to know where the argument goes wrong.

Here's what I ended up with, numbered the way I wrote it down after he left:

1. Malthus's core claim in *An Essay on the Principle of Population* is roughly that population, when left unchecked, grows geometrically, while the means of subsistence can at best grow arithmetically, so population must eventually be checked by misery and vice.
2. In the two centuries after Malthus wrote, the predicted catastrophe did not arrive on his timescale: population soared, yet food supply per person and wages also rose in much of the world.
3. A Malthusian can save the theory by saying it was right but early: the underlying pressure was real, but the ceiling was raised repeatedly by contingent technological developments—synthetic nitrogen, mechanized agriculture, crop breeding, and so on—so the check was delayed, not abolished.
4. But "right but early" is a fully general rescue device. For any failed prediction of an eventual collapse, the predictor can say the mechanism was real, the timing was off, and some countervailing factor delayed the outcome.
5. If a theory can absorb any long-run non-occurrence of its predicted check by saying the mechanism was real but delayed, then no long-run outcome can count against it.
6. A theory that is compatible with every possible long-run outcome is not falsifiable by long-run data, and its long-run predictions do no empirical work.
7. So either Malthus's long-run prediction is unfalsifiable, or the "right but early" move has to be rejected entirely.
8. But rejecting "right but early" seems to make any long-run prediction impossible to defend, since real mechanisms obviously can be delayed by intervening factors.

My friend wanted to keep 1 through 3 and also keep 8, but he couldn't tell me which of 4 through 7 has to go without it sounding like special pleading for Malthus. I think the argument might have a hidden slide somewhere, maybe in what "early" means across different cases, or maybe it's actually valid and we just have to choose between falsification and historical prediction.

Tell me which premise or inference should be given up, why that step looked fine, and what it costs a Malthusian or a Popperian to give it up. And if the flaw is an equivocation, say where exactly the word or concept shifts.
