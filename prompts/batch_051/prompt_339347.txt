I am writing a term paper on the boundary problem in democratic theory: the question of who belongs to the demos, the people whose inclusion makes a decision democratic before we can even evaluate the decision itself. I have narrowed my framework to three candidate principles. The all-affected interests principle says that the demos for a decision is everyone whose interests are significantly affected by it. The all-subjected principle says that the demos is everyone who is subject to the binding laws or coercive power of the body making the decision. The formal citizenship principle says that the demos is simply the citizens or legal residents of the relevant jurisdiction.

I want to test these principles against a case my seminar could not settle. Consider a city that is about to vote on a congestion charge: any vehicle entering its central zone on weekdays between 7 a.m. and 7 p.m. would pay a daily fee, with the revenue going to transit improvements. A large share of the affected drivers live outside the city limits. They commute in daily, work in the city, use its streets, and pay some municipal taxes through their employers, but they cannot vote in city elections and their own towns have no role in the decision. The charge would fall heavily on them, while many of the city's own residents would experience cleaner air and faster buses; the commuters could avoid the charge by taking a train, though it would add roughly forty minutes each way. Do these commuters belong to the demos for the congestion-charge decision? Go through the three principles honestly, noting where each gives a clear answer, where it underdetermines the answer because affectedness and subjection come in degrees, and where it has to be stretched to fit the case. Then say what the case reveals about the framework: should the demos be fixed by formal membership, or should it vary from decision to decision in light of who is affected and who is coerced? If it should vary, state a precise criterion for drawing the boundary that handles this case, and flag one further case it would likely get wrong.
