I have been trying to work out whether the familiar rule/list distinction in linguistics survives the recent wave of connectionist and large language models, and I keep getting stuck on the notion of an exception. Consider an influential picture of inflection: a productive rule generates regular forms (walk/walked, and even novel stems), while irregular forms are stored in a lexicon as exceptions, and suppletion is the limiting case in which the stored form shares no phonological material with the base (go/went, good/better). On this picture the very idea of an exception presupposes a rule for it to be an exception to.

Now suppose that the best available models of inflectional behavior are single distributed networks — or large language models — with no separate lexicon and no explicit symbol-manipulating rule. The same learned weights produce regular, irregular, and suppletive forms, with no component corresponding to 'add -ed' or to a lexical entry for went. Does the rule/exception distinction survive this? Does go/went still count as an exception to a rule when the system that produces it contains no rule of the relevant kind? I want to know whether the original picture is refuted, repairable, or merely redescribed — and, if it is repairable, what the strongest repaired account of 'grammatical rule' says once rules and stored exceptions are no longer architecturally distinct.
