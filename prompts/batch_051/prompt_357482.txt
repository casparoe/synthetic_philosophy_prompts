I started a new job a few months ago and the company has this thing where they give out a quarterly "Courage Award." The winners are usually people who spoke up in a big meeting against a decision, admitted a costly mistake in front of everyone, or told a senior leader their plan wouldnt work. Last quarter it went to a project lead who told the execs their launch schedule was fantasy. Good for her, honestly. But the thing is, the same word gets used for what my uncle did in the Marines—he got a medal for pulling a wounded guy out of a vehicle that was on fire while they were taking fire. He was scared out of his mind. The company winners are scared of, like, a bad review or getting side-eyed in the hallway, and theres alot less at stake. Are those even the same virtue? It feels like the company is borrowing a word from the military without the military part.

So I'm trying to understand: what does a practice of handing out medals or awards for "courage" or "valor" presuppose about what courage actually is? Does it commit you to saying courage needs physical danger and real fear, or does it just mean doing something hard and right at a personal cost? Do the company award and the military medal presuppose the same theory of courage, or are they quietly contradicting each other? And if we thought courage was only what the military medal implies, what would my company have to change about the award—or about using "courage" as a corporate value at all—to be consistent? Please answer in American English.
