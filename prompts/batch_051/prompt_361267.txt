I'm taking a philosophy of law course this term and I keep getting stuck on the same thing. The law will say with a straight face that a corporation is a person when it wants to—it can own property, enter contracts, sue and be sued, commit torts, and get criminally fined. But it can't vote, marry, adopt, or go to prison, and nobody would say it has a mind in the ordinary sense. Then there's an estate: after someone dies, their estate can still be a party to a lawsuit, as though this non-thing were somehow still doing things. And in admiralty law you can sue the ship itself rather than the owner. I cant tell weather the law is just using "person" as convenient shorthand—a fiction everyone knows is false and doesn't take literally—or whether it actually treats corporations and estates as real legal entities of a different kind, entities it brings into existence by its own act. What does that whole pattern presuppose about the ontology of legal entities, like what it takes for something to count as a legal thing at all? Does the practice genuinely require some particular theory (fiction theory, aggregate theory, realism about group agents, whatever), or is it compatible with several of them at once? And if we concluded these are all pure fictions, would anything in the practice have to change, or is calling it a fiction just restating the puzzle rather than solving it?
