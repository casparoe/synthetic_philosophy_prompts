I'm a philosophy PhD student working on moral epistemology and AI, so this is basically my whole life right now. The pessimist position in the moral testimony debate says that forming moral beliefs on someone else's say-so is defective in a way that forming ordinary empirical beliefs on testimony isn't. One strand is about understanding: moral understanding—as opposed to just correct moral belief—requires you to grasp the reasons why, to be able to answer the follow-up "why?" questions yourself, and testimony only ever hands you the answer, not the reasons. Another strand says taking moral testimony is a failure of autonomy or proper moral agency, because you're outsourcing a judgment you should make for yourself. Optimists think the asymmetry is overblown; we lean on others for moral insight all the time, and it's fine.

The development I keep thinking about is LLM moral advice. A chat model fine-tuned on a huge corpus of human moral discourse doesn't just say "X is wrong"; it'll walk through analogies, consider counterexamples, tell you which considerations seem to be doing the work, and revise when you push back. So it looks less like a testifier saying "take my word for it" and more like a Socratic interlocutor. But there's no person on the other side—no one with experiences, no one whose epistemic position your actually trusting, no one who stands behind the verdict. It's closer to a compressed, interactive average of a lot of human moral talk.

Does the pessimist argument survive this, or does the LLM case break it? Which premise is actually under pressure: the claim that testimony only transmits answers and not reasons, the claim that the source's agency and epistemic position matter, or something else? Is there a repaired version of pessimism that still says something true about getting your moral views from an LLM, but is weaker than the original? Or does the LLM case force optimists to admit that there's a kind of source their accounts don't cover either?

I care less about whether we should use LLMs for moral advice and more about what the case does to the original argument.
