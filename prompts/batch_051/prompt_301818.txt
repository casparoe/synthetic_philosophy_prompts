I'm taking a seminar on the species problem, and I've realized my friend—a systematics grad student—is a kind of oracle on this without ever stating her rule. So I've been keeping a list of her verdicts:

- On the Ensatina salamanders around California's Central Valley, where the forms at the two ends don't interbreed but they're connected by a belt of adjacent populations that do, she says: one species.
- On parthenogenetic whiptail lizards—all-female, reproducing by cloning, no gene exchange—she says: obviously a species, and any definition that says otherwise is broken.
- On two crickets in the same meadow that look identical and will hybridize fine in the lab, but don't mate in the wild because their songs don't match, she says: two species, no hesitation.
- On two mouse populations on opposite banks of a river that differ by a single enzyme variant, where nobody knows if they would interbreed if the river were gone, she says: probably one species until somebody shows otherwise.
- On a fossil lineage that changes gradually over several million years and got split into three named chronospecies at the two clearest morphological breaks, she says: that's bookkeeping, not biology; it's one evolving lineage.

Now someone in the seminar raised a case I'm going to put to her. Two color forms of an alpine flower, blue and white, grow side by side. They are completely interfertile, and pollinators move between them all the time; hybrids are viable and fertile but do badly in both forms' microhabitats, so the blue and white patches stay stable generation after generation with no genetic barrier at all.

What should I predict she'll say? I care less about the final label than about the rule: is she a biological-species-concept person with a fallback clause for asexuals and fossils, or a lineage/cohesion person, or something else entirely? Which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog to the flower case, and what follow-up case would cleanly separate the candidate rules?
