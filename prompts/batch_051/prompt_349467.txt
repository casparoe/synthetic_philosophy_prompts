I have a standing rule for interpreting what other people say: when a remark admits of several readings, I adopt the one that makes the speaker's beliefs come out as true and as coherent as possible, within broad limits of plausibility — with strangers, in political arguments, and even when the most natural reading is false or offensive. A relative recently told me that a proposed law would "make everyone equal in practice"; I read him as saying it would subject everyone to the same enforcement procedures, because that is the reading on which he is nearly right. My defense is roughly the one behind Davidson's principle of charity: interpretation can only get off the ground if we assume, by default, that a speaker mostly believes what is true and mostly reasons as we do, since meaning and belief have to be solved for together and neither is fixed independently. A friend objects that I have lifted the principle out of radical interpretation, where it is a constraint on a field linguist facing a wholly alien language with no dictionary, and turned it into a private rule for everyday listening. He says it tells us nothing about how much rationality to attribute to a particular person with whom I already share a language, and that I am, in effect, editing other people into agreement with me.

I want to know whether my policy is defensible, and whether there is any principled rate of charity. When I suspect I have been too charitable, I cannot tell whether I have properly relaxed a constraint that was never meant to apply in this setting, or have simply started noticing real error. Is there a genuine difference between the charity radical interpretation needs and my everyday policy, or is my friend drawing a line that does no work? And how could I detect, from the inside, that charitable interpretation has become motivated listening?
