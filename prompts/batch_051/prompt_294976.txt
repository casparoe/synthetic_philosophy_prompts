I’m teaching an undergraduate philosophy of language course, and I set the following exam question on Putnam’s Twin Earth. I have one student answer I’d like a second opinion on before I assign a grade, because the student writes well but I’m not sure whether the argument is subtly off.

Exam question: The Twin Earth thought experiment standardly compares Earth and Twin Earth at a time before chemistry. Consider a diachronic version. Suppose Oscar on Earth and Twin Oscar on Twin Earth are exact internal duplicates in 1750; both use the term “water” for the clear, drinkable liquid in their respective environments; and neither knows anything about its microstructure. By 1850, chemists on Earth have determined that Earth’s liquid is H2O, while chemists on Twin Earth have determined that theirs is XYZ. By 1950, both communities have absorbed these discoveries and use “water” accordingly. Do the extension of “water” in Oscar’s mouth and the extension of “water” in Twin Oscar’s mouth differ in 1750? If not, when and how does the difference arise? Does your answer support or complicate Putnam’s view that meanings are not in the head?

Student answer: The 1750 extensions were the same: both speakers associated “water” with the same cluster of observable properties, and extension is determined by such descriptions. The difference arises only after the chemical discoveries, when each linguistic community stipulates that the term picks out H2O on Earth and XYZ on Twin Earth. So the extension changes, but the meaning does not, because the old descriptive meaning remains: something like the clear liquid that falls as rain and fills lakes. This is a problem for Putnam’s slogan, since all the semantic work is being done by community stipulations and descriptive beliefs, which are internal psychological states. Hidden kinds only matter after speakers learn about them.
