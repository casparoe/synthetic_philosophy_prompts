I am a retired physical scientist, and I spent my working life treating testimony the way I treated my instruments: a report is a channel whose output earns credibility through calibration. That cast of mind makes me strongly attracted to reductionism about testimonial justification — the view that believing what someone says is justified only when, and to the extent that, it is underwritten by ordinary inductive inference: known correlations between reports of that kind and the facts, plus background evidence about the speaker's competence and sincerity. The theory has genuine virtues. It is parsimonious, it posits no special epistemic faculty for accepting others' words, it smoothly explains why we discount known liars, why we rescale trust after a track record, and why testimony weakens under conflict of interest; it assimilates a puzzling source of belief to a well-understood one.

And yet I cannot shake the opposite intuition. If a colleague tells me at a conference that the afternoon session has moved to another room, or a technician reports that a sample measured 7.3, I take myself to be justified in believing it outright and immediately, without having assembled any record of that person's reliability. A child who accepts a parent's word, or someone who believes their birth date on family testimony, does not seem to be doing bad epistemology. Testimony appears capable of grounding belief directly, defeasibly but non-derivatively, rather than licensing an inference I happen not to have performed. The strongest anti-reductionist version of this I know adds a structural point: the observations by which I might calibrate reporters are themselves often delivered through testimony — published results, instrument manuals, lab lore — so the reductive chain must either go infinite or bottom out in something more basic, which already looks like the position it was meant to avoid.

So I hold a theory I admire and an intuition I trust, and they seem to conflict. I do not want a verdict that simply picks the side I already lean toward. I want both positions built as charitably as possible, and then a negotiated answer: should the intuition be debunked — perhaps as a confusion of default trust with justification, or of reliability with being justified? Should reductionism be amended or abandoned — for example, through a distinction between local and global reductionism, or between having a standing entitlement and having inductive evidence, or between full belief and provisional acceptance? Or is there a reading of "inference" and "justified" on which both survive?

Please do more than adjudicate: say which of the two you would give up or amend, what that move costs, and what would change your mind — which argument, case, or stipulation would lead you to a different verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
