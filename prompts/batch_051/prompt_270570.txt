Im a novelist, not a philosopher, and for a near-future novel I need to work out how a scheme would actually behave once people start gaming it. In the book, an international AI standards body introduces a "sentience attribution escrow" rule—not because they have solved consciousness, but as a coordination device. Heres the mechanism as I currently imagine it.

Any lab that wants to run a training job above a compute threshold has to pay a deposit into escrow first, say one cent per petaflop-hour of expected compute. After the run, the lab files a ledger that splits the compute into two buckets: "inert" computations (pure bookkeeping, no plausible moral weight) and "live" computations (anything the lab thinks could, under any serious theory, be the kind of processing that matters morally). An audit panel then reviews the ledger. If a majority of panellists agree a slice was live, that portion of the deposit goes into a welfare-and-monitoring fund, and the lab is barred from deleting, merging, or fine-tuning away the relevant circuits without a second review. If they agree it was inert, the lab gets that portion back. The two catches: panellists are paid per audit, but half of each fee sits in a five-year bond that they forfeit whenever a later review overturns their classification in either direction. And anyone—labs, advocacy groups, the general public—can pay a filing fee to request a re-review of any ledger entry, with no limit on how many times.

I want to treat this as machinery, not as a moral position. Walk me through what it incentivizes. What do labs do to make their most morally-ambiguous compute look inert, and does that change what they actually train or how they measure it? How do bonded panellists vote on borderline cases, and what does that do to the meaning of the "live" label over time? Who files re-reviews, and what happens to the welfare fund in practice? Where does the scheme get gamed first, and can any of the failure modes be patched without turning it into a full-blown metaphysics court?
