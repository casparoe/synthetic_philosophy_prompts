I've been in a reading group on the deep history of inequality, and we've gotten stuck on the old debate about whether the agricultural revolution was a mistake. The anti-farming side argues that foragers had more varied diets, shorter work weeks, better health on average, and a lot more autonomy; farming bought us famines, crowding, epidemics, hierarchies, and backbreaking work, and then it trapped us, because once populations grew you couldn't just walk back to foraging. The other side says this is a romantic fantasy, that farming fed far more people and made cities, literacy, and the rest of what we call civilization possible, and that calling it a mistake is itself confused because no group ever sat down and chose it. The weird thing is, the argument as gone in circles for a year, and both sides mostly accept the same archaeological picture: stature and dental health declined after the Neolithic, work hours probably went up, and sharp inequality shows up with storage and sedentism. But nobody moves. My suspicion is that the real disagreement isn't about the facts but about what we're evaluating — average individual welfare, the total number of lives lived, long-run human flourishing, or whether a slow unplanned transition can be a mistake at all. Is this a verbal dispute about the word 'mistake', a substantive disagreement about what makes a human life go well, a disagreement about the unit of evaluation, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a missing fact, a better-framed question — or should we expect the argument to just stay stuck?
