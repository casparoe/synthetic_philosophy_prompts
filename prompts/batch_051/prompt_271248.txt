Please describe the most important implications of the reference class problem for how we should interpret and act on individualized probabilistic predictions. By the reference class problem I mean the following: a prediction about a particular case—a defendant, a patient, a loan applicant—ordinarily proceeds by placing that case in a class of similar cases and assigning it the base rate for that class, yet a single case can often be placed in any of several defensible reference classes with sharply different base rates, and the statistical machinery alone does not settle which class is the right one. With that framing, address at least three questions. What does the problem imply about the objectivity of risk scores and similar individualized predictions? What does it imply about how decision-makers should treat such scores in high-stakes settings such as pretrial detention, medical triage, or hiring? And does the problem support a general retreat from individual-level prediction toward coarser or explicitly subjective judgments, or only a more careful, transparent handling of reference-class choice?
