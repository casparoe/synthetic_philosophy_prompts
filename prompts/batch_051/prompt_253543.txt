I'm auditing a climate ethics seminar, and the course handout uses the non-identity problem to argue that future generations have much weaker claims on us than environmental rhetoric suggests. A classmate and I got into an argument over it, and I realized I can't tell how much of the handout to trust. The handout summarizes the position something like this: "Derek Parfit's non-identity problem shows that our usual way of thinking about harm to future generations falls apart. Any climate policy we choose will change who gets born, because the whole course of social and economic life shifts and different people eventually come into existence. So the particular people who live in the hotter world owe their existence to the very policy that warmed it; if we had cut emissions instead, those same individuals would never have existed at all. And since being alive, even a hard life, is better than never existing, they are not worse off. Justice is about making sure no particular person is left worse off, and climate change leaves no particular person worse off. It therefore wrongs no one, and so societies owe future generations no duties of justice — only, at most, reasons of benevolence or aesthetic preference."

My instinct is that there's a slide somewhere between "these particular people are not worse off than they would have been had we chosen differently" and "no one has been wronged." But I can't tell whether that distinction is doing real work or whether I'm just resisting an uncomfortable conclusion. Please go through this summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a careful philosopher working on intergenerational justice would accept as a fair statement of the non-identity problem, which parts are conflations or overstatements, and whether the final claim — that there is no duty of justice to future generations — actually follows from the accurate parts. If my suspicion is misplaced, or if the handout is fairer than I'm giving it credit for, tell me that plainly. Please write your answer in American English.
