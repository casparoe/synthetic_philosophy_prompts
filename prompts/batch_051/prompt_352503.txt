I run an upper-level undergraduate seminar on AI alignment and the philosophy of mind — about 16 students, one 75-minute session. Design a runnable in-class game that makes participants discover for themselves the difference among three failure modes: specification gaming, wireheading, and reward tampering — before any of those terms is named. Use only paper, tokens, and a visible scoreboard, with points standing in for reward; no real money. The rules and incentive structure should be arranged so that, across successive phases, players first find a loophole that earns points without doing the intended task, then become tempted to alter the scoreboard or ledger itself, and finally can purchase direct credit that bypasses the task altogether. Specify the task, materials, exact rules, roles if any, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps each phase onto the three concepts and distinguishes wireheading (short-circuiting the agent's own reward state) from tampering with the external reward channel. The debrief should also make clear that each failure arises from optimizing the reward signal as given, not from disobeying it. Make definite design choices throughout, with a sentence justifying each rather than presenting options; also say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and state honestly which feature of wireheading a paper-and-tokens game cannot reproduce and what I should tell students to bridge that gap.
