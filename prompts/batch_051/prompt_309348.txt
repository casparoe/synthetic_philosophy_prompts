I'm drafting my philosophical profile on self-prediction and agency for a working group, and I'd like an audit of whether the pieces cohere before I circulate it. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Standard Bayesianism about self-prediction. Rational agents have precise degrees of belief in every factual proposition, including propositions about their own future actions, and those credences should be responsive to all the evidence the agent possesses.

(2) Ordinary self-prediction. It is often rational, and sometimes required, to predict your own future behavior from your track record and from base rates. If I have snoozed my alarm on four of the last five workdays, I should assign something close to 0.8 to snoozing it tomorrow.

(3) Deliberation crowds out prediction. If I am currently deliberating about whether to write a letter of recommendation I promised to write but am strongly inclined to punt on, it would be defective to assign a determinate probability—0.7, say—to the proposition that I will write it. To fix such a number is to treat a question that is presently up to me as though it were already settled by facts about me as a mechanism. While I am genuinely deciding, the matter has to remain open in a way that excludes determinate self-prediction.

(4) Compatibilism. This openness is not a metaphysical claim about alternative possibilities. My reasons can causally determine what I choose while the choice remains free and mine, so determinism is not what is at issue in (3); the norms of deliberation are.

(5) Predicting and deciding are distinct. Deciding to write the letter is not a forecast of a state I am waiting to observe in myself; it is a commitment that settles the question. And one cannot be genuinely committed to writing the letter while assigning a non-negligible probability to not writing it.

(6) Anti-base-rate-neglect. Evidence about my own past behavior and about people in my situation should not be suppressed merely because it concerns me. If 80 percent of people in my exact situation do not write the letter, then absent specific evidence that I differ from them, treating myself as an exception is self-serving bias.

Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and then tell me which single revision would do the most to restore coherence. I'd also like to know where you're least certain: which argument or consideration would most likely make you change your mind about the deepest tension, and where your reasoning here is most likely to fail.

At the very end, on the last line, write \answer{...} containing the number of the commitment (1 through 6) whose revision would do the most work, or the word consistent if the package is already jointly tenable.
