I have a view about word meaning that I cannot talk myself out of, and I would like an honest assessment of it from someone who knows the philosophical landscape. I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so I will state it in the vocabulary I work in.

I have started calling it semantic dispatch. When I look at a function call like open(x) in code, nobody asks for the meaning of open in isolation. What open does depends on the type of x, the call site, and the surrounding program state; that is simply how polymorphic functions work. I have come to think words work the same way. A word is the name of a dispatch function. Its arguments are the sentence, the speaker, the audience, the goals of the conversation, and a large body of shared background. Its return value is whatever the word contributes on that particular call. Meaning, on this view, is not a thing a word has; it is the trace of a computation that is rerun each time the word is used. A dictionary entry is a cache of the most common return values, not the authoritative meaning.

The central claim is that polysemy is not an edge case. Words such as bank or run are not defective versions of a cleaner kind of word; they are the normal kind, and a word only looks monosemous because its dispatcher almost always routes to the same implementation. I would say there is no semantic core that remains the same across every use; there is only the semantic range, plus a cached prototype that we mistake for the core.

Two cases already trouble me. Proper names feel less like dispatch functions and more like constants bound to different objects by different people. And logical words such as not or every seem so regular that the dispatcher story looks like machinery doing no work. Still, I keep thinking the view explains why definitions feel circular, why translation is hard, and why new uses of a word often feel like the same word rather than a different one.

Could you tell me three things: whether this is a coherent view or just an engineering metaphor posing as a theory; what it gets right and whether something close to it has already been worked out under another name; and where it breaks, including a candid statement of whether the proper-name and logical-vocabulary problems are patchable or fatal. I would rather have the strongest version of the idea criticized than the version I happened to sketch.
