(1) A griefbot is a system that simulates a particular dead person's conversational behavior, trained on their texts, voice messages, etc., so that a grieving person can keep talking to a convincing stand-in.
(2) If building or using a griefbot wrongs anyone, it must wrong the deceased, the bereaved user, or the bot itself.
(3) The deceased no longer exist, and nonexistent things cannot be wronged, so the deceased cannot be wronged.
(4) The bereaved user can give informed consent to the interaction, and if the bot makes their grief worse they can stop; a voluntary tool that sometimes causes transient distress does not thereby wrong its user.
(5) A chatbot trained on the deceased's data is not a sentient being, so it cannot be harmed or wronged.
(6) If none of the candidate parties is wronged by the creation or use of a griefbot, then creating and using one is morally permissible.
(7) If a griefbot reduces the user's suffering and wrongs no one, then using it is not just permissible but, all else equal, morally good.
(8) Therefore, building and using griefbots is permissible and, in typical cases where it helps, morally good.

I keep squinting at (3) and (4), but I cant make the objection stick. Is the whole thing valid? If not, which step actually fails, and under what reading is it false? If the argument is valid, is the conclusion as strong as it looks, or does 'all else equal' in (7) do more work than I want?
