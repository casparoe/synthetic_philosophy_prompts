Last week I overheard two of our graduate students, Elena and Marcus, going at it about the two-envelope problem, and I told them I'd referee. I scribbled down the back-and-forth as best I could, but I couldn't settle it cleanly. Here's my reconstruction:

Elena: Take the usual switching argument. You open your envelope and find $10. The other envelope is either $5 or $20, equally likely, so its expected value is $12.50, more than your $10, so you should switch. But the same reasoning applies whatever amount you found, so you'd always switch — absurd given the symmetry of the setup. The mistake is the "equally likely" part. The setup only says one envelope contains twice the other; it says nothing about how the pair of amounts was chosen. Conditional on seeing ten dollars, whether the other is five or twenty depends entirely on the prior over possible pairs, and with any proper prior it can't be fifty-fifty for every amount. Symmetry does give you something real: before opening, the two envelopes have the same expected value. So there's no unconditional reason to switch, and the paradox is an artifact of a hidden uniform prior that can't exist.

Marcus: You've attacked the weakest version of it. Run the argument before opening. Let x be the amount in my envelope. The other envelope is the larger one or the smaller one, each with chance one half; if it's the larger it holds 2x, if it's the smaller it holds x/2. Its expected value is (1/2)(2x) + (1/2)(x/2) = 1.25x, which beats x for every x. That version never conditions on a particular observed amount, so you can't blame it on a bad conditional. And here's a proper prior where switching is genuinely favorable. Suppose the possible pairs are (1,2) and (2,4), each chosen with probability one half, then you get a random envelope. Unconditionally the two envelopes have the same expectation, fine. But open and see $2: conditional on that, the pair is (1,2) with probability one half and (2,4) with probability one half, so the other envelope is worth (0.5)(1) + (0.5)(4) = $2.50, more then the $2 in hand. So equal going-in expectations don't tell you what to do once you've got the evidence in hand. The real lesson is about expected-value reasoning when you implicitly average over all amounts with equal weight, and about conditional and unconditional advice coming apart.

Elena: Your (1,2)/(2,4) case is my point wearing fancier clothes. Seeing $2 is evidence about which pair you're in; you switch because of your prior, not because of prior-free logic. The original paradox pretends to get its fifty-fifty for free. The opaque version still smuggles a conditional in: for a fixed x it gives "the other is x/2" and "the other is 2x" probability one half each, but with any fixed pair one of those has probability one and the other zero. The only way to make it fifty-fifty for every x is an improper uniform prior over infinitely many amounts, and then the expected values are infinite, so 1.25x isn't a well-defined expectation in the first place. I'll grant that the going-in expectation isn't the whole story about what to do after you look. I just don't see what's left once the prior does all the work.

Marcus: I'll grant the original argument is invalid. My point is that the usual dismissal is too fast. Your own concession — that the going-in expectation doesn't settle what to do after you look — is where the action is. In the (1,2)/(2,4) case, after seeing $2, switching is genuinely favorable despite the unconditional symmetry. So the real question isn't whether the switching argument is valid. It's whether a policy of switching on some observations and staying on others can outperform sticking, and how that coheres with dominance reasoning. That's a live question, and it doesn't evaporate just because you say "you need a prior."

They were both looking at me at that point, and I genuinely went in circles. Please adjudicate, point by point: who is right about which specific claims, what are they both missing, and who is more right overall?
