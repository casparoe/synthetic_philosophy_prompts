A friend of mine has been teaching himself Hex, and last weekend he spent an hour walking me through the standard strategy-stealing proof that the first player has a winning strategy. As he gave it, the argument assumes the second player has a winning strategy, has the first player make an arbitrary opening move and then steal that very strategy, and appeals to the assumption — uncontroversial in Hex — that an extra stone of one’s own color can never hurt, in order to handle the cases where the stolen strategy would prescribe moving into a cell the first player already occupies. Since Hex cannot end in a draw, it follows that the first player has a winning strategy. What struck me was that the proof never says which opening move to play, or how to continue; it simply forces the conclusion that some winning strategy exists for the first player.

I realized, in the middle of the argument, that I have a firm general policy about proofs of this kind. I do not count myself as believing that a player has a winning strategy unless the proof either displays the strategy or provides some finite procedure that will find it. When someone offers me a non-constructive existence proof — and a strategy-stealing argument above all — I suspend judgment: I will concede that the derivation is formally valid, but I will not say, in any sense that matters to me, that the first player has a winning strategy. I hold much the same attitude toward non-constructive existence proofs in game theory generally, but I noticed a wrinkle in my own position. I accept non-constructive existence proofs elsewhere without complaint; I am perfectly content, for instance, to believe that a continuous function which changes sign has a zero even when no specific zero can be computed from the proof alone.

My friend called my policy a confusion between truth and utility. He insisted that a valid proof of existence establishes the existence claim full stop, and that my inability to play the strategy has no bearing on whether it exists. That challenge has left me unsure whether my policy is earning its keep or whether I have singled out games because I want something more usable from a proof about them. Is this policy defensible, and if so, what feature of game-strategy existence proofs is it tracking? Or is my friend right? I would also like to know whether a corrected version of the rule can be stated — one that preserves whatever epistemic caution is useful here while discarding whatever is mere obstinacy — and under what conditions withholding assent after a strategy-stealing proof is rational rather than a refusal to update.
