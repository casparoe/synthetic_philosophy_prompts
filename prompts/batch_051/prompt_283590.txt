I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about the cult of effort — grit culture, no pain no gain, the psychology of desirable difficulties, all that. I want to run a sidebar quiz for readers: something like "what do you actually think effort is for?" But it should sort people by what they actually say about cases, not by which self-help label they vibe with.

Write me a nine- or ten-question multiple choice quiz plus a scoring key. Each question should be a concrete little scenario — climbing the same peak the easy way or the hard way, a founder who grinds for years and fails, practicing an instrument with and without struggle, a pill that makes your work feel effortless, two identical paintings where one took a year of agony and one took an afternoon — that kind of thing. Please make sure the effortless-pill case and the identical-paintings case are in there; I don't care about the rest of the cases. Three or four answer options each, and nobody should be able to tell which answer goes with which philosophical position just from the wording. No question should just ask whether effort is valuable for its own sake.

The positions the quiz should be able to land people on are: (1) instrumentalism — effort is just a cost you pay for an outcome, so the same outcome with less effort is always at least as good; (2) a constitutive view — for some achievements the struggle is part of the value, so the identical outcome reached without it is not the same good; (3) an aretaic or character view — the valuable thing is the disposition to strive and the strengths it builds, even when the project fails; (4) a self-constitution or existentialist-ish view — struggling is how you become somebody, so the value is in what the effort makes of you rather than what it produces; (5) an effortless-action view — effort is a sign something is misfiring, and the ideal is mastery that feels easy. If there's another coherent leaf I've missed — like a flat "effort is bad and ease is the default" view — add it, but only if your scoring key can actually reach it.

Build it from the logical space rather than from the list of names: work out which combinations of answers hang togehter, then locate the real positions among the occupied combinations. Tell me which answer combinations are logically possible but empty, and for each say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because nobody would want to live there, or just because the view hasn't been worked out. And flag any two leaves the quiz can't reliably tell apart — I'd rather print that honestly then pretend the quiz is sharper than it is.

Audience is smart general readers, not philosophy people, so keep the cases everyday-ish and the answer options sounding like things an actual person would say. The scoring key should map patterns of answers onto the positions, and say what a scattered, all-over-the-place pattern means. And please write the whole thing in American English — my copy desk will kill me if British spelling leaks in.
