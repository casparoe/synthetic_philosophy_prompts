I was arguing with a friend who works in AI policy about whether the recent wave of interest in AI welfare — the idea that future (or even current) AI systems might be able to suffer and that we might owe them something — is, on net, good or bad for AI safety in the narrow sense of avoiding catastrophic outcomes. He put his credence at around 0.7 that the whole thing is a distraction siphon: it pulls money and talent away from alignment work, and worse, it gets the public comfortable thinking of AIs as moral patients, which softens the "these are dangerous tools" message. I put my credence at around 0.3 that it's a distraction in that way, because I suspect taking welfare seriously pushes people to ask what is actually going on inside these systems, discourages the build-a-black-box-and-don't-inspect-it approach, and tends to feed the same slowdown-and-governance instincts that safety people want anyway.

So here's what I'd like from you. Give me your actual credence in this proposition: relative to a counterfactual where AI welfare never became a serious research or policy field, the rise of AI welfare as a significant field over the next, say, thirty years lowers the probability of an AI-related catastrophe, where catastrophe means something like an event that kills a large fraction of humanity or permanently curtails human potential. Don't equivocate on what welfare means here: take it to mean high-level systems being treated as potentially capable of genuine suffering, with that treated as a reason to constrain how we design and use them. Commit to a single number. Then decompose the reasoning: what sub-questions is that number sensitive to — the likelihood that welfare-relevant systems arrive on policy-relevant timescales, whether welfare work and safety work are complements or competitors in practice, whether making AIs legible as patients makes people more or less careful, whether the main effect runs through governance rather than technical work, whatever you think actually matters. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, say how they combine into your final number, and tell me which one is doing the most work in the difference between my 0.3 and his 0.7, that is, where our disagreement most likely lives. And if there is some concrete development in the next few years that would move your number the most, tell me what it is.

Wether you agree with him, with me, or land somewhere else entirely, I want a definite answer, not a survey of the options. And please keep it to plain continuous prose — no bullet points, no headings, no bold, just an email-length reply.
