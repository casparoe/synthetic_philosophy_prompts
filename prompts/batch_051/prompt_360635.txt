In C.S. Peirce's "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" he writes, in the middle of the transubstantiation discussion: "…we can consequently mean nothing by wine but what has certain effects, direct or indirect, upon our senses; and to talk of something as having all the sensible characters of wine, yet being in reality blood, is senseless jargon."

Now I'm trying to apply this to a dispute about an AI translation system. One person says a system that uses a compositional internal grammar really understands language, while another says a system that produces identical translations on every input we can currently think of, but uses a giant lookup table, only behaves as if it understands. At first glance Peirce's wine line seems to settle it: if the sensible effects are the same, the talk of a "real" difference beneath them is empty. But I'm unsettled because the two systems might diverge on future inputs, and that is a conceivable practical effect. Does Peirce's "conceivable" let the hidden-mechanism claim back in? Or is that move importing a Cartesian difference between how things seem and how they really are that the wine passage was meant to kill?

Apply the passage to the case and tell me where exactly you draw the line. If Peirce would say the two systems are the same concept, is that a feature or a flaw of pragmatism for this sort of case?
