Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between an aggregationist and an anti-aggregationist about the torture vs. dust specks choice, understood as follows. Option A: one person is tortured for fifty years. Option B: 3↑↑↑3 people (an astronomically large but finite number) each gets a single dust speck in the eye, a brief, trivial, but nonzero irritation. The forced choice is between these two outcomes and no other; there is no compensation, randomization, or ex post mitigation.

The aggregationist holds that the badness of many tiny harms can add up across persons so that, for sufficiently many dust specks, Option B is the worse outcome. The anti-aggregationist holds a lexical-priority view: the torture is worse than any number of dust specks, because severe harms belong to a different order of badness from trivial irritations. You may refine these statements in the preamble, provided each side remains recognizable as giving the conflicting answer to which outcome is worse.

The deliverable is the document such a collaboration would produce, formatted as follows.

1. Preamble. State the choice and its presuppositions in language both parties accept, including what a dust speck is, what the torture is, and the sense of worse at issue. Flag any presuppositions the parties cannot jointly accept and adjust the wording accordingly.

2. Agreed claims. Give numbered claims both parties sign. These should go beyond definitions: formal constraints each side accepts, claims about what the case is not a test of, claims about which considerations are irrelevant or shared, and any nontrivial common ground about how the dispute must be resolved. Neither party should feel that a claim has been worded to slant the later disagreement.

3. Disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, state the question in neutral terms, then give the wording each side would accept as a fair statement of its own position, column by column. A competent partisan should be able to sign his or her column without needing to correct it. Indicate which disagreements you judge substantive and which, if any, are partly verbal.

4. Cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side from its answer or its central rationale. For each crux, specify a test, argument, or case that both sides agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance.

5. Verbal disputes. State which portions of the disagreement, if any, you conclude are verbal rather than substantive, and why; if you conclude that none is, defend that.

The document should engage the underlying questions of additivity across persons, the separateness of persons, whether betterness is continuous or admits of discontinuities, and whether the same judgment applies within a single life and across lives. Do not reduce the disagreement to a bare clash of intuitions, and do not adjudicate the dispute; the deliverable is a statement of agreement and precise residual disagreement, not a verdict.
