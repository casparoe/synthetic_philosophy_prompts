I am a novelist. I write literary fiction, roughly one book every two or three years, and my process has until now depended on a single originating image arriving of its own accord; I then spend months building the book outward from it. I want to replace that passive reliance on inspiration with a procedure I can run deliberately, and I am drawn to Douglas Hofstadter's knob-twiddling account of creativity. As I understand the account, creation is seldom ex nihilo: one begins with an existing structure — a plot shape, a scene, an image, a genre convention, a familiar story — identifies the conceptual dimensions along which it could vary, and then turns one or more of those knobs, each a little or a lot, to produce new work that remains recognisably kin to the source while becoming something one would actually want to write. The skill lies in knowing which knobs are load-bearing, how far each can turn before the result collapses into either a copy or an unrelated idea, and how knobs turned together interact.

Turn that account into a reusable procedure for a working novelist. The constraints are these: I have about a week between drafts; I cannot do external research in that week; I work alone; and before I draft, my sceptical editor must approve a one-page outline, so the procedure must terminate in something presentable rather than in a private feeling of possibility. I expect to run the procedure several times a year, whenever I begin a new project or find a draft going inert, and the cost of getting it wrong is months of work on something derivative or incoherent.

Give me a checklist or a decision tree in words. For each step, say what question I am asking, what counts as a sufficient answer before I may proceed, and where the step should send me back or stop me. The real work is in the translation: preserve what the knob-twiddling account actually claims, make it usable to a novelist who will never read the theory, and mark the points where the procedure is aesthetic judgement wearing a procedural costume — in particular the decisions about whether two variations are still variations of one theme, whether a knob has been turned too far or not far enough, and whether an interaction of knobs has produced something new rather than something merely busy.

Then run the procedure on these three germinal ideas:

1. A widow in a small coastal town finds that every house on her street receives the same misaddressed letter on the same morning.
2. A proof-reader of court transcripts begins to find episodes from his own childhood described in the margins of the cases he is assigned to correct.
3. A novelist wakes to discover that the book she is writing was published, word for word, by a stranger thirty years earlier.

For each, show enough of the procedure at work that I can see which knobs you identified, how far you turned them, and why. Include at least one case in which the procedure gives me an answer I will not like — a variation it tells me to abandon, a knob it tells me not to turn, or a project it refuses to endorse. Then give an honest account of what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of novel it will misfire on, the parts of writing it cannot reach, and the assumptions about creativity it makes that might be false.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the claims you have made about Hofstadter's model and about the procedure itself, and single out the point you are least sure of.
