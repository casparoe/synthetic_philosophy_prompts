I am a teaching assistant for an upper-division course on experimental philosophy, grading a midterm short essay. The question was written by the instructor, students had twenty-five minutes and no notes, and the exam did not require citations. I am a first-time grader and want to calibrate my standards rather than go on a general impression, so please assign a score out of 10 and give the point-by-point comments that justify it: for each substantive claim in the answer, indicate whether it is accurate, overstated, or mistaken and explain why; flag any conflations or unsupported steps; and close with the single most useful comment I should write in the margin. Do not penalize prose style.

Exam question (short essay): Describe the two chairman cases Knobe used, state the asymmetry he found, and explain what he takes this to show about the folk concept of intentional action. Then assess whether the data license his conclusion: consider at least one competing explanation and say how well it handles the asymmetry.

Student answer (transcribed verbatim):

Knobe asked subjects about a company chairman. In one vignette, the vice-president proposes a new program that will increase profits and also harm the environment; the chairman replies that he doesn't care about harming the environment and cares only about profits. In the other vignette, the program will increase profits and help the environment; the chairman says he cares only about profits, not about helping. In both cases the program is adopted purely for profit. The asymmetry is that roughly eight in ten subjects say the chairman intentionally harmed the environment, while fewer than a quarter say he intentionally helped it. In other words, the bad side effect is judged intentional and the good side effect is judged unintentional.

Knobe's conclusion is that moral considerations are part of the folk concept of intentional action. Because the chairman's beliefs, desires, and expressed attitude are held constant across the two cases, a purely descriptive belief-desire psychology cannot account for the difference; the only thing that changes is the moral quality of the side effect. This shows that applying "intentionally" is not a morally neutral factual judgment; moral evaluation enters into the concept itself.

The obvious deflationary reply is that subjects are responding to something other than intrinsic moral badness, for example to the violation of a norm in the harm case. But harming the environment just is morally bad, so this reply merely restates the moral explanation under another label. The chairman's attitude cannot explain the asymmetry either, since he expresses the same indifference in both cases. Since the deflationary alternatives collapse back into the moral account, the data license Knobe's stronger conclusion that the folk concept of intentional action is partly constituted by moral evaluation.
