A colleague summarized the two best-known paradoxes about rational belief for me as follows: the lottery paradox shows that you can rationally believe, of each individual ticket in a million-ticket fair lottery, that it will lose, while also rationally believing that one ticket will win; the preface paradox shows that a careful author can rationally believe each sentence in her own book while also rationally believing that at least one sentence in it is false. Together, she said, these cases establish that rational belief is not closed under conjunction, and therefore that it is a mistake to demand that our beliefs be mutually consistent — rationality only requires that each individual belief be probable enough on its own. Something about the step from the two examples to that moral strikes me as too quick, but I cannot locate the problem myself. Is this a fair summary of these paradoxes and of what they show? I would like to know which parts a careful epistemologist would accept, which parts are subtly misstated, and whether the final conclusion about not needing consistency actually follows once the accurate parts are separated out.
