Write a dialogue between two epistemologists who disagree about whether knowledge is closed under known entailment. One defends a tracking, or sensitivity, account of knowledge: roughly, a true belief counts as knowledge only if the believer would not have held it had it been false, and this condition is applied separately to each proposition believed. The other treats closure as a structural requirement: if someone knows p and knows that p entails q, then they are at least in a position to know q, and no acceptable theory of knowledge may imply otherwise.

Situate their exchange around two cases. In the first, a visitor at a zoo sees a zebra in the zebra enclosure and forms the belief that the animal is a zebra; the question is whether she is also in a position to know that it is not a mule cleverly disguised as a zebra. In the second, someone reasons from "I have hands" to "I am not a handless brain in a vat," and the question is whether that inference transmits knowledge.

Have the two characters work through the details rather than restate slogans: how each would block the skeptical argument; whether the tracking view is committed to the conjunction "I know it is a zebra, but I do not know it is not a cleverly disguised mule," and why that conjunction strikes many as absurd; whether the closure view can explain ordinary hesitation about the mule proposition without surrendering closure; what epistemic role each assigns to deduction; and whether their disagreement is best understood as a disagreement about knowledge itself or about something adjacent, such as assertion, evidence, or the semantics of "knows."

They should respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talk past one another. Neither should be a strawman; each should make at least one genuine concession, and each should modify at least one formulation under pressure. By the end of the dialogue, the reader should see exactly what the residual disagreement is.
