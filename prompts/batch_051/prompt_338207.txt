Question: In the ethics of randomized controlled trials, explain the tension between a physician's duty of personal care and the design of a randomized controlled trial. What is clinical equipoise, and how is it supposed to answer that tension? Be precise about whose uncertainty matters, and identify at least one residual problem or objection.

Student answer: A physician enrolling a patient in a randomized trial is committed to offering the patient the best care she can, but the trial requires that treatment be assigned by chance rather than by the physician's judgment about what is best for this particular patient. Clinical equipoise is meant to dissolve this tension. It is the requirement that there be genuine uncertainty in the expert medical community about which of the trial arms is superior. Freedman introduced clinical equipoise to replace the earlier therapeutic equipoise standard, which had required the individual treating physician to be genuinely uncertain about which treatment is better. Under clinical equipoise, the relevant uncertainty belongs to the community of experts rather than to the individual physician, so a trial can remain ethical even once individual clinicians begin to form private preferences. A physician with a settled personal view that one arm is better may therefore still enroll patients, provided enough reasonable experts disagree. Since the judgment is a community-level one, clinical equipoise fully discharges the physician's therapeutic obligation and resolves the conflict between research and therapy. A residual problem is that community uncertainty is hard to measure in practice, since expert opinion is rarely surveyed directly.
