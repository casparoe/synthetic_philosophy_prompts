Question:
Explain what heterophenomenology is and why its defenders regard it as the right starting point for a science of consciousness. Contrast it with both first-person introspection and third-person behavioral observation, and say what kind of conclusions, if any, a proponent of the method thinks it can support about the reality or status of conscious experience.

Student's answer:
Heterophenomenology is Dennett's proposed method for studying consciousness from the third person. The investigator records everything the subject says about how things seem to them, typically after prompting them to introspect and report their experience. These reports are treated as data, but not as direct evidence about the brain or about a private inner realm; they are treated, in the first instance, as expressions of the subject's beliefs about their own experience. The central analogy is with an anthropologist who carefully records a people's testimony about their gods without thereby committing to the existence of those gods. The point of this neutrality is to take the first-person perspective seriously while remaining scientifically respectable: the subject is granted full authority as the only possible witness to their own conscious life, so the method never second-guesses what a subject says about their own experience. Because heterophenomenology only describes what subjects say and remains agnostic about whether those sayings are true, it is neutral between all theories of consciousness. A method this permissive cannot favor an eliminativist conclusion about qualia or inner representations, so Dennett's further claims about consciousness go beyond what the method itself can support. What distinguishes the method from introspectionism is that introspection is a private, first-person activity, whereas heterophenomenology is public and third-person: the data are transcripts and recordings that any investigator can inspect. What distinguishes it from behaviorism is that it takes subjects' reports seriously as meaningful speech rather than ignoring them. Heterophenomenology is therefore not a theory of consciousness but a neutral, purely descriptive method of data collection, and it leaves all substantive questions — including whether conscious experiences are real — open.
