I sit on a national health service committee that must decide, out of a fixed budget, which new treatments to fund. Our recent decisions are these: we funded a smoking-cessation program at 12,000 currency units per QALY; our usual acceptance range has been roughly 20,000 to 30,000 per QALY; we funded an intensive-care expansion at 45,000 per QALY, expressly because it rescues identifiable patients who would otherwise die imminently, even though that ratio exceeds our usual range; we declined a drug for a chronic, non-fatal condition at 55,000 per QALY as not cost-effective; we funded an end-of-life cancer therapy at 75,000 per QALY, citing terminal illness and short life expectancy; and we declined an ultra-orphan treatment for a fatal disease at 250,000 per QALY because the gain per patient was small and the ratio was far beyond anything we have accepted. Observers have accused us of inconsistency in how we treat statistical and identified lives. We must now rule on a treatment for an acute, imminently life-threatening infection, costing 60,000 per QALY, that would rescue identified patients but is not an end-of-life treatment for a terminal illness. If we want to be consistent with our own precedents, how should we decide? State the rule or rules that fit our track record, say what each implies for the new case, identify the closest previous decision, and specify a further case, real or hypothetical, that would best discriminate among the plausible rules.
