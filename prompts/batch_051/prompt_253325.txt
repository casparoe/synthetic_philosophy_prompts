Whenever I must assign a probability to a single case—a patient's response to a drug, a flight's cancellation, a project finishing on time—the case belongs to many reference classes, and different classes yield different probabilities. My standing policy is this. I will not choose the class after seeing what the candidate classes imply: the class must be specifiable in advance, without reference to the outcome or to the number I want. Among classes meeting that constraint, I use the most specific class that contains at least thirty cases and whose base rate has been roughly stable over the last three years. If two or more eligible classes survive and their rates differ by more than ten percentage points, I report the interval spanning them rather than a point estimate. I keep this rule even when a much narrower class, such as people with this patient's exact biomarker signature, is tempting but has only a handful of cases.

A colleague says the policy is a bias dressed up as a rule: the thresholds are arbitrary, and the deeper choice—which features count as candidate classes at all—is still my private judgment, so the reference-class problem has merely been relocated.
