I have been reading the Confucian classics on my own, in translation, and I am trying to work out whether there is a defensible picture of right and wrong inside them. The picture I keep half-seeing is this: a person is not mainly an isolated chooser who happens to have relationships, but the occupant of roles — son, mother, sister, friend, clerk, citizen — and each role has its own standard. The question in a hard case is often not "what do impartial rules require?" but "what would it be to be a good son here, or a good friend, or a good official?" Yet when I test that thought against the judgments I actually make about particular cases, it starts to fray. I want to lay out those judgments and ask whether one principle, or a small set of principles, can capture what I actually think.

My judgments, as near as I can make them honestly:

1. A father has stolen a sheep. In one of the texts, a ruler tells Confucius of a son who reported his own father for the theft. Confucius answers that in his village fathers and sons do not hand one another over, and that what makes a person upright is protecting one another in this way rather than reporting. My gut sides with Confucius. Reporting a theft is normally right, but the son who reports his father strikes me as cold and misshapen, and I do not admire him.

2. A friend who works in the office that issues building permits has been taking envelopes of cash to push applications through. He asks me to say, if anyone asks, that I know nothing about it. I know. I would not lie for him, and I think I would eventually report him. I would lose sleep over it, but staying silent feels like joining the corruption rather than being loyal.

3. An abusive former partner comes to my door asking where a friend now lives. I say I do not know even though I do. The lie does not trouble me, and I would tell it again.

4. I sit on a hiring committee. A close friend is one of two finalists, and the other candidate is plainly better qualified. I recuse myself. If I were forced to vote, I would vote for the better candidate, not for my friend; afterward I feel I owe my friend a candid explanation, not because he is owed the job but because I owe him something.

5. My neighbor's elderly parents need help after surgery. I bring one meal and offer to run an errand. My own mother needs the same kind of help, and I spend all my leave, rearrange my work, and stay with her. The two cases feel different in kind, not just degree, and I do not think this is because I believe my mother is more valuable as a person than my neighbor's parents.

There is also a case I keep going back and forth about, and I would like the principle to settle it. My cousin took care of me through a long illness when we were young. He has recently been taking small sums from the till at the family shop, and my aunt wants to report it to the police. I could stay quiet, which would let it continue for a while, or I could tell my aunt what I know. I am torn.

The nearest I can come to a principle is something like this: duties are tied to relationships and roles; closer relationships ground thicker duties; and right action is doing well the role that is actually called for in the situation, so that one should sometimes be a good son before a good citizen rather than solve every conflict with an impartial rule. But that seems vague and maybe circular.

What I want from you is a precise statement of the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts, with an explanation of which verdict motivates each clause. Test it against the cousin case and against any further case you think is diagnostic. Then tell me plainly which of my stated verdicts it cannot accommodate, and whether I should give up that verdict or revise the principle. Finally, tell me whether the view that survives really amounts to the role-based ethic I half-glimpsed in the Confucian texts, or whether it collapses into the ordinary thought that we may favour our own.
