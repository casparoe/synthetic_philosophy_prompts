A buddy in my program has been going on about something he calls 'free-floating rationales' — the idea that there are reasons for actions even when no actual mind has ever grasped them, the way a poison mushroom is still a reason not to eat it even if nobody ever walks past. I tried to write the argument out and now I think it proves too much, but I can't find the bad step. Here it is:

(1) A reason for an action is a fact that counts in favor of that action.
(2) Whether a fact counts in favor of an action dosen't depend on anyone's actually believing, knowing, or otherwise having that fact in mind; a poison mushroom counts against eating it whether or not anybody notices.
(3) So reasons are free-floating: there can be reasons without any actual reasoner whose situation makes them salient.
(4) From (3) it follows that there is a reason for me to not eat a specific poison mushroom on a specific exoplanet I will never visit, and a reason for me to prevent every harm it is logically possible for me to prevent, since each corresponding fact counts in favor of an action and no actual reasoner's situation is needed to pick the fact out.
(5) But that's absurd. My actual deliberation can't be answerable to a limitless heap of reasons about exoplanets and merely possible harms; reasons are supposed to guide an agent, and an unguided cloud of facts does nothing to guide anybody.
(6) Therefore (2) is false: facts become reasons only when a mind takes them up as bearing on a live choice.

I want to keep (2), because rejecting it sounds like making wrongness depend on whether someone happens to notice the relevant facts. So where does this go wrong — and if it doesn't, which premise is cheaper to give up than I think?
