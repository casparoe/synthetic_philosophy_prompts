I am a PhD student in the anthropology of religion, and I keep running up against a question my committee treats as either settled or unanswerable: whether “religion” is a coherent category. On one side, the genealogical critique has convinced me that the term carries Protestant and modern Western assumptions—about belief, about texts, about the separateness of the religious from the political—and the standard edge cases make definitions by necessary and sufficient conditions look hopeless: Theravada Buddhism is minimally theistic, Confucian rites can look more like civic ethics than worship, and “secular religions” such as civil ceremonies or intense fandoms satisfy some criteria but not others. On the other side, I worry that “it is just a constructed folk category” has become a reflex rather than a claim anyone has tried to put at risk.

Grant that “religion” is a folk category with a messy history. It could still be the case that, cross-culturally, the loosely associated features—practices and narratives oriented toward superhuman or quasi-superhuman agents, costly ritual, moralized norms, community boundary-marking, special experiences of the sacred, afterlife-related commitments—cluster together far more than chance, the way some folk biological categories track real discontinuities. Or the apparent clustering could be an artifact of the record: if ethnographers coded their material using the category, then of course it looks joined-up. So the conceptual claim I want tested is whether “religion” picks out a genuine family-resemblance kind, defined by stable non-random feature clustering across societies, or only a loose, historically contingent grouping. What would a serious test look like? I can imagine operationalizing it with cross-cultural datasets—coding a sample of societies on a battery of candidate features and comparing the resulting structure against a null model such as randomly shuffled feature assignments—or with sorting experiments in which people classify unfamiliar cultural practices, but I am not sure either addresses what I actually want to know. Please set out the strongest concrete test you can: the data, the operationalization of the features, the comparison or null model, what result would count as evidence that the category has structure worth taking seriously, and what result would support the deflationary position. Then be explicit about what the test would not settle: for example, a positive clustering result would not vindicate the folk definition or the term’s normative uses, and could still be compatible with the category’s being a local historical formation that happens to fit; a negative result would not show that particular traditions are unreal, only that the superordinate grouping does little work.

Finally, please say how confident you are in the proposed test’s design and in the conclusions you draw from it, and single out the point you are least sure about.
