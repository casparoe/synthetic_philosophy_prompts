As a retired physical scientist who has only recently come to academic philosophy, I have been puzzling over an apparent tension inside the standard practice of regulatory cost-benefit analysis, and I would like a careful reconstruction of the view of utility that the practice presupposes.

The practice is this. When a regulation protects a good that is not directly bought and sold — quiet near an airport, a small reduction in the risk of fatal injury, the continued existence of a remote wetland — the analyst is instructed to assign it a dollar value. For many such goods the preferred procedure is to infer that value from actual market behavior in an adjacent market: differences in house prices between otherwise similar quiet and noisy neighborhoods are taken to reveal how much residents would pay for quiet, and differences in wages between more and less dangerous jobs are taken to reveal how much workers would accept for a small change in mortality risk. The resulting dollar figures are then added up across individuals as measures of the regulation's benefit. Yet many of the same manuals also permit, and sometimes require, analysts to use responses to hypothetical survey questions about willingness to pay when no related market exists, even while they describe actual choices as the more reliable evidence.

I can see at least three different things one might mean by "utility" here: a mere index constructed from a person's choices, carrying no claim beyond consistency and prediction and certainly no claim about the person's welfare; a substantive quantity, such as preference-satisfaction or something like happiness, that choices reveal only imperfectly; and a money-metric quantity of well-being that can in principle be added and compared across persons. The practice seems to move among these senses without acknowledging it. When it infers values from market choices, it sounds behaviorist; when it treats those dollar values as measures of welfare and sums them across the population, it sounds as though utility must be a cardinal, interpersonally comparable magnitude of a kind that an ordinal choice-index could never supply; and when it accepts hypothetical survey answers, it seems to abandon the behaviorist insistence that nothing but actual choice counts.

What theory of utility and welfare does this practice strictly presuppose, as opposed to what it merely fits well with? Which rival theories of utility would force a change in the procedures, and which would still license them? And is the practice internally consistent, or does it quietly combine commitments that cannot be held together? I would be glad of an answer that stays close to the actual features of the practice rather than to textbook definitions.
