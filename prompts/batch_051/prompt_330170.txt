I do home care for a woman with diabetic neuropathy, and I keep noticing something weird. Some days she says her feet are burning and she's climbing the walls, can't think about anything else. Other days she tells me the same burning is there but it doesn't bother her — like she's describing a smudge on a window rather than pain. That got me reading about people with something called pain asymbolia (pretty sure I'm butchering that) who can still tell you where a pain is and how strong it is, but it doesn't seem bad to them. The pain-sensation and the badness pull apart.

I want a reusable decision procedure I can actually run on a shift for what to do when a client reports pain, or doesn't report pain, and I have to decide whether it's nothing, something to watch, or something to escalate to the nurse. I make this call a few times a week, I have no medical training, and I've got about five minutes per client. Ground the procedure in the idea that pain has at least two pieces that can come apart: the bit that tells you something's wrong (and where, and how much) and the bit that makes it awful. Don't give me jargon, don't give me citations.

Make it a checklist or a flowchart in words: the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a good enough answer to move on, where the procedure stops with a "no," and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) my client on a burning-but-it-doesn't-bother-her day, (2) a client with a small clean cut who is panicking and crying, and (3) a client with a known diabetic foot ulcer who reports no pain at all. Include at least one place where the procedure tells me to do something I won't like, and say honestly what the procedure can't settle. Keep the whole thing under 900 words.
