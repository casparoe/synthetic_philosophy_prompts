Here's one for the list. I run a small causal-inference reading group that's basically a rationalist-adjacent meetup: 10–16 people, mostly people who can nod along to d-separation when it's drawn on a board and have seen "faithfulness" thrown around, but would flunk a quick quiz on how it differs from minimality. We have a 90-minute slot, and I want one session where they actually run into the distinction instead of me lecturing about path cancellations and measure-zero parameter spaces for the fifth time. The idea I want them to leave with is this: minimality says no edge is redundant (delete it and the graph stops being Markov to the data); faithfulness says no edge is invisible (omit it and you miss a dependence that's really there). They feel like the same claim, but they come apart exactly when a distribution can be re-parameterized by a graph smaller than the true one.

Design me a classroom game for that. The mechanic I have in mind—but change it if you have something better—is that pairs get a sealed envelope containing a true three-variable DAG over binary X, Y, Z, plus a 2x2x2 contingency table of counts (say out of 200) generated from it. They see only the table. They have to test the relevant marginal and conditional independence statements, reconstruct the graph or graphs, and then bet chips on which graph was in the envelope before opening it. I'd like at least three acts: (1) two teams with different true graphs discover they get literally the same independence pattern (chain vs fork), so the skeleton-plus-v-structure level is the right place to stop; (2) a collider round where the v-structure orientation is actually forced by the pattern; (3) a final unfaithful "trap" table where the true graph has three edges but the smallest Markov graph has two—like an extra X→B edge whose effect cancels against X→A→B so X and B come out marginal independent. Include a scoring system that doesn't use real money but keeps stakes legible, a tight timeline, and a debrief that maps each act onto the formal claim. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guess: people will confuse sampling noise in the counts with unfaithfulness, so I may need tables with exact zeros or precomputed tests), and be honest about what the game can't reproduce—I suspect real unfaithfulness is measure-zero while near-unfaithfulness is everywhere, so the clean little trap table might overstate how easy the problem is to spot.
