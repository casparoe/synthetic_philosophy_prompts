I'm a philosophy PhD student working on speech acts and the ethics of deception, and I've gotten into a long argument with my officemate that I'm hoping you can adjudicate. The stakes feel taxonomic, but they turn moral pretty fast: we both agree that if it's a lie it's more culpable than if it's "merely" misleading, which is exactly why the boundary matters to us.

Our working definition is this: S lies to H iff (i) S asserts p, (ii) S believes p is false, or at least does not believe p is true, and (iii) S intends to get H to believe something false. Some versions tighten (iii) to "get H to believe p itself is false," and some people add (iv) that p actually is false. We can't agree on whether the intention condition (iii) is even necessary.

Here's the case. At a press conference, a reporter asks a politician: "Did you have any contact with the CEO while the bribery investigation was ongoing?" The politician answers: "I have not spoken with the CEO since the investigation began." That's literally true: he hasn't spoken with the CEO; he's only exchanged emails and used an aide as a go-between. But he chose that sentence precisely because he knows the reporter will infer—and print—that he had no contact at all with the CEO, and that's the false impression the public ends up with.

Act as a philosopher of language who works on lying and speech acts, and work through this carefully. Start by brainstorming what "asserts p" could mean here and whether the proposition people take away ("he had no contact at all") is asserted, implicated, or something in between. Then go through criteria (i)–(iii)/(iv) one by one and tell me how the case fares on each. Finally, say whether the case qualifies as a lie overall, and whether the fact that the answer feels a little forced either way suggests the criteria should be revised—for example, by counting knowingly false implicatures as lies, or by droping the intention condition entirely. Please also tell me how confident you are in each step of your answer, and single out the point you're least sure about.
