I help run a neighborhood tool library — roughly ninety member households, three volunteer coordinators, and a board that reviews contested sanctions. We face three or four decisions a month about how to respond to members who free-ride: tools come back late, damaged, or not at all, and some members never do a checkout shift or contribute to the repair fund while others carry the load. Each coordinator has about twenty minutes per case, none of us has legal training, and the board will only overturn us for inconsistency; a bad call can cost us volunteers or lend the place the feel of a clique. We disagree about the right stance. One coordinator wants a rigid three-strikes rule. Another wants near-total leniency because we are a community rather than a business. I keep arguing that what we are actually managing is the shared expectation that we will all still be here next year, and that every response either strengthens or quietly spends down that expectation.

I have come to think about it this way. With a stranger, or a one-time transaction, trust is a gamble and there is little to be learned from it. With an ongoing member, a lapse is a move in a long back-and-forth, and our response is the next move. Watching this group, I believe cooperation survives when we start each member off as someone to be trusted; notice problems quickly and calmly; respond with something proportionate and legible rather than doing nothing or jumping to permanent exile; and, after one honest slip, let the person back in cleanly rather than holding the mistake over them, because otherwise two sides can end up punishing each other forever over a single accident. It also seems to matter whether the person actually has a future with us: someone moving away next month is playing a different game from someone who hopes to borrow for years, and the procedure should notice that difference. And we have to be much harder on deliberate freeloading than on bad luck, even though telling them apart is a matter of judgment rather than a checkbox.

Please write the decision procedure I can hand to the coordinators and have them run the same way every time, without my being in the room. Set it out as a table with these columns: Step; Question to ask; What counts as a good-enough answer to move on; Action if yes; Action if no or where the step stops; and Where judgment cannot be eliminated. After the table, give a brief JSON summary with fields decision_procedure (a condensed list of the steps), worked_cases (an array of objects with fields case, outcome, and reasoning), and limitations (a list of strings). The procedure should include, in order, how to establish the member's history and remaining time with the library, how to classify the present incident, how to choose a proportionate response, and how to reset or escalate. Mark clearly the steps that must be handed to a person because they turn on values or on uncheckable judgment, such as distinguishing an accident from carelessness. Then run the procedure on three cases: a reliable five-year member who returned a borrowed power washer late twice in a row, with a broken part, during a family crisis; a well-liked member who organizes our social events but has never done a checkout shift and is repeatedly late with high-demand tools; and a first-time member who borrowed a cordless drill and has now been silent for a month. If the procedure yields a conclusion I or the other coordinators will resist, say so plainly rather than softening it, and do not exempt the second case from the logic in order to spare anyone's feelings. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do and what it assumes about the group's size, stability, and memory, and what happens to it when those assumptions fail.
