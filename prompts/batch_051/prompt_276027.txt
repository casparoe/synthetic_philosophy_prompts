Two members of my housing cooperative, Mara and Jonah, spent an hour after a house meeting arguing about a compromise that had been proposed. I stayed behind and listened; I never formed a settled view. I am less interested in our specific food rule than in the general dispute, because the argument kept turning on claims about the structure of moral compromise.

For background: the house had been deadlocked between those who wanted every shared meal to be plant-based and those who wanted no house restriction on what members cook or serve in the common kitchen. The compromise on the table would keep the plant-based default for shared meals but allow one clearly advertised, opt-in non-plant-based meal per month, paid for only by those attending. In exchange, the house would double its monthly donation to a farmed-animal welfare organization, adopt stricter sourcing standards for any animal products served, and add a surcharge to the non-plant-based meal to help fund that donation.

Their argument went roughly like this.

Mara: This is a trade of the most straightforward kind. Each side gives ground on something it cares about less in order to get something it cares about more. The plant-based side keeps the default; the others get one opt-in meal a month; and you, Jonah, get more money flowing to a charity you yourself think does good for animals. Everyone is better off by his or her own standards than under the present deadlock. Rejecting that is preferring a worse state of affairs to a better one.

Jonah: You keep saying "by their own standards," but that is precisely what is in question. If someone believes it is wrong to serve animal products, she does not believe it is wrong on twenty-nine days a month and permissible on the thirtieth. The donation does not cancel the wrongness of the exception; it makes the whole package easier to swallow. The house would still do what I think it should not do, and under this compromise I would be partly authoring it.

Mara: You already accept trade-offs of this structure within your own view. You think factory farming is a graver wrong than lower-intensity farming, so you would spend more to stop the first than the second. That means your own commitments have weights. Once you grant that, the only question is whether the total package — the default, the exception, the donation, the sourcing rules — ranks higher than the package you currently have. I am confident it does for the other side. The question is whether it does for you.

Jonah: That assumes every consideration sits on one scale and differs only in weight. My view says the exception is a wrong of a kind that no donation offsets, not a lesser wrong to be balanced against a greater good. Not every wrong has a price at which I will perform or endorse it. And this is not just my private conscience: it is a rule the house adopts together. A rule has a meaning. This one would turn animal use into something the house treats as acceptable according to a schedule, and I do not want to co-author that meaning even if the animal welfare calculation at the margin comes out positive.

Mara: Then put the meaning in the ledger. The gains-from-trade picture does not require that animal use be negotiable or that the donation make the exception right. It only says: if an agreement, expressive costs included, ranks higher for each party than no agreement, then it is better than no agreement. You cannot coherently say "these things cannot be traded" and then keep negotiating the size of the donation or the sourcing rules. Negotiating just is trading.

Jonah: There is no inconsistency. Negotiating may reveal that no point exists that I can accept, or that I could accept only under conditions you rightly find worse. The deeper problem is that your framework assumes the two systems already share enough structure to compare packages. You count "the exception" as one discrete item with a cost. I count it as the house losing the one thing that made its default more than a dietary preference. If the two of us do not parse the options into the same units, then "each ranks the package higher" may be an artifact of the ledger rather than a fact about us. A bargain could still be struck, but it would be a bare diplomatic settlement, not each side acting well by its own lights.

Mara: Call it a diplomatic settlement if you like. Between people whose values genuinely conflict, a settlement both sides prefer to the alternative is not second best. It is what respecting each other as self-governing people requires: I do not get to impose my ranking on you, you do not get to impose yours on me, and if there is a point we both prefer we should take it. Purity is easier; it is not obviously better.

Jonah: Respect points the other way as well. A diplomatic settlement treats our deepest views as fixed blocs to be routed around rather than as claims about what is right. I am not asking to impose my ranking on you. I am asking whether the house should keep the one shared rule that does not require any member to participate in what she takes to be a wrong. If we are invoking respect, respect for someone can require not making her co-own a practice she takes to be wrong.

They did not resolve it, and I could not referee it. Please go through their exchange step by step and adjudicate it: who is right about which specific points, where each overstates or misses something, and what both of them are failing to consider about the structure of compromises between value systems. If you think one of them has the stronger position overall, say so and explain why.
