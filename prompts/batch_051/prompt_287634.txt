The guy I argue with insists that the only serious way to think about normativity is indirect: the right thing is whatever I'd end up wanting after ideal deliberation — all the relevant facts, no biases, perfect reasoning — and that actual judgments matter only as fallible guesses at that endpoint. I keep saying "the endpoint of ideal deliberation" is doing a lot of work. It sure sounds like a partly empirical claim about actual minds: that there is a stable endpoint, and that as you move real people toward it (more facts, less bias, better reasoning) their judgments start to converge on it, rather than drifting wherever the setup pushes them. So design the strongest test of that you can. Pick a specific domain of evaluative disagreement where convergence isn't already the cheap cultural default. Give me the manipulation, the measures, and the controls, and say exactly what pattern of results would count for and against treating idealization as something with a real target. I specifically want you to definately handling path-dependence: if people get to the same endpoint no matter what order the information and arguments arrive in, that's much stronger than within-condition convergence alone. Then be honest about what a positive result would still not settle — the step from "we would converge" to "that is what's right," whether the convergence is built into the choice of idealizing conditions, and whether finite reflection can ever claim to approximate a literally infinite ideal. And tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and which point you're least sure of.
