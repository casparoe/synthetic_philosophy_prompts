I have come to defend the following view. When a single agent faces the same kind of risk repeatedly, standard expected-value reasoning systematically misleads about that agent's prospects. Expected value is an ensemble average: it averages across many parallel realizations of one choice. An agent who lives through a sequence of multiplicative gambles experiences a time average instead, and when the process is non-ergodic the two diverge. For example, a gamble that multiplies wealth by 1.5 or by 0.6 with equal probability raises expected wealth by five percent per round, but its time-average growth rate is roughly negative five percent per round, so an individual who repeatedly accepts it is with high probability impoverished. I draw the normative conclusion that, for repeated multiplicative risk, a single agent should evaluate prospects by their time-average growth rate rather than by expected wealth, and that habitually maximizing expected wealth in such settings is an error.

I want to know what would count against this view. I do not mean the arithmetic, which I take to be settled; I mean the step from the divergence of the two averages to the claim that the time-average growth rate is the right criterion for action. Which observations, arguments, intuitions, or formal results should move me, and which should only embarrass the view? Does the position collapse into expected-utility maximization with a logarithmic utility function, and if so, does it retain independent normative content? Does it survive bounded time horizons, additive rather than multiplicative payoffs, consumption flows rather than a fixed stock of wealth, and pooling risk with others exposed to the same gamble? What does the view predict or explain that its rivals do not, and would the failure of such predictions count against it? For each family of objection, say what would have to be true for it to succeed, and which escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which of them a committed defender of the time-average criterion would themselves concede as counting against the view rather than dismiss as a misunderstanding. Finally, say whether the view, in its strongest form, has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that would be a problem for a claim of this kind. I flag that the two objections I currently find most worrying are the reduction to logarithmic expected utility and the finite-horizon problem; I do not want them weighted upward on that account.
