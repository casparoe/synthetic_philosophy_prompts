I'm in a moral philosophy class and we keep coming back to utilitarianism, and the objection I can't stop poking at is the separateness of persons one. The way I understand it goes like this. Utilitarianism treats well-being as a quantity you can sum across people and then tells you to pick whichever option has the biggest total. But no one ever experiences the total. If I take on a small pain now so that my future self gets a bigger pleasure, that makes sense to me: the cost and the benefit happen inside one life, and the gain can offset the loss. But if we impose a small pain on you so that some third person gets a bigger pleasure, the utilitarian says that's the same kind of move, just across two ledgers, and it really doesn't feel like the same kind of move. The objection says the difference is exactly that: utility isn't pooled, its locked inside separate lives, so there is no single subject whose gain can offset another subject's loss the way my own future can offset my present.

My friend thinks I'm making this way too complicated and says the objection is just armchair hand-waving, which is what got me bothered. I don't want to argue right now about whether the objection is correct; I want to know whether its point can be tested at all. Is the separateness intuition a stable feature of how people actually make moral judgments, or is it a rationalization people reach for after they have already flinched at what the utilitarian math says? The only test I can imagine is the comparison above: give people structurally identical tradeoffs, one where the cost and benefit fall on the same person across time and one where they fall on different people, hold the amounts and probabilities equal, and see whether they reject the interpersonal version much more than the intrapersonal one. But I can't work out what that would prove. So here's my question: how would you test a claim like this concretely, and what would a clean positive result settle and not settle? What pattern would count against the intuition being a real feature of moral judgment rather than a story people tell after the fact? What else should I screen off — like people being bad at keeping totals equal, or caring more about one concrete victim than a statistical crowd — and what part of the objection would stay forever out of reach of any experiment?
