I've been trying to settle what I should think now in light of what I will think later, and my case-by-case reactions refuse to add up to a tidy rule. If I learn that later tonight a colleague will show me a decisive argument about a hypothesis and that I will assess that argument competently, and I expect that afterward I will assign probability 0.85 to the hypothesis, then 0.85 seems to me exactly the probability I should assign right now; refusing to adopt it would be like insisting I know better than someone who I expect to know strictly more. But I feel no pull at all from my future self when the future self is impaired. If I know I'll soon be given a drug that reliably makes people wildly overconfident about lottery tickets, and I know that in an hour I'll assign credence 0.9 to the claim that my own ticket came up, my current credence stays at the sober one-in-a-million value. Likewise, if I know I'm about to lose a piece of relevant evidence — an amnesia drug will erase one datum bearing on the question — and I can predict that tomorrow-me will be at 0.4, that fact doesn't move me now; tomorrow-me simply knows less. A friend proposes that the right principle is a simple one: at any time your credence in a proposition should equal your current expectation of the credence you will assign it at a specified later time — the principle I've heard called Reflection. That rule handles the first case but seems to command exactly the wrong thing in the drug and amnesia cases, so it needs qualification, though I don't know how far to push the qualification.

The case that gives me most trouble sits between the clear ones. Suppose a course of training means next-week-me will be faster and far less sloppy at probabilistic reasoning than I am now, but between now and then I will lose contact with a source I currently trust on this question, so next-week-me will be sharper but strictly less informed. I can't tell whether I should defer to that future self fully, partly, or not at all; my provisional principle — defer to the expected future self exactly when she is at least as reliable and at least as well informed as I am now — appears to require a comparison of two different advantages that I cannot carry out from where I stand. There is also a case where the expected-future-credence idea feels right but gives me no number: I know I'm about to receive a large batch of relevant evidence whose content I cannot now anticipate and that I'll process it flawlessly. I can't calculate what I'll think, so I can't use the rule directly; the most I can honestly say is that I shouldn't now adopt a credence I am confident my future self would regard as too high, nor one I'm confident she'd regard as too low.

I'd like to know whether there is one principle of self-deference that captures the verdicts I'm confident in, repairs the Reflection rule rather than merely listing exceptions, and resolves the sharper-but-less-informed case. If no single principle does all of that, then which of my verdicts should I overturn, and what does the surviving principle say about a case I haven't listed: one where I know my future self will have spent an hour more thinking through the same body of evidence with the same competence, and I can predict where that extra reflection will land — should my present credence move to match it immediately?
