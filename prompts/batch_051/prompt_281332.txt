I teach an upper-level seminar on responsibility for collective and institutional wrongdoing, and I would like a diagnostic instrument for the opening session: a decision tree whose leaves are the main philosophical views about complicity, reached entirely through responses to concrete cases rather than by asking students which label they identify with. The deliverable should be built from seven or eight brief cases, each followed by two or three forced-option responses. These questions should function as branch points, so please construct the logical space first: identify the principal questions on which positions about complicity come apart, map the answer-combinations those questions allow, and only then place the defensible positions as leaves, noting which combinations look unoccupied, unstable, or merely unexplored, and saying why in each case.

At least the following leaves should be separable: a strict causal-participation view, a shared-intention view, an enabling or facilitation view, an endorsement or expression view, a benefit-based view, and a structural or role-based view. Include at least these cases: a person lends a car to a friend he suspects will use it for a robbery; a person buys shares in a company after its history of pollution has become public knowledge; a guest at a professional dinner laughs at a bigoted joke; and a clerk processes paperwork for a policy she knows will be used to deport people unlawfully. I have one substantive suspicion I would like the instrument to test rather than assume: I suspect people often run together two different questions — whether the agent made the wrong worse or more likely, and whether the agent made the wrongdoer's norm violation his own — and that forcing these apart would populate the leaves differently than the familiar labels predict. Please build the tree so those two questions come apart, and for each case explain which leaves the question discriminates between and where each answer sends the respondent.
