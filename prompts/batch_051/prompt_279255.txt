Last night a friend and I spent hours arguing about freeganism: specifically, whether a vegan who keeps rescued battery hens may eat their eggs, whether someone who abstains from meat may wear leather taken from a cow that died of old age, and whether any of us may eat a beef dish a restaurant is about to discard. My friend kept reaching for the proverb "waste not, want not" as if it settled things. His line was that once the egg, the hide, or the plate of food already exists and would otherwise be wasted, using it cannot be wrong, and refusing it is what needs justification. I find this unsatisfying, because the proverb appears to compress several distinct claims—about thrift, about permission, about obligation, about causing demand, about complicity, about respect for the animals—and I suspect the readings strong enough to settle the argument are false, while the true readings are too weak to deliver his conclusion.

I would like a careful disambiguation of "waste not, want not" as it functions in this debate. Please lay out the distinct precise principles it could be expressing, as a menu, and give for each a verdict: trivial, plausible, or false, and whether it actually supports the conclusion that one may (or should) use these byproducts. Pay special attention to two fault lines our argument kept crossing. First, is the fundamental objection to animal products that purchasing them causes harm by increasing demand, or that using them expresses an attitude toward animals as resources? Second, is "using what would otherwise be wasted" a permission, an obligation, or merely a neutral fact that can be outweighed by reasons of symbolic protest, purity, or identity? My goal is to show my friend which readings would have to be true for his slogan to do the work he made it do, and which readings actually count against him.
