I'm a software engineer and I help run a small discussion group at a nondenominational church — about a dozen adults, 75 minutes a week. Lately we keep circling one problem: several people hold to sola scriptura, that Scripture is the only authority and its clear enough on the things that matter, but we still end up with three different readings of the same passage and somebody has to "win" or we punt it to the pastor. I want to run a session that makes the group feel why that keeps happening, without me standing up and lecturing about it.

Design a classroom-style activity (a game or structured simulation) for this group that stages the regress of interpretation: the text is the only authority, but whenever people disagree, somebody's interpretation still has to function as the authority. I want the activity to actually produce a live disagreement about a text in the room — ideally with people sincerely appealing to the plain meaning of the words against each other — before anyone names what's going on.

Constraints and things I'd like:
- 75 minutes total, so include timing for both the activity and the debrief.
- Use a deliberately ambiguous shared text, not actual Scripture at first. I don't want it to turn into a proxy fight about the Bible. Something like a one-page "house constitution" or a spec for a shared space would fit how my brain works — feel free to lean on software analogies (a spec with no reference implementation, no compiler for natural language, whatever gets the point across).
- The core rule of the game should be roughly: the text is the sole authority; nobody may appeal to outside knowledge, tradition, authorial intent, majority vote, or the pastor. The activity should let them discover that they still end up smuggling in an interpreter.
- Please actually write the ambiguous text(s) and the dispute cases as part of the materials, not just describe them.
- The takeaway I'm after is less a doctrine than a felt problem: two people can both be reasoning from the text alone and both be certain, and the text by itself doesn't arbitrate between them.
- End with a debrief that maps moments from the game onto the sola scriptura problem: the regress of interpretation, the claim that Scripture is clear about the important stuff, and the question of who decides what the text means. Be frank about what the analogy can't capture (e.g., the real text is Scripture held to be inspired, not just a deliberately gappy spec) and where the facilitator has to supply that piece.

And give me a short troubleshooting section: the likely failure modes on a first run (everyone agrees on the first dispute so no real argument happens, somebody refuses to drop "common sense", whatever else) and concrete fixes for each.
