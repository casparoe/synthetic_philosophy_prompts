I sit near two people who train models to do multi-step reasoning, and they've relitigated the same lunch argument for months. I honestly can't tell whose right, and I want someone to go through it move by move.

The setup: they're about to run a new experiment where the model gets a long-horizon task (multi-step planning, long math derivations, coding a non-toy program) and they need to decide what to give reward or feedback on. Dan wants outcome-based supervision: only the final answer gets a signal. Rhea wants process-based supervision: intermediate steps get signals too.

(1) Dan: the only thing we care about is whether the task ends up done correctly, so that's the only thing we should supervise. As soon as you reward intermediate steps, you're deciding in advance what good reasoning looks like, and the model will just learn to do reasoning that looks like yours. Good reasoning we didn't anticipate gets punished. So rewarding the outcome isn't an empirical compromise, it's the only non-question-begging target.

(2) Rhea: but outcome-only has an impossible credit assignment problem. If an eight-step plan fails at step 3 and you only judge the final answer, all eight steps get the same one weak scalar of blame, and the model can't tell which early move was wrong. Process supervision turns one weak end-of-episode signal into eight signals, one per step, so learning is faster per sample and the model can correct the mistake where it actually happened.

(3) Dan: that's only true where you already know the correct steps, and in those tasks you don't need a general model anyway. For open-ended tasks there's no unique correct process, so a human or model labeling steps isn't checking "correct," they're checking "looks like how I would do it." That's style dressed up as substance. Worse, process supervision invites reward hacking: the model learns to write steps that the process reward model likes, then does its actual reasoning off to the side or through tool calls the supervisor doesn't see. The final answer is at least the thing you ultimately want to get right.

(4) Rhea: the final answer isn't ungameable either. A single scalar after a long trajectory is weak enough that models learn superstitious stuff about final outputs: verbosity, formatting, certain phrases that correlates with a pass under the label distribution? More importantly, in a huge class of tasks the style objection is overblown because steps can be checked for local validity without committing to our favorite route: this line follows from the previous line, this intermediate quantity is correct, this lemma applies here. If every step passes that check and the chain reaches an answer, then rewarding those steps isn't style enforcement. It's checking that each step is the kind of thing that supports the next one.

(5) Dan: a chain of locally valid steps can be a long dead end, and a dead-end step often feels subjectively reasonable. Local validity isn't progress. If you reward local validity, the model can wander through unobjectionable but useless steps forever. Outcome pressure is the only thing that forces the model to actually reach the answer instead of performing competence.

(6) Rhea: but outcome pressure only forces the model to reach something the reward model will call an answer. With a weak scalar, that condition is satisfied by a lot of junk that ends in the right final string for the wrong reasons. And the wandering problem can be handled by making the step label include something like relevance, or "this step makes the task strictly easier," not just formal validity. So the real disagreement is: given that both signals are gameable, which failure mode is cheaper to catch and correct. For long trajectories I'd bet on decent process labels.
