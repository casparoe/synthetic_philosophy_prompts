Consider two verdicts that ordinary life delivers with equal confidence.

First: a man who cannot say what he loves about his partner — who can name no trait or quality of hers that anchors his love — is usually thought to love badly. We call his love blind, unmoored, a projection; we treat a love answerable to nothing in the beloved as defective as love.

Second: a woman who loves her partner for his warmth, wit, and generosity, and whose love tracks those qualities the way an appraisal tracks its object, is also usually thought to love badly. If, upon meeting someone warmer, wittier, and more generous, she found her love transferring, or judged that it ought to transfer, we would say she never loved the person at all, only a set of transferable properties that he happened to instantiate, and that persons are not fungible in the way her attitude makes them.

The asymmetry is that love is expected to be responsive to the beloved's qualities but not responsive in the way appraisal is responsive to its object. A love indifferent to all qualities is criticized; a love obedient to them too faithfully is equally criticized. The very same sensitivity to traits appears to be both required and forbidden.

What principle, if any, generates both verdicts? Offer the strongest account you can of what a person loves when they love a person — the concrete individual, the individual's properties, the individual's history, a relation, or something else — and use it to test whether both ordinary verdicts can be preserved at once.

Address the hard case directly: if the beloved's kindness is a genuine reason for love, why is greater kindness in a rival not a better reason, and why is trading up not licensed by the same principle that makes the first man's love defective? If instead love bestows value on the beloved rather than appraising value already there, say what becomes of the first verdict — whether the answerless lover escapes criticism altogether — and whether you can accept that consequence.

Conclude with a verdict: can one principle keep both judgments, or must one be revised? If one must give way, say which, and at what cost to the ordinary understanding of love.
