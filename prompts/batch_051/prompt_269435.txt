For a piece I am writing on the science of indecision, I have been reading through the decision-theory literature on choices among equally good options, and the old puzzle of Buridan's ass keeps generating an argument that seems to prove too much. Set out cleanly, it runs like this. (1) If two options are perfectly symmetric—equally good, equally costly, equally supported by the available information—then nothing about the options can favor one over the other. (2) If a person nevertheless selects one of them, the selection must be explained by something in the person rather than by anything in the options. (3) Standard revealed-preference logic treats such a personal tilt as a preference, so the chosen option simply is the one the agent preferred. (4) It follows that a choice between perfectly symmetric options cannot occur; any actual choice reveals that the options were not symmetric for the chooser after all. (5) That conclusion seems to prove too much, because it makes genuine indifference invisible by definition and leaves the theory with no resources for tie-breaking rules, lotteries, default choices, or the familiar experience of choosing without feeling a preference. Where does this argument go wrong, and does a defensible account of choosing among equals have to abandon revealed preference, or only the claim that revealed preference tracks how the choice felt from the inside?
