The debate over whether computational irreducibility limits explanation has, as far as I can tell, been running in a loop for years, and I suspect the loop itself is the thing worth understanding. Let me set out the terrain as I hear it. One side, in the spirit of Wolfram's work, points to systems whose rules are trivially simple — elementary cellular automata such as rule 30, certain iterated maps — but whose behavior cannot be foreseen by any general shortcut. For such systems, they argue, there is in principle no faster path: to know the state at step n you must run something like n steps of the computation, so prediction collapses into simulation. From this they draw a limitative conclusion about explanation. If the only way to say what the system will do is to simulate it, then the future behavior cannot be compressed into anything shorter than the behavior itself; an account of a later state that is no shorter than the state is not an explanation but a replay. The other side replies that this builds compression into the very idea of explanation, which is exactly what is in question. To explain an outcome, they say, it is enough to specify the generating rule, the initial condition, and the fact that the evolution is deterministic; carrying out the trajectory is a legitimate form of derivation, much like working through a long proof. If you can hand someone the exact mechanism that produced a pattern, you have understood the pattern, and asking for a closed-form shortcut is an aesthetic preference rather than an epistemic requirement. The first side then objects that a story with no advance purchase on what will happen is not understanding but retrodiction; the second side answers that anticipation is a practical convenience, and that this whole exchange merely recapitulates, in computational dress, an older question about whether explanation must license prediction or only exhibit the productive structure.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about any fact about computation. Both sides seem to accept that the systems in question are computationally irreducible in the relevant sense, and both accept that the rule determines the behavior. The real disagreement looks to me like one about the aims of explanation: one side treats prediction, or at least compression, as constitutive of explanation; the other treats exhibiting the mechanism as sufficient. But I may be mislocating the fault line.
