I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am planning a feature on what early modern political philosophy can illuminate in contemporary democratic practice. For one section, I want a disciplined counterfactual rather than a summary: how would Spinoza assess microtargeted political advertising, understood as the use of behavioral data to deliver emotionally tailored campaign messages to very small groups of voters?

A model answering this should not need external sources, so I am supplying the Spinozist positions I want treated as fixed premises for the exercise. Please do not spend the response restating or contesting them.

- The commonwealth exists for peace and security of life and for the freedom to use one's own judgment; its aim is not to make citizens rational or virtuous.
- The multitude is led by the affects—hope, fear, indignation, group loyalty—more than by reason, and realistic politics must take that as its starting point.
- In a democracy the transfer of natural right is not absolute: citizens hand their right to the collectivity of which they remain members, so that rule is fundamentally the power of the many rather than of one or of a few.
- The sovereign's right is coextensive with its power; when a government tries to regulate what it cannot effectively control, it weakens itself instead of achieving its aim.
- A broad liberty to hold and express one's own judgment can be allowed without endangering public peace, but speech or conduct that is seditious—that tends to dissolve the bonds of obedience holding the commonwealth together—falls within the sovereign's legitimate reach.
- The more serious threat to a free commonwealth comes from factional or clerical actors who manipulate superstition and fear to turn the multitude against its own interest.
- Because rulers and citizens alike act from passion, stability depends on institutions that make serving the common good the path of self-interest rather than on anyone's private virtue.

Taking only these premises as your Spinoza, give me exactly five predictions, numbered 1 through 5 and ordered from the one you are most confident in to the one you are least confident in, about how Spinoza would judge microtargeted political advertising as a feature of democratic elections. Each item should be one or two sentences, stated as a definite claim, and should name the premise or pair of premises doing the work; where two premises would pull in opposite directions, say which one wins and why. No introduction, no conclusion, and nothing outside the numbered list.
