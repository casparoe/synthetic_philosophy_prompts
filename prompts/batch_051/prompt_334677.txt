I don't have any philosophy background, but I've been mulling over something I think gets called the paradox of deontology, and I'm trying to turn it into a short essay. Here's the idea so far.

People who think it's wrong to kill one innocent person even to stop five other murders usually defend the ban by pointing to how awful it is to be killed. My draft argues that this defence keeps threatening to flip on itself: if what justifies the rule is the awfulness of people being killed, then five people being killed is worse than one, so the rule should tell you to do the one killing to prevent the five. The standard fix is to say no, the rule is not about minimising deaths; it's a constraint on what you personally may do, whatever the consequences. But then the rule seems to stop being about the victims at all and becomes about you — keeping your own hands clean or preserving the shape of your own agency.

My main claim is that this isn't necessarily a fatal problem: any rule addressed to me has to be about what my choice makes true, not about the total number of killings in the world, because I can't choose that total directly. But then I hit a wall, because I can't see why anyone else should care more about the shape of my agency than about the five lives that would be lost if I keep my hands clean. And that feels like a worse kind of self-centredness than the one the deontologist was trying to avoid.

Could you give me exactly five comments on this idea, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion? Number them 1 to 5. I'm most intrested in whether the distinction I'm drawing between what my choice makes true and the total number of killings in the world actually does the work I want it to, whether the final worry is as serious as I think, and what the weakest step in the arguement is.
