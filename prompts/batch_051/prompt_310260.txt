In my ethics seminar, the assigned textbook ends its chapter on well-being with a boxed summary that runs roughly as follows. Hedonism is the view that pleasure is the only thing that is good in itself. For the view to be more than a slogan, 'pleasure' must pick out a single introspectible feeling, one felt quality of pleasantness shared by every pleasant state. But the states we call pleasant share no such quality: the comfort of a warm bath, the satisfaction of finishing a difficult proof, the thrill of watching a rival fail, and the relief when a pain subsides are different in kind, not merely in intensity and duration. That is the heterogeneity problem for hedonism. Since the one common feeling hedonism needs does not exist, 'pleasure' refers only to a disunified heap of states, so talk of comparing or adding up pleasures is empty. This is why hedonism is now widely rejected. I think the objection is getting at something right, but the summary seems to move very quickly from 'these experiences differ' to 'hedonism is refuted.' I want to know what to trust in it. Which parts of the summary would a careful hedonist accept, which parts overstate or conflate distinct claims, and does the final conclusion follow from the accurate part? If a hedonist can grant that pleasant experiences are heterogeneous and still hold that pleasure is one kind of thing, what could the relevant unity consist in?
