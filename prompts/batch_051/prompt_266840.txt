I've been going back and forth with a housemate about Leibniz, and I want a second opinion. The usual move is to point at some appalling disaster and say the actual world obviously isn't the best possible one. But he says that's judging the world on the wrong metric altogether. His version: God isn't maximizing the summed happiness of creatures; he's maximizing a trade-off between variety and order — the sheer range of different things that exist and the amount of reality that gets filled in, weighed against how simple and general the natural laws are. A world with fewer local horrors but messier, ad-hoc laws would rank lower than ours, he says, the way a program full of hard-coded special cases is worse than one with a short uniform rule even if the uniform rule occasionally segfaults. Local evil isn't a bug so much as the price of keeping the lawbook short.

I find this almost too convenient; its like he's defined best so that the actual world automatically wins, and every complaint gets reclassified as a failure of taste on our part. Write an essay on whether this Leibnizian defense really carries weight or is just moving the goalposts. Develop the strongest version of the defense and the strongest version of the objection, but then commit to a verdict. Also tell me which consideration would most likely flip you to the other side, and where the weakest link in your reasoning is.
