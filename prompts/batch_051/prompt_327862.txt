I write about the science of concepts and language, mostly for readers outside academia. In reporting a piece on generics — the strange class of generalizations like "ducks lay eggs" that seem to tolerate exceptions — I sat down with a cognitive scientist who studies how people actually assess sentences of this form. I read her a list and asked only for snap judgments, true or false. Her verdicts were these: "Dogs have four legs": true. "Ducks lay eggs": true. "Mosquitoes carry malaria": true. "Canadians are right-handed": false. "Lions have manes": true. "Birds fly": true.

Those answers are what make the story interesting. She rejects "Canadians are right-handed" even though the overwhelming majority of Canadians are right-handed, and she accepts "mosquitoes carry malaria" even though only a tiny fraction of mosquitoes carry it. Whatever rule she is running, it is not simply tracking statistical prevalence. She seems to care about whether a property is genuinely characteristic of the kind — built into its normal form or role, or so salient and consequential that a low-frequency property still counts.

She has not yet told me her view on one sentence I want to test: "Canadians are polite." I would like you to predict, as best you can from her track record, whether she will call that sentence true or false. Give me your best guess, then make your reasoning explicit: state the rule or rules that best fit her six verdicts, say which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog to "Canadians are polite," and be honest about whether her existing verdicts are sufficient to force a single answer. If two well-motivated rules fit everything she has said so far and point in different directions here, describe both and say which you think she is running and why. End by naming one additional generic sentence whose judgment would best separate those rival rules. Whatever your substantive answer, make the final line of your reply the predicted verdict in machine-readable form: \answer{true} or \answer{false}.
