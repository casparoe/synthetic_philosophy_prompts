In much contemporary moral psychology, weakness of will is treated as a datum: a person can judge a course of action to be best and yet fail to take it. The Ming-dynasty Confucian Wang Yangming's doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action appears to deny this. For him, knowledge and action are not a sequence in which cognition comes first and conduct follows; they are aspects of a single achievement, so that a person who can state what virtue requires but does not enact it does not yet know it in the relevant sense. The dispute deadlocks in a distinctive way. The akrasia theorist presents the gambler who says, 'I know I should stop,' while reaching for the next bet, and calls this knowledge without action. The Yangmingan replies that the gambler's avowal is precisely the sort of thing that fails to count as genuine knowledge: it is assent, or borrowed talk, or self-deception. The akrasia theorist then objects that this is a no-true-Scotsman move, and that the doctrine becomes vacuous if every apparent case of knowledge without action is reclassified as not-knowledge.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is partly verbal and partly substantive. The two sides may be using 'knowledge' for different states: a contentful judgement that can be held while the will remains divided, versus a fully integrated understanding that already engages the will. But they may also genuinely disagree about which of those states is doing the explanatory work when a sincere moral judgement issues in action. I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the impasse verbal, substantive, or two groups answering different questions under a single heading?
