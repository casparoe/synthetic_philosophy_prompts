I'm taking a philosophy of maths module this term and I've got a bit stuck on a hunch about the indispensability argument. As I understand it, the argument says our best scientific theories quantify over mathematical objects in ways we can't easily rewrite away, and since we're committed to those theories we should be committed to the numbers too. My lecturer keeps reaching for the same examples to show maths doing genuine explanatory work — cicadas with prime-numbered life cycles, honeycombs and hexagons — where the mathematics seems to explain the pattern rather than just describe it.

But the more I look at how science is actually done now, the more I wonder whether the argument was built for a picture of science that's quietly disappearing. In climate modelling, protein folding, or epidemiology, the "theory" is often a giant simulation or a trained model that no one can print out as a clean set of laws. The mathematics is definitely in there — in the code, the architecture, the equations of the simulation — but it doesn't show up the way the old examples do, as a fact about primes explaining a biological pattern. Scientists often flat out say the aim is prediction, and treat the model's inner workings as a black box.

So does the indispensability argument survive when our best science moves from law-based theories to computational prediction? Which of its premises is actually under pressure — is it the claim that mathematics is indispensable, or the claim that being indispensable to our best science is a guide to what exists? And if it does survive, what does it now commit us to: numbers, algorithms, whole computational models, or something else?
