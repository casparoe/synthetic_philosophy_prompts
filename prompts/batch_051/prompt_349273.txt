I've spent the last few weeks trying to decide whether I actually accept David Benatar's asymmetry, and I find that I do, at least provisionally. The view, as I understand it, is this: the presence of pain is bad and the presence of pleasure is good; the absence of pain is good even when there is no one who enjoys that good; but the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is someone who is deprived of it. On this basis, the comparison between a life that is lived and that same life's never being lived is not symmetric: nonexistence gets credit for the pain it avoids but incurs no debit for the pleasure it forgoes, which is why Benatar can say that coming into existence is always a harm even when a life is, on its own terms, well worth living.

I'd like you to take that position at face value and tell me what would count against it. I'm not asking for a general critique or a summary of objections; I want a map of its exposure. What would have to be true—about value, about the metaphysics of nonexistence, about our reactive attitudes, or about the structure of practical reasons—for the asymmetry to be false? Distinguish considerations that would be decisive if established from those that would merely embarrass the view, and identify any apparent counterexamples that dissolve once you see they are aimed at a stronger thesis about the badness of all lives rather than at the asymmetry itself. Be honest about the worst problem for the view: the suspicion that the asymmetry is a projection of our retrospective self-interested preferences onto impersonal value, and that it cannot be stabilized without collapsing into an ordinary person-affecting principle that no longer yields the anti-natal conclusion. I would find it most persuasive to be shown a case, an argument, or a formal result in value theory in which the asymmetry must be revised or abandoned on pain of incoherence, rather than merely being given an intuition that competes with it.

Finally, say whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—whether its defenders can always redescribe any alleged defeater as a misunderstanding of how value is indexed to persons—and, if so, whether that insulation is a defect in an axiological claim of this kind or a reasonable feature of a claim about the value of absent persons. Please keep the response under 1,200 words.
