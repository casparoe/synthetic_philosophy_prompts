I've spent the last few weeks inside a dispute about whether public life has its own moral logic, and I've come out of it pulled in two directions, each of which seems to me strongest exactly when I try to articulate the other side to someone else. The first is a view I'll call moral single-source: the thought that a political role cannot change the deontic landscape. Whatever a mayor, a minister, or a general may permissibly do is settled by the very same principles that bind an ordinary person in an emergency, because offices are social arrangements and cannot on their own generate new permissions or prohibitions. A soldier or a spy is not excused from a moral demand merely because a role asked it of them. The attraction here is genuine: it gives morality a kind of architectural simplicity, it blocks the slide toward a two-track ethics in which reasons of state become a standing license, and it explains why we resent officials who treat their office as a costume that changes who they are.

But I also have an intuition, almost a felt datum, from the cases that first made the problem famous. In the standard sort of case, an official orders something that would be wrong for anyone to do in normal circumstances—think of authorizing the torture of a detainee thought to know the site of an imminent bombing—and, by hypothesis, this is the only way to avert a far greater disaster and the official's judgment about the facts is right. My intuition is that the official ought to do it, and that having done it, they are in a different moral position than before: not merely sad, not merely unlucky, but answerable, marked, under a burden to acknowledge what they did without their office laundering the acknowledgment away. The single-source view seems to deny that anything of this sort can exist; all it can offer is regret, which is a mood rather than a moral verdict. And yet I am not sure this intuition is more reliable than the argument.

I don't want the tension resolved by stipulation—for example, by simply defining regret as a kind of remainder—unless the stipulation is made to earn its keep, and I'm not looking for a mechanical ranking of the two sides. What I'm after is a diagnosis of which should yield. Is the intuition a product of imaginative over-coherence, quietly picturing the case so that the conclusion is already assumed? Or does the single-source view owe me an account of why office-holding cannot create a genuine residue, and does it fail to pay that debt? Is there a stable third position on which a political role changes the first-personal relation—what the agent owes afterward—without changing the all-things-considered verdict on the act? And if all three can be made internally consistent, what considerations should decide among them?
