I am a journalist covering science and ideas, and I am preparing a long feature on what it means to say that a machine or a person has learned a concept from a very small number of examples. The phrase is used constantly in discussions of few-shot learning, but I suspect it is covering several quite different achievements. I would like a working definition of "learning a concept from a small number of examples" that can be applied uniformly to the following cases; I have my own instincts about some of them, but I want the definition to force the verdicts rather than the other way round.

(a) A child shown one photograph of a tapir who later identifies tapirs in new photographs, including ones taken from unfamiliar angles, while rejecting similar animals.

(b) A radiologist who has reviewed four scans of a rare lesion and then flags it in new patients' scans.

(c) A nearest-neighbour classifier that stores every training image and labels each new image with the label of the stored image most similar to it.

(d) A large language model given three worked examples in a prompt, which then continues the pattern correctly on genuinely new inputs.

(e) A person who solves a Bongard problem—finding the rule that distinguishes six left-hand drawings from six right-hand drawings—and can state the rule in words.

(f) A system that sorts the twelve panels of a particular Bongard problem correctly but performs no better than chance on new panels instantiating the same rule.

(g) A child who uses the word "uncle" correctly for her own relatives but cannot say whether a newly introduced person would count as one.

(h) A rat rewarded for choosing one of two shapes after only two training trials, which later chooses the rewarded shape at novel sizes and orientations.

(i) A student who has memorised a rule in words but cannot apply it to examples that differ superficially from those he rehearsed.

(j) A linear classifier fitted to two points from each of two classes in a two-dimensional feature space and tested on new points.

I would like the following: a definition precise enough to return a verdict for each case; the definition applied to all ten cases, with an indication of which verdicts are borderline and what additional fact about a case would settle the matter; an explicit account of where concept learning ends and mere memorisation, retrieval, or pattern matching begins, and whether that line can be drawn in the same way for humans and machines; and finally, a note on which of your verdicts is least secure and what evidence would change it. If your definition delivers verdicts that clash with ordinary usage, either defend the mismatch or revise the definition.

Please separate what is settled about how the people and systems above actually behave from what is genuinely contested among cognitive scientists and machine-learning researchers about which of them count as concept learners, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. I would also be grateful if the answer were written in British English.
