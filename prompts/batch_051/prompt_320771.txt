I keep running into this and I can't sort it out. If a city announced tomorrow that it was putting up a brand-new statue of a long-dead general who ordered massacres — even if he also donated the library — almost everyone would say that's wrong, and future generations would be right to blame them. But if the exact same statue has been standing in the main square since 1920, a lot of the same people say it's fine to leave it, or at least that keeping it is just neutral preservation while ripping it down is the loaded political act. Same bronze, same man, same deeds; the only thing that changed is whether the honor is being started now or was inherited. And street names make it worse: people who'd fight a new statue of the guy will walk past the avenue named after him every day and never see it as the same kind of problem. So what I want to know is whether anything actually justifies that gap. Is there a real moral difference between initiating a commemmoration and merely failing to end one, and between a figure on a plinth and a name on a map? Give me the strongest principle that could justify the asymmetry, push it against the hard cases (how old is old enough? what if the street name is all that's left and nobody remembers who he was?), and then say honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept or given up.
