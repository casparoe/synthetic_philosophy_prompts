I teach critical thinking to a mixed-ability Year 12 class — 17 students, one 75-minute double period — and we've just spent three lessons on causation in history. The trouble is that my students can now say 'causes can be overdetermined, so no single factor is the whole cause' with perfectly straight faces, but it hasn't shifted how they actually think; the next essay they hand in will still say the Black Death caused the Peasants' Revolt and leave it at that. I'd like to run a session that makes them feel the overdetermination problem themselves before it gets named, so the vocabulary has something to hook onto. The usual examples — two arsonists setting the same house on fire, two snipers shooting at the same instant — leave this lot cold; there's no historical texture for them to get invested in. Could you design a activity in which the class has to operate as some kind of commission or inquest trying to assign causes for a made-up historical event (a rebellion, a famine, a bridge collapse, whatever works), where the preloaded background is arranged so that at least two causes are each sufficient for the outcome? They should run into the weirdness themselves: removing either cause leaves the event in place, even though everyone at the table wants to say both causes were real and important.
