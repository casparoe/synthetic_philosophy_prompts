I've been trying to get my head round what quantum mechanics does to Leibniz's principle that two things can't be exactly alike in every respect and still be two. I've put together an argument of my own, and it seems to show the principle goes, but the conclusion feels a bit too strong, so I suspect I've smuggled something past myself.

(1) If two things are genuinely two, there must be something true of one that is not true of the other; otherwise 'two' is just a manner of speaking and there is really only one.
(2) Take the textbook state of the two electrons in a helium atom (the spin-singlet ground state). The physics is completely symmetric between them: every single-electron property or observable gives the same answer for each electron, and swapping their labels leaves the joint state exactly the same (or at most flips its overall sign, which changes no observation).
(3) So, by (1), the two electrons are not genuinely two.
(4) But quantum mechanics also says the atom really does contain two electrons, not one.
(5) Hence Leibniz's principle fails at the quantum level: there are distinct things that differ in no respect at all. To make sense of that, each electron must have a primitive thisness, a bare identity over and above all it's properties and relations.

Is this argument valid? If not, tell me exactly which step fails and what the correct version of that step would be. If it is valid, are we really stuck with primitive thisnesses, or is there a third way of describing the electrons that keeps the principle without them?
