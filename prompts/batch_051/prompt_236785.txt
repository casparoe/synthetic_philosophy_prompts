I have been reading around the foundations of arithmetic for years, both in the specialist literature and in rationalist-adjacent blog and forum commentary, and one dispute has stalled in a way that makes me suspect the parties are no longer answering the same question. The mathematical facts are common ground. The natural numbers can be modelled in set theory in at least two standard ways. On the von Neumann reduction, each number is the set of its predecessors: 0 is the empty set, 1 is {∅}, 2 is {∅,{∅}}, and 4 has 2 among its elements. On the Zermelo reduction, each successor is the singleton of its predecessor: 0 is the empty set, 4 is a fourfold nesting of singletons, and 2 is not among its elements. Addition, multiplication and induction are preserved under both reductions, and under indefinitely many others. So the small question around which the dispute turns — is 2 an element of 4? — receives one answer relative to the first reduction and the opposite answer relative to the second, while nothing internal to arithmetic chooses between them.

The set-theoretic reductionist is drawn, and perhaps committed, to saying there is a fact of the matter about whether 2 is an element of 4: one reduction is correct about what numbers really are, or at any rate the question has a determinate answer once a reduction is fixed, and anyone who denies this owes an account of what '2' denotes if not a set. The structuralist replies that numbers are only positions in the natural-number structure; that the structure fixes successor, order, addition and multiplication but not elementhood; and that a question not invariant across isomorphic copies of the structure is not a genuine question about numbers. On this view 'Is 2 an element of 4?' is at best a question about a chosen model, much as 'Is the knight carved from this block of wood?' is a question about a token piece rather than about the role 'knight' in chess.

My working theory is that the real disagreement concerns explanatory obligations: what must a theory of arithmetic account for before it counts as saying what numbers are? The reductionist wants a theory to assign a reference to every numeral, so any elementhood sentence built from numerals must end up true or false. The structuralist wants a theory to account for the relations that make the theorems of arithmetic true, and treats questions that are artefacts of a particular reduction as not needing answers. On this diagnosis, the two sides agree on all the mathematics and on all accepted sentences of arithmetic, and differ only about which further sentences demand a verdict. But I can see at least two rival diagnoses. One is deflationary: 'number' is being used differently — sometimes for a particular object, sometimes for a position fixed only by its structural role — so the dispute is close to verbal. The other is that a hidden semantic claim is doing the work, for instance that '2' in ordinary mathematical discourse is a rigid designator whose reference outruns any chosen reduction, and the argument about elementhood is downstream of that.

I want a diagnosis rather than a survey. Is this a substantive metaphysical dispute, a verbal dispute about the word 'number', a disagreement about explanatory burden, or two research programmes posing different questions under a single heading? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal invariance result, a distinction between object-questions and structure-questions, an explicitly relativised account of elementhood, or something else? And be honest about whether no progress should be expected, because the parties differ on a starting intuition about whether every well-formed question about numbers must have an answer.
