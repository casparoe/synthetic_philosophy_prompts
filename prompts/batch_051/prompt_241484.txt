I have been mediating a year-long argument between two friends, and I would like you to produce the document they should have written instead of going in circles. The dispute concerns whether, when we judge whether a change makes a family, a city, or the whole world better, we should care about the average level of well-being or the total amount of it. One friend, an actuary, holds that the average is what matters: adding people makes things worse whenever it lowers the average quality of life of everyone involved, even if the total amount of happiness goes up. The other, a pediatric nurse, holds that the total is what matters: if each added person has a life that is good from the inside, then the larger group is better in the sense that counts, even if the average dips. The same argument keeps surfacing in concrete decisions: whether to have a third child when both parents suspect it will slightly reduce the attention, money, and energy available per child while still being a loved and welcome life; whether a city should keep building housing when newcomers slightly lower current residents' average reported satisfaction while increasing the total number of people living decent-to-happy lives; and whether the extinction of a species should be mourned when its last members lead only barely positive lives and their absence would not change average welfare anywhere.

Please draft this as the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the two camps, which you can call the Average Camp and the Total Camp. I would like the document structured as follows. Begin with a numbered list of claims both camps can sign, including claims about cases in which average and total move together, claims about what each side is not committed to, and claims about what the disagreement is ultimately about. Then provide a disagreements table: for each point of disagreement, give neutral shared wording, followed by a formulation each camp would accept as a fair statement of its own view. Then identify the cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling them would cause at least one camp to revise its view; for each crux, describe a concrete case or observation both camps would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts that case would show. Finally, tell me whether any part of the dispute looks to you to be verbal rather than substantive, and if so, specify which part and why. The goal is not to decide who is right but to produce a statement both friends could sign while seeing exactly where they still differ.
