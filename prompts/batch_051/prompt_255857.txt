I'm a PhD student in computational linguistics, and I recently sat through a long argument between two colleagues after a seminar on neural machine translation. Mara is a semanticist; Daniel builds and evaluates transformer-based translation systems. Each was persuasive at different moments, and I couldn't referee them. Their exchange, compressed, went like this:

(1) Mara: We should stop describing these systems as translating. Translating is something an agent does in order to convey what a text means. What these systems do is select strings by their statistical fit to a training corpus. The output is often fluent and useful, but fluency is not semantic competence, and no amount of well-formed output adds up to understanding.

(2) Daniel: That simply moves the goalposts. The system reliably maps sentences in one language to sentences that bilingual speakers judge equivalent in meaning, register, and implicature. If that is not understanding, what behavioural evidence would establish understanding? If the criterion is one no computational system could ever meet, the word has stopped doing work.

(3) Mara: There is behavioural evidence of the right kind, and the systems fail it. When a pronoun has two plausible antecedents and only one fits the situation, I have seen systems pick the wrong one and produce a fluent translation that asserts the reverse of the original. Their errors are not noise; they are fluent, confident, and semantically blind, which is precisely what one expects from a device with no grip on what the words are about.

(4) Daniel: Human translators also misresolve ambiguous pronouns, and nobody concludes that they don't understand the language; they are guessing under uncertainty. The difference is one of degree of world knowledge and robustness, not a categorical absence. Internally, these models learn representations that track syntactic roles, sense distinctions, and coreference tendencies—that is rudimentary semantic structure.

(5) Mara: "Rudimentary semantic structure" is a metaphor doing philosophical work. A vector learned to predict tokens is not answerable to the world; the system cannot distinguish the river bank from the financial bank except through co-occurrence statistics. When a text exploits that ambiguity, or means both things at once, the system does not preserve the double reading; it picks whichever paraphrase the training distribution favours. That is not shallow understanding but no understanding, supported by good statistics.

(6) Daniel: Whether it preserves ambiguity is an empirical matter, and it sometimes does. The demand that understanding requires answerability to the world proves too much: children and simultaneous interpreters often cannot explain how they resolved an ambiguity, yet they understand. If understanding is graded—which it plainly is in animals and in learners—then the system has a thin grade, not none.

(7) Mara: The interpreter has seen rivers and banks; the model has seen tokens. A translator recreates a text in a new language because the original has done something to them, and they choose what to preserve—sound, register, allusion. A system cannot be moved by a text; it can only optimise a learned objective. On passages where what matters is not propositional content but tone or imagery, it flattens the text precisely because it has no idea what would be lost.

(8) Daniel: That sets an aesthetic standard many human translators also fail, and it shows limited capacity, not absence. If the real worry is that we will trust these systems for tasks they cannot do, that is an engineering and policy worry we can state without denying the partial competence they demonstrably have.
