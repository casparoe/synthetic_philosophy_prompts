I have been asked to teach a lesson in my world history class on the idea that the word "collapse" may misdescribe what happened in places like Easter Island. My classmates already know the standard story: the islanders cut down their palm forests to move the great statues, and this caused soil erosion, famine, warfare, and a population crash before Europeans arrived. I want the lesson to complicate that story without implying that nothing bad happened. Could you help me plan the sequence of the lesson rather than write it for me? I need to know what conceptual distinction must come first—for example, the difference between the end of a particular political or ceremonial system, a fall in population, and the disappearance of a people—and what has to be in place before students can reread the Easter Island evidence as something other than a straightforward failure. I would like a single carrying example to run through the lesson; I think Easter Island suits the purpose, but I need to know which features of the case to foreground and which to leave out, so the lesson does not become a dispute about one island's facts and does not drift into trying to survey the Maya and the Bronze Age as well. What misconceptions will students predictably form when they hear that the collapse story is contested—for instance, that no ecological damage occurred, or that the standard account has been debunked rather than complicated—and at what point in the sequence should each misconception be addressed? Finally, how should I check by the end that they have understood the underlying idea, namely that calling an episode a collapse is an interpretive choice about what counts as the society and what counts as its end, rather than merely learned new facts about Easter Island?
