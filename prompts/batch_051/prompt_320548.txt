I'm a reporter who covers science and tech — mostly AI — and I've been chewing on a parallel I can't decide whether is deep or just verbal. In machine learning there's this familiar failure mode: you optimize a model hard against one score, and past a certain point you get a system thats superb on that single objective and nearly useless everywhere else — overfitting, Goodhart's law, brittle generalization, however you want to frame it. The philosophical argument about moral saints (Susan Wolf's, I think?) seems to me to have the same shape. The idea there is that someone who makes moral goodness the single dominating aim of their life — not just a very decent person, but one who is always trying to be as morally good as possible — ends up impoverished in every non-moral register: no taste, no humor, no passions, nothing to talk about at dinner. So my question is whether a moral saint is basically a person overfit to one objective function. I can read it as a genuine point about single-minded optimization under tradeoffs, or as a lazy metaphor, since a human being doesn't literally have a loss landscape. What exactly would need to be true about moral psychology — what tradeoffs, what counts as the training distribution, whether there's anything like regularization — for the ML failure mode to really transfer? And what is one thing the parallel would predict about moral saints that is almost certainly false?
