I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about names that don't pick out anything real, and about what's true in fiction, and I keep going in circles. I was reading Frege's essay "On Sense and Reference" (the old translation, so it's a little stiff) and this sentence stopped me: "The words 'the celestial body most distant from the Earth' have a sense, but it is very doubtful if they also have a reference." I gather he means a phrase can have meaning even when there's no object, and maybe a whole sentence using it is neither true nor false. But my gut calls on cases don't line up with that.

Here are my calls. In the Sherlock Holmes stories, "Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street" feels true in the story, but if a friend tells me that as a claim about the actual world I'd say it's false, not just neither-true-nor-false. "Sherlock Holmes does not exist" feels plainly true, not truth-valueless. "Pegasus was a winged horse" is true as myth but false as zoology. "The celestial body most distant from the Earth is cold" I can understand, but I want to say you can't say that, because there isn't any such body, yet the sentence isn't meaningless. Also "Vulcan (the planet astronomers once searched for inside Mercury's orbit) was sought by astronomers" seems true historically even though Vulcan doesn't exist. Is there one principle that captures all this, or close? Does Frege's sentence already give it, or does it need fixing? And what does the principle say about this one I go back and forth on: "Sherlock Holmes was created by Conan Doyle" — that seems true, but it's about a nonexistent thing, so I don't know where it lands.
