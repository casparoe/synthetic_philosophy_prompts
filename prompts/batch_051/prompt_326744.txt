I'm taking a criminal law course this semester, and my professor has spent the last week on a made-up state statute that makes it a misdemeanor to sell any food that is unsafe, adulterated, or misbranded. The statute has no mens rea language beyond the word "sells," and she's been using it to push us on when punishing without fault is justified and when courts quietly build a fault requirement back in. She refuses to state the rule outright; she just gives her verdict on hypo after hypo, and I've been writing them down.

Her track record so far: she convicted a diner owner whose walk-in fridge had been failing for days and kept serving chicken out of it; she convicted a smoothie chain whose employees skipped the required berry-rinsing step and used a supplier with a recent contamination warning; she convicted a corner-store owner who sold soured milk because he trusted the dairy's invoice and never looked at the bulging cartons. But she acquitted a retiree who sold home-canned peaches at a church fundraiser, where one jar's seal failed after processing and the buyer got sick; the professor said the retiree had done everything a reasonable home canner could do.

Next week's problem is a meal-kit company that buys precooked, vacuum-sealed chicken from an inspected processing plant and handles, stores, and ships it under temperature monitoring with complete logs. A listeria outbreak gets traced to the chicken, but the contamination originated at the processor's facility and was not detectable by the meal-kit company. The company is charged under the same statute. What should I predict her verdict is, if she's being consistent with those four earlier cases?

Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the two or three most plausible rules consistent with her past verdicts; (2) what each rule predicts for the meal-kit case; (3) your best guess about her verdict, with the closest analog among the earlier cases; (4) what principle she'd be committing herself to if she went the other way; and (5) a further hypo that would cleanly seperate the candidate rules.
