I am facing a medical decision and would welcome help thinking it through. My doctors have proposed a treatment whose outcome is controlled by a genuinely quantum event: whether a particular atom in a prepared state decays within a fixed interval determines whether the drug is activated in the intended way or in a damaging way. The physics, as it has been explained to me, assigns a very large amplitude to the intended configuration and a very small amplitude to the damaging one—roughly one in ten thousand when the amplitudes are squared. I have come to accept the branching picture of quantum mechanics, on which, at the end of the interval, the world splits so that both configurations occur, and there is no further fact about which branch is the real one or which of the resulting people is really me.

My partner says the decision is straightforward: the damaging configuration has probability one in ten thousand, the expected outcome is strongly positive, and I should consent to the treatment. I cannot see why this follows. If I consent, there will be a branch in which the drug is activated in the damaging way and a successor of me suffers the harm; the small squared amplitude makes that branch no less actual. In what sense, then, is the damaging outcome improbable? And if it is not improbable in any sense that should guide a deliberating agent, what justifies weighing it so lightly against the intended outcome? Is my partner's reasoning sound, and if it is not, what is the correct way to reason about choices once the branching picture is taken literally?

Please write your answer in British English.
