I teach an upper-level philosophy of language seminar with fifteen students and a single 90-minute session. Design a classroom game that lets students run into Quine's indeterminacy of translation before I state it. The game should put small teams in the position of field linguists who may elicit only native assent or dissent to whole sentences under controlled perceptual conditions, while the native informants are working with private semantic rules that coincide over every sentence the linguists can test in the game yet diverge as soon as the manuals are extrapolated. By the end of play, each team should be able to produce two defensible but mutually incompatible translation manuals from the same transcript of elicited behavior, and should feel the pull of insisting that there must nonetheless be a hidden fact about which one is right. Give me the roles, the invented vocabulary and stimuli, the rules for what linguists may ask and what informants may answer, a scoring rule that requires no real money, the materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes. Then write a debrief that maps what happened onto the gavagai case, onto the claim that translation is underdetermined even by all possible behavioral evidence, and onto the difference between that claim and the tamer claim that the evidence merely happened to be sparse. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run the game—for instance, informants leaking their semantic rules or players reducing it to a codebreaking puzzle with one hidden right answer—and how to turn each failure back toward the point. End by saying honestly which feature of Quine's thesis the game cannot reproduce and what I will need to supply in a short closing lecture.
