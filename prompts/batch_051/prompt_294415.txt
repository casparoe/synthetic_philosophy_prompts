In my photography class this year, the teacher has repeatedly put forward an analogy I cannot quite shake: a photograph, he says, is like a window. When you look at a photograph, you are not merely looking at a picture of a thing; you are seeing the thing itself, just delayed, because the light reflected by the subject is what formed the image. From this he draws a stronger conclusion: that photographs carry a special kind of evidential authority that handmade images lack, because a photograph places the viewer in direct perceptual contact with the past, whereas a painting or drawing always passes through an artist's mind and hand.

For our school philosophy club I want to take this analogy apart rather than simply repeat it. Please give me a systematic audit of the photograph-as-window analogy. I would like you to do all of the following: lay out the proposed correspondences, such as what plays the role of the window frame, the glass, the light, the scene outside, and the act of looking, with a small table if that helps; identify the specific respects in which the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the teacher's conclusion from the ones that are merely incidental; identify what the window image quietly imports that a photograph may not have, since a window is live, can be looked through from different angles, and offers a continuous view, while a photograph is a fixed two-dimensional artifact that was framed, selected, and possibly altered by someone; and then assess whether the conclusion about the special evidential status of photographs survives once those differences are on the table.

Finally, I want you to commit: state a definite verdict on whether this analogy can actually support the teacher's claim, or whether a different analogy, such as a footprint, a fossil, or a cast, would carry the same weight more honestly. Give your reasons for the verdict and do not settle for merely listing both sides.
