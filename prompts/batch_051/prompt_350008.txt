I propose the following four marks of a science.

(i) Empirical testability. The programme's central claims concern how nature or computational systems actually behave, and they could in principle be shown false by observations or by systematic computer experiments.
(ii) Explanatory unification. The programme accounts for many apparently unrelated phenomena with a small set of simple rules.
(iii) Predictive novelty. It yields specific, non-obvious predictions that go beyond the evidence it began with and that can be checked independently.
(iv) Reproducible rigour. Its claims can be stated precisely, recomputed, and publicly criticised.

Consider, as a borderline case, the programme Stephen Wolfram advanced in A New Kind of Science (2002). The programme holds that we will understand complexity best by exhaustive exploration of simple computational rules, especially one-dimensional cellular automata, rather than by the usual equation-based models. Its showcase cases include rules such as Rule 30, a deterministic three-cell cellular automaton whose evolution looks random; Wolfram's suggestion is that rules of this kind underlie the apparent randomness and complexity found in nature, from turbulence to biological pattern formation. The programme also advances the principle of computational equivalence: roughly, that almost all processes that are not obviously simple are equivalent in their computational sophistication, so that there is no important intermediate grade between simple and universal systems.

Does this programme qualify as a science under the four marks above? Reply in exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Items one to four should assess marks (i) to (iv) in order, saying plainly where the programme meets the mark, where it falls short, and where the mark is too blunt to settle the matter. Item five should state whether the borderline character of this case shows that one of the four marks needs revising, and if so, which mark and how. Write in British English.
