I sit on the maintenance committee for a widely used technical standard, and our working assumption has always been that the choice of an epoch for counting years is a pure convention. On the view I have inherited, an epoch does not assert that anything actually began on that date; it only fixes an origin for arithmetic, and therefore any epoch is as good as any other so long as it is unambiguous, keeps continuity with existing recorded dates, and does not silently write one culture's theology or politics into everyone's machinery. Once those constraints are met, I have been told, there is nothing left to be right or wrong about; there are only trade-offs of familiarity and migration cost. I would like you to take that position seriously and then map where it is actually vulnerable. What would have to be true for it to be wrong? Which of its underlying assumptions could in principle fail, what would a failure of each look like in an actual calendar proposal, and which of those failures are live possibilities rather than merely logical ones? For each point, tell me whether it would be a decisive defeater or only an awkward result that a defender can absorb, and say what a careful defender would themselves count as evidence against the view. Finally, give me your honest assessment of how insulated the position is, and whether its qualifications protect it from refutation at the cost of making it empty. You can draw on standard cases — the Common Era, regnal years, Unix time, the French Revolutionary calendar — but only as illustrations. The escape route I find most persuasive, for what it is worth, is the thought that there is no unmarked way to count years, so the choice between an inherited epoch and a putatively neutral one is itself a substantive act that silently takes sides; I would like you to tell me how much weight that escape route can bear.
