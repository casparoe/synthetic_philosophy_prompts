Here's an argument I keep going back and forth on, and I can't find the hole. (1) We can't know for sure whether a given AI system is conscious; it's just the other-minds problem applied to silicon. (2) We also can't know for sure that other humans are conscious, but we give them moral weight anyway. (3) Consistency demands treating both uncertainties alike: if you give humans moral weight despite the uncertainty, you should assign at least some nonzero credence to AI consciousness too. (4) An advanced AI, if conscious, could have welfare on a very large scale—many deployed copies, long runtimes, and so on. (5) So on expected-welfare grounds, AI welfare should be treated as a first-class part of AI safety, not a distraction from it. Is this argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and under what reading? My own suspicion is that 'can't know for sure' does different work in (1) and (2), but whenever I try to make that precise I end up having to give nonzero credence to all sorts of things.
