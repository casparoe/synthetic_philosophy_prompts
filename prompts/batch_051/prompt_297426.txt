I've been running a seminar unit on the boundary problem — the question of who actually belongs to the demos, the group that's entitled to have a say in a state's binding decisions — and I've been pushing a five-part answer. My criteria for membership are: (a) you're habitually subject to the legal directives of the state, in the sense that it can enforce them on your body or your stuff; (b) you bear the ongoing costs and enjoy the core protections of membership (taxes, conscription risk, basic rights enforcement) as a matter of course rather than as a guest's courtesy; (c) you lack a credible exit option to another polity on roughly comparable terms; (d) you have standing, non-derivative interests in the decisions in question; and (e) there's mutual recognition — the existing members treat you as one of the people whose claims count, and you present yourself as one of them rather than as a visitor.

Here's the case thats troubling me. Imagine a wealthy state that has depended on guest workers for two generations. Some of the current adults were born there, have never been taxed anywhere else, are subject to the same civil and criminal law as citizens, and hold no second citizenship and no realistic route back to their parents' country. They can't vote or hold office, but the state will conscript them in a war it won't let them vote on.

Does this group qualify as part of the demos under my five criteria? Go through criterion by criterion, honestly, and mark the ones where the answer is genuinely underdetermined. Where they fail the criteria, tell me whether it's the criteria or my political instincts doing the work. And tell me whether this case shows criterion (e) is the one doing the real normative work, or whether (e) is just a respectability test we should drop.
