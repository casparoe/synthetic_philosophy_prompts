I am working through the final chapter of Principia Ethica, and I want to test whether Moore’s conclusion about the value of friendship and beauty can be reconstructed as a valid argument rather than as a set of appeals to intuition. Treat the following passage as the primary target (Principia Ethica, Chapter VI, §113):

“By far the most valuable things, which we know or can imagine, are certain states of consciousness, which may be roughly described as the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects. No one, probably, who has asked himself the question, has ever doubted that personal affection and the appreciation of what is beautiful in Art or Nature, are good in themselves; nor, if we consider strictly what things are worth having purely for their own sakes, does it appear probable that any one will think that anything else has nearly so great a value as the things which are included under these two heads.”

Here is my reconstruction of the argument that leads from Moore’s method to his conclusion:

(1) A kind of thing has intrinsic value only if it passes the isolation test: if it existed absolutely by itself, a properly reflective judge would still judge its existence to be good.

(2) The test must be run by isolating each candidate, not by asking what is necessary here and now or what we would regret losing.

(3) Run that way, bare pleasure, bare knowledge, bare virtue, and the mere existence of beautiful objects either fail the test or show only negligible value.

(4) Two kinds of complex wholes pass the test decisively: the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects.

(5) In those wholes, the value does not reside solely in the pleasure they contain; the other constituents, which have little or no value in isolation, are equally responsible for the value of the whole.

(6) Therefore personal affection and aesthetic enjoyment are not only good in themselves, but are by far the greatest goods we can imagine; and all other goods and duties are justified only insofar as they bring about as much of these two as possible.
