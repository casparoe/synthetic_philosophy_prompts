Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the gender-equality paradox is real: the differences between men's and women's educational and occupational choices are wider, not narrower, in countries that score higher on standard measures of gender equality. One way to interpret this is that greater equality lets underlying preferences express themselves more freely; another is that these preferences have themselves been produced by gendered environments, so the resulting choices are not straightforwardly free. Deciding between these interpretations requires a working definition of what it is for a career choice to be free.

I want such a definition, tested against the following cases.

(a) A woman in an affluent and strongly egalitarian country, under no financial pressure and actively encouraged to consider every field, chooses nursing rather than engineering because she finds nursing more appealing.

(b) A woman chooses engineering solely because her family has threatened to disown her if she does not.

(c) A man would prefer to work in childcare but becomes a software engineer because he anticipates constant mockery in the former occupation; he regards the mockery as regrettable but simply how things are.

(d) A woman becomes a teacher because every adult woman she admired as a child was a teacher, and no one ever presented other professions to her as realistic possibilities; she says she might well have chosen differently had she seen more women in engineering.

(e) A person chooses a sex-atypical occupation after a long period of reflection, largely in order to defy gender expectations, and now wholeheartedly endorses the choice.

(f) A person in severe poverty has exactly one realistic avenue of paid work and sincerely says that taking it is their choice; they show no sign of resentment.

(g) A person follows a stable, felt preference towards an occupation, a preference very likely formed by childhood exposure to strongly gendered toys, media and role models, but one they have since reflected on and now fully endorse.

I am confident that (b) is not free and that (a) is free; I am uncertain about (c) through (g), and I suspect the dispute between the two interpretations above turns on how one handles (d) and (g).

Propose the definition, apply it to each case, and say where it yields a verdict you would be reluctant to defend or no verdict at all; then revise the definition or defend those verdicts, and indicate which classification you find least secure. If you think the phrase does two distinguishable jobs—one concerned with the absence of external constraint and one with whether the preference is authentically the person's own—give both working definitions and state the cases on which they disagree.
