I am a PhD candidate in political philosophy, and I have been asked to serve on a university working group that advises the president on whether to issue a formal public apology for the university's past wrongs. From the 1890s through the 1960s, the university refused to admit students from one ethnic group, and it accepted large gifts — including named buildings and endowed chairs — from benefactors whose wealth depended on that group's forced labor. The administrators and benefactors responsible are now dead, as are nearly all of the people directly excluded.

The working group is split. One side argues that an institution cannot meaningfully apologize for what people long dead did: those who would now speak for the university committed none of it, and an apology directed at people who are no longer alive is addressed to no one in particular. Another side replies that the university is a continuing institution that still holds the money, buildings, and standing these wrongs helped create, and that it therefore should give a public accounting of itself. A third side argues that an apology without concrete measures — changes to admissions, or material support for surviving descendants — would be empty words regardless of its symbolic intent.

I have been asked to draft a short internal memo setting out what the group should recommend. I am not looking for a legal or historical assessment; I want help with the underlying question of whether such an apology is worth making now, to whom it would be addressed, and whether its value depends on accompanying actions. Please say how confident you are in the position you argue for and which point you are least sure about. Please write the answer in American English.
