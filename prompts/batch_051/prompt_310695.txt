I'm redesigning an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "The Ethics of Consent" that draws its cases from medicine, research ethics, sexual ethics, and contract, and I want to restructure the opening weeks around a question of method rather than a survey of positions. In past years, students came away thinking consent theory is just a heap of edge cases with no fixed core. This year I want them to start by specifying what a theory of valid consent would have to get right before they meet any named account.

Write me the working document I would hand out: a list of desiderata that any adequate account of valid consent should satisfy, each paired with the case or consideration that motivates it, and each marked as either non-negotiable (a theory that drops it is not a theory of consent at all) or contested (a commitment of a particular camp, not a point of consensus). The list should cover: the difference between mere assent and valid authorization, and what consent so understood contributes to the moral permissibility of another person's action; the standard invalidators — threats, deception, incompetence, and the subtler cases of undue inducement and asymmetric information — and which of them defeat consent entirely as against merely making an act regrettable or wrong on independent grounds; why consent can be given while not "meant," and whether a change of mind that is never communicated changes anything for someone who acts in reliance on the earlier signal; the scope of consent, meaning consent to which act under which description of that act; whether the understanding consent requires is scalar or a threshold matter; and when withdrawal is enough to revoke authorization that is already in progress. I especially want the section that comes after the list: identify which subsets of the requirements sit in tension — for example, requirements pushing consent toward tracking the agent's actual will versus requirements making consent publicly knowable and reliable for others, or requirements of full disclosure versus the fact that ordinary competent adults consent competently under partial understanding. Where two requirements pull against each other, say whether that is outright inconsistency, an unavoidable trade-off, or simply a difference in how stringently the theory sets the bar. Then say which single requirement does the most work to exclude otherwise plausible candidate views, and briefly indicate how four broad styles of theory — procedural/autonomous, substantive/well-being-based, rights-waiver, and communicative-performative — would fare against your first three non-negotiable requirements. Please do not turn this into an annotated bibliography or reading list; I am not asking for citations, and I will supply the texts myself.
