I keep noticing a pattern in how natural languages handle ambiguity, and I would like to know whether it has an established name or whether I have only half-seen something.

The familiar data: "bank" is both a financial institution and the side of a river; "pitch" can be a throw, the highness or lowness of a sound, or a dark resin; "pen" is both a writing instrument and an enclosure. Yet conversation almost never stalls over these. Context, syntax, and topic silently select the intended reading, and the ambiguity becomes visible mainly when a sentence is built to lead us down one path first, as in garden-path humour. Languages also lean heavily on pitch and tone to keep forms apart: in Mandarin, the same sequence of consonants and vowels, ma, does different lexical work according to its pitch contour (mother, hemp, horse, scold). To a speaker of a non-tonal language those look like homophones; to a Mandarin speaker the contour is part of the word itself.

The pattern I am after sits underneath this. Languages seem to let ambiguity accumulate exactly where disambiguation is cheap — among short, frequent, highly predictable items — while keeping rarer or more specialized vocabulary specific. It is as if the lexicon trades away formal distinctness against something else: one short form does several jobs and saves articulation and memory, provided the context is rich enough to reimburse the cost. Where context cannot be relied upon to select the reading, languages appear less willing to tolerate the ambiguity in the first place. The whole arrangement looks like an equilibrium in which a language carries about as much homonymy as its speakers can comfortably resolve, and not much more.

I know the usual labels — homonymy, polysemy, pragmatic disambiguation, redundancy — but I am after the tolerance itself: the fact that a language holds a certain level of ambiguity because context makes it cheap to resolve, rather than either eliminating ambiguity or letting it run unchecked. Is there an established term for this trade-off? If not, what would you call it? In either case, a bare name would not settle it for me: I want a precise account of the phenomenon and its edges, and of how it is distinct from plain homonymy, from polysemy, and from ordinary disambiguation.
