I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been going in circles on something while reading Locke's Second Treatise for a piece on charter cities and seasteading. In section 119 of the Second Treatise, Locke says, in effect, that just being within a government's territory—even barely traveling on the highway—gives your tacit consent to its laws and obligates you to obey them for as long as your there. Fine. But here's the thing nagging at me. When someone actually does the explicit version of that—reads a charter city's contract, agrees to its rules, moves in voluntarily—a lot of my readers treat that person as a dupe or a victim. The same readers don't think twice about the idea that they themselves are bound by the laws of a country they never chose at all. So the asymmetry is: unconsented presence counts as consent to a state, but explicit signed consent somehow doesn't count as consent to a private government. I want to know whether anything principled justifies this, or if it's just pro-state bias wearing a theory. Give me the strongest case for the asymmetry you can make, then tell me straight whether you'd keep it.
