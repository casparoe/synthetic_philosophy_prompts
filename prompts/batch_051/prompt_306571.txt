I chair the engineering review board of a company that builds static-analysis and workflow-automation tools, and almost every week we have to rule on a claim about what computation can or cannot do. A team will argue that a feature is "undecidable, so we should drop it"; a vendor will pitch a tool that "solves the halting problem in practice"; a research proposal will claim to "go beyond the limits of Turing machines." The reviewers are strong engineers but not logicians, each decision must be made within a one-hour meeting, and my sign-off is the final step. We have made expensive mistakes in both directions: killing workable features on bad impossibility arguments, and buying tools whose limits were hidden inside the word "practically."

Write me a reusable triage procedure for such claims, grounded in what the Church-Turing thesis actually establishes and in the distinction between its status as an analysis of algorithm, its status as an empirical conjecture about physical devices, and the fact that it says nothing about resource-bounded computation. Build it as an ordered set of questions to put to any impossibility or possibility claim, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with "no," and where it must hand the decision to a person because the underlying question is genuinely open. Tell me which distinctions must survive the simplification and which formal details can be dropped without distorting the thesis, and why.

Then run the procedure on three of our recurring cases:
1. A vendor claims its sandboxed analyzer "decides" whether arbitrary JavaScript functions terminate, because it runs each function under a timeout and uses heuristics to classify the rest.
2. A team wants to kill a proposed security analyzer on the grounds that taint tracking is undecidable in general.
3. A research group proposes an analog neural controller and claims that, because its weights are real-valued, it computes functions no Turing machine can compute.

For each, give the decision the procedure returns and the reasoning behind it, and make sure at least one decision comes out against what the team originally wanted. End by stating plainly what this procedure cannot settle: where the philosophical open questions live, and what the procedure will get wrong if someone applies it mechanically.
