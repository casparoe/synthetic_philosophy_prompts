I'm puzzled by how a certain rule has become normative among quantitative traders and professional gamblers, and I'd like you to reconstruct what it commits a person to. The rule is the Kelly criterion, or log-optimal betting. Here is the concrete practice as I understand it. In Kelly's 1956 paper in the Bell System Technical Journal, A New Interpretation of Information Rate, he considers a gambler at a racetrack who has a private wire that gives him a noisy report of the winner before the track announces it and updates the odds. Kelly's analysis says that if the gambler bets a fixed fraction of his bankroll on each opportunity, the fraction that maximizes the expected logarithm of the bankroll has a special status: over many races, this policy dominates any other fixed-fraction policy in the sense that the log of the bankroll grows faster, and in the limit the bankroll under this policy will almost surely exceed any bankroll under a different fixed-fraction policy by an arbitrarily large factor. The maximum is achieved by the fraction f equals bp minus q over b, where p is the true winning probability, q is one minus p, and b is the net odds received on a winning bet. My question is not about the math. It is about the practice that has grown up around this result. Many sophisticated bettors and traders treat Kelly sizing as the right way to size a position when they have an edge. They will say things like full Kelly is optimal but I use half Kelly because of uncertainty in my edge, or Kelly maximizes long-run growth so it is the rational benchmark. Yet the same people will often deviate from log utility in other settings: they buy insurance against losses they could easily absorb, they care about drawdowns and track yearly returns, and they sometimes prefer a lower-growth strategy with less volatility.
