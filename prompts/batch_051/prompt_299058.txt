I keep coming back to a laboratory phenomenon I learned about once, and I have managed to turn it into an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept. I would like to know where the argument goes wrong, or, if it does not go wrong, which claim is cheapest to abandon.

The phenomenon is this: a red spot is flashed briefly on the left, then a green spot is flashed briefly on the right; observers report seeing a single spot travel across the gap and change from red to green around the midpoint of its path. The midpoint occurs before the green spot is flashed. So the later flash appears to reach backward and determine a color that was seen before it happened.

There seem to be only two ways to describe what is going on. Either the experience itself is assembled late, after the second flash has arrived, so that by the time the experience appears it is already a finished reconstruction. Or an earlier experience is produced first and then overwritten, so that one is left remembering a green midpoint that was never actually experienced. I have been treating these two as exhaustive, and whichever one I start from, I find myself sliding into the following argument.

(1) The green midpoint is part of the experience.
(2) The green midpoint depends on a later event, the second flash.
(3) So at least one part of the conscious present is determined by events that come after it.
(4) There is no principled boundary between this case and perception generally, because all perception takes time and integrates information over a brief interval.
(5) Therefore, at every moment, what I take myself to be experiencing now is determined by what comes later.
(6) But if my experience at every moment is determined by what comes later, then there is no original, unrevised experience left over; the notion of what I actually experienced loses its object.
(7) So the distinction between experiencing something and remembering an edited version of it collapses across the board.
(8) That distinction does not collapse. The difference between looking at a page now and remembering looking at it a minute ago is as certain as anything I know about my own mind.

I want to give up one of the steps between (3) and (6), but each step has looked unavoidable to me so far. Which step should be rejected, and what would rejecting it commit me to? If the best move is to reject (4), then I need an account of what makes the illusion special, because I would otherwise be claiming the same mechanism operates in the lab but not in ordinary life.
