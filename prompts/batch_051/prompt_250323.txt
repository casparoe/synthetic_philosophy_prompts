I'm a novelist, and I've been revising a book where the emotional lives of the characters carry the whole plot, which has forced me to notice a weird double standard in how ordinary people treat emotion. Here's the thing. If a woman's husband dies and she spends a month barely able to get out of bed, we treat her grief as not just healthy but almost required; if she instead spent that month flatly and competently closing out his estate, we'd think something had gone wrong with her. But put the same woman on a jury and find out the defendant reminds her of her sister's attacker, and we'd expect her to recuse herself; or let an air-traffic controller bring that same grief into the tower and we'd call it impaired performance. So the very same feeling seems to count as part of being a full human being in one room and as bias or noise in the next. The old fights between Stoics and Romantics is maybe just this problem wearing historical costumes. I want to know wether any single principle can justify the asymmetry — something that tells us when an emotion is part of responding well to a situation and when it's a distortion — or whether we're just inconsistent and have learned to live with it. Please act as a philosopher working on emotion and value. Before committing, brainstorm at least two candidate principles, then defend the strongest one and test it against nearby cases: anger at an unjust boss, dread in a professional investor, a surgeon operating on her own child, a judge grieving a friend. Then give me an honest verdict on whether the double standard should be kept, revised, or dropped.
