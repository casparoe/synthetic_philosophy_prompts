I've been arguing with a housemate about what makes consent valid, and I wrote down my gut calls on a bunch of cases to figure out if there's one rule underneath them. A blackout-drunk person who can barely stand says yes to sex: not valid. A person who has had three drinks and is tipsy but lucid, and says yes without any pressure: valid, no problem. Someone agrees to sex on the condition that a condom is used, and the other person slips it off halfway through: not valid consent to the act that actually happened. Someone says yes because the other person threatens to send an embarrassing old video to their boss if they refuse: not valid. Someone says yes because their partner of two years says they'll break up with them otherwise: I'd call that valid, though it's an ugly way to ask. Someone lies about having had a vasectomy, and the partner consents partly because of that lie: I lean not valid, because the deception is about a central risk of the act. Someone lies about being married, and the partner consents partly because they'd never sleep with a married person: I keep flip-flopping. It feels like a serious lie about a condition, but it also feels more like the breakup case than the vasectomy case.

The two I really can't place are these. A boss tells an employee they'll stop getting the good shifts unless they have sex: clearly not valid. But a boss offers an employee a promotion if they'll have sex: my gut says that's also not valid, but I can't say why without, it seems, also making the breakup case invalid. Maybe I should just accept that? I don't know.

Is there a principle about competence, coercion, and deception that captures all or most of these verdicts? Where does it come down on the married-lie case and the promotion case, and which of my initial calls would it force me to revise? And what does it say about a further one: someone sleeps with a person on a first date partly because that person said they were a famous actor, when they're actually just a production assistant?
