I just got done arguing with my friend for like an hour about the Hart-Fuller debate. He's convinced Fuller basically ended positivism, and he kept summarizing Hart's side in a way that felt a bit off but I couldn't put my finger on. His version went something like this. Hart says law is just a system of rules a society happens to enforce, and whether those rules are moral has nothing to do with whether they count as law — that's the seperation thesis. So under Hart's view, Nazi laws were full-fledged laws, and German citizens had a legal obligation to follow them, even if they also had a moral reason not to. Fuller replied that this is absurd because law has an inner morality: rules have to be general, public, non-retroactive, clear, consistent, stable, and actually enforced as written. If a regime systematically violates those principles — like the Nazis did with secret decrees and retroactive statutes — then it doesn't really have law at all, just a system of terror. So Fuller showed that you can't separate law and morality the way Hart claimed, because even the procedural requirements are moral requirements, and an evil legal system fails them. Therefore positivism is refuted because morality is built into the very concept of law.

I can tell some of this is off — especially the part about Hart and the Nazis, I remember Hart saying something about a law being law but too wicked to obey, not that it creates a duty to follow it — but I'd like an accuracy audit from someone who knows the actual positions. Go through his summary and tell me which parts are accurate, which are common simplifications, which are subtle misrepresentations, and what a defender of Hart would say he got wrong.
