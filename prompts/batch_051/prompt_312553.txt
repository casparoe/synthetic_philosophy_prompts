I'm a novelist, and I've been tinkering with a story where a village's dialogue is built out of loose folk generalizations — "mushrooms near the creek are poison," that sort of thing. I fell down a rabbit hole about the sematics of generics, and I've half-convinced myself of something that can't be right, but I can't find the sleight of hand. This is the argument, numbered so you can tell me exactly where it breaks (or whether it doesn't).

(1) "Ducks lay eggs" is true, even though only adult female ducks lay eggs and only during part of the year.
(2) "Ducks are female" is false, even though every duck that lays eggs is female — so being female is at least as widespread among ducks as laying eggs is.
(3) So the truth of these two generics can't be decided by how many ducks satisfy the predicate; if numbers did the work, the second sentence would be at least as true as the first.
(4) The difference must come from something non-statistical: laying eggs counts as a characteristic thing ducks do, while being female doesn't. Likewise "mosquitoes carry malaria" is true even though almost no mosquito ever does, because carrying malaria is striking and dangerous.
(5) But whether a property is "characteristic" or "striking" is a fact about our interests and explanatory habits, not about how the property is distributed in the world.
(6) So either the truth conditions of generics are weirdly interest-relative, or these sentences aren't really true or false but only apt or useful. Both feel bad, since "ducks lay eggs" feels true in the plainest sense.
