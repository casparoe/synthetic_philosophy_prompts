I'm trying to get straight what Stephen Wolfram is actually claiming in A New Kind of Science, and a colleague who reviews popular science gave me this summary. Wolfram's central charge is that science has been trapped in an equation-based, continuum approach and has therefore ignored the vast space of simple computational rules. Once you actually run those rules — elementary cellular automata, for instance — you find that even extremely simple programs generate behavior as rich and complicated as anything seen in nature, from shell patterns to turbulence. His Principle of Computational Equivalence then says that once a system passes a very low threshold of complexity, it is a universal computer, capable in principle of computing anything any other computer can, so a brain, a waterfall, and a rule-110 cellular automaton are all doing computations of the same level and there is no meaningful hierarchy of computational sophistication above that threshold. From this, Wolfram concludes that science should broaden its methods to include the systematic enumeration of simple programs and matching them to observed behavior, rather than relying so heavily on traditional equation-based modeling; and he takes the fact that cellular automata generate complexity as strong evidence that the universe is itself a giant cellular automaton. I suspect this summary is partly right and partly quietly loaded, but I can't tell which parts are which. Which parts would a careful defender of Wolfram accept, which are misrepresentations or overstatements, and do the conclusions he draws survive the corrections?
