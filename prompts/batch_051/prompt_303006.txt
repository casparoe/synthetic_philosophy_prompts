Design a 75-minute classroom activity for a philosophy of language seminar with 16 students that makes participants feel, before they hear it named, the force of the idea that languages live with homonymy because ambiguity is mostly tolerated and disambiguated on the fly by context, pitch and tone, and conversational repair. The activity should be a coordination game played under a deliberately impoverished but genuinely homonymous shared vocabulary, and the participants should discover the point by doing it, not by being told.

Structure the game in three rounds. In the first round, participants may send only written one-sentence clues using the restricted vocabulary, so that context and syntax must do the disambiguation. In the second round, allow spoken clues with pitch, stress, and intonation but no extra words, gesture, or writing, so that prosody becomes the variable that changes what can be coordinated on. In the third round, introduce pre-planted items with two live readings that survive both channels, forcing participants either to repair the exchange through clarification or to find that coordination fails. Participants should not hear the terms homonymy, polysemy, or ambiguity tolerance until the debrief.

Specify everything needed to run it: the exact rules, the full lexicon of permitted words with their multiple established senses, the target cards, a scoring scheme that requires no real money, a timeline for each round, and a minimal facilitator script. The scoring should reward successful disambiguation, penalize use of forbidden channels, and in the third round reward correctly identifying that an item is genuinely ambiguous rather than guessing one reading.

Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the underlying idea: the surprising ease of round one onto the claim that context carries most of the disambiguating load unnoticed; the changed performance in round two onto the work done by pitch, stress, and intonation; and the breakdowns or repair sequences in round three onto the limits of ambiguity tolerance and the role of meta-linguistic repair. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run and how to handle it, and be explicit about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply.
