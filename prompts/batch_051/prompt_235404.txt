Here's a puzzle I keep chewing on since the last batch of prestige biopics. Take a real dead person. If a novelist gets inside their head -- invents a childhood wound, puts words in their mouth, maybe rearranges the record -- we mostly shrug and say that's what fiction does; sometimes we even call it penetrating. Put the same inventions in a film with a "based on a true story" title card and people treat the screenwriter like a forger: that meeting never happened, she'd never have said that. Is a real difference being tracked, or are we just grading the two by the label on the tin? I can see a genre-contract story: by calling itself true, the film invites a different kind of trust, so the same move is fair play in one frame and a lie in the other. But that seems to easy -- a writer could dodge every legitimate complaint about misrepresenting a real life just by saying "fiction." And it might also stand in for distance: we let the five-hundred-years-dead be used as material in ways we wouldn't for the recently dead, though both are equally beyond caring. Give me the strongest principle you can find that draws the line, test it against a nearby case that almost crosses it, and tell me whether the asymmetry should stay or go.
