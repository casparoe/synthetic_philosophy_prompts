Act as a philosopher of mind who also works closely with empirical sleep researchers. Think carefully before you answer, and write in American English.

Our lab is planning a study, and I need help designing it so that it actually bears on a longstanding conceptual dispute. The common-sense view says that a dream is a stream of conscious experience that unfolds during sleep, and the morning report is the dreamer's more or less faithful memory of that unfolding. A Malcolm-style challenge holds that our only criterion for saying someone has dreamed is the waking report, so the idea of an independent, report-independent experience during sleep may be misleading or even without clear content. A Dennett-style challenge allows that something memory-related happens during sleep, but denies that the dream narrative is the record of a concurrent conscious process; the narrative might instead be composed or loaded into memory at the moment of waking.

I want the strongest feasible test of the claim that at least some dreams are temporally extended conscious experiences during sleep, as opposed to waking constructions. The lab has access to overnight polysomnography with EEG, EOG, and EMG; awakenings from REM and NREM sleep at varying latencies after an auditory cue; targeted memory reactivation via cues during sleep; trained lucid dreamers who can produce prearranged eye-movement signals; and both immediate and delayed dream reports.

First, brainstorm several possible experimental designs, including their weaknesses. Then select the one you judge strongest and specify:

1. the conditions and measurements in enough detail that a lab could evaluate the design;
2. the outcome pattern that would count as strong evidence that dreams are experiences occurring during sleep;
3. the outcome pattern that would count as strong evidence for construction at waking or retrieval;
4. exactly what even a favorable result would not settle, including whether it can answer the Malcolm-style point that the waking report is the basic criterion, whether it can close the gap between neural correlates and subjective experience, and whether it can establish that all, most, or only some dreams are experiences.

Be precise about the inference at each step, and distinguish clearly between what is operationalized and what remains conceptually open. Treat this as a real experimental design problem rather than offering generic philosophy-of-mind advice.
