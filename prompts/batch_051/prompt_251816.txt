Next month I'm giving a work-in-progress talk in a mixed philosophy colloquium and I want a prep sheet for the Q&A, not a defense of the paper. My line is on Avicenna's flying man, and I'm taking it deflationary. The usual teaching version goes like this: a person is created all at once, fully formed, blindfolded, floating in air or a void with his limbs spread so nothing touches, no sensations coming in; he wouldn't know he has a body, but he'd still know he exists; so what he grasps as existing can't be the body, and boom, the soul is an immaterial substance. My paper argues the thought experiment itself only gets you something much narrower — a primitive non-sensory self-awareness, an 'I' that's given to itself before any faculties or even the body are in view — and that the step from that bare self-awareness to full immateriality doesn't happen inside the example. It gets brought in afterwards from his background psychology and physics, for instance his seperate argument that a body can't recieve indivisible intelligibles. So the flying man isn't a standalone proof of dualism, and it's not a proto-Cartesian cogito either; it's more like the starting datum Avicenna then builds on with other machinery.

The audience is a general philosophy department, mostly not Avicenna specialists, but there are a few I'm worried about. There's a historian of medieval philosophy who works on hylomorphism and will resist anything that sounds like I'm lifting Avicenna out of his soul-as-form framework. There's a philosopher of mind who works on self-awareness and will want to know whether the primitive self-awareness I'm left with does any contemporary work — and I suspect she might actually prefer the stronger standard reading because it makes Avicenna more directly relevant to the mind-body problem. Then there's a Kantian epistemologist who is suspicious of any "given to itself" talk and will attack the idea of a pre-conceptual self; that's the one I'm least ready for. And there'll be grad students, probably asking whether the sensory-deprivation setup is doing any real work on my reading.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way those people would actually phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and where each reply is weakest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks and say why it hurts. I already suspect the philosopher of mind will say I've made Avicenna boring: if the flying man only yields self-awareness, then it doesn't answer the mind-body question and I shouldn't pretend it's interesting. My instinct is to say "right, that's the point," but I don't want to sound like I'm ducking.
