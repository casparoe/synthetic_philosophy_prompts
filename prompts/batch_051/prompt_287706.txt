Hey, I'm a political economy PhD student and my advisor has this pet institutional design he keeps making me stress-test, so I'm outsourcing the job to you.

The proposal: the country moves to near-open borders for labour. Any adult with no serious convictions in the past ten years can enter as a provisional worker, no numerical cap, and take any job. For the first six years provisional workers pay ordinary income tax plus a 12% surcharge on earned income. One third of the surcharge goes into an individual escrow account that's refunded, without interest, when they complete the six years and get permanant residence; if they're convicted of a crime during the period, the escrow is forfeited. The other two thirds flows to the municipality they're registered in, not to central government. During the six years they get no cash transfers—no unemployment, housing benefit, child benefit, or public pensions—but they do get emergency medical care and their kids can attend state schools. After six years of continuous residence and either employment or enrolment in school or training, they automatically get permanent residence, the surcharge stops, and they're in the welfare state on the same terms as everyone else. Municipalities can set the surcharge anywhere between 8% and 15% for people registered in their territory.

I don't want a verdict on whether this is fair or efficient. I want you to walk through how it would actually play out: who moves, what employers do, how municipalities strategize—do they compete for provisional workers, or game the registration rule?—what happens to wages and to the political coalition behind the welfare state, where the whole thing breaks first, and whether the failure modes are fixable or baked in. Assume the country already has a generous but politically contested welfare state and a labour market where the jobs migrants would take are partly informal.
