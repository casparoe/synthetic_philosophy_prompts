I'm giving a seminar paper next week to our bioethics centre on why the therapy/enhancement distinction should be dropped from bioethical analysis. My thesis isn't the usual 'enhancement is fine' line; its that the distinction is doing no independent normative work—when you press it it's either a statistical claim about the species norm or a moral judgment smuggled in as biology, so we should stop asking 'is this therapy or enhancement?' and argue directly about welfare, fairness, and coercion for the intervention in front of us. Sketch of my argument: the boundary cases (a stimulant for a kid just below the ADHD cutoff versus just above) show the line isn't drawn by biology; 'normal function' is a statistical artifact, not a moral baseline; and any attempt to firm up the line by saying what counts as genuinely therapeutic imports a substantive view about which capacities are worth restoring. The audience is a mixed one: a couple of disability-studies scholars who think the 'fixing people to a normal baseline' frame is the problem from the start, a clinician who thinks the distinction is actually load-bearing for deciding what gets funded, and a philosopher sympathetic to liberal eugenics who worries that dropping the distinction leaves no brake on enhancement arms races. For each objection, also say what argument or evidence would actually change my mind—don't flatter me. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
