I just spent a long argument with a friend about whether Newton’s bucket experiment really supports substantivalism about space, or only shows that rotation cannot be reduced to relative motion among ordinary material bodies. We kept appealing to the same passage from the Scholium to the Definitions in the *Principia*, and we talked past each other because neither of us clearly separated the claim that rotation is absolute from the claim that space is a substance. I am now writing my upper-level philosophy of physics final, and I want an essay question that forces students to do better than we did. I have paraphrased the bucket passage below so the question can rely on it without requiring the students to have the text in front of them.

In the Scholium, Isaac Newton asks us to imagine a bucket suspended by a rope and filled with water. The bucket is made to rotate; at first the water remains flat, then as the water takes up the bucket’s motion it climbs the sides and becomes concave, and if the bucket is then stopped the water continues rotating and remains concave. Newton argues that the concave shape reveals the water’s true rotation about its own axis, because the concavity does not track the relative rotation between the water and the bucket: there can be relative rotation without concavity, and no relative rotation with concavity. The question should force students to confront whether this example supports substantivalism—the view that space exists independently of matter and has its own structure—or whether it leaves open a relationist view in which all motion is ultimately motion relative to other material bodies. Please write the question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, total 20 marks, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each wrong. A top answer should not merely recite the labels.

Exam question:

Newton’s bucket is often presented as an argument for absolute space. But does it establish substantivalism, or merely that the water’s concave surface cannot be explained by the water’s rotation relative to the bucket? Answer by (a) stating precisely what Newton needs the example to show, (b) presenting the strongest relationist response to that argument, and (c) assessing whether relationism can fully account for the bucket without positing absolute rotation. In your assessment, distinguish two issues: whether the bucket supports absolute rotation, and whether accepting absolute rotation commits one to substantival space.

Model answer:

Newton’s bucket is best understood as a two-step argument. The first step is an argument about motion: the concave surface of the water is an observable effect that indicates the water is really rotating, and this real rotation cannot be reduced to the water’s rotation relative to the bucket. At the beginning of the experiment the bucket rotates while the water is flat, so there is relative rotation between bucket and water but no concavity. Later the water rotates with the bucket and is concave, so there is little or no relative rotation between bucket and water but concavity. Finally the bucket is stopped while the water still rotates and remains concave, so again there is relative rotation and concavity together. The concavity therefore does not pair off with the relative motion of water and bucket. Newton concludes that the water’s true circular motion is something over and above its relative motion with respect to this or that particular body.

The first step, however, only refutes a very crude relationism that analyzes a body’s motion solely relative to a single nearby body. A more sophisticated relationist concedes that the bucket and water are too limited a system, but denies that the only alternative is absolute space. On this view, the water’s concavity is explained by its rotation relative to the rest of the material universe, above all the fixed stars. If the water rotates relative to the large-scale distribution of matter, then centrifugal effects appear; if it does not, they disappear. The relationist’s strongest response is therefore to widen the reference system from the bucket to the universe: the concave surface tracks a global relational quantity, not a local one.

This response has real force. It correctly identifies that the bucket thought experiment, taken alone, is underdetermined: the same observable facts are consistent with absolute rotation and with rotation relative to a suitably chosen global material frame. To that extent, Newton’s bucket does not compel substantivalism. But relationism now faces a serious burden. The relationist must specify the relation to the fixed stars in a non-circular way, and must explain why centripetal forces should arise from relative rotation to distant matter at all. Newton objects that if the bucket and water alone existed in an otherwise empty universe, the same concave surface should presumably occur under real rotation, yet no relative rotation to other matter would be available. The relationist can either bite this bullet—perhaps by saying the thought experiment is physically impossible or undefined—or accept that rotational effects depend on something beyond ordinary material relations.

This reveals the central distinction a strong answer must draw. The bucket supports the claim that rotation is absolute, in the sense that not all rotation can be analyzed as relative rotation among ordinary material bodies. But absolute rotation need not entail substantivalism. One can hold that rotational motion is a primitive dynamical feature of a body without reifying space itself as an independently existing substance. The substantivalist goes further: space is an entity with parts and structure, and a body truly rotates when its motion changes with respect to space itself. The relationist can accept absolute rotation as a primitive fact about a body while denying that this fact requires a substantial spatial container. The bucket therefore defeats a purely relational account of all motion, but it does not by itself prove substantivalism. What it does is force relationists to explain the inertial structure that makes rotational effects possible. The strongest relationist response either invokes the global matter distribution, or treats the bucket as showing that rotation is a primitive dynamical state; both moves avoid local relationism, and neither requires accepting substantivalism as Newton understood it.

Marking scheme:

1. Accurate account of Newton’s inference from the bucket facts to the claim that concavity tracks true rotation, not relative rotation between water and bucket. (4 marks)  
   Typical error: candidates misstate the experimental stages, or say the relative rotation is zero at the start, flattening the actual contrast.

2. Clear statement of the relationist response that expands the reference frame from the bucket to the large-scale material universe. (4 marks)  
   Typical error: candidates treat relationism as limited to the water-bucket system and declare it refuted without considering the fixed stars.

3. Assessment of whether the global relationist move can explain centrifugal effects. (4 marks)  
   Typical error: candidates assert that Mach’s move obviously works or obviously fails, without identifying the burden of specifying the relation non-circularly.

4. Distinction between absolute rotation and substantival space. (4 marks)  
   Typical error: candidates run the two together and claim that refuting local relationism automatically establishes that space is a substance.

5. Final evaluation that the bucket rules out crude local relationism but leaves open either a global relational account or a primitive-rotation account. (4 marks)  
   Typical error: candidates conclude Newton’s argument proves substantivalism outright, rather than forcing a more sophisticated relationist position.
