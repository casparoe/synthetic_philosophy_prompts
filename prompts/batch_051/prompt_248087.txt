I keep using a criterion for evaluating proposed changes to concepts, and I want it stress-tested before I lean on it any harder. I call it semantic backward compatibility. A proposed replacement for a concept is acceptable only if it can still do, at least as well, every job the old concept was actually doing for the people who used it: licensing their inferences, marking the distinctions they relied on, keeping their practices coordinated. It is not enough that the replacement does the reviser's preferred job better, and it is not enough that it survives a philosophical reconstruction of the concept. My stock example is the demotion of Pluto: the new classification did a specialist astronomical job cleanly, but it quietly stopped doing the schoolroom and communicative work the old 'planet' category was doing, and on my criterion that makes it a failed piece of conceptual engineering even if the science was sound. I know this sits near familiar demands for continuity or platitude preservation, and I know the strongest worry: some of the work an old concept does is work we should refuse to do. At that point semantic backward compatibility starts to look like conservatism in disguise. I want an honest assessment. Is the criterion coherent? Does it already exist under another name? Is it genuinely distinct from those familiar demands, or does it collapse into one of them? Give me the best case against it, and tell me whether that case kills it or only narrows it.
