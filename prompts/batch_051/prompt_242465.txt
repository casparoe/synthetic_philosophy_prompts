I am at work on a novel whose protagonist is a cognitive psychologist, and a long scene between her and an evolutionary psychologist turns on a technical dispute I want to render accurately rather than merely decorate with jargon. The dispute concerns the architecture of the mind. On one side is Jerry Fodor's position that the mind is modular only at the periphery: input systems such as perception and language are domain-specific, informationally encapsulated, fast, and mandatory, whereas the central systems responsible for belief fixation and deliberation are domain-general — any belief may in principle bear on any other, a property Fodor marks with the term isotropy, and the confirmation of a hypothesis depends on the whole structure of one's beliefs, a property he calls Quinean. On the other side is the massive modularity thesis associated with evolutionary psychology, which treats the mind as a large collection of specialized, content-specific computational adaptations — for face recognition, social exchange, intuitive physics, theory of mind, and the like — with no substantial domain-general central processor standing behind them.

For the scene I need a comparison that goes beyond the standard textbook contrast. Compare how each of these two positions would explain the ordinary human capacity to construct, comprehend, and keep track of extended counterfactual narratives — fictions in which characters, motives, and worlds are stipulated rather than reported. On each view, what does a reader's mind do when it imports real-world knowledge into a story, quarantines whatever the story suspends, and updates what is true in the fiction as events unfold? Is that processing better described as the global, unencapsulated work of a central system, or as the coordinated output of many domain-specific mechanisms operating under the looser encapsulation that massive modularity can allow? Keep distinct the properties of domain-specificity and informational encapsulation, since the two positions weight them differently. Then say which of the two positions you judge to give the more defensible account of this capacity, and what the weaker position leaves unexplained.
