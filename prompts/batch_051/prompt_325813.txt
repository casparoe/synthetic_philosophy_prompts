I got in an argument over the holidays with a friend who's studying mathematical logic — he'd read something about the liar paradox and wouldn't shut up about how self-refrence makes logic explode. He sent me an explianer article, and it's been bugging me ever since, because the steps feel slippery but the conclusions feel enormous. The article's summary goes roughly like this. The classic liar sentence, 'This sentence is false,' shows that self-reference can generate contradiction, and the standard repair is just a ban on self-referential sentences. But then Curry's paradox is supposed to be nastier: the sentence 'If this sentence is true, then the moon is made of cheese' is enough on its own to prove the moon is made of cheese, and since the proof never touches negation, the article says the usual anti-self-reference fix can't block it. Yablo's paradox then removes self-reference entirely: you lay out an infinite list of sentences, each one saying 'every sentence later than me on this list is untrue,' and you still get a contradiction, though the article admits the sentences are still gesturing at the whole list, so maybe self-reference is just hiding there in the background after all. The piece closes by saying Curry and Yablo show that no consistent theory of truth is really possible, and that the real culprit behind all the paradoxes was never self-reference but the unrestricted use of if-then reasoning. I want to know whether this explainer is being fair — go through it bit by bit and tell me what a philosopher of logic would nod along with, where they'd wince, and whether that final lesson actually follows from anything true in the summary.
