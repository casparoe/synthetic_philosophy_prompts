I've been going back and forth about whether the state should fund literature, and I keep getting stuck on one point. The line I hear most often is roughly that reading serious literary fiction trains people in understanding other minds, and since a society where people are better at that is more decent, there's a public interest in subsidizing the fiction that produces the effect. But over the last ten years or so the replication crisis has been pretty rough on this exact research: several of the headline findings about fiction and empathy/theory of mind have not replicated cleanly, and the meta-analytic estimates look small and fragile. I'm trying to figure out wether that actually kills the public-funding argument, or whether the argument was never really standing on the empirical claim in the first place. If it does stand on it, what's the strongest repair that's honest about the new evidence — maybe a much weaker, "sometimes, for some readers" version? And if it can't be repaired, are we stuck with something like "fiction is intrinsically valuable," which sounds suspiciously like subsidizing some people's expensive hobby? I want to know which premise actually takes the hit and what a defensible version of the argument says now.
