I sit on the advisory board of a private foundation that funds work on the long-term future of sentient life. At next month's meeting, we must set the structure of a new grants program with an annual budget of roughly twenty million dollars, and the board is split over whether to set aside five to ten million of it specifically for work on "suffering risks" (s-risks): the risk that the future will contain astronomical amounts of suffering — for example, because artificial systems are deployed in ways that create vast numbers of suffering beings, or because a small number of actors lock in institutions that keep sentient life in permanently miserable conditions. Proponents argue that suffering reduction has been systematically neglected in long-term giving, and that stakes that large justify a dedicated program even if the probability of accomplishing anything is modest. Opponents reply (1) that the underlying asymmetry — the idea that preventing suffering matters far more than creating or preserving happiness — is a contested moral premise that a grant-making body should not quietly assume; (2) that estimates about the far future are so speculative that they can be used to justify almost anything, since a tiny probability multiplied by an astronomically large number can yield whatever expected value one wants; and (3) that a carve-out would divert resources from near-term causes whose results the board can actually verify.

I need to circulate a short written opinion before the board meets. Please draft it: it should recommend a concrete structure — a dedicated s-risk program, a pilot with a fixed review date, no carve-out, or some other specific arrangement — with reasons the board could act on. It should engage the strongest version of the objections on the other side rather than treating them as merely timid or unambitious, and it should say what the decision really turns on, philosophically, without turning into a seminar paper.
