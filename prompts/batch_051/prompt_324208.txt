Please give me a working definition of proof beyond a reasonable doubt that gets the following six cases right. I want a definition that is a usable test—something I could apply to a description of the evidence alone to decide whether a conviction would be justified under the criminal standard—not a one-line paraphrase such as the usual instruction that a reasonable doubt is the sort of doubt that would make a reasonable person hesitate to act. I want a committed answer: choose a definite definition, apply it to all six cases, and where the definition gives a verdict that conflicts with my stated view or seems controversial in the cases where I am unsure, either revise the definition or defend the verdict.

Assume that each case is a criminal trial on the same charge, that the evidence described is the whole of the admissible evidence bearing on the disputed fact, and that the trier of fact should not convict unless the standard is met.

1. Direct identification. A bartender identifies the defendant as the man who robbed the till; the gun used in the robbery is found under the defendant's bed with the defendant's fingerprints on it; and the defendant's bank account shows a deposit matching the amount taken, made the next morning. I take it that this clearly meets the standard.

2. Blue bus. A pedestrian is struck by a bus at night and the driver flees. The defendant is the only Blue Bus Company driver running a bus on that road at that hour, so if the bus was a Blue Bus Company bus, he was its driver. The only evidence tying the crash to the Blue Bus Company rather than the Red Bus Company is that the Blue Bus Company operates 80% of the buses on that road at that hour. There is no eyewitness identification of the company, no paint, no debris, and nothing else connecting this particular bus to the crash. I take it that this clearly fails the standard.

3. Gatecrasher. Exactly 1,000 people are inside a rodeo, and only 499 tickets were sold. The defendant is one of those 1,000 people. The only evidence that he entered without paying is that 501 of the 1,000 people inside must have entered without paying. I take it that this clearly fails the standard.

4. Prison yard. One hundred prisoners are in a yard when a guard is killed in a coordinated assault. Surveillance footage shows that 99 of the 100 prisoners took part in the assault and one stood aside, but the footage cannot distinguish which one stood aside. The defendant is one of the 100, and no other evidence bears on what he did. I lean toward thinking this fails the standard, but I cannot yet say why: the evidence implies a 99% probability that the defendant took part.

5. Database cold hit. DNA from a burglary is searched against a database. Exactly one profile matches—the defendant's—and the laboratory reports that the probability that an innocent, unrelated person's profile would match is 1 in 10 million. The defendant was not a suspect before the search, and nothing else connects him to the burglary. I am genuinely unsure whether this meets the standard.

6. Rare tire track. A hit-and-run leaves a tire track from a tread pattern fitted to only 1 in 100,000 vehicles of the relevant type, and the defendant owns a vehicle with that tread. There is no eyewitness and no other link between the defendant and the scene. I am also unsure whether this meets the standard.

A definition would be most useful if it explains the first three cases and the prison-yard case together, and if it either draws a sharp line between the database hit and the tire track or argues convincingly that no sharp line should be drawn.
