I'm preparing a problem sheet for a reading group on rational choice, and I've assembled an argument that seems to prove far too much. I want a diagnostic account of where it goes wrong — or, if it doesn't go wrong, an honest accounting of which premise I'm being forced to abandon.

Fix the St. Petersburg game G as follows. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; if the first heads occurs on toss n, the player is paid 2^n dollars. Assume an infinitely wealthy payer so that the usual point about a real banker's finite vault is stipulated away.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) A rational agent is required to choose the act with the greatest expected monetary value, and to be indifferent between acts whose expected monetary values are equal.
(2) The expected monetary value of G is the sum over n ≥ 1 of (1/2^n)(2^n), i.e. 1 + 1 + 1 + ..., which is infinite.
(3) For any p > 0, let T(p) be a ticket that gives its holder probability p of playing G once and otherwise pays nothing. Since the expected value of T(p) is p times the expected value of G, EV(T(p)) = p × ∞ = ∞.
(4) For any finite sum V, ∞ > V. Thus every T(p), even for p = 10^-9, has greater expected value than any guaranteed finite payment.
(5) In addition, EV(T(1/2)) = EV(T(10^-9)) = ∞. So by (1), a rational agent should be indifferent between a fifty-fifty chance of playing G and a one-in-a-billion chance of playing it, and should prefer either to any finite sure payment.
(6) Therefore the agent should be willing to exchange any finite fortune, however large, for a ticket that with probability 0.999999999 pays nothing — and should not even prefer the fifty-fifty ticket to the one-in-a-billion ticket.
(7) But no acceptable principle of rational choice can require that.

Each step looked harmless on its own, but the conclusion is unacceptable to me. I'd most like to keep premise (1) in some principled form and keep the protest in (7), but I can't see how to hold both without special pleading.
