I'm teaching Macbeth in a course on tragedy, and I want one session to use predestination and free will as the lens rather than fate in general. Give me a reading of the play through the Augustinian–Calvinist–Arminian debate about grace, election, and the freedom of the will — one that takes the theology seriously and also lets the play push back. Keep these distinctions sharp: divine foreknowledge versus divine decree; the compatibilist claim that the outcome is fixed while the act remains a chosen act versus the libertarian claim that the agent could have refrained at the moment of choice; and unconditional election and hardening versus resistible, enabling grace. On this frame: Do the Weird Sisters' prophecies function as foretelling, as decreeing, or as temptation that works on a will still able to refuse? Is Macbeth best read as a reprobate whose hardening is willed from eternity, or does the play mark a real turning point at which he could have turned back — and where is it? What in the play supports the Arminian thought that Macbeth's own ambition, not a divine decree, does the deciding work, and what resists that reading? Where, if anywhere, does the play distinguish its own tragic necessity — the logic of character, circumstance, and consequence — from theological determinism, and where does it blur them? And what does Shakespeare's rendering of Macbeth's guilt — the visions, the murders, Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking, the emptiness of the final soliloquy — add to the debate that the treatises cannot say in their own mode? Point me to the scenes I should assign for close reading and say what each shows. End with a judgment about where the play refuses the available categories and whether that refusal is itself a philosophical point.
