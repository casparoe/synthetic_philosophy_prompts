I'm a law clerk for a state court of appeals judge, and I keep hitting the same question: when may a court ignore what a statute plainly says because the result is absurd? We see a few of these a month — sentencing, benefits, zoning — and I usualy only have an afternoon to write the bench memo before conference. The judge is a plain-language textualist who distrusts 'legislative purpose' talk, and the stakes run both ways: read around a text that should've applied literally and we get reversed; apply a text that was absurd and we look callous.

I think 'absurd' is doing at least three different jobs. (1) The words command something impossible or self-contradictory, so the statute can't be applied as written. (2) The literal reading defeats the obvious point of the statute — it produces the opposite of what it's plainly for. (3) The literal reading is just very harsh, dumb, or unfair, the kind of thing no reasonable legislator would've wanted on purpose. I need a reusable decision procedure that separates these three and says which actually justify abandoning the text and which are a back door to legislating. An ordered list of questions, with what counts as a satisfactory answer at each step, a clear stop when the answer is no, a flag where it's a values call for the judge, and honesty about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on these three, and don't rig them so they all come out the way we'd want. At least one should end 'apply the literal words even though everyone in chambers hates it.' (a) A benefits law lets a claimant file 'no later than 90 days after the agency action,' but a companion rule says filing is allowed only 'after the agency issues its final report,' which must come 'no earlier than 120 days after the action.' (b) A wildlife statute makes it 'unlawful to move any animal across a state line into a national park'; the state's own endangered-species program, created the same session, wants to truck a breeding pair in. (c) A tax statute gives a homestead break only if the owner uses the home 'exclusively as the owner's primary residence'; a retired woman lives there year-round but rents one spare room to a nursing student to cover the mortgage. Finally, say what the procedure can't do — where even a well-run version is really just a policy fight in disguise.
