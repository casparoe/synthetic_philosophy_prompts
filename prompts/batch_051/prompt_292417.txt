I have a set of gut verdicts about what counts as alive, and I would like to know what principle, if any, best captures them. I'll list the cases first.

A flame is not alive, even though it takes in fuel, grows, and gives rise to more flame. A crystal forming in a saturated solution is not alive.

A typical virus, say the sort that infects a tobacco leaf, sitting inert on the leaf seems not alive. Inside a cell, while it directs the cell to make copies of it, I still want to say it is not alive, but I cannot say why it differs in kind from the next case. An obligate intracellular bacterium, which can divide only inside a host cell, is alive. I have no principled way to state the difference between these two without appealing to something like whether the thing has its own membrane, which feels irrelevant to what it is doing.

A mule is alive, though it cannot reproduce. A worker honeybee is alive, though it never reproduces. A bacterial endospore, dried out and inert for years, is alive, just dormant. Together these make me suspicious of any account that requires the individual to reproduce.

A prion, a misfolded protein that causes normal proteins to adopt its shape, is not alive, but it does make copies of itself in some sense, and I cannot say which sense of copying counts. A short stretch of RNA that gets copied by a cell's machinery and never builds a coat or leaves the cell is not alive. A transposon, a segment of DNA that inserts copies of itself elsewhere in its host genome, is not alive, even though it has heredity and can evolve. I notice that the line I keep drawing may be about little more than position relative to a cell membrane, which worries me.

A slime mold makes this vivid. As separate amoebas, each is alive. When thousands aggregate into a crawling slug, I want to say the slug is alive too, but not whether it is one organism or many. A population of human cancer cells kept dividing in a dish for decades is alive, but it is not a human, and perhaps not an organism at all. A cell whose genome has been stripped down in a lab to the smallest set of genes that still allows it to grow and divide in a rich medium is alive, and I would call it an organism. A machine that finds energy sources, repairs itself, and builds copies of itself is not alive, but I cannot give a reason that does not also threaten several of the things I called alive.

I also have a tentative principle: to be alive is to be a self-maintaining system that reproduces with variation and thereby evolves by natural selection. But that principle makes the mule and the worker bee nonliving, which I reject; it lets the flame in too easily unless I add a further stipulation, and adding that stipulation feels ad hoc; and it treats the transposon and the lab-built cell far too similarly.

I am looking for the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best fits these verdicts. Please state each candidate precisely, say whether my tentative principle can be repaired or should be abandoned, indicate which of my cases motivates each clause, give a verdict on at least one case I have not listed (for example, a human embryo, a cloud, or a self-replicating program running in a simulated world), and say plainly which of my verdicts each candidate forces me to abandon. I would rather see two or three honest candidates that disagree than a single definition stretched to cover everything.
