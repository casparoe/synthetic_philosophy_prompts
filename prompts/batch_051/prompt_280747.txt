I am putting together a fiction-and-philosophy reading group session on self-reference, and I want to set Jorge Luis Borges's story "The Circular Ruins" beside the conceptual apparatus Hofstadter develops around M.C. Escher's Drawing Hands. The key notions are these. A tangled hierarchy, in Hofstadter's sense, is a loop in which what would ordinarily be a lower level acts back on the higher level that produces it, as when each of Escher's two hands is drawn by the other. The inviolate level is the level outside such a loop — the artist who drew both hands, or the rules outside a formal system — which has to stay outside the loop for the loop to be definable as a loop at all. For reference, in Borges's story a wizard arrives at a ruined circular temple and undertakes the task of dreaming a man into physical existence, perfecting him over years; near the end, he sends the son out with the memory erased so the young man will not know he is a dream, and he worries that fire — the one element able to expose a dream — will reveal the truth. When the temple burns, the wizard walks into the flames, finds he is not consumed, and realizes that he himself is also being dreamed by someone else.

I would like a close reading of the story through that lens, with no verbatim quotation, built from your knowledge of the plot. Does "The Circular Ruins" present a genuine tangled hierarchy, or only a descending chain of dreamers with no bottom? Does it require, imply, or pointedly deny an inviolate level — and in what sense, if any, do the author or the reader occupy that level? Be faithful in both directions: say where the Hofstadter concepts genuinely illuminate the story, where the story resists them (for example, whether an infinite regress of dreamers is the same thing as a strange loop), and what the story makes vivid about groundedness that the drawing of the two hands does not. I am especially interested in the structural details — the circularity of the temple, fire as the test, the induced amnesia, and the placement of the ending's revelation — and in whether the wizard's realization at the end retroactively changes the status of everything he made.
