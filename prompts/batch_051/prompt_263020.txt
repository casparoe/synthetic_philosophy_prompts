I'm a novelist, not an academic, so forgive me if I'm stumbling into something that already has a tidy name. I'm drafting a book where two characters are drawn into a ruinous bidding war, and I want to write the trap accurately rather than just using it for drama. The pattern I keep noticing runs like this. Both people have already put in something unrecoverable — legal fees, months of work, pride. The object they are bidding on is now worth less to both of them than what they've already sunk. And the cruel feature is that the loser does not just walk away having lost the contest: in the pure version, the loser still pays their final bid and receives nothing, while in the softer real-life version, the loser simply forfeits everything they've already put in. At each step the sensible move looks like going one more round, because one more round costs less than crystalising the loss by stopping. But both people reason this way, so they drag each other past the point where even the winner would have been better off never starting. The original object — a company, a house, a relationship — quietly stops being the prize; the prize becomes not being the one who blinked.

I know the sunk cost fallacy, but that seems one-sided, a person fooling themselves, whereas this needs at least two people and, in the pure version, that rule that the loser loses too. I also keep coming across escalation of commitment, but I can't tell whether that is the whole phenomenon or only the psychological backdrop. So my question is: is there an established name for this two-sided trap, and how exactly does it differ from ordinary sunk-cost thinking and from plain competitiveness? Where are its edges — does it actually require the loser-pays rule, or would two rivals simply throwing more and more money at a fixed prize count? I'd rather have the boundary lines drawn than a bare label. And if no distinct name exists, what would you call it?
