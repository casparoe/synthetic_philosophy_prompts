Here are my verdicts about who may legitimately govern when the rules run out, and I would like to know what principle, if any, makes them cohere. (1) After a coup abolishes the constitution and scatters the old legislature, a self-appointed council of surviving mayors starts issuing decrees to keep water and electricity flowing and hospitals open; I regard its rule as provisionally legitimate, though nothing in the old constitution authorizes it. (2) When a president invokes a vaguely worded emergency clause to suspend an election and then renews the suspension year after year, I think the first suspension may have been lawful but the later renewals are not. (3) If a parliamentary faction boycotts every session so that no quorum can form, and the remaining members pass an emergency budget, I think the budget is valid: the absent side is manufacturing the gap rather than being caught in it. (4) If a natural disaster kills the president, the vice president, and the whole line of succession overnight, and a group of surviving ministers rules by decree for three weeks, I regard that as legitimate; but if an ordinary coalition deadlock has left a country without a government for eighteen months, I do not think the caretaker cabinet may do whatever it judges necessary. (5) A judge whose term has expired, and for whom no successor has been confirmed, may decide routine cases for two years, but I would not accept the same judge's handing down a major constitutional ruling in that period. I notice that I keep appealing to necessity in some of these cases but rejecting it in others, and I cannot say what marks the difference. Is there a single principle—about necessity, good faith, filling a gap rather than exploiting one, or something else—that delivers all these verdicts? If not, which verdicts should I abandon, and what would the resulting principle predict about an official who ignores the only remaining procedural rule in order to save the very system the rule was designed to serve?
