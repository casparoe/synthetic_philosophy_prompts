I am drafting a statement of my views on the relationship between the rule of law and the traditional doctrine that ignorance of the law is no excuse, and I would like an audit of the following commitments before I have to defend it.

Here is the position I hold.

(a) A legal system satisfies the ideal of legality only if its norms are public, prospective, and sufficiently clear that a diligent person can ordinarily discover what is required of them. I accept this as a guidance condition on the law itself.

(b) The traditional doctrine that ignorance of the law is no excuse is, in its general shape, sound. In the ordinary case, a person may not turn their failure to consult the law into a defense; a system that routinely allowed ignorance to defeat liability would be unworkable and would reward deliberate incuriosity.

(c) This no-excuse default extends to mala prohibita offenses: conduct wrong only because the law makes it wrong. Filing deadlines, licensing categories, numerical regulatory thresholds, and analogous rules are legitimate, and violations of them may properly be punished when the violation is knowing or culpably negligent.

(d) Nonetheless, it is unjust to convict someone under a rule that they could not, with reasonable diligence, have known existed at the time of acting: a rule that was never published or distributed, a secret administrative practice, or an unforeseeable judicial construction. A publicity failure of this kind is a legal defect, not merely a political grievance.

(e) I am also drawn to a distinction between first-order and second-order ignorance. First-order ignorance is not knowing what the applicable rule requires, although one knows, or reasonably should know, that one is acting inside a regulated domain. Second-order ignorance is not knowing that there is any body of rule to look for at all. My working view is that second-order ignorance can excuse when it survives reasonable diligence, while first-order ignorance almost never excuses, since it is ordinarily the residue of an inquiry one had reason to conduct.

(f) Finally, I hold that strict criminal liability, punishing a violation without any finding of fault with respect to the wrongfulness of the conduct, is almost always unjust; but I accept that civil penalties and administrative consequences may be imposed without individualized fault, as a cost of operating in a regulated field.

Please assess whether these commitments hang together. Rank the tensions by severity instead of treating them all as equally serious. For each genuine tension, say which commitments pull against one another, what exactly has to give, and what the least costly revision would be and what it would cost. For any apparent tension that dissolves on closer inspection, say why it dissolves. Finally, say what argument or consideration would most likely lead you to a different verdict about the package as a whole, and where your diagnosis is least secure.
