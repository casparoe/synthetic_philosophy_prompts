You're an expert on Wittgenstein's family resemblance ideas and on incentive design, and you've been stuck on a university assessment committee. Think carefully before you answer — start by brainstorming the pressures on students, graders, and the department, and only then lay out the analysis.

My department is piloting a "family resemblance" grading scheme. Every year it picks six essays as paradigm A-papers and six as paradigm C (failing) papers. A new essay gets an A or C depending on which cluster the graders judge it resembles more: there are no published criteria, graders don't have to agree on any single feature the exemplars share, and they're actually barred from naming a decisive feature when defending a grade — they can only say it "looks like" the A's or the C's. Appeals are heard by a second panel that regrades by resemblance alone.

Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position. What does it incentivize students to write? How do graders' judgments and the content of the category "A" itself drift as the exemplar pool turns over each year? Where does it get gamed first, and can those failure modes be fixed without creeping back into a de facto rubric?
