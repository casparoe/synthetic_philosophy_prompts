I am a novelist, and I have agreed to give the twenty-minute opening address at a two-day workshop on fiction and deliberate climate intervention, jointly convened by a writers' association and an atmospheric-science institute. I am writing a novel set in the early years of a solar geoengineering program, and I keep finding that my drafts reach for exactly the endings this speech must refuse. The audience will be a deliberate mix, in roughly equal thirds: working scientists and policy analysts who study geoengineering, novelists and editors, and interested members of the public. The speech has to land for the scientists without losing the writers, and for the writers without condescending to the scientists. I want it organized around three features of the problem, used precisely rather than as decoration. First, moral hazard: the mere prospect of a speculative fix can weaken the felt urgency of cutting emissions, so the speech must not stage a rescue or let the room feel that this gathering means the problem is already in hand. Second, termination shock: if stratospheric aerosol injection were halted abruptly while greenhouse-gas concentrations remained high, the masked warming would return as a rapid pulse rather than over the slower course it would otherwise follow, so the speech must not build toward a sudden release of tension or imply that the intervention could later simply be switched off. Third, the problem of who decides: there is no settled global authority, and a single state or even a wealthy private actor might deploy such a technology unilaterally, so the speech must not resolve into one narrator, one protagonist, or one decision-maker who saves the story. I want the speech's form to obey these constraints — a novelist working through a narrative problem, not a policy briefing — and to leave the audience productively unsettled rather than soothed. Please write the speech in full.
