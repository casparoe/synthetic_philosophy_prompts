I am a retired natural scientist, and I have noticed a rule I apply in arguments about shared resources. When someone proposes that a common-pool resource—a fishery, an aquifer, an irrigation district—can be kept sustainable by its users themselves, through informal norms, mutual monitoring, and graduated sanctions for violators, I discount the proposal almost to zero. My instinct is that without an external enforcer or clear, tradable property rights the logic of the tragedy of the commons will eventually reassert itself, whatever the good intentions; last month I opposed a groundwater self-metering scheme on just these grounds. An economist friend tells me I am using one parable as a prior, since many common-pool resources have been managed locally for centuries and the record is far from uniformly disastrous. I want to know whether my shortcut is defensible. Is there a principled level of skepticism, rather than either accepting a local scheme or zeroing it out? Under what conditions does self-governance of a subtractable resource beat external control, and when does it predictably fail? In your answer, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study this, and do not present a contested camp's view as the consensus.
