Suppose that in 2030 a cheap, safe drug becomes widely available that selectively strips away the unpleasant felt quality of the emotions a fiction elicits while leaving comprehension and aesthetic attention fully intact: someone watching a tragedy still sees that the protagonist is ruined and that the scene calls for grief and pity, but the grief does not hurt and the pity does not ache; the same holds for the fear elicited by horror and the sorrow elicited by sad music. Nothing else changes. Predict, with a visible chain of reasoning, what happens over the following twenty years to the production and consumption of tragedy, horror, and sad music, and to the value audiences place on them. I am less interested in a single story than in an argued forecast: for each claimed effect, say whether it follows from catharsis, from the idea that negative emotion is converted into pleasure, from a pleasure in mastery over otherwise unpleasant states, or from the sheer felt richness of the experience, and how confidently you hold each link. Which predictions are robust across those theories, which rest on questionable assumptions about whether audiences actually seek out the negative feeling or merely tolerate it, and what would have to be the case for tragedy to fade out rather than adapt?
