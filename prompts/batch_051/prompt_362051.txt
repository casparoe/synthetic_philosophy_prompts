In the preface to his *An Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798), Thomas Malthus writes: "It is an obvious truth, which has been taken notice of by many writers, that population must always be kept down to the level of the means of subsistence; but no writer that the Author recollects has inquired particularly into the means by which this level is effected: and it is a view of these means which forms, to his mind, the strongest obstacle in the way to any very great future improvement of society."

I have been developing a notion I call "time-indexed falsification," and I want it tested against Malthus. The idea is this: a prediction about a long-run social or ecological dynamic cannot be falsified by just any counterexample, but only by counterexamples occurring inside the temporal window that the theory's own mechanism actually specifies. Malthus is often treated as refuted by the twentieth century: population grew enormously, food per person grew too, and mass starvation did not become the permanent human condition. But the quoted passage suggests his central claim was not a dated catastrophe; it was the claim that population must always be kept down to subsistence by *some* check, and that the nature of those checks is the real obstacle to indefinite improvement. On my view, if a theory says "when X approaches Y, Z will happen," then evidence from periods when X was not approaching Y is not yet falsifying, but evidence from periods when X was approaching Y and Z did not happen is decisive against it.

I want an honest assessment. Is "time-indexed falsification" a coherent rule for distinguishing a theory that is right but early from a theory that is being perpetually rescued by moving the goalposts? When I apply it strictly to Malthus, does his position survive or fail? And is this distinction already worked out under another name, or have I just reinvented a familiar piece of philosophy of science with looser terms?
