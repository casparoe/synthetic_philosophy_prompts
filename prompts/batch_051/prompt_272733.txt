Next month I'm giving a department seminar on a chapter from my book project. The chapter argues that early modern history looks different if we treat the 1815 Tambora eruption, the introduction of smallpox to the Americas, and the Little Ice Age as actors in the sequence of events rather than just background conditions. I don't mean they have intentions or agential control, and I'm explicitly not saying climate or germs determine outcomes; my claim is that our default language of "conditions" versus "actors" underestimates how much explanatory work these non-human causes do, and that we can grant them that role without sliding into environmental determinism. The audience is a mixed early-modern/Atlantic-world history department: most of them are social and cultural historians who are deeply suspicious of anything that smells like geographic or climatic determinism and will press me hard on the word "actors," and there's one environmental historian in the room who thinks the chapter is too timid because I keep hedging with "without sliding into determinism." Write me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order this crowd will raise them, phrased the way they'd actually put them, which ones have real force, what I should concede up front, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks, with a two-minute reply for it and a note on where that reply is soft.
