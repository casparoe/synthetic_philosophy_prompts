Next month I am giving a paper at a workshop that brings together political philosophers and formal social-choice theorists. The thesis I am defending is that the rule used by the parties behind the veil of ignorance to choose principles of justice should be minimax regret rather than maximin, and that this substitution leads to a revised version of the difference principle — one with strong but not lexical priority for the worst off.

The compressed argument of the talk runs as follows. In the two-class societies that dominate textbook discussion, maximin and minimax regret select the same principles, which is why the difference between the two rules has gone mostly unnoticed. Once the model includes at least three representative social positions — say, least advantaged, middle, and most advantaged — the rules come apart. Plain maximin cannot register how the middle position fares once the worst-off position is held fixed; its lexicographic refinement, leximin, does register it, but only by forbidding even arbitrarily small losses to the worst off in exchange for large gains to the middle. Minimax regret, by contrast, gives each position weight because the chooser might end up in any of them, but gives it weight without any assignment of probabilities. A position's claim is measured by the shortfall between what the chosen principle gives it and what some other available principle could have given it; the worst-off position tends to carry special weight because that shortfall is characteristically largest there, but the middle position does not drop out of the calculation as it does under maximin. The paper concludes that this yields a version of the difference principle with strong but not absolute priority for the worst off, and that this is closer to the deeper structure of Rawls's argument than the usual decision-rule reading of maximin.

The room will include: (i) two careful Rawlsians who think the original position is not genuinely a decision problem, and who hold that the difference principle expresses a condition of reasonable agreement among free and equal persons rather than the output of any decision rule; (ii) a Harsanyian Bayesian who holds that decision under ignorance is rationally admissible only insofar as it reduces to expected utility relative to some prior, and who will describe minimax regret as an ad hoc ambiguity rule wearing a different name; (iii) a formal choice theorist whose main concern will be whether minimax regret is stable under redescription of the state space and under changes to the menu of alternatives; and (iv) several uncommitted graduate students who know Rawls well but have not worked through decision theory in detail.
