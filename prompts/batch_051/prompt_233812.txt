I've been reading about two debates that I never thought had anything to do with each other, and now I can't unsee the parallel. The first is about life without parole: one side says a finite crime can't deserve an endless sentence, people change, and a punishment with no possible exit is basically giving up on a person. The other side says it isn't really about the original act anymore, it's about the kind of person the act revealed, and some people forfeit their right to be part of society, like, permanently. The second debate is about hell: annihilationists and universalists say a finite lifetime of sin can't justify eternal conscious torment and that a loving God wouldn't write anyone off forever, while the eternal-torment side says some people make themselves into the sort of person who belongs outside heaven, and the eternity follows from their settled character rather than from the size of their crimes.

I can basically line the terms up: society maps to heaven, life without parole maps to eternal torment, the possibility of parole or release maps to annihilation or universal salvation, the incorrigible criminal maps to the damned. My question is whether this is a real structural identity or just two debates that both happen to involve the word forever and a locked door. If it's real, what has to be true on both sides for arguments to transfer, and does anything actually transfer: if I became convinced that LWOP is wrong because people are capable of change, should that pressure me toward universalism, or if I think hell is just, should I become more okay with LWOP? And what does the parallel predict that's false — for example, in the legal case we can reopen a sentance if new evidence shows innocence, but that seems to have no counterpart if hell's judge is infallible, so maybe that's where the two debates come apart?
