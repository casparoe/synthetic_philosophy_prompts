While revising a course unit on self-knowledge, I recently had occasion to return to Moore's paradox after a student asked why so much weight is placed on a sentence that is not even false. I set the following question and received the answer below; I am grading it cold and would appreciate a second assessment. The question was: Distinguish the omissive and commissive versions of Moore's paradox. Explain why the resulting sentences are thought to be absurd even though they can be true, and discuss whether the absurdity is best located in what is believed or in what is asserted. The student answered as follows: Moore's paradox arises for first-person present-tense conjunctions such as It is raining but I do not believe that it is raining. The omissive version is p and I do not believe that p; the commissive version is p and I believe that not-p. These sentences are not formal contradictions because the weather and my mental state can come apart, so both conjuncts can be true. The paradox is created by the asymmetry with past-tense and second- or third-person analogues, which are not absurd in the same way. Moore himself located the source of the absurdity in the act of assertion rather than in belief. He held that asserting p conventionally expresses the belief that p, so the speaker of It is raining but I do not believe that it is raining is doing something like saying I believe that p and I do not believe that p at the level of what is expressed. This speech-act explanation is now generally accepted as the best solution, though some writers continue to defend belief-level accounts.
