You are advising a private foundation that has committed roughly six researcher-years — the equivalent of two postdoctoral researchers for three years — to foundational work on indirect normativity. By indirect normativity I mean the family of views on which at least part of what we ought to value, want, or do is fixed not by our actual judgments but by what we would value, want, or endorse after some specified idealization: full information, vivid appreciation of consequences, freedom from bias and time pressure, coherence of preferences, becoming a person one would on reflection wish to be, or a long-run process of open-ended reflection. The foundation funds philosophy and formal theory, not surveys, experiments, or policy implementation. Its broader interest is in whether any idealization-based method can provide a non-circular grounding for normative standards, so the agenda should be judged partly by how much it de-risks — or invalidates — that broader project.

The program officer wants a defensible research agenda, not a survey and not a reading list. She is deciding among the following candidate lines of work (add one or two more if something important is missing):

1. The underdetermination problem. Counterfactuals about what an idealized version of us would endorse are radically underdetermined: which information is added, in what order, represented in what way, and from which actual starting attitudes can each change the endpoint. Is this underdetermination fatal? Or can it be absorbed by treating the idealization procedure itself as part of the normative standard, so that different procedures simply define different standards?

2. The authority problem. An idealized counterpart is in important respects a different agent. Why should its verdicts bind us? Deference to the ideal can look like alienation, or like letting a hypothetical being vote in our place. The problem sharpens when we move from one person to many: whose idealization counts — each individual's, a collective's, future generations', all sentient beings' — and how do those verdicts aggregate without re-importing an unidealized decision procedure?

3. The path-dependence problem. Idealization is a process, and the route may matter. If the order of revisions or small differences in the starting point change the endpoint, there is no well-defined limit of ideal deliberation, only something like an attractor landscape with basins, cycles, or non-convergence. When does the process have a unique fixed point, and what should the theory say when it does not?

4. The reflexivity problem. The choice of idealization procedure is itself a normative choice. Would we choose this procedure under ideal conditions? What idealizes the idealizer? Any answer seems to invite a regress, and the standard way to stop it — a fixed point of the procedure applied to itself — may be non-unique, empty, or only trivially self-endorsing.

5. The residue problem. Even a fully specified procedure carries substantive commitments in its design: which cognitive defects count as biases, whether impartiality is demanded, whether the currency is preferences or values. Can a procedural standard be specified without smuggling in the very conclusions it is meant to generate, and if not, is that residue a bug or the honest price of doing normative theory?

The program officer wants your defended judgment about which of these problems — or others you add — should be worked on first, and why. For each line of work you prioritize, state: (i) why it is upstream of the others, that is, which further questions depend on settling it and which downstream attempts to specify values by simulating or extrapolating idealized deliberation would change most if it came out one way rather than another; (ii) how tractable it is with the tools researchers in this area actually have — decision theory, formal epistemology, philosophy of action, moral psychology — rather than with unavailable formal machinery; and (iii) what concrete result or development in the next two years would move it up or down in your ranking. Then give the portfolio: how you would allocate the six researcher-years and why, what you would ask each funded project to establish, and what would make you reallocate first. Be explicit about the trade-offs among importance, tractability, and neglectedness.
