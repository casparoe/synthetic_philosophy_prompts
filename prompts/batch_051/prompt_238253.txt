I cover health and science policy for a living, and a few weeks ago I sat in on a cost-effectiveness committee meeting that's been nagging me since. The same group voted, in the morning, against funding a colorectal screening expansion that their own analysts projected would prevent around ninety deaths a year at something like forty thousand dollars per quality-adjusted life year. In the afternoon they approved roughly the same annual outlay for a drug for one named patient with a rare cancer, at a cost per QALY that came in in the six figures. Several members called the second vote the proudest minutes of their term. Nobody in the room treated it as a contradiction. And I didn't either, at the time.

Since then I've kept noticing the same split everywhere: a transit agency will spend millions to make one dangerous intersection safer after a child is killed there by name, but won't spend the same money on an intersection its own data says will kill someone within ten years. Health systems set QALY thresholds and then suspend them for the right media-covered patient. The economists around me talk as though there's a clean distinction here — an "identified life" versus a "statistical life" — and a value of a statistical life that's supposed to make these allocations consistent. But I can't tell wether that distinction is tracking something morally real, or just rationalizing an inconsistency in how we respond to faces and stories. So I'd like you to give me the strongest principle you can find that would justify treating identified lives as worth more than statistical lives, and then your own verdict on whether it actually survives a case where the numbers are fully known on both sides. If it doesn't survive, should we keep the practice anyway, or try to override it?
