I've been trying to get straight on whether 'religion' picks out one kind of thing or is just a word I use for a bunch of stuff that happens to sit together in my head. So I went through some cases and wrote down my gut verdicts, and now I want to know weather there's a principle behind them or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Ordinary Christianity (Catholic, Presbyterian, doesn't matter) feels like religion, no hesitation. Theravada Buddhism as laypeople actually practice it, at least as I understand it—no creator god, monks, alms, merit, nibbana—also feels like religion, also no hesitation. Confucian ancestor rites don't: a family keeps an altar, leaves food, bows, asks the dead for guidance, and it still reads to me as family ethics plus etiquette; if someone insisted it was religion I'd push back. A civic cult of the war dead—national holidays, flags folded just so, a tomb of the unknown soldier, a minute of silence—doesn't feel like religion either, even though it has ritual and something close to sacredness. A secular congregation that meets on Sundays, sings together, hears a talk about living decently, drinks coffee, and explicitly says it isn't a religion? Not religion, but then I catch myself: if that isn't religion, why is Theravada religion? A joke church that exists only to mock religious tax exemptions and insists it's a religion? Not religion, though I can't say why except that it's unserious. And a movement that believes a superintelligent AI will eventually arrive and resurrect its faithful followers by simulating them—with moral rules, converts, apostates, a promised future—I keep flipping on. Half the time it sounds like a religion, half the time just an ideology with bizarre beliefs.

My current rule is something like: a religion requires relating to unseen agents that can help or harm you. That gets Christianity; it gets Theravada if devas count; it gets the joke church, since nobody there is actually relating to anyone. But it wrongly makes the Confucian ancestor rites religion, which I don't want, and it doesn't explain why the civic war-dead ceremony gives off a sacred feel that the Sunday coffee service doesn't. It also just relocates the AI question to whether a future superintelligence counts as an unseen agent. Can you extract a principle that gets these verdicts right, or close? I'm open to whatever actually works—sacred/profane, ultimate concern, belief plus practice, family resemblance. Be precise about the principle, and tell me straight which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. Then apply it to the AI-resurrection movement and to one more case I keep wobbling on: a community that keeps strict purity and food rules, wears special clothing, and insists that only doing the practices correctly matters, while having no settled beliefs about gods at all. Is that last thing a religion on your principle?
