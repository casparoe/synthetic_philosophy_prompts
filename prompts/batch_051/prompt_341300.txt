I have recently been reading about the rule of law and keep returning to a disparity that bothers me. Consider two statutes, identical in wording, each making some minor offense punishable. The first is recent and has been enforced without interruption; the second has never been repealed but has not been enforced for generations. Two people commit the same offense, one under each statute, and both are prosecuted. Most of us, including people who take the rule of law seriously, regard the first prosecution as unremarkable and the second as unjust; some legal traditions even recognize a doctrine, desuetude, under which a long-unenforced statute may be set aside. Yet the text, the legislative act, and the conduct are identical in the two cases. What, if anything, justifies treating a dormant law differently from an identical live one? I can see candidate principles—reliance, fair notice, the idea that a rule's force depends on a continuing practice of enforcement—but I am not sure any of them answers the harder question of why the lapse should be ratified rather than repaired. Give me the strongest justification you can find, test it against nearby cases, and tell me whether the asymmetry should be kept or given up.
