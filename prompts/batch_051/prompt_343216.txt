I've been returning to a parallel between Adam Smith's account of the impartial spectator and the trained reward model used in reinforcement learning from human feedback, and I can't settle whether it is a genuine structural identity, a weak analogy, or a category mistake. I'd like you to decide which it is, and to defend the verdict rather than settling it by feel.

On Smith's side: sympathy is the operation by which we place ourselves in another person's situation, and society functions as a mirror in which we learn to see ourselves as others do. But Smith is careful to say that the judge we finally consult is not the actual, nearby crowd — who are partial, ignorant, and caught up in the heat of the moment — but an imagined, fully informed, disinterested spectator whose approval or disapproval we internalize as a standing tribunal over our own conduct. On the RLHF side: human raters compare candidate outputs, a reward model is trained to predict those comparative judgments, and the policy is then optimized against the learned reward model rather than against live raters. The reward model thereby becomes a fixed, internal critic that scores candidate actions before they are produced, and the policy generalizes the raters' preferences to situations the raters never saw.

The tempting mapping runs as follows: actual spectators correspond to the human raters; the impartial spectator corresponds to the trained reward model; the correction of actual spectators' partiality, ignorance, and bias corresponds to regularization, aggregation, and whatever generalization the training procedure induces; and Smith's claim that we should care about the impartial spectator's verdict rather than actual applause corresponds to the claim that the policy should optimize against the reward model rather than chase the crowd's immediate praise. If the mapping held, one would expect reward-model overoptimization — where optimizing the learned critic ceases to track what the raters actually value — and reward hacking, where the policy exploits the critic's blind spots, to have recognizable moral analogues in Smith's system, and Smith's account of how we learn to be our own judges would be, at roughly the level of function, a specification of how learners internalize and idealize a population's judgments.

But there are obvious signs the parallel might be superficial. A learned reward model is a statistical summary of what raters in fact preferred and can inherit their biases; Smith's impartial spectator is defined normatively as fully informed and disinterested, and its verdict is supposed to have authority even when no actual observer would give it. It also has a point of view — it enters into both the actor's and the sufferer's situations — whereas a reward model scores outputs without itself being a simulated observer at all.

Please give me a precise assessment: state the mapping as exactly as it can be stated, identify what would have to be true on each side for results to transfer across the analogy, and name at least one concrete thing the analogy predicts that is false on the Smith side, or one thing Smith's account can do that the reward model by itself cannot. Where do the structures genuinely align, and where do they come apart in a way that matters?
