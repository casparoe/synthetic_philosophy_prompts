Legal systems sometimes leave statutes on the books long after they have ceased to be enforced, a condition known as desuetude. One view is that such dead-letter laws are a defect in the rule of law: they invite arbitrary enforcement, allow officials to ignore rules they dislike, and leave citizens unable to know whether dormant conduct will suddenly be punished. Another view is that desuetude is itself a source of legality: sustained non-enforcement is a decentralized way of repealing obsolete rules, and reviving a long-unenforced statute defeats the settled expectations that law is supposed to protect. Write an essay on whether, and under what conditions, long-unenforced statutes should be treated as having lost their force. Weigh the strongest arguments on each side and defend a definite conclusion; in doing so, address how the principle should constrain courts, prosecutors, and police rather than treating the law as a system of rules independent of its enforcement decisions.
