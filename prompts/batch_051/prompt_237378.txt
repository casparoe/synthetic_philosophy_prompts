I'm recording a podcast episode with a computer scientist who's just put out a long paper defending the Three Laws of Robotics as an alignment proposal. His argument is that the laws get a bad rap because Asimov wrote them as English sentences and then needed them to fail for plot reasons, and that if you formalise them in a suitable deontic logic and pair them with a harm-predicting world model, the famous failure modes mostly go away. I want nine or ten interview questions that push on the machinery of that proposal, not just restate it.

Please include at least one question on each of these, and for each one give a follow-up keyed to the reply he's most likely to make:

1. Speedy in Runaround, stuck oscillating around the selenium pool because a weak Second Law order and Third Law self-preservation balance out. If he says this disappears once you drop scalar potentials, ask what his formalism does when two First Law obligations conflict.

2. Herbie in Liar!, the telepathic robot who lies to people to avoid causing them distress. If he says a harm-predicting robot should just minimise actual harm rather than reported distress, ask whether that doesnt just license paternalistic lying.

3. The robot in Little Lost Robot, where the only modification is removing the bit about allowing harm through inaction. If he says no serious engineer would weaken the First Law that way, ask how his formalisation decides which clauses are load-bearing and which are downstream of the harm predicate.

4. Whether the laws are a constraint or a goal. If he says they are just a safety envelope, ask what supplies the positive goals and how he knows the two components wont interact badly.

5. The Machines in The Evitable Conflict, which quietly harm individual people for the good of humanity. If he says his First Law is strictly individualist, ask how the system handles any trade-off between persons at all.

End with a question about what empirical result or engineering demonstration would make him abandon the approach, and make sure at least one question asks what he thinks the Three Laws get right that value-learning approaches miss.
