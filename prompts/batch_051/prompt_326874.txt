In my high school psychology class we read a passage from William James's 1884 essay "What is an Emotion?", and it has divided my study group more than anything else this semester. The passage runs:

"Without the bodily states following on the perception, the latter would be purely cognitive in form, pale, colourless, destitute of emotional warmth. We might then see the bear, and judge it best to run, receive the insult and deem it right to strike, but we could not actually feel afraid or angry."

James appears to be making a subtraction claim: if you could take away all the bodily changes that follow an exciting perception, the leftover mental state would not be an emotion at all but only a cool judgment about what to do. My classmates keep calling this an unfalsifiable armchair guess, but I do not think that is quite right; a claim that says "remove the body and the emotion collapses into a thought" seems to invite certain kinds of evidence, even if it resists others. I would like you to design the strongest possible test of the claim in that passage and to be explicit about what the test could and could not settle. What concrete experiment or observation—using real, known findings where they are relevant, and describing the setup precisely enough that I could explain it to my class—would bear on whether felt emotion is nothing over and above the feeling of bodily changes? What pattern of results would count in favor of James, what pattern would count against him, and what part of his claim—the felt quality of an emotion, the causal order between body and feeling, the idea that emotion is the perception of the body rather than something merely correlated with it—would no experimental result settle even in principle? If the evidence came out mixed, which version of his view would survive and which would have to be weakened or abandoned? I am not looking for a list of sources; I want the reasoning and the test itself.
