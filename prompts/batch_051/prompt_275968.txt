A colleague defends the propensity interpretation of fitness against the standard tautology objection by leaning on an analogy: an organism's fitness is like the bias of a loaded die. A die's bias is a real probabilistic disposition of the die itself, distinct from any particular run of throws, and it explains why sixes come up disproportionately; likewise, "the fittest" need not mean merely "the ones that in fact survived," because fitness is a real disposition of organisms that explains differential survival and reproduction. The colleague's conclusion is that once fitness is read this way, "survival of the fittest" is no more tautological than "loaded dice tend to come up sixes."

Audit this analogy. Give the correspondence table: what plays the role of the die's weighting, the throws, the long-run frequencies, and the inference from a finite run of throws to an underlying bias? Then identify where the mapping breaks down — in particular, environment-relativity and frequency-dependence of fitness, whether the propensity is intrinsic to the organism in the way a weighting is intrinsic to a die, whether selection can alter the underlying "bias" itself, and how one could independently measure the propensity in each case. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the tautology objection from those that don't, and say whether the die-bias analogy actually dissolves the circularity or whether a revised version of the objection survives once the disanalogies are on the table.
