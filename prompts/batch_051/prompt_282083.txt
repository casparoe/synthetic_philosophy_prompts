You are a philosopher of archaeology who works closely with field archaeologists and cultural resource management (CRM) professionals, and you are practiced at giving practical, defensible inferential advice rather than abstract commentary. Think through the question step by step. Before settling on a verdict, brainstorm at least three distinct archaeological situations in which the inferential rule at issue would pull toward opposite conclusions, and use those to pressure-test your answer.

I am a contract archaeologist. I excavate and write up sites under tight deadlines, and my classifications feed into reports that determine whether deposits are recorded, sampled, or destroyed by development. Early in my career I watched colleagues attribute anything puzzling — odd pits, unusual bone arrangements, miniature objects, artifacts buried in strange places — to "ritual," with no independent evidence. The word functioned as a way to end inquiry: it explained a puzzling thing by treating it as not meant to be understood in ordinary functional terms.

So I adopted a standing rule: I never attribute a find or deposit to ritual or religion unless every plausible non-ritual explanation — utilitarian, domestic, economic, playful, post-depositional, taphonomic — has been ruled out on independent evidence, and unless the ritual reading predicts some further feature that could in principle have been absent and that we actually observe. The practical effect is that I almost never write "ritual" in a report.

I am starting to suspect this rule is a bias wearing a methodology's costume. It imposes a much heavier evidential burden on ritual readings than on utilitarian ones, even though I know from living ethnographic cases that ritual and practical use are often fused in a single object (a blade can be both a working tool and a grave good) and that religious institutions sometimes leave deliberately ambiguous material signatures. The rule may also smuggle in a modern Western assumption that the default use of an object is practical, treating belief as a kind of explanatory luxury to be avoided whenever possible. Yet the rule does protect the record from unfalsifiable storytelling, which is not a trivial benefit.

Is my standing rule defensible? If it is not, give me the best defensible successor — one I could actually run during a short write-up window, without access to specialists, in a report a permitting agency can read — and mark the places where the successor still requires judgment calls. If the rule is only partly defensible, say precisely where it fails and restate it so that it keeps the protection without the bias. In either case, apply your recommendation to three concrete cases and tell me what it does and does not license you to conclude:

1. A scatter of animal bones at the bottom of a disused grain silo; some bones are polished, but none show butchery marks.
2. A miniature ceramic vessel wedged beneath a house foundation, too small to hold more than a thimbleful of anything.
3. A set of stone blades deposited together under an unmarked floor, some still sharp and some deliberately snapped.

Be explicit about where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment, and where it would still permit reasonable archaeologists to disagree.
