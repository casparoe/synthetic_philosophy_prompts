I am taking a course on decision theory and practical rationality this term, and a point that came up in seminar has been bothering me: can you sensibly assign probabilities to your own choices while you are in the middle of making them? A classmate offered the following argument that the whole idea is self-defeating.

(1) To deliberate about whether to do A or B is to treat both A and B as open alternatives.
(2) Treating both A and B as open alternatives requires that you are not certain you will do A and not certain you will do B.
(3) So, if A and B are the only alternatives and your credences are defined over your own action, deliberation requires your credence that you will do A to be strictly between 0 and 1: the first non-certainty supplies the upper bound, and the second supplies the lower bound because B is the only alternative to A.
(4) But assigning a credence strictly between 0 and 1 to the proposition that you will do A is just what it is to regard your doing A as an event to be predicted from your evidence, in the way you would regard tomorrow's rain.
(5) If you regard your doing A as an event to be predicted from your evidence, then you are no longer treating it as something you will settle by deciding.
(6) Therefore, the credence that step (3) says deliberation requires is a credence that step (5) says is incompatible with deliberation. Assigning probabilities to your own choices while deliberating is therefore incoherent.

I half-suspect the trouble is in step (4): having a credence in "I will do A" seems different from treating the action as an external event one is trying to predict, but I cannot make the distinction precise. Is the argument valid? If it is valid, tell me whether I should accept the conclusion; if it is not, identify exactly which step fails, explain what the corrected version of that step would say, and tell me whether the conclusion can be repaired so that some version of it holds. I would appreciate the answer in American English.
