I'm a novelist, and I keep tripping over an asymmetry in how my readers (and, honestly, I) judge characters. I'm drafting a scene where a man gives an alibi that is literally true: he says he was at the library at nine, and he was. But he says it in a way that makes his wife believe he wasn't at the bar earlier, when he knows he was and knows that's what she's really asking about. Every word he says is true; he has just arranged the true words so that she infers the false thing. My writing group called him manipulative and gross, but not a liar. If I revised the scene so he simply said "I wasn't at the bar," they'd call him a liar and treat him as a different and worse kind of person. Same false belief planted, same harm, same motive — but the literal falsehood flips the verdict.

I want to know whether that gap can be defended or whether it's just a tic of ordinary moral psychology, becuase as a novelist I have to decide how much my reader is allowed to hate this man, and I suspect my answer depends on whether the distinction is real. Is there a principle that makes asserting what you believe is false worse than arranging for someone to infer it? Maybe something about the act of assertion, or what we owe a listener, or the kind of person the liar makes himself into? Test the principle against the hard cases — the technically true answer under oath, the friend who always answers a slightly different question than the one you asked, the person who says something true in a tone that means the opposite — and give me an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned.
