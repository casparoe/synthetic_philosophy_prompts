I've been going back and forth on a forum about the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis, and the discussion keeps collapsing into two camps: people who say quantum computing killed the thesis, and people who say it has nothing to do with the thesis at all. I suspect both are wrong in slightly different ways, but I'm having trouble making the distinction precise. The classic argument for the thesis, as I understand it, is that several independently motivated formalizations of mechanical procedure—Turing machines, the lambda calculus, and the general recursive functions—all pick out the same class of functions, and that this unforced convergence is strong evidence that the informal idea of effective computability has been captured exactly. Quantum computation looks like the first serious physical proposal to sit awkwardly with this story, but I can't tell whether it actually bears on the argument or only on a different claim about how fast things can be computed.

Here is the specific question. Grant, for the sake of argument, that Shor's algorithm provides a polynomial-time quantum procedure for integer factorization, that no polynomial-time classical algorithm for factorization is possible, and that a quantum circuit can still be simulated step by step by an ordinary Turing machine at exponential cost. Under those assumptions, does the convergence argument for the Church–Turing thesis survive unchanged? Does quantum computation expose a premise about physical realizability, or about what counts as a machine, that the convergence argument was tacitly leaning on? If what really fails is only a stronger thesis, state that stronger thesis precisely, and then say whether the original Church–Turing thesis ends up on weaker epistemic footing, stronger footing, or exactly the same footing as before—and why.

Please take the role of an expert in computability theory and the philosophy of computation, and treat this as a conceptual question rather than a request for a textbook survey. Begin by separating the different versions of the thesis and making the convergence argument explicit, including the premise you think is doing the real work. Then evaluate whether quantum computation bears on that premise or on a neighboring one, and close with a definite verdict: does the argument survive, does it need repair in a weaker but still important form, or should it be replaced?
