In a philosophy of mind seminar I've been attending, there is a move that keeps getting made as though it settles everything at once: the causal exclusion problem for mental causation is a non-issue because the mind stands to the brain roughly as software stands to hardware. A word processor crashes, saves files, and sorts lists — it genuinely does things — even though all the physical work is done by the machine's circuits; the program just is the hardware's activity described at a higher level. By analogy, mental states are not competing with neural causes and cannot be excluded by them, because they are simply what the neural activity implements. This analogy is being asked to do serious argumentative work: it is supposed to show that epiphenomenalism is off the table and that no mental-cause-versus-physical-cause competition ever genuinely arises. I want to know how much of that conclusion the analogy can honestly carry, rather than simply illustrate.

Please audit it systematically. First, construct the correspondence table: what plays the role of the program, an instruction or program state, the hardware, circuit-level events, inputs and outputs, the stored program, and the designer — and for each, give the mind/brain counterpart and judge whether the mapping is strong, partial, or weak. Second, lay out the disanalogies, and for each say whether it matters for the intended anti-exclusion conclusion or is a harmless difference. In particular, I want to know whether the analogy quietly imports a picture of the brain as a designed, discrete, cleanly layered digital system with a specifiable program, and whether the anti-exclusion conclusion depends on that imported picture. Finally, give a verdict: does the software/hardware analogy dissolve the exclusion worry fully, partially, or not at all, and is there a better analogy that preserves what is right about it while avoiding what is wrong?
