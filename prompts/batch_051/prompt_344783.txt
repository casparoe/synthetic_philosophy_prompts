I spent my career in the natural sciences and have only recently begun reading philosophy, so I ask your indulgence for a question that sits between textual history and speculation. I have been working through Alan Turing's 1950 paper in Mind, Computing Machinery and Intelligence, and in particular its closing section on learning machines. What interests me is not what the paper says but what its commitments imply for debates Turing never lived to see. I would like you to write that extension: a reasoned prediction of his views, derived as strictly as possible from the machinery he actually proposed, and flagging where his 1950 framework simply lacks the resources to decide a question.

Take the following as background constraints. Turing argues that building an adult-level mind from the start is the wrong approach; the better route is to build a machine with roughly a child's initial capacities and then educate it. He sketches a teaching process in which an initially unorganised network is modified by signals of pleasure and pain, supplied by a teacher whose interventions must be limited so that the machine retains its own initiative. He also floats the idea of giving the machine sensory input—something like sense organs—and then teaching it to understand and produce language as one would teach a child, rather than programming full competence outright.

Against that background, what would Turing make of the following? First, large language models trained by next-token prediction on text and code, with no child stage, no sensory organs, and no teacher in his sense: would he regard them as an instance of his programme, a proof that the child stage can be skipped, or a degenerate shortcut? Second, reinforcement learning from human feedback and similar alignment techniques, in which a pretrained model is shaped by human rankings: is this his teaching by pleasure and pain, his warning about excessive external interference, or some combination he could defend? Third, the embodiment debate: does his sensory-input suggestion commit him to the view that a system trained on language alone cannot become a full participant in the imitation game, or could he consistently accept today's text-only systems? Fourth, the spread of machine tutors in actual classrooms: where exactly would he draw the line between a machine that is educated and a machine that educates, and would he see handing children over to machine tutors as the realisation of his programme or a category mistake?

Please lead with your bottom-line prediction in the first sentence, then work through the four questions in order. Throughout, separate what you can genuinely derive from Turing's stated commitments from what is merely plausible or being projected onto him, and end by naming the one question on which you think his 1950 framework is genuinely silent. Paraphrase rather than quote; no bibliography is wanted. Keep the whole response to 800 words at most.
