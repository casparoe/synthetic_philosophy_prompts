I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been trying to understand a dispute about the dollar auction: an ascending auction for a fixed prize, say a twenty-dollar bill, in which both the highest and second-highest bidders must pay their final bids, but only the highest bidder receives the prize. The familiar psychological reading is that the overbidding this game reliably produces is escalation of commitment — participants keep bidding in order to justify the money they have already sunk, even when the smart move was to stop. A game theorist I interviewed disagrees. He argues that once two players have entered the bidding, each additional raise is often the correct forward-looking calculation: the player is choosing between a guaranteed loss and a chance to recover the prize, which is precisely the structure of an all-pay auction. On his account, the sunk bids explain why the situation is painful, but they are not what drives the next bid.

I want to know whether these two accounts can be separated by evidence rather than by disciplinary temperament. Please propose the strongest empirical test, or a small battery of tests, that would distinguish the rational all-pay account of dollar-auction escalation from the escalation-of-commitment or bias account. Be specific about the manipulation, the key behavioral measures, and the pattern of results that would favor each reading. Then tell me exactly what a clean positive result in either direction would and would not establish — including whether the remaining ambiguity is a fixable limitation of your experimental design or a genuine conceptual looseness in the idea that one can classify an escalated bid as rational.
