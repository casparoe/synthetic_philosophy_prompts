As a high school student who has been reading about language and thought, I've become stuck on generics — sentences like 'ducks lay eggs' and 'mosquitoes carry malaria' — and on how strangely tolerant they are of exceptions. Both are accepted as true even though most ducks never lay an egg, and only a very small fraction of mosquitoes carries malaria. One explanation I've encountered says that when people accept a generic, they are not agreeing to a statistical generalization about most members of the kind; they are agreeing to something about the kind itself — that the property is characteristic, striking, dangerous, or otherwise relevant to what the kind is like. I'll call this the kind-relevance claim: a speaker's willingness to accept a generic is driven more by how striking or characteristic the property is for the kind than by how prevalent the property is among its members.

I want to know whether, and how, this conceptual claim could be tested empirically rather than left at the level of intuition. Please design one concrete test: specify at least one manipulation and at least one measurement, say exactly what pattern of results would count in favor of the kind-relevance claim and what pattern would count against it, and then say what even a clean positive result would not settle — in particular, whether it would establish a fact about the meaning of generics, a fact about how speakers make judgments, or something else.
