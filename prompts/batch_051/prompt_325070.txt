I work as a software engineer on a team that ships probabilistic models, and a recurring argument in our reading group has started to bother me. A colleague likes to say that choosing a Bayesian prior is like choosing the initialization for a gradient-descent optimizer. On a well-behaved loss surface, he points out, two runs started at different points converge to the same minimum, and by analogy two Bayesians who start with different priors will converge to essentially the same posterior once they have seen enough of the same data. His conclusion is practical: when the dataset is large, prior selection is a low-stakes implementation detail, like which random seed you picked. I don't have formal philosophy training, so I'm not sure whether the analogy is doing real intellectual work or just carrying his conclusion. I'd like you to audit it. First, give an explicit correspondence table: what maps onto the initialization, the loss surface, the current iterate, the step size, the stopping criterion, and the local curvature? Second, identify where the mapping breaks. I have heard, for example, that priors only wash out under conditions such as the model being well-specified and the true parameter being in the support of both priors, and that two agents' predictive distributions can merge even when their parameter posteriors do not. I'd like to understand which of these are genuine disanalogies with optimization and which are just subtleties that don't affect his practical point. Please separate convergence in the limit from behavior at any finite sample size, and say explicitly whether his conclusion — stop worrying about the prior — survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If there is a better analogy for what Bayesian convergence theorems actually guarantee, I'd like to hear it.
