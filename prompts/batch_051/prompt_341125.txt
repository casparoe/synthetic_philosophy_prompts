I find myself pulled in two directions by the way Marx writes about machinery and automation, and I would like a careful adjudication.

On one side, his analysis presses on me because it does so much with so little. From a single fact about capitalism — that living labor is the source of surplus value and socially necessary labor time is its measure — Marx derives a set of strikingly unified predictions about labor-saving technology: it will be introduced even where labor is already cheap; it will throw workers into a reserve army that disciplines wages; it will deskill work by transferring craft knowledge into the machine; it will be adopted or held back according to profitability rather than human need; and it will push the system toward crisis, because capital that replaces living labor with fixed capital slowly cuts away the very substance from which surplus is extracted. He grants the progressive potential of machinery too: it could lighten toil and shorten working time, and his claim is that capitalism systematically wastes that possibility. The theory has an explanatory economy I do not find in the usual talk of displacement, transition, and compensating jobs.

On the other side, I have an intuition I cannot quiet: that the labor-saving character of a technology is a property of the technology, not of the social form in which it happens to be introduced. A loom, a container crane, or an automated diagnostic system that makes a task cheaper under capitalism would make it cheaper under any industrial society; and the societies that have adopted automation most aggressively have, on the whole, become richer, healthier, and able to support more people at higher material standards. I do not want a theory whose upshot is that the long-run material benefits of automation are an illusion or mere ideology.

The two sides seem to contradict one another, yet I am unwilling to settle for simply choosing one. Could you walk me through what each option costs?

1. Should the intuition be debunked as a short-run, aggregate-level artifact? If so, what exactly is wrong with it, and what kind of evidence would restore it?
2. Should the theory be amended so that it separates automation-as-such from automation-under-capitalist-relations — so that one can affirm the productivity of the first while criticizing the deployment of the second? Is that a genuine amendment, or only a restatement of a distinction Marx already draws, and does drawing it actually dissolve the conflict?
3. Is there a reading on which the intuition and the theory are compatible — the intuition being a claim about the development of the productive forces, the theory a claim about the social relations through which those forces are realized? Or does that reading dodge the real disagreement, which concerns whether a technology can be class-neutral at all?

A good response would say, for each move, what the positive burden is: what would have to be true for the intuition to deserve to be dropped, what would have to be true for the theory to deserve amendment, and what would have to be true for the compatibility reading to be more than a comforting compromise.
