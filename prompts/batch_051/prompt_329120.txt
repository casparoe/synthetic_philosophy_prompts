Last month I lost a chargeback dispute over a subscription I thought I'd cancelled. The bank's decision came down to the fact that I'd clicked "I agree" to terms I hadn't read, and the auto-renewal clause was in there. I mentioned this to two friends and they got into a long argument about wether that click was really agreement, with me mostly just listening.

One friend said: "Clicking 'I agree' isn't real agreement. Nobody reads those boxes, you can't negotiate a single line of them, and the button is designed to be clicked unthinkingly. If that's agreement, the word has stopped meaning anything."

The other came back: "But contracts in general don't require you to read every term or be happy with them. A click is a legally significant act, like a signature. If clicks didn't count, nearly all standard consumer contracts—software, insurance, leases, credit cards—would stop working."

First: "There's a difference between ordinary terms and traps. A clause waiving your right to sue, or an auto-renewal buried on page 40, shouldn't bind you just because of a click."

Second: "Then the click isn't what's doing the work—disclosure and unconscionability are. If a term is buried or one-sided, the law's answer should be to refuse to enforce that term, not to pretend no agreement was formed at all."

First: "But that assumes you had a fair chance to see and understand the terms. Nobody can read hundreds of these forms. A practice structured so almost nobody understands what they're binding themselves to is coercive in practice, even when the box gets ticked."

Second: "If you call that no-agreement, you undermine every mass transaction. Either standard-form contracts bind through something like a click or signature, or they don't bind at all—and I don't think you want the alternative."

First: "Maybe I do. Maybe 'agreement' here is just a legal fiction that keeps mass commerce running, and the bank shouldn't be allowed to use that fiction against him."
