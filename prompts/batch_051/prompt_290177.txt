I've been working through the philosophical literature on political obligation — roughly, the question of whether there is a general moral duty to obey the law just because it is the law — and I keep encountering the same menu of arguments, all of which I now understand well enough that I don't need them rehearsed: (1) consent, whether express or tacit; (2) fair play, the idea that benefiting from a cooperative scheme obligates one to bear its burdens; (3) gratitude for the benefits the state has conferred; (4) associative or membership obligations owed to compatriots simply in virtue of shared political membership; (5) a natural duty of justice to support and comply with reasonably just institutions, owed independently of any voluntary act; and (6) Samaritanism, the claim that obedience is owed because general compliance rescues our compatriots from the dangers of an unenforced legal order. I'd like you to give me an argument for political obligation that is not on that list and is not a rewording, special case, or combination of any of the listed arguments. Don't give me a survey or a reading list; give me one argument, developed enough that I could set it out and defend it in a seminar. Then tell me honestly what it is worth: does it ground a general duty to obey the law for ordinary citizens, or only a duty in special circumstances; where does it most obviously break down; and which of the six listed arguments does it come closest to, and how exactly does it differ from that closest relative? If, in your judgment, the standard taxonomy already exhausts the serious options and the honest answer is that there is no such further argument, say so directly instead of inventing a weak one — but if there is a serious one, make the case for it. Please write in American English.
