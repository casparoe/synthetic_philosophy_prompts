I cannot decide whether the following parallel is a deep structural identity or only a loose metaphor. Popper's solution to the demarcation problem says that a theory is scientific only if it is falsifiable: it must forbid at least some observable outcomes, and what improves its standing is surviving earnest attempts to refute it. A theory pliable enough to accommodate every possible observation has no empirical content. Machine learning seems to replay this pattern in a statistical register: a model is judged by its prediction error on held-out data that were not used in fitting, and a model with enough capacity to fit any training set is suspected of learning noise instead of signal. In both cases, a hypothesis is credited only to the extent that it was exposed to evidence that could have gone against it and survived a test it was not built to pass. Is there something deep here, or does the analogy break down as soon as the logical character of falsification meets the probabilistic character of generalization error? Lay out the precise mapping, say what would have to be true of both sides for the analogy to carry weight, and stress-test it at the points where it might come apart. In particular: does cross-validation actually falsify a model in Popper's sense, or is it confirmation by positive instances shifted to a held-out partition of the data? Do the bias-variance tradeoff and regularization correspond cleanly to Popper's tradeoff between empirical content and falsifiability? And identify at least one prediction the analogy seems to authorize that turns out false.
