As a retired natural scientist who now reads epistemology, I have been compiling a list of reasons why the familiar asymptotic claim—that, as data accumulate, the prior washes out—should not settle the question of whether prior specification matters. My entries so far are these:

1. The classical merging and consistency theorems are limit results; at any finite sample size the posterior may remain strongly prior-dependent, and the speed of approach to the limit is not uniform across priors or true parameter values.
2. The standard results require the prior not to rule out the true data-generating process; if the prior's support is too narrow, the posterior concentrates on a false answer rather than on the truth.
3. Agreement between agents is weaker than truth: under misspecification, two Bayesians with different priors can converge on a common but false posterior.
4. The theorems are framed within a fixed model and a fixed event space; they say little once agents revise their model class, add hypotheses, or re-partition the space as data accumulate.

I believe these four capture the standard reservations, so I am not looking for a restatement of any of them. Please provide one further consideration that is genuinely distinct—not a special case, rewording, or combination of the above—and that has practical force for a scientist deciding how much effort to put into prior specification. Then assess honestly whether this further consideration is as serious as the four I have listed, and explain precisely how it differs from them.
