I keep finding myself defending the claim that play is an end in itself, not a means to something else — not to creativity, learning, social bonding, recuperation, rehearsal for adult life, or whatever other payoff people usually reach for. The strongest version of the claim, to my mind, is the one you get in Huizinga and Pieper: a culture that can only justify play by pointing at its productivity has the relation backwards, and the game is the real point, not the work that pays for it. Play can have all those payoffs, obviously; I just don't think it's value rides on them.

What would count against this? I don't mean the standard utilitarian comeback ('if it has no payoff it's a waste of time'). I mean the cases and results that would make a committed defender squirm. For one: play is full of internal goals — games have objectives, scores, winning — so is 'end in itself' even a stable description, or does it quietly collapse into 'a means to goals that happen to be trivial'? Then there's the darker side: if play is good for its own sake, why do we balk at cruel play, or at the person whose entire life is gambling or grinding a game? Does the view have the resources to tell play apart from compulsion, or has it insulated itself so that nothing could count against it? Does it actually risk anything — should a world with less play, or more play, look different on this view than on the rival view that treats play as one instrumental good among others? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and flagged by which ones a defender should concede rather than dismiss. End by saying straight out whether the position is well insulated, and whether that insulation is a feature or a bug.
