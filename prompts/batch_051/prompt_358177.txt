Two people in my reading group got into an argument about the Luring Lottery, and I could not settle it, so I want an adjudication. The setup they were discussing is a two-player symmetric game with no communication. Each player independently chooses either C or D. If both choose C, each gets $100. If both choose D, each gets $1. If one chooses D and the other C, the D-chooser gets $1,000,000 and the C-chooser gets $0. The question was what a superrational player—one who assumes the other is also superrational and that identical reasoning in identical situations yields identical strategies—should do.

The first friend argued that superrationality requires evaluating only symmetric strategy profiles. Among pure strategies, both-choose-C gives $100 each while both-choose-D gives $1 each, so C is superrational. The second friend replied that this is too quick: once mixed strategies are admitted, the superrational strategy is to choose D with probability just under half—about 0.49995—because that maximizes expected payoff under the assumption that both players use the same mixture. This yields an expected payoff around $250,000, far better than $100. The first came back that this shows the criterion is incoherent: if both players can reason their way to the same mixed strategy, they could equally well both reason their way to C and guarantee $100 each. The mixed strategy only looks better because it averages over outcomes where one gets $1,000,000 and the other gets $0, but from a first-person perspective, if I choose D, I cannot assume the other will choose C; if our reasoning is symmetric, the other is as likely to choose D as I am. The second answered that the symmetry assumption is about decision rules, not realized outcomes. Each player independently asks, “What is the rule R such that, if both of us follow R, each of us maximizes expected payoff?” That is a well-defined fixed-point question, and its answer in this payoff matrix is the mixed strategy. The first then said that question already presupposes that the other will follow whatever rule I identify as optimal conditional on their also following it, which is a metaphysical assumption about aligned reasoning, not a decision-theoretic argument. The second replied that for ideal reasoners in a perfectly symmetric problem, that assumption is just common knowledge of rationality, no more mysterious than Nash equilibrium.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say who is more right overall. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
