I run an upper-level undergraduate seminar on self-reference and social systems with fourteen students and a single ninety-minute session. I want a classroom game — with roles, written rules, and small real stakes — that makes the participants feel the force of a tangled hierarchy and the inviolate level before any names are given to them. By a tangled hierarchy I mean the situation in which one level of a system governs another along one route while the governed level governs the first along another — the structure of Escher's Drawing Hands, in which each hand draws the hand that draws it. By the inviolate level I mean the level that cannot be modified from inside without already relying on the very power whose authorization is being questioned, so that every attempt to justify it either pulls the ladder up or closes into a loop.

The game should make the idea happen rather than present it. I want the group to spend the hour building a small self-modifying rule system: for example, a points economy or a simple card game run under a written constitution with explicit layers — rules of play, rules for changing the rules of play, rules for changing the change-rules, and possibly one more layer beyond that. The design should quietly arrange for the hierarchy to tangle: something produced at the lowest layer (scores, success in the game, popularity, accumulated chips) determines who sits at the highest layer (who may propose or ratify amendments), while that highest layer sets the terms under which the lowest layer is played. Then, about two-thirds of the way through, give them a concrete reason to amend the very rule that confers their amendment power, so that they run themselves into the loop — or freeze a level in place because they cannot amend it without standing on it.

Write this up as a complete facilitator guide. Specify the roles and how many of the fourteen students take each role; the physical setup, whether cards, chips, a whiteboard, a shared document, or something else; a timeline for the ninety minutes; the exact starting rules, including any rules that are deliberately underspecified and what that underspecification is for; the trigger that leads the group to try to change its own change-rules; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the ideas of stacking levels, the loop, and the inviolate level. The debrief should say what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run — a faction hoards amendment power, players dispute scores instead of noticing the structure, someone proposes scrapping the rules and starting over, the group treats it all as a joke — and how the facilitator should handle each. It should also be frank about which feature of the idea the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply directly afterwards: for instance, a real inviolate level can be an implicit presupposition of a system rather than an explicit rule anyone can point to, and the option of walking away from the game may be an exit that actual formal or social systems do not always offer. I want the names and the direct statement of the idea to come from me only after the debrief has already gotten them to articulate what they felt.
