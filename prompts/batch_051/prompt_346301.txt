I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm finishing a feature about a long-running dispute over the logic of time travel. After a year of moderated exchange, my two main sources have agreed to be treated as parties to an adversarial collaboration. One defends the single self-consistent timeline picture: there is exactly one actual history, and a time traveler cannot change the past because whatever the traveler does was always woven into that history, so the grandfather can never be killed and apparent paradoxes are impossible. The other defends the branching timeline picture: journeying to the past creates or splits off a new branch, so a traveler can kill the grandfather on that branch while the branch from which the traveler departed remains untouched, and the riddle dissolves through branching structure rather than through global consistency constraints. Write the document these two should sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The document should not simply restate each side. The work is in finding formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted. In particular:

- State, as a numbered list of agreed claims, what both sides can sign about the grandfather paradox itself, about the difference between changing the past and acting in the past, about what the paradox has to assume to be a paradox, and about the sense in which the traveler's departing timeline cannot contain a contradiction. Include claims about self-defeating and self-fulfilling attempts where you think both can agree on the description.

- Then provide a disagreements table. Its rows should be the precise points where they part ways, for example: whether there is one actual history or many; what a successful killing of the grandfather would be true of; whether constraints operate on events or on histories; and whether the branch's grandfather is the same individual or only a counterpart. Phrase each side's position in wording that side would accept for its own view.

- Identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both sides accept bears on it, such as an armed and uninterrupted traveler attempting the killing, a machine that destroys itself an hour before its first use, or a note delivered to the traveler before the traveler decides to send it, and record each side's prediction for that case in advance.

- Flag any part of the dispute you judge to be verbal rather than substantive, and say which part and why.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then the disagreements table, then a short cruxes section with predictions. The whole response must be at most 800 words.
