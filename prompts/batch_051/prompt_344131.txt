I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I run the scoring tooling for a product-decisions group at a mid-size consumer software company; please assume the terms I introduce below are my entire philosophical vocabulary. Every week we score about six proposed changes to our product and rank them for our head of product and a legal and policy reviewer, who mostly accept our ranking unless something is flagrantly wrong; the decisions are consequential in aggregate even though none of them is life-or-death. The people who run the scoring are engineers and product managers with no training in moral philosophy, and we have about thirty minutes per proposal.

Today we reduce each proposal to five numbers: how many users are affected; how much the change helps or harms the typical affected user; how severe the effect is for the worst-affected users; how easy it would be to roll the change back if we are wrong; and how much we trust the effect estimates. We normalize these numbers to a common scale and take a weighted sum. Our recurring fights all sit in the places where that weighted sum tells us to ship something we are not prepared to defend, or to shelve something we are not prepared to give up.

I suspect the underlying question is how values of different kinds should be combined. I have picked up three labels from discussions outside work: additive, where subscores simply add and one dimension can compensate for another; multiplicative, where the total is more like reach times depth times confidence, so that a near-zero in any factor collapses the whole score; and lexical, where one dimension has priority over the others and cannot be bought off by any amount of a lower-priority factor. The same structure seems to appear in a question I once saw debated: whether the value of a population is the number of conscious beings times their average well-being, or the sum of each person's well-being, or whether consciousness comes first and well-being merely breaks ties. I am not asking you to settle that population-level debate; I mention it only because I think our scoring problem may have the same skeleton.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure my team could actually run, grounded in whatever is genuinely defensible about the additive, multiplicative, and lexical options, that takes those five numbers and yields an overall verdict on a proposal. The procedure should specify which questions to ask in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops on its own with an accept or a reject, and where it must hand the decision to a human because the remaining question is one of values rather than arithmetic. It should be usable by someone who cannot reconstruct the underlying theory, should fit inside thirty minutes per proposal, should say plainly what it cannot do, and should mark every step that only looks mechanical but is really a judgment call dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on the three proposals below and tell me what it would have us do. If it overturns a verdict my team currently likes, say so directly; I would rather find out now than run a procedure that flatters us.

Proposal A shortens the main workflow by about one second for roughly forty million users and appears to harm no one. Proposal B makes that same workflow genuinely usable for about two thousand screen-reader users for whom it is currently nearly unusable, so the per-person gain is enormous but the number affected is small. Our current weighted sum ranks A far above B, and several of us think that ranking is wrong.

Proposal C would personalize a feature using behavioral signals that fall outside our current data policy. It would improve the product slightly for about ten million users, but it would quietly fold moderately sensitive health-related inferences into the profiles of about three hundred thousand users who have deliberately turned on the strongest privacy setting we offer, without asking them again. Our weighted sum favors C strongly because the aggregate benefit is large and the per-person exposure is diffuse; some of us believe C should be rejected no matter how large the benefit.

Proposal D promises a large gain for a medium-sized group of users, but the estimate comes from a model fit to weak data, and our confidence in it is low. Proposal E promises a modest gain for the same group with near-certainty. Our current weighted sum lets D outrank E because it treats low confidence as a small subtraction rather than multiplying the expected benefit by the probability that the effect is real; a few of us think the multiplication would reverse the ranking.
