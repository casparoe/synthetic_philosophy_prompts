I've been arguing with a German friend about the Kirchensteuer, the church tax they have over there. Members of the Catholic or Protestant churches get a surcharge deducted from their income by the state, which then hands it over to the churches. If you want out, you have to go to a civil office and formally declare you're leaving the church. My friend says this is just a way of financing religious communities and it doesnt force anyone to believe anything, so it's fine even by liberal standards. I'm not so sure. I keep thinking about Locke's Letter Concerning Toleration, where he argues, roughly, that the civil magistrate's power is wholly outward, consisting in fines, prisons, and the sword, and that such force cannot produce sincere religious belief because belief is an inward persuasion of the mind; therefore the care of souls cannot belong to the civil magistrate. That argument seems to cut against any state entanglement with religion, but the German system is strange because it's coercive only in a financial sense—it doesn't tell anyone what to believe, yet it does use state machinery to sustain churches. So I'd like you to analyze the German church tax system through the lens of that Lockean argument. Be disciplined about the mapping: what counts as outward force here, whether the exit option changes the analysis, and what it would mean for Locke's principle if the state's role is not to enforce orthodoxy but to serve as the church's tax collector. Does the case support Locke's theory, does it reveal that the theory is too blunt to handle modern establishments, or does it force a revision of what "force" means in church-state debates? I don't need the history of the Kirchensteuer in detail; I need the conceptual mapping.
