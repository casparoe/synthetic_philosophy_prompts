I keep noticing a strange echo between two parts of my life that have nothing to do with each other, and I can't decide weather it's a real structural parallel or just me liking my own idea too much. In a theology seminar we've been going through accounts of idolatry, and the thing that stuck with me is that the problem with an idol is not usually that the object is worthless. Idols are usually good finite things — the law, the nation, the family, a sacred image — that have been pulled up one rung and put in the place of the ultimate. The tradition we read even has seperate words for honoring a finite thing (dulia) and worshiping the ultimate (latria), and its core claim is that when a finite good gets promoted to the top of the ordering, it stops functioning as a window and starts deforming both the worshiper and the thing it was supposed to point toward. Then at my job, where we work on recommender systems and dashboards, I saw what felt like the same shape in Goodhart's law: a metric starts life as a proxy for something richer — satisfaction, community, solvency, actual learning — but once the metric itself is optimized as the target, people and models start to game it, and it stops tracking the underlying thing. The measure gets "promoted" the way the idol does: a subordinate good becomes the end, and now it corrupts the very value it was supposed to serve.

What I can't work out is whether this mapping is precise enough to be interesting. Is the parallel the same rank-error structure — a lower good treated as top good — or does it break in an important way? One obvious difference: idolatry is about a willed misranking of loves, while Goodhart effects can happen with nobody intending to misrank anything; the incentive structure does it on its own. Does that kill the analogy, or is it the same structure showing up at two different levels? If I try to run the mapping seriously, what would have to be true on both sides for conclusions to transfer — e.g., does the claim that idolatry corrupts the idolater predict that optimizing a proxy corrupts the optimizer, and is that actually true? And can you name a concrete place where the parallel makes a prediction that turns out false? I'm honestly not sure if this is a deep point about means being promoted to ends, or just a loose metaphor with a theology coat on.
