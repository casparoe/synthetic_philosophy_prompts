So the DMV where I live makes you pass two tests to get a license: a written one on the rules of the road—right of way, speed limits, what the signs mean—and a behind-the-wheel one where an examiner sits in the passenger seat and grades you on merging, parallel parking, checking your mirrors before a lane change. The two tests are not substitutes in either direction. You can memorize the entire driver's handbook and still fail the road test because you roll through a stop sign or misjudge a gap in traffic. And a farm kid who's been driving since he was twelve can fail the written test even though he drives fine. Nobody at the DMV finds this wierd.

What view of knowing-how versus knowing-that does this practice presuppose? Does the fact that the state refuses to take the written test as sufficient evidence that you can drive mean it's committed to know-how being a seperate kind of thing from knowing-that? Or could a hardcore intellectualist—someone who thinks knowing how to drive is just knowing a very large set of facts about driving—say the road test is only a cheaper way of checking facts that would be impossible to put on a multiple choice exam, like facts about your own body's position? Reconstruct the implicit theory, but be clear about what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits well with. Would an intellectualist DMV look any different in practice, and what would the state have to do for its testing regime to actually rule intellectualism out?
