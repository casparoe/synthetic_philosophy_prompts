I keep circling back to statutes of limitations, and I'm not sure what picture of time and responsibility they actually commit a legal system to. Start with the cleanest version of the puzzle. Take an assault that is caught on video, with a confession, a cooperative witness, and an offender who is alive, competent, and remembers the act. If the state prosecutes a month later, nobody thinks the delay matters to the charge. If the clock has run out, the same state will not prosecute at all, even though the only thing that has changed is the number of days between act and trial. What could justify that, apart from the practical fact that evidence usually decays? Notice the practical story doesn't fully cover the case, because I just stipulated that the evidence is fine and everyone remembers.

Here's where it gets stranger. Most systems I know draw the line unevenly: murder can be prosecuted decades later, but theft or fraud or assault eventually can't, and some offenses get their clocks restarted by the victim's age or by the offender leaving the jurisdiction. So the practice seems to say that some wrongs decay with time and some dont, and that facts about the victim can start or stop the clock. Is there one coherent theory behind all that, or is the doctrine a patchwork of different commitments bolted together?

I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory or theories. Does the practice presuppose that the passage of time itself changes blameworthiness — that the offender becomes, in a morally relevant sense, a different person? Or that retributive reasons have an expiry date even when the harm is permanent? Or is the whole thing a proxy for epistemic and social concerns: fading memory, the need for finality, letting people plan their lives without a permanent cloud over them? Then push on the line-drawing: what view of wrongdoing would license no clock for murder but a clock for embezzlement? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. And tell me, if we replaced the presupposed view with a flat one — say, that moral wrongs do not decay at all — what would the legal system have to change? Where would that be absurd, and where would it be an improvement?
