Last night a friend and I argued far too long about Fitch's paradox of knowability, and we both came away frustrated: each of us is convinced the other has dodged the real issue. She holds a broadly Dummettian semantic anti-realism about truth — truth, she says, cannot outrun our capacity in principle to know it, so every true proposition is knowable (p → ◇Kp). I incline the other way: I think there can be verification-transcendent truths, propositions that are true even though no one could ever come to know them. We do agree that Fitch's derivation puts her principle under genuine pressure as stated: provided knowledge distributes over conjunction and is factive, and provided the relevant notion of possibility obeys the usual modal rules, substituting the conjunctive proposition p ∧ ¬Kp into the principle appears, in the classical form we were arguing over, to force the conclusion that every truth is actually known — a conclusion she rejects as firmly as I do.

We have now agreed to write up our disagreement as an adversarial collaboration rather than continue talking past one another, and I would like you to draft the document that such a collaboration would produce. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher working on philosophical logic and the realism/anti-realism debate. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming about the formal derivation itself and about the ways the anti-realist might reformulate the principle without abandoning its motivating spirit. Then write the statement in two parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims that both camps can sign: claims about the formal machinery the derivation requires, the status of the Moorean conjunction p ∧ ¬Kp, what the derivation shows and does not show, and what remains open after it. Second, a disagreements table, with columns for the issue, a neutral formulation, the exact wording each side would accept for its own position, and whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. After the table, identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — and for each crux describe a case, argument, or formal test that both sides would agree bears on it, recording in advance what each side predicts it will show. We have our own guesses about where the cruxes lie — whether the principle is meant to range over propositions about our own ignorance, and whether the anti-realist can restrict its scope without surrendering what motivated her view — but you should not feel bound by those guesses. Do not write a dramatised exchange or a verdict on who is right; the deliverable is the constructive joint document itself, with the residual disagreement made exact. Finally, if you conclude that part of the dispute is an equivocation over words such as possible or knowable, say which part and why, and keep that separate from the genuine disagreements.
