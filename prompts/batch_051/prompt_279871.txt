I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a piece on what Bertrand's chord paradox did to the principle of indifference. The paradox asks for the probability that a randomly drawn chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and the familiar difficulty is that different natural ways of drawing a chord "at random" return different numbers. I want the centrepiece to be a joint statement that two opposed camps could actually sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, not a restatement of their usual talking points.

The two camps are these. The partition camp holds that the principle of indifference—roughly, assign equal probability to alternatives between which one has no information—yields probabilities only relative to a chosen partition or parametrisation of a problem. On their view, Bertrand's three answers show that an under-specified problem has no privileged partition, so the principle cannot supply unique, rationally mandatory probabilities from ignorance; an agent may legitimately adopt different priors, or suspend judgement, until something further—a real generating process, a loss function, a stipulation—picks out a parametrisation.

The invariance camp holds that the paradox is an artefact of an ill-posed question, not a refutation of the principle. A genuine probability problem, they say, must at least implicitly specify the physical set-up or the invariance class of the random object; once that is fixed, indifference over the appropriate invariant measure gives a unique answer. On their view, the three textbook solutions—1/3 from two uniformly chosen endpoints, 1/2 from a uniformly chosen radius point with a perpendicular chord, and 1/4 from a uniformly chosen midpoint in the disc—are correct answers to three different physical questions, not three equally good answers to one question.

Draft the joint statement that these two camps could both sign. It must include:

1. A numbered set of agreed claims, about what the three standard solutions actually assume and compute; about what the principle of indifference says pre-theoretically; about what is and is not established by the fact that different parametrisations yield different numbers; and about which parts of the dispute are merely verbal.

2. A disagreements table, with one row per genuine residual disagreement. Columns: (a) the neutral question; (b) the wording the partition camp would sign; (c) the wording the invariance camp would sign; (d) the crux whose settlement would move at least one side; and (e) a test, case, or argument both camps accept as bearing on the crux, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

3. Two cases threaded through the statement. First, a machine that draws a chord by selecting two points independently and uniformly at random on the circumference, so that both sides must state what they predict and why no residual disagreement remains. Second, the cube-factory problem: an inspector knows only that a machine produces cubes whose side lengths are between zero and one metre; indifference applied to side length returns one-half for the proposition that the side length is under half a metre, while indifference applied to volume returns one-eighth for the same proposition. Each side must state what it takes this example to show.

Hard length limit: 1,000 words for the entire document, including the table. British spelling throughout.
