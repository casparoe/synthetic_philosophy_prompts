I am preparing a talk for a political theory workshop whose regulars are mostly liberal-egalitarian political philosophers and public-law scholars. Many work in a Rawlsian or Berlinian idiom: they are committed to reasonable pluralism and public reason, or to a value pluralism that treats deep conflicts among genuine values as structural rather than resolvable; several think of social order chiefly as a problem of coercion, coordination, and enforceable rights; and a vocal minority suspect that talk of social harmony has historically functioned as a demand that the marginalized absorb conflict for the comfort of the dominant. They are broadly sympathetic to liberal constitutionalism and correspondingly allergic to communitarian or perfectionist appeals to community, shared values, or social cohesion.

The talk defends the thesis that order and harmony, properly understood, are distinct and legitimate public values; that liberal pluralism has a structural blind spot for them; and that this blind spot leaves liberal theory unable to account for the affective and cultural conditions of its own institutional stability. By harmony I mean, not the absence of conflict or dissent, but a standing condition in which a society's practices, institutions, and citizens' settled dispositions are sufficiently mutually reinforcing that most citizens can comply with shared norms without experiencing them as alien impositions. I argue that this condition is not merely a sociological fact or a private good, but a value that political institutions may legitimately aim at, and that recent Confucian political thought and certain strands of conservative social thought have the conceptual vocabulary to articulate it, even where they overstate how much shared ethical substance it requires. The talk proceeds in three steps. First, I distinguish three senses of harmony—absence of conflict, aesthetic unity or fit among parts, and the congruence just defined—and argue that only the third could be a distinctively political value. Second, I argue that liberal public reason and neutralist accounts of legitimacy habitually misread the third sense as one of the first two, and therefore cannot register what it might be rational for citizens to want. Third, I sketch how a liberal state could promote the institutions, practices, and civic dispositions that sustain such congruence without adopting a comprehensive doctrine or abandoning the priority of individual liberty.
