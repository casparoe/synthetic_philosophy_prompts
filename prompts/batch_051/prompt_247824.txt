I've gotten myself into a position I need stress-tested. In arguments about the science of consciousness, I've ended up defending something close to Dennett's heterophenomenology: when we study consciousness, we can't treat subjects' reports as direct observations of their private experience. We should treat them as data about what the subject believes is going on in their stream of experience. The method is to collect first-person reports, work up a neutral description of how things seem to the subject (a heterophenomenological world), and then the real scientific job is to explain why things seem that way, rather than to explain the seemings themselves as if they were accurate reports of some inner theater. I keep saying this is the only workable method, because it doesn't smuggle in a commitment to qualia or a Cartesian theater, while still taking the first-person data seriously.

What would count against this? I want a map of how the view could fail, not just a list of objections. I'd personally be moved most by a persusive case that the method, applied honestly, systematically misses a real feature of consciousness, not just that it annoys people by reading their reports as fictions.
