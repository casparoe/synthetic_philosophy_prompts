I've been thinking about Kant's antinomies next to something my officemate runs into all the time in machine learning, and I can't tell wether I've found something or just pattern-matched my way into a false friend. In the antinomies, reason seems able to prove both that the world has a beginning in time and that it can't have one; both proofs work by reductio, and Kant's diagnosis is that we've pushed concepts like causality and composition beyond the only conditions under which they do legitimate work. The contradiction isn't evidence about the world, it's a symptom that the faculty is operating outside its jurisdiction. On the ML side, my officemate was complaining that you can train two networks, or the same network under different random seeds, to essentially identical validation loss, and they'll be confidently wrong in opposite directions on inputs far from the training distribution — the people in his lab call it underspecification, and they treat the disagreement between equally good models as a sign that the model is being asked to answer questions its training data doesn't actually settle.

The parallel I keep seeing is this: in both cases there's a competence (reason, a fitted model) that's reliable inside a domain, a question that forces it outside that domain, and then a pair of confident, incompatible answers generated from the same constraints — and in both cases the failure is supposed to tell you about the tool rather than the territory. Is this anything deeper than the word 'underdetermination'? If it is real, what would have to be true of both sides for the Kantian move — refuse to answer the question as stated and diagnose the contradiction instead — to transfer over as something like 'only trust in-distribution predictions'? And give me the thing the parallel predicts that turns out false, the place where it strains hardest.
