Consider two settled features of contemporary labor regimes. France sets the legal standard working week at thirty-five hours, with overtime permitted only within statutory limits; Japan recognizes karoshi, death from overwork, as a compensable work-related cause of death, while very long hours persist in much of its white-collar employment. The obvious reading is that the two practices encode opposite valuations of work and leisure: the French cap treats work as a disutility to be rationed and leisure as the good to be protected, whereas the Japanese treatment of karoshi treats work as a duty that may legitimately make unlimited claims on a life. I suspect that reading is too quick, but I cannot say exactly where it fails. What does each practice actually presuppose about the value of work and leisure and about whose judgment should govern the use of time? Could the thirty-five-hour week presuppose not that work is bad but that employment is a scarce good to be shared, or that the state may limit working time to protect health, family, or civic participation even when workers would choose more? Does recognizing karoshi as a compensable death presuppose that overwork is admirable, or only that employers owe a duty not to make lethal demands? Where are these practices philosophically underdetermined, so that rival theories of the good life could each justify the same rule? And where should the Weberian Protestant work ethic thesis, the claim that disciplined work became a moral calling, be placed here: as a third view that competes with both arrangements, as a historical backdrop against which the French rule can be read, or as something neither regime genuinely presupposes?
