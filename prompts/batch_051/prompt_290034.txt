I've been a longtime reader of rationalist and effective-altruist forums, so the wild-animal-suffering position was familiar to me in outline, but I had never seen it compressed into a numbered argument until a friend produced the following version, and now I cannot find the exit. He reasons: (1) If a sentient life contains more suffering than happiness in expectation, then, other things equal, it is better that this particular life not be lived than that it be lived. (2) Most wild-animal lives are like that: the overwhelming majority of individuals in many species die well before maturity, and their deaths—and much of their short lives—involve starvation, parasitism, injury, exposure, or being eaten. (3) Therefore, other things equal, a world containing fewer such lives is better than a world containing more of them. (4) Habitat destruction is, among other things, a way of causing a world to contain fewer such lives; a drained wetland or cleared forest supports fewer sentient animals. (5) Therefore, other things equal, habitat destruction is welfare-improving. (6) By the same reasoning, so is a deliberate program of sterilizing or humanely culling wild populations to keep their numbers low. (7) But that cannot be right: a moral view that tells us to welcome, or even subsidize, the destruction of species-rich ecosystems has gone wrong somewhere, and the fact that an apparently clean argument gets us there should make us suspicious of one of its steps.

I would most like to keep two commitments at once: that the suffering of wild animals matters morally, and that the destruction of a thriving ecosystem is a loss rather than a gain. Please think carefully, acting as an expert in population ethics and welfare biology. Start by steelmanning each numbered step in turn; then identify the first step you would reject or modify, explain why it looked safe, and say what we are committed to if we give it up. If you think the argument is valid, say which premise is the least costly to abandon and price the alternatives.
