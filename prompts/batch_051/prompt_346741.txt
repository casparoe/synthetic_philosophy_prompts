A colleague at work argued that our platform's governance—its terms of service, dispute-resolution process, and user policies—is not government in any serious sense: a state is one kind of entity and a corporation is another, and the difference is one of kind, not degree. I went down a rabbit hole reading about early modern trading companies to sharpen my response, and I have ended up with an argument that seems to prove too much. I cannot find the bad step.

(1) The chartered trading companies of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries—the English and Dutch East India Companies, the Hudson's Bay Company—were business corporations: joint-stock, privately owned, and profit-seeking. Yet they raised armies, made war and peace, levied taxes, operated courts, issued currency, and governed subject populations over defined territories.

(2) All of those governmental powers were exercised under the authority of a single written instrument: a royal charter granting specified powers to a named legal person. The charter, not anything in the company's internal structure, is what authorized the exercise of what looks like public authority.

(3) A modern state has the same anatomy. A written constitution brings a legal person into being, assigns powers to offices, and prescribes procedures for exercising them. Structurally, a constitution is just a state's charter.

(4) The usual distinguishing features do not hold. States are said to pursue public purposes, but the chartered companies were also chartered for public purposes—colonization, trade, order in their settlements—and their instruments spoke of the public good. States are said to be permanent, but these companies governed for a century or more. States are said to maintain a territorial monopoly of force, but the companies maintained standing forces within their chartered territories. States are said to be territorial while a company is only an address, but the companies claimed and ruled over territory outright.

(5) That leaves what feels like the strongest difference: a company's powers are delegated by a grantor standing outside it, whereas a state's powers are supposed to be underived. But this collapses on inspection. "Underived" either means no one granted the state its powers, in which case the state's claim rests on an ungrounded assertion rather than a title, or it means the people granted them, in which case the state is just an entity whose powers are delegated by a grantor—the people—in the way a company's powers are delegated by a grantor—the crown. Either way, no difference in kind emerges.

(6) Therefore a state is a corporation in the same sense the East India Company was a corporation, and what we call sovereignty is corporate governance described from the inside.

(7) But then constitutional law is a branch of corporate governance, citizenship is a stake in a firm, and there is no principled difference between a government and a sufficiently autonomous private platform or private military contractor that runs its own rules, courts, and enforcement. I am not prepared to accept that conclusion.

I want the actual diagnosis rather than a label. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Which premise or step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it commit me to? I would rather keep the historical claims in (1) and (2) than accept (7), but I do not trust that instinct.
