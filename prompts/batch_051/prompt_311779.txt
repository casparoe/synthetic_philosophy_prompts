Here is an argument from a seminar on consciousness that I now suspect proves too much. Its conclusion — that judgments and reports are just as unearned as the qualia the argument sets out to eliminate — strikes me as unacceptable, yet I cannot find the step where the argument fails. I would like to keep the elimination of qualia if I can, but not at the cost of losing judgments and reports; I want to know what keeping it costs.

Setup: the Chase–Sanborn thought experiment involves two coffee tasters. Chase says the coffee now tastes different from how it used to taste to him; Sanborn says the coffee tastes exactly the same but he no longer likes it the way he used to. As the case is standardly told, nothing further that either taster or an observer could discover — physiological, behavioral, or introspective — settles whether the taster's experience itself changed or whether the taster's verdicts and reactions about it changed, in either taster's case.

Now the argument:

(1) For any sincere report of how things seem to a subject, there are at least two readings: a qualitative reading, on which a felt quality changed, and a verdictive reading, on which only the subject's judgments, standards, memories, or reactive attitudes changed.

(2) The Chase–Sanborn case shows that these two readings are empirically equivalent: every piece of evidence available from the subject and from observation can be accommodated by either reading without remainder.

(3) When two readings are empirically equivalent and one of them posits an extra item — qualia with an intrinsic qualitative character that outruns every functional role — the extra item is an idle wheel, and the leaner reading should win.

(4) Therefore qualia are an unearned posit and should be eliminated from our account of the mind.

(5) But the same standard, applied one level down, eliminates verdicts as well: a subject's report of having judged, remembered, or changed a standard can itself be redescribed as the confabulation of just such a judgment, since the evidence for the judgment is no more direct than the evidence for the qualia it was meant to replace.

(6) So if the Chase–Sanborn standard is sound, not only qualia but judgments, reports, and the very contrast between an experience and a verdict are unearned posits.

(7) That conclusion cannot be accepted, because the eliminationist needs judgments and reports in order to state the Chase–Sanborn argument in the first place; without them the argument has no subject matter.

(8) Hence the standard that eliminates qualia proves too much: either the argument fails at some earlier step, or the eliminationist is committed to eliminating the instruments of their own argument.

Where does this go wrong, if anywhere? I can imagine resisting step 3 or step 5, but each seemed forced by the steps before it. Name the step or slide, explain why it looked plausible, and say what the competing positions have to give up in order to reject it. Then say how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point you are least sure of.
