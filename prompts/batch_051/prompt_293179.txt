I'm a reporter who covers criminal justice and ideas, and next month I'm giving a lunchtalk at a sentencing-reform policy lab. The room will be mostly empirical folks—sociologists, data scientists, a couple of former public defenders, one law-and-economics type—who all genuinely want to shrink prison populations. My thesis, which they'll hate, is this: a defensible decarceration program can't be purely consequentialist; it needs a retributive side-constraint, roughly that the state may never punish someone more than they deserve, whatever the aggregate benefits. My argument goes: (1) pure consequentialism in principle licenses punishing the innocent when the numbers work out; (2) any view that licenses that can't ground a stable, rights-respecting reform program; (3) the only available backstop is a desert ceiling; (4) so even reformers with no love for retribution should accept a thin, limiting retributivism. Prepare me for the questions I'll actually get. Which objections will come first, phrased the way that crowd would phrase them, in rough order of how likely and how damaging they are? For each, give me a short reply and say where that reply is weakest. Be blunt about which objections have real force. Where should I concede, where should I hold, and what's the one reply that has to land for the talk to survive? Include the objection I'm probably hoping nobody asks. And tell me where this prep sheet itself is most likely to be wrong: what would change your ranking, or your sense of which of my premises is the weak joint. Finish with a last line of the form \answer{...} naming the one objection I most need to have an answer ready for.
