I am a novelist. I am drafting a novel in which a city's prosperity is sustained by one person's concealed degradation, and I have been using Ursula K. Le Guin's The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas as my touchstone. Before I let that story tutor my own, I want a precise account of its ethical architecture, so that I inherit the device and not the confusion.

Treat this as a work of criticism: an existing story read through a philosophical lens. The lens is the aggregation problem in normative ethics — in its familiar form, the question whether many tiny harms, such as a dust speck in the eye for an astronomically large number of people, can ever outweigh one person's prolonged torture, and more generally how far one may add up small units of welfare across separate persons. Omelas looks like the mirror image: Le Guin stipulates that the happiness of an entire city, composed of many ordinary pleasures none of which is individually momentous, depends on keeping one child in abject and unrelieved misery, and that every adult knows this. Where the dust-specks problem weighs many small pains against one great pain, Omelas weighs many small pleasures against one great pain. I want to know whether those two problems really are symmetrical, and what the story establishes if they are not.

Please take the role of a philosopher who works on aggregation and its critics. Do not retell the plot; assume I know the story. Begin by brainstorming three or four plausible readings of its relation to the aggregation debate — for instance, as a counterexample to unconstrained aggregation, as a dramatization of a lexical or threshold view of catastrophic harms, as a comment on the separability assumptions behind additive reasoning, or as something else. Then commit to whichever reading, or combination of readings, you find most defensible.

In doing so, address the following, at whatever length each deserves:

1. Whether Omelas tests the same principle as the dust-specks case or a distinct one, and whether a reluctance to aggregate small pains carries over to aggregating small goods.
2. The work done by the story's stipulation — the child's misery is not an accidental cost but a necessary condition of everything good in Omelas, and the citizens' happiness is partly a happiness that knows and accepts this. Does that violate the separability that additive reasoning requires, and does it turn the story into an argument about complicity rather than about the adding up of welfare?
3. The ending: the ones who walk away neither free the child nor stay and deem the trade acceptable. Is that a coherent normative position, or an emotionally legible but philosophically evasive exit?
4. The strongest available reply on behalf of a sophisticated defender of aggregative ethics — one that takes the story's stipulations seriously rather than dismissing them.

Conclude with a paragraph on what Le Guin's story can and cannot do as philosophy: what it shows convincingly, what it obtains only by stipulation, and what a novelist ought to retain or reject when borrowing its structure.

Keep the entire response under 900 words.
