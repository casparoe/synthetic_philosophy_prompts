You hear it all the time from civil libertarians and law-and-economics writers alike: a central requirement of the rule of law is that criminal rules be predictable in advance, so that a person of ordinary intelligence can tell which conduct will be punished. American courts have a doctrine for this, void for vagueness, under which a criminal statute can be struck down if it gives no fair notice of what it prohibits or invites arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement. Yet the same legal system makes vagueness load-bearing. Proof beyond a reasonable doubt, the reasonable person, gross negligence, reckless disregard, and dishonesty all have open texture; jury instructions turn on them, and courts rarely treat them as unconstitutional. A statute whose operative term hands police and juries unguided discretion is invalid; an instruction that hands juries the same kind of discretion is the backbone of every trial. What does this pattern presuppose about what legal language is doing, or about what it takes to follow a rule at all? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, separating what the doctrine strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Then tell me what would have to change if we replaced that implicit theory with the demand that every term in a criminal statute be reducible without remainder to non-vague language. Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
