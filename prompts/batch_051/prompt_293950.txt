I keep going around in circles on this argument and want to know whether it actually goes through. Its conclusion is that expected value reasoning forces a kind of fanaticism — taking a tiny chance of an astronomically huge good over a near-certain ordinary good. (1) Expected value is the sum, over outcomes, of probability times value. (2) So an act with a single possible payoff of value V at probability p has expected value at least pV. (3) For any p, however small, and any ordinary good with value G, there's some V big enough that pV > G. (4) So for any ordinary good and any probability, however small, there's an act with a higher expected value. (5) If rational agents should maximize expected value, then for any ordinary good you should prefer some tiny chance of a huge payoff to that good for certain. (6) So expected value theory seems to force fanaticism; but fanaticism is absurd, so something's gone wrong. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails? If it is valid, which premise is the best thing to reject — the unboundedness in (3), the move from higher expected value to 'should prefer' in (5), or the claim in (6) that fanaticism is absurd? Please seperate what's mathematically settled from what's genuinely contested in decision theory, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
