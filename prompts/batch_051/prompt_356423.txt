I am a retired analytical chemist, and since leaving the laboratory I have been working through epistemology with the same habit I brought to forty years of instrument work: I want to know the limits of a measuring process before I trust its output. That habit is what drew me to the debate over luminosity and the KK principle, and it is the theme I would like you to develop.

Please write a dialogue between two epistemologists who have just left a colloquium on self-knowledge. One accepts the KK principle: if a person knows that P, then she is in a position to know that she knows that P. The other follows Timothy Williamson in rejecting the principle on the grounds that no non-trivial condition is luminous; a condition is luminous just in case, whenever it obtains, the person is in a position to know that it obtains.

The Williamsonian should actually run the anti-luminosity argument rather than merely state his conclusion. Consider a subject who warms very gradually from feeling cold to feeling warm across a long series of moments, each one imperceptibly different from the next. If feeling cold were luminous, then at any moment at which she feels cold she would be in a position to know that she feels cold. But knowledge of such an inexact condition requires a margin for error: because the judgment would be made by a method that gives the same verdict at the next, imperceptibly different moment, a position to know that she feels cold at one moment would ensure that the verdict would be true at the next. Iterating along the series seems to force the false conclusion that she feels cold throughout. The lesson the Williamsonian draws is that the condition of knowing is itself not luminous, and that is exactly the failure of the KK principle.

The defender of KK must be a worthy opponent, not a caricature. Allow her the strongest available replies: distinguishing being in a position to know from actually knowing, questioning whether the margin-for-error premise is compulsory or a disguised skeptical demand, or pressing on whether the argument has really shown anything about epistemic conditions rather than merely about inexact sensory ones. The characters should answer one another's actual moves rather than deliver set speeches.

At some point I would like them to consider whether introspection is more like reading a well-calibrated instrument or like taking a measurement near the resolution limit of an instrument. That is the analogy I keep reaching for as a former bench scientist, and I want to know whether it illuminates the disagreement or quietly rigs it. Aim for roughly 1,500 words. By the end, a reader should see what the failure of luminosity would mean for self-knowledge, and the two should be able to say, at least provisionally, where the disagreement actually turns.
