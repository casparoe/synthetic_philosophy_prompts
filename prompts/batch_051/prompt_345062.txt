I have been working through the following argument against the many-worlds interpretation, and I have reached the point where I suspect it proves too much, but I cannot say exactly where it goes wrong.

Background as I understand it. In Everettian quantum mechanics there is no collapse; the universal state evolves unitarily, and after a measurement the state is a superposition of outcome terms. Each term describes a branch containing an observer who sees that outcome, and each branch is equally real. The squared modulus of a branch's amplitude is supposed to give the probability of the corresponding outcome — this is the Born rule.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) Consider a measurement with two possible outcomes whose amplitudes have squared moduli of 0.99 and 0.01. On the Everettian picture, both outcomes occur: there is a post-measurement observer who records the 0.99 outcome and one who records the 0.01 outcome, and each is a genuine future self of the pre-measurement observer.

(2) Before the measurement, a rational agent knows her future contains both observers. Since the physics gives her nothing with which to single out one future self rather than the other, the principle of indifference applies: she should divide her credence equally, giving each outcome a probability of one-half.

(3) Therefore the Everettian agent should assign probability one-half to the 0.99 outcome and one-half to the 0.01 outcome, regardless of the amplitudes.

(4) But the Born rule assigns probabilities 0.99 and 0.01, and the Born rule is not optional: it is the empirical core of quantum mechanics, confirmed whenever statistics are gathered over repeated trials. An agent who bet at even odds on the 0.01 outcome would lose systematically.

(5) So the many-worlds interpretation delivers the wrong probabilities — or, if branch probability is really no more than branch counting, then it delivers no usable probabilities at all. Since an interpretation that cannot recover the Born rule is inadequate, the many-worlds interpretation is refuted.

I would like to keep the first premise if I can, and I would also like to keep the Born rule as a datum; the argument tells me I cannot have both. My question is this. Please work through the argument step by step and tell me: which step, if any, should be rejected; why that step looked so natural; what rejecting it costs — in particular, what an Everettian must then do to recover the Born rule, or whether the problem simply reappears elsewhere; and whether the argument can be repaired by amending one of the premises. Before settling on your diagnosis, briefly canvass at least two different places a reader might reasonably locate the error, and say which you find most defensible and why.
