I spent forty years mapping strata and reconstructing old depositional environments from scraps of rock, so I'm used to underdetermination; the difference is that in geology you can usually go drill another hole, and with past human beliefs there's nowhere left to drill. Lately I've been reading excavation reports for fun, and one described a burial: an adult with two pots at the feet, a scatter of ochre near the skull, and a carved bone pin on the chest. The authors called it a ritual specialist burial and said the pots were provisions for the afterlife. Maybe, I thought. But then I worked myself into the following argument, and now I dont see how to get out of it.

(1) A burial gives you only objects in a spatial arrangement; you never observe the belief behind the pots or the ochre. (2) The same arrangement could be produced by lots of different beliefs and practices: provisions for an afterlife, mourning gifts, a display of status, a taboo on reusing the dead person's things, or an attempt to keep the dead from returning. (3) So picking one account over the others requires something beyond the objects, usually analogy with living people or background assumptions about human nature. (4) But the analogy or assumption is only licensed if you already know the past people were relevantly like the comparison group, and you can't know that without knowing the very beliefs and institutions you're trying to reconstruct. (5) So the standard inference from grave goods to afterlife belief is circular, or at best arbitrary among equally good alternatives. (6) And if that's right for grave goods, it's right for every other reconstruction built from objects: granary versus temple, palace versus meeting house. (7) So no belief or institution can be reconstructed from material remains alone.

That conclusion seems to prove too much, since it would make almost all talk of ritual, religion, status, and even everyday functional attributions into guesswork. I find myself wanting to keep the modesty in (1) and the generality in (6), because the problem really does look general to me, but I can't stomach (7). So I'd like a diagnosis: which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? If the live options are rejecting (4), rejecting (5), or biting the bullet on (7), what does each one commit me to downstream?
