At dinner last night my housemate (a CS PhD, kind of insufferable about it) tried to explain the frame problem to me, and here's the summary he gave:

"The frame problem is the problem of formalizing which facts stay the same when an action happens. If I paint a block red, its position doesn't change, and you don't want to write a separate axiom for every fact that isn't affected by the action. It looked hard in the 60s and 70s, but once people built nonmonotonic logics and successor-state axioms, the technical problem was basically solved. The so-called philosophical frame problem is just philosophers dressing up the obvious point that you can't enumerate every downstream consequence of an action. Any halfway-decent planning system already handles the real issue, so people who still talk about it as a deep unsolved problem are mostly confused."

Some of this matches what I vaguely remember from a cognitive science class, but the last sentence feels off, like he's dismissing something philosophers of mind took to be genuinely hard, and I dont know enough to say where. Is his summary fair? Please seperate (1) what a careful person working on this would accept, (2) what's a simplification, and (3) what's a conflation or just wrong, and tell me whether his bottom line (that the problem is basically solved and the rest is confusion) survives the corrections.
