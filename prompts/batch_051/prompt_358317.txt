I run a film discussion group and we're doing Kurosawa's Rashomon next, and lately I can't stop overlaying it with a debate I keep running into in the philosophy of history. On one side is the old ideal that the historian's task is to establish the past as it actually happened, and that the truth of an event exists independently of who tells it. On the other is the skeptical view that there is no finished story sitting in the past waiting to be discovered: every account is assembled by someone with motives, expectations, and a narrative of their own, so different tellings are not just errors heaped on one real event but partly different objects. The film seems purpose-built for this problem. It gives a bandit, a wife, a dead samurai speaking through a medium, and a woodcutter, each telling a story about a death in the woods that cannot all be squared, and then wraps those stories inside the priest and woodcutter's own retelling at the gate.

I want a committed reading, not a survey of possibilities. Does Rashomon ultimately leave room for a single real event that memory and self-interest have distorted, a truth a patient investigator could in principle dig out, or does it make the stronger case that the event is nothing apart from the tellings, and that no one, including the woodcutter, can get outside their own interested account? Make the best case for your answer, point me to the scenes that force the issue, say where the film resists or complicates that reading, and tell me what the film can add to the historian's debate that an essay arguing for either side cannot.
