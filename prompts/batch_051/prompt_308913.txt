I am currently taking a democratic theory seminar in which we are working through the boundary problem: for any collective decision, who belongs to the demos that is entitled to authorize and contest it? My professor has so far refused to state the principle she is using, and has instead given case-by-case rulings about who belongs to the demos for a state's permanent lawmaking, roughly its national electorate. Her rulings so far are these:

- A non-citizen who has lived and worked in the state for nine years, is subject to the same civil and criminal law as everyone else, and pays full taxes: included.
- A citizen by birth who left twenty-five years ago, owns no property in the state, earns no income taxed by the state, and has no intention of returning: not included.
- A tourist one week into a two-week visit, who must obey local law while present: not included.
- A sixteen-year-old citizen who lives in the state, attends school, works part-time, and pays income tax: included.
- A citizen serving a four-year prison sentence, due for release and resettlement in the same municipality, and currently disenfranchised under the law: included.

I am trying to work out which principle is actually doing the work for her. Three principles I can formulate fit everything she has said so far: an all-subjected principle, on which everyone pervasively subject to the state's coercive law belongs; an all-affected principle, on which everyone whose interests are importantly affected by the decision belongs; and a stakeholding principle, on which everyone with a durable stake in the political community's long-term future belongs. A fourth principle, membership for those who contribute through work and taxes, fits most of the rulings but is difficult to square with the prisoner.

The case for next week is a non-citizen who has never set foot in the state and is not subject to its laws, but who lives in a town immediately across the border whose only drinking-water source is being contaminated by a plant licensed and regulated by the state; the state's regulator has exclusive authority over that permit. What does each of the three principles entail for this case, and should she belong to the demos for the permitting decision, and in what form? Then give your best prediction of how my professor will rule, with your reasons, and identify the further case that would most cleanly separate whichever of the three principles remain in contention.
