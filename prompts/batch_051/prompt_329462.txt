I'm a PhD student in environmental philosophy, and I'm putting together a teaching module on risk and the energy transition. I keep running into people who treat HBO's Chernobyl as if it pretty much settles the question—you watch it and obviously no fission plant should ever be built or kept running, case closed. I dont think that's obviously what the series shows, but I also cant shake the feeling that the anti-nuclear reading is doing real work in it. Here's what I want: read the miniseries through the precautionary principle and the wider ethics of nuclear power in the energy transition (the Germany-exit-vs-France-fleet fight). Where does the show actually land on whether a well-run, transparently regulated nuclear fleet is justifiable? Does the disaster belong to the RBMK design and the Soviet command system, or to the technology as such? And I'd like you to be honest about the places where the show resists the pro-nuclear-nuance reading—because it spends a lot of time on liquidators' acute radiation sickness and an invisible poison that turns the whole landscape into a threat, which looks like it's doing a lot of philosophical work toward a strong precautionary stance. Which scenes would you point to for the strongest version of each side, and what does the show add to the policy-level argument that neither the German nor the French case quite gets at? I'm less interested in whether the show is historically accurate than in whether it's a good object for thinking through tail risk, acceptable risk, and the way an aesthetic treatment of catastrophe might stack the deck.
