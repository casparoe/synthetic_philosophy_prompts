Everettians and their critics have argued about quantum immortality and quantum suicide for years, and no one moves. I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not a verdict.

The shared setup is this. A subject rigs a lethal device to a quantum event—say, a photon whose spin decides whether the device fires—and sets the survival amplitude very low. Under many-worlds, the device both fires and does not, and the subject survives in at least one branch. From outside, an observer expects, with overwhelming probability, to see a death. The fight is over the first-person story. One camp says death is never experienced from the inside, because there is always a branch in which the subject lives, so the subject should expect to survive every run; quantum suicide is at least a private test of many-worlds, perhaps even a good bet. The opposing camp calls this a confusion among probability, possibility, and existence: that a survival branch exists does not fix how likely the subject is to find themselves in it, and the only non-arbitrary basis for self-locating expectation in a branching universe is squared amplitude, which makes the bet disastrous. A third camp says the reasoning smuggles in a continuous self that rides only the branches where the body keeps working—something the formalism never supplies.

My working hypothesis is that the genuine fault line is not the physics but the meaning of a first-person expectation for a branching observer: whether it should track branch count, branch weight, or decision-theoretic utility, and whether some of these are even well-defined. Diagnose the structure of this dispute. Is it verbal? Is it a hidden formal or empirical question? Is it an unarguable clash of intuitions about self-location and probability? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress—a no-go theorem, a decision-theoretic argument, a sharper definition of the relevant probability, or a reason to declare the question ill-posed?
