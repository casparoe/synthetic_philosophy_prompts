I teach a third-year undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of biology, and I am writing the final examination. I would like one essay question, to be answered in 45 minutes, marked out of 25, on the problem of defining life and the status of borderline cases. The question must not be one a student can answer by reciting the standard definitions or listing properties. It should force students to show that they understand why the main rival accounts of life — briefly, organisational accounts that tie life to self-production within a boundary, metabolic accounts that tie it to the maintenance of a far-from-equilibrium state, and evolutionary accounts that tie it to natural selection — pick out different entities as alive, and why a case that splits them is a problem for the definitional project itself rather than a curiosity at its margins.

I want the question built around the following constructed case, which you may refine as you see fit. Researchers maintain a synthetic vesicle in a flow reactor. It encloses a minimal catalytic system, imports nucleotide and amino acid precursors along with an energy source, and continually synthesises its own membrane components and catalysts, keeping itself far from equilibrium. When mechanically extruded, a vesicle gives rise to daughter vesicles that repeat the process, so the system reproduces. However, the template is not copied from parent to daughter: each generation's template is synthesised afresh by the research team and inserted, so replication is effectively error-free and no variation is ever inherited. The result is a system that is self-maintaining and reproductive but has no population-level evolution. The question should present this case, with whatever detail is needed to make it vivid, and ask students to reach a considered verdict on whether the system is alive, grounding their answer in at least two of the accounts above, and to say what their verdict shows about whether life admits of a definition of the necessary-and-sufficient-conditions kind.

Please provide three things. First, the question itself, worded as it would appear on the paper. Second, a model answer of roughly 650 words that commits to a verdict and defends it: it should say which criterion it gives priority to and why, confront the strongest objection to that choice, and draw an explicit lesson about the project of defining life rather than leaving both sides on the table. Third, a marking scheme for the 25 marks that names the specific moves separating a first-class answer from an upper-second answer, and identifies the specific misunderstandings that should cost marks — for instance, treating a definition of life as a dictionary definition, sliding between 'alive' as an operational category and life as a natural kind, or assuming that a single hard case refutes an entire account. I would prefer that you commit to the best version of the question and to one defensible verdict in the model answer, with reasons, rather than offering me options to choose among. No bibliography or citations are needed; the materials should stand on their own arguments.
