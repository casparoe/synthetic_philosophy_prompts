Ok so I just spent a couple hours arguing with a friend about word embeddings and whether the geometry is real meaning or just co-occurrence statistics, and I walked away with a bunch of verdicts that I can't fit into one picture. He kept quoting the Mikolov et al. 2013 paper 'Distributed Representations of Words and Phrases and their Compositionality' at me, especially this passage:

"This compositionality suggests that a non-obvious degree of language understanding can be obtained by using basic mathematical operations on the word vector representations."

I think that sentence gets something right and something a little too strong at the same time, but I can't say where the line is. Here's where I've landed on cases:

- vec("king") - vec("man") + vec("woman") landing nearest "queen" feels real to me. It's not a coincidence of the corpus; the space has picked up an actual relation about monarchs and gender, or whatever you want to call it.
- But vec("doctor") - vec("man") + vec("woman") landing near "nurse" I will not call a semantic relation. That's the model learning a stereotype, which is a different kind of fact than a meaning.
- "hot" and "cold" are often extremely close, and I'm fine with that. Similarity is about being swappable in context, not about being synonyms. They're distributionally similiar because they fill the same slots.
- The idiom failures — "Boston Globe" not being composed from "Boston" and "Globe", and "Canada" + "Air" not giving "Air Canada" — I read as showing that vector arithmetic doesn't do real semantic composition. Phrase vectors are a patch, not a theory of composition.
- A static vector for "bank" smears the money sense and the river sense together. I don't think that's a deep objection; it's just a limitation of one architecture.
- A rare word that occurs six times has a junk vector, but I don't take that as a point about meaning either. That's just an estimation problem.
- "justice" has a vector, and I don't think the vector is the concept. It encodes how the word is used, which is all I'd want from a use-based account anyway.

So my verdicts say: analogies are real, stereotypes aren't, antonyms are fine, composition fails, polysemy is fixable, sparse data is irrelevant, and meaning is use. Is there a single principle about when the geometry of an embedding space is carrying semantic structure and when it's just carrying corpus regularities, that captures all of these? And which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? I'd also like the principle applied to a few cases I haven't listed — say, cross-lingual aligned spaces, or a space after debiasing. Most of all: does the Mikolov sentence about "a non-obvious degree of language understanding" survive my verdicts, or are my verdicts secretly a rejection of it with extra steps?
