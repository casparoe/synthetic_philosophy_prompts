Two members of my seminar argued about whether a long-run prediction can be defended as "right but early," and they used Malthus as the case.

(1) The first said the Malthusian prediction — that population must eventually outrun the food supply and be checked by famine — has spent two centuries failing: food output per person has on average risen, and famine has become rarer and increasingly a matter of politics rather than ecology. The only thing keeping the prediction alive is the phrase "not yet," and a prediction that can always be deferred another century is consistent with every observation; it is unfalsifiable and pins nothing down.

(2) The second replied that the first was testing the wrong object. Malthus did not set a date; he made a structural claim about a finite planet — that multiplicative population growth must eventually run against diminishing returns on land, with the balance restored by misery, vice, or moral restraint. Industrial agriculture did not refute that structure; it changed the parameters and bought time. "Right but early" is a legitimate verdict when the limiting mechanism is real but operates more slowly than the forecaster assumed.

(3) The first came back that this move is too powerful: it would let any catastrophe claimant — a doomsayer, an astrologer — absorb every failed deadline by saying the interval was longer than expected. A claim with no sell-by date is compatible with any evidence whatsoever, so it is not a testable prediction.

(4) The second replied that the alternative overcorrects. If a long-run claim is refuted the moment the catastrophe has not yet arrived, we would have refuted all reasoning about finite resources, climate thresholds, and other slow-binding limits, regardless of when they bind. The real distinction, they said, is between a dated forecast and a conditional: "if population grows faster than yields, the difference must eventually come out somewhere" is not falsified by a century of innovation. It is falsified only if the antecedent stops holding — for example, population growth durably falls to zero before the ceiling binds — or if yields are shown not to be bounded in the relevant way. The structural claim makes contact with evidence; it merely refuses to wear a date.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say who is right about what at each stage, whether the two are partly talking past one another, and what each would have to concede before the dispute could actually be settled.
