I'm making a handout on quantified modal logic, and I want one clean little countermodel as the centerpiece — not the usual presentation with boxed universal quantification, but a model where the Barcan formula and its converse visibly come apart in their existential readings. Build me the smallest variable-domain Kripke model you can — worlds, accessibility relation, domain function D, and an interpretation of a single unary predicate F — that meets all of these specs:

- Accessibility is reflexive and transitive but not symmetric (so S4-ish; from a later world you can't get back to an earlier one).
- Domains are increasing: whenever world w can see world u, D(w) is a subset of D(u).
- Every domain is nonempty.
- At most two worlds and two objects total.

With quantifiers at each world ranging only over that world's domain, I want the converse Barcan formula, ∃x◇Fx → ◇∃xFx, to be true at every world, and the Barcan formula, ◇∃xFx → ∃x◇Fx, to have a false instance at the root.

After you present the model: first, evaluate both formulas at the root and at the other world and show exactly where they diverge; second, identify wich structural feature of the setup is doing the work and argue briefly that nothing smaller would have done the job; third, say what happens when you remove that feature — do the two formulas collapse back together, and under what fix (constant domains? symmetric access?)? Finally, translate the model into a plain-English "two ways things could be" story, and end with the strongest objection a defender of the Barcan formula — say, an actualist who doesn't want merely possible objects in the range of the actual world's quantifiers — would press against treating that story as a genuine counterexample.
