I've been going back and forth with someone on a LessWrong-adjacent forum about whether it's coherent to say a current LLM is actually being irrational when it refuses a harmless request, and I keep rebuilding the same argument and insisting it must be sound until someone tells me I'm equivocating. My cleaned-up version goes:

(1) The intentional stance says that if the most reliable and cheapest way to predict a system's behavior is to treat it as a rational agent with beliefs, desires, and goals, then that's the right stance to adopt. (2) For a large language model, the only tractable way to predict refusals, confabulations, and vulnerable sycophancy is to treat the model as believing things about the user's request, the subject matter, and the norms of conversation. (3) So the intentional stance is warranted for LLMs, and claims like "the model believes the request is harmful" are genuinely explanatory, not just loose talk. (4) But belief is exactly the sort of state that can be true or false, well- or poorly-supported, rational or irrational. (5) Therefore, if the model has beliefs in the sense established in (3), then some of those beliefs can be straightforwardly evaluated as irrational, for instance when the model believes a clearly harmless request is harmful. (6) So it's perfectly coherent to say the model is being irrational when it refuses.

My friend says the trouble is in moving from (2) to (3): being forced to treat a system as if it believes something doesn't make it a believer, any more than treating a chess engine as "wanting" to defend its queen makes it literally want anything. I think they're confusing the usefulness of a stance with the question of whether it's true, but I can't pin down exactly were the cheat supposedly happens. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what's the strongest patch that still leaves room for the intuitive conclusion?
