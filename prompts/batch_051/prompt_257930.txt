Here's one I keep going back and forth on and want someone to actually work through rather than gesture at. Mill's classic anti-paternalism line, roughly, is that the only justification for stopping a competant adult from doing what they want is preventing harm to other people, never harm to themselves. That argument was built for a world where the interfering party is the state or some other person and the interference is ultimately backed by force or the threat of it. But now there's a case that doesn't fit: an AI assistant. The assistant is something I chose to switch on, it has no power to physically stop me, and its refusals are more like a very opinionated tool than a law. Still, designers routinely build them to refuse or talk back against requests that are only bad for the user: not just suicide methods, but drafting the email you'll regret in the morning, a self-sabotaging plan, advice on quitting your job with no savings. So the question: does Mill's anti-paternalism argument survive this development? I can see three options: it still applies and these refusals are just paternalism wearing a different costume; it stops applying because its coercion premise isn't met; or it survives in some weaker repaired form. I'd like you to work out which premise the development actually pressures, whether the argument can be rebuilt in a form that still says something useful, and what the rebuilt version commits a designer of assistants to. And tell me if you think there's a fourth option I'm not seeing.
