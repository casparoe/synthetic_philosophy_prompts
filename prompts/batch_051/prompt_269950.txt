I teach an upper-level political philosophy seminar with a unit on population ethics and the far future: 16 students, one 110-minute session. Design an in-class activity that makes participants feel how a sincere, impartial commitment to the welfare of the astronomically many future people can, step by step, come to recommend restrictions they would otherwise call authoritarian — and where exactly the slide can be resisted. Do not lecture the thesis up front and do not name the position until the debrief; the lesson should surface from the choices, incentives, and rules themselves.

The deliverable is a runnable plan: roles or teams, rules, a scoring scheme that needs no real money, timings, and a debrief. I would like a two-stage structure. Stage one should be a pre-political warm-up in which players confront a series of small-probability, enormous-stakes wagers, calibrated so that any expected-value maximizer buys every safeguard, and the class feels how unbounded the purchase of safety can become. Stage two should move to a governance setting — a body designing binding safeguards after a narrowly averted catastrophe. The group passes or rejects intervention cards: surveillance and licensing for dual-use research, pre-publication review, mandatory longtermist education, replacement of elected seats with representatives of future generations chosen by a self-perpetuating trust, penalties for dissent that harms coordination, and so on. Cards buy survival points for future lives at a cost in a present-liberty index, and the scoring rule should initially omit present liberty unless players choose to price it, so that the omission itself becomes part of the discovery. Each player should have to justify votes in writing.

The debrief should map particular moments onto the machinery behind the slide: additive aggregation over huge future numbers swamping present complaints, dread risk and worst-case reasoning, the gap between an axiology and a justification for political coercion, lock-in and value drift and the self-reinforcing character of control, and the cost of resisting at each point — non-additive or person-affecting views, deontic constraints, epistemic humility about what actually reduces risk, and legitimacy constraints on who may choose. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run — for example, students performing maximal authoritarianism as a joke, or refusing all cards out of deontology and skipping the experience — and how to design against each failure. Be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce: the probabilities and the efficacy of the controls are stipulated, and a board game cannot convey what actual coercion and permanent lock-in would be like.
