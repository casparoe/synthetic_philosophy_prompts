I'm co-organizing a five-week summer school on logical uncertainty and logical induction for people who already know the basics — they've been through the main results and the usual commentary. We want to spend each week on one open conceptual question, and I keep arguing with the other organizers about how to pick them. The point isn't to prove new theorems or build algorithms; it's to work out which conceptual knots in this stuff are actually load-bearing if the goal is a normatively satisfying account of how a bounded reasoner should assign credences to deterministic-but-uncomputed consequences, like claims about what a program will do before you run it. Some of the candidate questions floating around: whether being unexploitable by polynomial-time traders is really a rationality constraint or just a tractability artifact, whether logical update should look like Bayesian conditioning at all, how to assign credences to self-referential sentences without exploding, and whether the actual object of interest is the limiting belief state or the finite-time trajectory. I want you to pick exactly five questions, in the order you'd schedule the five weeks, and for each give a sentence or two defending the choice — whether it's upstream of the other questions, whether five days of mostly conceptual work can genuinely move it, and what kind of answer would make everyone reconsider the rest of the framework. No intro or conclusion, please, just the five numbered items.
