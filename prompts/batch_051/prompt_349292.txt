In my graduate seminar on fundamentality this term, one assumption keeps presenting itself as though it were constitutive of metaphysical grounding: grounding is not only explanatory and asymmetric but well-founded. Every chain of grounds must terminate in a fundamental fact; infinite descent and grounding circles are ruled out before the discussion even begins. The idea felt immediately familiar to me, because I came to philosophy from the semantics of programming languages and proof theory, where the same structural ban appears. A total function recurses on strictly smaller arguments until it reaches a base case; a proof is standardly a finite, well-founded tree. In all three settings, a chain of because that never bottoms out is treated as vicious rather than merely long.

But the computational side of this parallel has a rigorous, mature counterpart that I rarely see imported into metaphysics: coinduction. Coinduction and coalgebra give precise semantics to infinite structures—streams, lazy data, ongoing processes—where there is no base case but only productive unfolding. A circular stream definition is perfectly well-defined because it is interpreted as a greatest fixed point rather than a least fixed point, and a coinductive proof establishes a property of such an object by showing the property persists under every observation. So my question is whether the metaphysician's well-foundedness requirement is the same kind of structural condition as the logician's demand for finite proof, in which case coinduction should make non-well-founded grounding thinkable in a disciplined way; or whether it is instead an explanatory demand—the demand that an answer to an in-virtue-of-what question eventually terminate in something ungrounded—in which case the formal permissiveness of coinduction is beside the point. I can see an argument for each answer, and I want to know whether this is a deep transfer of results or an analogy that collapses exactly where it needs to hold.
