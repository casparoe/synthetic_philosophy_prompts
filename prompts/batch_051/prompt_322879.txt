I am currently enrolled in a university course on the ethics of public office, and last week's seminar ended with an argument between two classmates that I have not been able to resolve. I have reconstructed the exchange below as faithfully as I can, and I would be grateful for a careful, move-by-move adjudication.

The case our professor used to introduce the problem of dirty hands was this. A mayor receives credible intelligence that a bombing is being prepared in a crowded city square and will likely kill dozens of people within hours. The only feasible way to stop it is to strike the apartment where the bomb is being assembled, but the strike will also kill a young child in the adjacent apartment. The mayor authorizes the strike. The bombing is prevented, and the child is killed. Afterwards, the mayor states that she does not regret the decision, would make it again, and is not going to pretend to lose sleep over it. The seminar broke into a dispute over whether that lack of regret should worry us.

Daniel said it should. He argued that the mayor's refusal to feel regret is itself a moral defect. The rightness of the outcome does not erase the wrong done to the child, and a responsible agent registers that wrong in the form of remorse. He claimed that the whole point of the dirty-hands tradition is that we need politicians who are capable of doing wrong things for good reasons and who suffer because of it. A mayor who orders such a strike and remains serene has treated the child as a mere entry in a cost-benefit ledger, and that disposition, he said, is the beginning of political corruption.

Mira replied that this is sentimental bookkeeping. She said that once the mayor has correctly judged the strike to be her all-things-considered duty, there is no leftover wrong for her to regret. Demanding that she feel guilt, Mira argued, is demanding that she feel bad about having done the right thing, which would be a false conscience rather than a virtue. On her view, what matters is whether the mayor gets hard decisions right in the future; whether she loses sleep is irrelevant, and treating sleeplessness as a sign of moral seriousness just selects for people who are good at performing anguish.

Daniel pushed back: "Right all things considered" does not mean "wrongless." The child's claim, he said, remains as a moral remainder; the act is both justified and wrongful in one respect, and remorse is how the agent acknowledges that the remainder is still there. Without that feeling, she has failed to recognize that a wrong was done to a particular person, and no public gesture can make up for that failure.

Mira answered that this overstates what a "remainder" requires. Once the wrong is admitted, she said, the remaining claim is answered by reparative things: compensation to the family, a public acknowledgment, a review of the decision, perhaps a memorial or a fund. None of that requires the mayor's inner state. She added that making regret the test of moral seriousness is gameable, since it rewards the mayor who cries on television and punishes the one who quietly does her job, even if the latter reasons better under pressure.

Daniel responded that reparative gestures without the agent's regret are hollow, and that the affective disposition has predictive value: a mayor who can order a child's death without finding it terrible has shown that she will find the next such order easier, while a mayor who registers the cost will be more reluctant to resort to such means. The performance worry, he said, cuts both ways, since a mayor who remains serene may equally be performing toughness.

I have not been able to decide which of them got the better of this, and both have asked me to referee. Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: identify what each move establishes, where each move goes wrong or overreaches, how far the ideas of moral remainders and reparative obligations can be pressed, and finish with a verdict on who is more right overall and whether the mayor's lack of regret should disturb us.
