One of the journals I help edit is drowning in submissions, and a colleage proposed this. For every manuscript you submit, you must also name one of your own already-published papers as collateral. While the new manuscript is under review, the collateral paper is delisted from the journal's site. If the new manuscript is accepted, the collateral paper is immediately restored. If the new manuscript is rejected, the collateral paper stays down for another twelve months and then returns with a note saying it was temporarily withdrawn as collateral. His pitch is that this is a handicap-principle screen: an author who believes their new paper is good will be happy to bet an old one, while an author who knows it is weak will not risk a paper that is still being read and cited. Walk me through how this actually plays out. Who submits, who stops, and does the delisting select for quality, for confidence, or for something else? Where is the first place it gets gamed? What does it do to junior authors without a back-catalogue, to coauthors of the collateral paper, and to reviewers who know a rejection costs an author a paper? Can the failure modes be fixed—for example, by scaling the required collateral by seniority, or letting people post a cash bond instead of a paper—without gutting the scheme? And is the handicap analogy even sound here, given that the real cost lands mostly on the people who fail?
