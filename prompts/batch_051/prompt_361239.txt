Act as a professional philosopher who works on group judgment and social choice, and write for a smart non-specialist. Before you settle on a procedure, take a step back and brainstorm the failure modes a committee like mine can hit — I want to see what you're designing against.

I chair a five-member promotion and tenure committee at a midsize regional college. We meet twice a month during the academic year, and each case gets about ninety minutes. Our decisions are essentially final; the dean signs off but has never reversed one, so people's careers are genuinely on the line. The faculty handbook says a candidate is promoted only if they are, in the committee's collective judgment, excellent in research, excellent in teaching, and satisfactory in service. In practice, each of us forms a yes/no on each of the three criteria, and then we hold a single up-or-down vote on promotion.

Last month we hit a mess. The individual votes looked like this:

- A: research yes, teaching yes, service yes → promote
- B: research yes, teaching yes, service yes → promote
- C: research no, teaching yes, service yes → don't promote
- D: research yes, teaching no, service yes → don't promote
- E: research yes, teaching yes, service no → don't promote

So research passed 4–1, teaching passed 4–1, service passed 4–1, but promotion failed 2–3. The committee spent the rest of the meeting arguing about what "the committee" had even decided. A colleague told me this kind of thing has a name — the discursive dilemma, or something like it — but a label doesn't tell me what to do next time.

I want a one-page standing procedure we can adopt and run for every candidate. It should specify:
- what to vote on first, and in what order,
- what to do when the votes on the criteria and the overall vote come apart like they did above,
- how to handle members who reject the idea that each criterion is a hard nessecary condition (say, weak research could be offset by extraordinary teaching),
- how to handle abstentions, ties, and the case where everyone agrees on the outcome but for incompatible reasons,
- where the procedure should stop and say this is a values decision the committee has to make, not something a rule can settle for it.

Be honest about the places where the procedure just relocates the judgment instead of eliminating it, and tell me what the procedure cannot do. Then run the procedure on three cases: (i) a candidate everyone agrees is excellent in teaching and service but split 3–2 on research; (ii) exactly last month's mismatch, with the votes above; and (iii) a candidate the committee personally dislikes and hopes to reject, but who meets all three criteria on every member's own yes/no assessment, and no one can point to a criterion they would actually deny. I expect your procedure to force an answer in (iii) that the committee will hate, and I want you to say so plainly.

Assume the members are intelligent academics but not philosophers, that nobody will read background theory, and that we have to get through the whole procedure inside the ninety minutes.
