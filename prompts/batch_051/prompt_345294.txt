Late last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether anti-corruption standards can be applied across cultures or whether they are Western impositions. She defended the following argument, and I still cannot decide whether it is sound. Here it is:

(1) An act counts as corrupt only if an official abuses her office by acting on a role-excluded reason of private advantage rather than on the public good.

(2) A customary gift to an official provides a role-excluded reason of private advantage only if it is tied to a particular official act, because only an exchange for an official act gives the official an incentive to decide one way rather than another.

(3) Many customary gifts — holiday gifts, tokens of respect, gifts after a service has been performed — are not tied to any particular official act.

(4) Therefore, such gifts provide the official with no role-excluded reason of private advantage.

(5) Therefore, such gifts are not corrupt.

(6) A standard that condemns conduct that is not corrupt simply in virtue of the fact that the conduct is gift-giving is overbroad.

(7) Therefore, international anti-corruption standards that treat all significant customary gifts to officials as corrupt per se are overbroad; they are not a neutral universal standard but a local Westernized norm.

I would like to know whether this argument is valid. If it is valid, identify the premise doing the most contested work and say precisely what would need to be true for it to hold. If it is invalid, identify the exact step at which the argument fails, explain what is being conflated or overlooked, and state how that step should be revised for the conclusion to go through.
