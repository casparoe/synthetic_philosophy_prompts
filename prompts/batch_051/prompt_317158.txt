I'm a backend engineer who spends most of my time writing static analyzers and type checkers, and I keep tripping over something philosophers call the problem of logical omniscience. My rough understanding, probably mangled: in standard possible-worlds semantics for knowledge, an agent knows a proposition just in case it's true in every world she considers possible. But then she automatically knows every logical truth, and whenever she knows P and knows that P implies Q, she knows Q. Actual people don't work like that, and neither does any program I've every written. I gather philosophers have tried to patch this with impossible worlds (worlds where the logical laws are weaker or inconsistent), awareness (you can't know what you're not aware of), a split between explicit and implicit knowledge, and algorithmic knowledge, but I only half-follow the differences between these fixes.

Here's my half-formed hunch, and I want you to take it seriously rather than wave it away as a metaphor: the whole dialectic is really about over-approximation, or maybe it flips to under-approximation somewhere and I want to know where. A possible-worlds model of knowledge feels like a sound but brutally imprecise static analysis that claims some invariant holds across every reachable state, except the state space is every possible world. The fixes I listed sound to me like the standard bag of tricks: tracking what's actually been computed, enriching the abstract domain, cutting off the fixed-point iteration, distinguishing what the algorithm could derive from what it has derived, modeling a resource-bounded reasoner. As someone fluent in both epistemic logic and formal methods, please restate the logical omniscience problem and the main attempts to fix it entirely in the vocabulary of static analysis and type systems (abstract and concrete states, transfer functions, soundness and completeness, over- and under-approximation, fixed points and widening, decidability), as precisely as you can, not as an analogy but as a translation. Then give me the residue report: what maps cleanly, what you had to fudge to force into the framework, and what in the philosophical debate has no counterpart in static analysis at all.

Finally, tell me whether the residue is mostly a sign that my over-approximation hunch is missing something important, or mostly a sign that the philosophers' idealization was shakier than it looked. And I want the self-critical part stated in advance: which mapping, argument, or counterexample would make you flip that verdict, and where in your own translation is the distortion most likely being introduced by the static-analysis side rather than by the philosophy? Start by brainstorming the candidate mappings before committing to one.
