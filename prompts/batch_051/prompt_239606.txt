I read philosophy of science as an amateur, and I keep noticing a pattern in the history of successful theories that I have never seen given a settled name or treatment, though I suspect it must have one. The pattern is this. When a mature and very successful theory gets replaced, the successor rarely discards the earlier theory's equations. It keeps them, usually as a limiting case or a special case, and scientists go on using the older formalism for calculations because it remains accurate in its own domain. But the interpretation of the central terms changes at the same time: the things the older theory treated as its basic furniture are demoted, reinterpreted, or quietly dropped, while the mathematics carries over nearly unchanged. The inheritance across the theory change is therefore unequal — the formal core is transmitted, and the ontology is not. I do not mean merely that a newer theory recovers an older one as a good approximation; I mean this specific combination of retained mathematics and abandoned entities.

Two examples make me think this is a general shape rather than a historical accident. The first is nineteenth-century light. Light was treated as a wave in a material medium, and a set of equations describing that wave was impressively accurate. When a later theory took over, the equations survived and remained standard for practical work, but the medium went from being a real substance to a useful fiction to nothing at all. The second is motion. The Newtonian equations for moving bodies survive inside the successor theory as an approximation, but what the terms "mass" and "force" point to gets re-described so thoroughly that it becomes hard to say whether the old theory's referents were discovered or invented. I have the same vague impression about heat, but the two cases above are the ones I trust.

I am fairly sure this connects to the pessimistic meta-induction — the argument that the history of abandoned successes gives us reason to expect today's successful theories to be abandoned too — but the pattern I am describing is not exactly that argument. It is the underlying two-track fact the argument trades on. My questions are therefore these. Does this specific two-track pattern have a name of its own, or is it just an instance of some larger idea I should know? I want the carving, not a label: whatever the nearest concept is, I want to know precisely how the pattern fits it, where the fit is loose, and where its edges are. And what is the pattern usually taken to show? On one side, the survival of the equations looks like evidence that we are tracking something real; on the other, the repeated failure of the ontology looks like evidence that today's entities will follow the light-bearing medium. How should this pattern affect the pessimistic meta-induction, and how does it bear on the rival thought that the success of science would be a miracle if our theories were not somehow on the right track?
