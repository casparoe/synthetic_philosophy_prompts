In "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (1878), C. S. Peirce writes, "Thought in action has for its only possible motive the attainment of thought at rest; and whatever does not refer to belief is no part of the thought itself." I have been trying to apply this to a case he did not anticipate. A present-day machine-learning system takes in a query, produces a long chain of intermediate computations, checks some of them against constraints, revises several steps, and finally emits an answer; yet we have independent reason to think that no state of doubt, belief, or "thought at rest" occurs anywhere in the process, even though the internal operations look like reasoning to an outside observer. Does Peirce's claim exclude this process from being thought, or does it only exclude it from being inquiry in the sense he was analyzing? What would each interpretation—the plain wording, the purpose of the passage, and the likely answer of Peirce if he had been asked about machines—imply, and which reading is most defensible?
