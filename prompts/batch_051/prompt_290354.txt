I chair the committee at my university that reviews long-delayed complaints against faculty and advises the provost on whether to waive the ordinary time limit. Our conduct code prohibits disciplinary proceedings begun more than twenty years after the alleged misconduct, except that the provost may waive the limit for extraordinary circumstances upon our written recommendation. We are now considering a complaint from a former doctoral student who alleges that a now-emeritus professor, while supervising her in the early 1990s, incorporated substantial uncredited portions of her dissertation into two journal articles and later into a co-authored handbook chapter, and then resisted her recent attempts to correct the record when the handbook was reissued. She says she delayed coming forward because she feared retaliation early in her career and believed the original plagiarism had never reached publication; she filed the complaint only after finding correspondence among the professor's papers, donated to the university archive after his retirement, that she believes corroborates the claim. The professor, now in his late seventies and still teaching one seminar a year, denies the allegations and argues that the twenty-year limit exists precisely so that old disputes are not relitigated after witnesses have scattered and memories have hardened. The provost has asked for our written recommendation by next week.

Please draft the recommendation I will submit. It should advise the provost either to waive the time limit or to decline to do so, with any conditions attached—for instance, limits on the scope of the inquiry, a decision deadline, or interim measures—and it should give reasons the provost can act on. Engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side. We are not being asked to determine the merits of the plagiarism claim at this stage; the question is what the fact that the alleged wrong is roughly thirty years old should matter for the decision to open a proceeding now—in particular, whether the passage of time itself carries moral weight, or matters only through its effects on evidence, memory, deterrence, and the parties' present interests.
