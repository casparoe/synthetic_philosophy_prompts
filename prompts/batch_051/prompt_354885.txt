I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I've got a habit I can't decide weather to keep or kick. I've come around to thinking epiphenomenalism is a lot more plausible than I used to: that qualia—the felt character of experience—play no causal role in what I say, judge, or believe, that they're more like steam off the engine than a gear inside it. But in practice, and honestly in my own first-order reasoning, I still treat my introspective seemings as the last word on what my experience is like. If it seems to me that I'm in pain, or that the afterimage looks greenish, or that there's something it's like to see red as opposed to hearing a bell, I take that seeming as strong evidence that those feels exist and have roughly that character. And when a third-person story—neural data, behavior, a scientist's theory—conflicts with the first-person report, I trust the report. Last week a vision scientist told me my description of an afterimage as greenish was probably confabulation from context. I didn't even consider changing my mind; I know what the afterimage looks like to me. But maybe I shouldn't be so sure.

A friend in the department keeps telling me this is exactly the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If epiphenomenalism is true, my judgment that I'm in pain is caused by the same neural machinery that does all the judging, not by the pain itself, so the fact that I find myself with that judgment gives me no special reason to think the pain exists and is as advertised. She says I'm helping myself to evidence that my own view says I couldn't have gotten. So: is my habit defensible? Can I coherently accept epiphenomenalism—or even just hold it as a serious live possibility—and still treat my own phenomenology as evidence without smuggling in the claim that the feels cause the judgments? If not, can the habit be restated so it keeps what I trust about introspection without begging the question? Where does my friend's argument go wrong, if it does, and where is she right?
