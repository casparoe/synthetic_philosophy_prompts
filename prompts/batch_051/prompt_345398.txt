I'm a retired natural scientist (physical chemistry, as it happens) who has recently gotten into political philosophy, and I now help convene a reading group for other retired scientists. We have been working through the debate about whether equality is valuable in itself or whether what matters is benefiting the worse off, and I have noticed that the group keeps talking past itself because "egalitarian" covers several different commitments. I would like a diagnostic decision tree that sorts a reader into a distributive view based on their answers to concrete cases, not on which label they prefer.

Please construct the tree as follows. Use the leveling-down case at or near the root: compare a two-person distribution of (10, 20) with a distribution of (10, 10), where the second is reached simply by lowering the better-off person and no one gains. The root question should ask whether the second distribution is in any respect better, and it should be worded without using "leveling down," "equality," or any of the leaf labels.

The leaves must include telic egalitarianism, deontic egalitarianism, prioritarianism (treat the priority weight as a continuous, strictly decreasing function of the recipient's absolute welfare), leximin, sufficientarianism, and utilitarianism. Build the tree from the underlying questions first, then place these named positions in the resulting space. For combinations of answers that nobody defends, mark the cell unoccupied and say briefly whether it looks incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate—for instance, where prioritarianism and sufficientarianism can be made to agree on every case in a restricted set—and give the additional question that would pull them apart.

Each internal node should be either a question about a concrete case or a forced choice between two specified distributions. Use numerical welfare levels and state that they are lifetime levels, with population held fixed unless a node explicitly concerns population size. Make sure the nodes separate the following questions:

(a) whether an unequal distribution can be better, in any respect, when the worse-off are in exactly the same position;
(b) whether inequality matters non-instrumentally, or only as evidence about how it was produced or as a feature of relationships between persons;
(c) whether a benefit matters more when it goes to someone worse off in absolute terms—and if so, whether the priority is continuous or absolute, where absolute means that no amount of benefit to the better-off outweighs even a tiny benefit to the worse-off;
(d) whether there is a threshold below which additional welfare has a categorically different urgency, and whether above any such threshold further inequality ceases to matter.

Aim for no more than eight internal nodes and eight leaves. Phrase every node so that a non-philosopher can answer it without knowing which leaf it leads to, and keep the whole instrument usable in a two-hour meeting.
