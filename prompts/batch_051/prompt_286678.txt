Last night I had a long argument with a friend about the Sleeping Beauty problem, and after we had gone back and forth for a while, he offered what he described as the definitive statement of the thirder position. I would like to know how much of his summary I can trust.

His summary ran as follows. The correct answer is one third, and the only reason the problem is controversial is that people conflate two different questions. On Sunday, Beauty is obviously justified in being fifty-fifty about the coin, since it is fair. But once she wakes, there are three possible awakenings she could currently be in: heads-Monday, tails-Monday, and tails-Tuesday. Because nothing in her experience distinguishes one of these awakenings from the others, rational consistency requires her to assign them equal probability. Only one of the three is a heads-awakening, so her credence in heads should be one third. Halfers, he said, are simply confusing the probability of the coin toss with the probability of finding herself in a particular interview. He added that bets settle the matter: if Beauty were offered even-money bets on heads at each awakening and kept her credence at one half, she would be vulnerable to a Dutch book, which decides the question.

I find the one-third conclusion plausible, but I suspect that my friend's summary may be less careful than the actual position, and I do not know enough about self-locating belief to identify the precise points of distortion. Which parts of his summary would someone who works on this problem accept, which parts would they regard as overstatements or contested moves, and does his conclusion follow from the parts that are uncontroversial? In particular, I am unsure whether the step from "there are three possible awakenings" to "they should receive equal probability" is acceptable, and whether the betting argument is really as decisive as he suggests.
