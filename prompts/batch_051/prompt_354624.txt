Two people in my reading group got stuck on Aumann's agreement theorem the other week, and Im still not sure who was right. Heres the argument, as close to verbatim as I can reconstruct it.

Maya, who had just read the Aumann paper, said: "The theorem says rational Bayesians can't agree to disagree. If two people share a prior, update by conditioning, and their posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge, then those probabilities have to be identical. That's a result, not a vague claim."

Jon, who does econometrics, said: "Sure, but that's not the part people actually want. What they want is the conclusion that if two reasonable people honestly tell each other their probabilities and talk it over, they'll end up agreeing — and if they don't, somebody's being irrational. And that doesn't follow from the theorem."

Maya: "It sort of does. If they sit there and exchange posteriors publicly, after a few rounds each knows the other's number, and knows the other knows their number, and so on. That's exactly what common knowledge means. Then the theorem kicks in and they have to agree."

Jon: "No — common knowledge isn't just 'everyone knows and everyone knows everyone knows' up to some finite level. It's the whole infinite hierarchy. Real conversations don't generate that. Telling someone your number over coffee doesn't get you to common knowledge, unless you add assumptions like everyone heard correctly, everyone knows everyone heard correctly, and so on forever."

Maya: "Fine, but the theorem is still about an idealized setup. In that setup, two agents with a shared prior and private information can't maintain different posteriors once those posteriors are common knowledge. So when two smart, honest forecasters go on disagreeing for years, the theorem is telling you at least one of those conditions failed — not just 'people are complicated.'"

Jon: "Right, and the condition that usually fails is the common prior. Two perfectly rational Bayesians with different priors can look at the same public information and walk away with different posteriors, and neither has done anything irrational. The theorem doesn't say disagreement always means someone's being unreasonable."

Maya: "But 'the priors differ' just pushes the disagreement back a step. If two people with different priors both look at the same evidence, they can't both be tracking the world equally well, so it's not like the disagreement became rational."

Jon: "Bayesian rationality doesn't require your prior to be true, or even uniquely reasonable. Priors are inputs to the updating rule, not outputs. You can't call someone irrational just for starting somewhere different — even if you think their prior is bad."

Could you go through this move by move and say who's right about which specific point, where each person overstates or misstates the actual conditions of the theorem, and what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'd rather know which individual moves hold up than who "wins" overall.
