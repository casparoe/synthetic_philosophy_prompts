In a course on moral philosophy, I encountered the following working criteria for a supererogatory act: the act must be morally good; it must be optional, so that the agent would not be blameworthy for declining; it must go beyond what the agent's ordinary legal, professional, and relational duties require; and it must involve a genuine cost or risk to the agent undertaken for another's benefit. Consider this case. A public-school teacher in a severely underfunded district realizes that the provided textbooks are largely unusable and that many of her students, who cannot count on much help at home, will predictably fall behind. Her contract defines a seven-hour day and does not require her to supply classroom materials or run after-school programs. She responds by spending a substantial part of her own salary on books and supplies and by running an unmandated study group three evenings a week, which costs her money, sleep, and time. She regards this as something her students are owed rather than as a heroic extra. At the same time, her union has explicitly urged teachers not to spend personal money on classrooms, arguing that such individual sacrifices absorb the funding cuts and allow the district to evade its institutional responsibility.

Is this teacher's conduct supererogatory? Work through the criteria one at a time, and where the verdict is unclear or the criteria pull in different directions, say whether the difficulty lies in the case, in the criteria themselves, or in the fact that this case sits at the intersection of individual duty and institutional injustice.
