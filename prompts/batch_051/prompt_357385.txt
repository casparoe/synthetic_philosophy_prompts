I'm a high school student trying to read Aristotle's De Anima on my own, and I'm stuck on a section that seems to pull in two directions at once. I don't have the translation in front of me, so I'll paraphrase the relevant lines rather than quote them exactly.

In the chapter where Aristotle discusses phantasia, he argues roughly that phantasia is not perception, because it can occur in dreams when the sense organs are not being stimulated in the ordinary way, and it is also not belief or knowledge, because belief involves conviction and is not something we can simply switch on at will, whereas we can deliberately bring images before our minds. He also describes phantasia as a kind of movement produced by actual perception.

Then, a few chapters later, in the discussion of thinking, he says something like this: every act of thinking is accompanied by, or makes use of, an image; even when the intellect is thinking of things that are not perceptible, it must think them by means of an image. He compares images to diagrams or pictures and says that the thinking part of the soul seems to think the forms in these images.

My problem is that I can't see how these two claims fit together. If thought always requires phantasmata, and phantasmata are just offshoots of sense-perception, then how is thinking anything more than a refined kind of imagining? And if the intellect is supposed to be able to grasp forms apart from matter, how can it still depend on images? Is Aristotle saying that images are only a necessary accompaniment of human thinking because we happen to be embodied, or is he saying that the intellect literally has nothing to think about except the content of images? For example, when I think about the incommensurability of the diagonal with the side of a square, I might have a picture of some particular square in my mind. Is the picture doing the thinking, or is the intellect reading something through the picture?

I would like your best pass at explaining what the text is saying here. Please separate what is settled and uncontroversial about these passages from what is genuinely contested among people who study Aristotle, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
