Write the joint statement that a Competitionist and a Deliberationist about argumentation produce after a six-month adversarial collaboration on the question: could argumentation be made into a sport, and would doing so improve reasoning? The Competitionist begins from the view that organized, scored competition would improve reasoning by imposing external standards, rewarding the discovery of decisive objections, and giving participants a stake in being right rather than merely agreeable. The Deliberationist begins from the view that competition would select for rhetoric, confidence, face-saving, and audience manipulation, and that reasoning improves through joint inquiry in which no one owns a position.

The document should have this structure:

1. Numbered claims both can sign. Cover at least: what motivated reasoning is and when it operates; what adversarial checking does well (for example, inviting someone to attack a view tends to surface objections that its holder would not have volunteered); the difference between being persuasive and being accurate, and between being right and winning; and the fact that institutions differ in whether their rewards track truth or victory. Phrase each claim so that neither side feels it has conceded more than the text of the claim actually states.

2. A disagreements table, with three columns: the neutral question; the Competitionist's formulation of their view in wording they accept; the Deliberationist's formulation of their view in wording they accept. Include at least these rows: (a) whether competitive motive can be redirected from victory toward truth by scoring rules (for example, points for conceding to a decisive objection, or delayed scoring against what turns out to be true rather than against the live audience's reaction); (b) whether performing in front of an audience under competition makes people update sooner and more publicly, or makes them entrench and perform conviction; (c) whether argument-as-sport would institutionalize motivated cognition or expose it to correction. For each row, flag any part of the disagreement that is merely verbal or a difference in weighting an agreed-upon factor.

3. A crux list. Each crux should be a sub-question such that settling it would actually move at least one side. For each crux, specify one concrete format-rule, case, or cheap empirical study that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts. The cruxes should reach into the mechanics of scoring and updating: whether a format scores individual argumentative moves or the accuracy of the conclusions participants converge on; whether a participant can earn points for conceding; whether the reward is delivered by the live audience, by delayed fact-checking, or by a prediction market on the claims made; and whether participants choose their own side or are assigned sides by lot.

4. A closing paragraph stating which parts of the dispute the two now regard as verbal or as differences of emphasis, and which parts remain substantive and would survive further conversation.
