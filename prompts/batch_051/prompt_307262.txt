Here's the rough criterion I was taught for a Lakatosian research programme. A programme has a hard core—the central commitments that get a kind of methodological immunity; you decide not to aim refutation at them—plus a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that soak up anomalies and get revised when something goes wrong, and a positive heuristic, a rough plan for how to develop the belt. A programme is progressive if each revision churns out novel predictions that then get corroborated; it's degenerating if revisions mostly get bolted on after the fact to explain the last failure while predicting nothing new.

Now consider a field that runs on large benchmark leaderboards, like modern language modeling or protein structure prediction. Most progress consists in scaling data and compute, changing architectures, and cleaning datasets; held-out performance keeps improving, and the models really do make novel predictions—a folded structure, a plausible continuation—that get checked and come out right. But there isn't much of a hard core beyond slogans like "scale helps" or "the benchmark is the target." I don't see a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses being revised so much as thousands of model instances replacing each other on a leaderboard. The predictions are real, but they don't come from a theory; they come from training. Maybe the scaling laws count as a positive heuristic, but it looks a lot thinner than the kind of positive heuristic Lakatos had in mind.

Is this a progressive Lakatosian research programme, or is it progressive-looking while missing the machinery the definition requires?
