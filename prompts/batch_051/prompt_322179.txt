Pearl's causal hierarchy separates causal information into three rungs—seeing (probabilistic association), doing (the effects of interventions), and imagining (counterfactuals). The do-calculus framework rests on structural causal models, which assume that a system decomposes into modular, autonomous mechanisms, each fixing how one variable depends on its direct causes; an intervention is represented as locally replacing one such mechanism while the rest remain intact. From this picture comes a well-known stricture: queries at the intervention and counterfactual rungs are not identifiable from purely observational, rung-one data. Given a joint distribution over observed variables, many different causal structures fit the data equally well and yield different answers about what would happen under an intervention; some causal assumption or structural model is needed before the do-calculus, or any other method, can answer those queries.

A recent development seems to put pressure on this stricture. Large language models are trained on text, which, from the standpoint of the hierarchy, looks like observational data: sequences of tokens produced by the world rather than data from randomized experiments. Yet these models routinely handle "what would have happened if...?" and "what caused what?" questions, and their answers are often judged correct by human standards. Some take this as evidence that the hierarchy's barrier is not as firm as claimed—that sufficiently rich learning from observational data can, in practice, climb to the higher rungs.
