In an archaeology seminar my professor kept saying "absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence" whenever anyone drew an inference from a gap in the material record. The case that got to me: a paper argued that because a settlement has plenty of adult burials under the floors but no infant burials anywhere, the community probably didn't treat infants as full persons yet. The professor waved it off with that slogan and moved on. I'm not sure that's fair—surely the absence counts for more or less depending on whether infant bones would have survived, whether anyone actually looked for them, and whether neighboring communities in the same period did bury infants. Can you spell out the distinct principles that slogan might be stating, from trivial to radical, and say which of them would actually justify dismissing the paper, and under what conditions an absence really is evidence of absence?
