I have been working through the Frege–Geach problem in a metaethics seminar, and a classmate offered a reconstruction of it that seems to me to prove too much: it appears to show not merely that expressivism owes us an explanation, but that expressivism cannot even allow ordinary moral reasoning to be valid. I would like to know where the argument goes wrong, if it goes wrong.

The argument runs as follows. (1) According to expressivism, the meaning of a moral sentence just is the attitude it conventionally expresses; for example, "Lying is wrong" expresses disapproval of lying. (2) A moral sentence makes the same semantic contribution whether it is asserted on its own or occurs embedded inside a larger sentence. (3) But the attitude associated with the atomic sentence is not expressed in embedded contexts: someone who utters "If lying is wrong, then persuading someone to lie is wrong" need not disapprove of lying, or of anything beyond the conditional demand itself. (4) If the attitude is all there is to the sentence's meaning, then wherever that attitude is absent, the sentence contributes no meaning. (5) So expressivists cannot assign the sentence a stable compositional meaning across conditionals, negations, disjunctions, and questions. (6) Yet the validity of ordinary moral reasoning requires the moral sentences occurring in it to have just such a stable semantic contribution: the inference from "If lying is wrong, then persuading someone to lie is wrong" and "Lying is wrong" to "Persuading someone to lie is wrong" is valid purely in virtue of its form. (7) Therefore, if expressivism is true, this elementary instance of modus ponens is either invalid or has no explanation. (8) No acceptable account of moral thought can leave so basic an inference invalid or unexplained. (9) Therefore expressivism is false.

My sympathies are with the expressivist idea that moral language is practical and action-guiding rather than a description of an independent moral reality, and I do not want to accept that ordinary moral inference is broken. But I cannot see which premise to give up without abandoning the view. Tell me which step is the right one to reject, why that step looked so plausible, and what an expressivist would have to accept—and pay—in order to block the conclusion.
