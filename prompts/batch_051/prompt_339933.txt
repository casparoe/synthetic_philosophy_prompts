Here's one I'd like you to work through, and answer in American English. Take the standard Newcomb's problem: a near-perfect predictor, a transparent box with a guaranteed $1,000, and an opaque box that has $1,000,000 in it just in case the predictor predicted you'd take only the opaque box. Causalists two-box, because the boxes are already fixed; evidentialists one-box, because their choice is evidence about what's in the opaque box. Now change one thing: instead of the opaque box paying out a million dollars to *you*, make the opaque box's payoff go to a perfect, conscious simulation of you. If the predictor predicted you'd one-box, the simulation gets a pleasant year in a comfortable virtual room; if the predictor predicted you'd two-box, the simulation is tortured for a year. You yourself get nothing from the opaque box either way, and the transparent box still has a real $1,000 for you if you take both. Assume the torture is bad enough that, if it were happening to you, you'd gladly pay the $1,000 to stop it. The simulation's fate was already settled by the prediction before you choose, and you'll never meet it or learn what happened to it. Also assume, at least in one version of yourself, that you genuinely care about the simulation's suffering because it's a faithful conscious copy of you.

Now work out what causal decision theory and evidential decision theory each say about this variant. Specifically: does the causalist's two-boxing survive, and does it survive even when the agent adopts the surrogate goal of caring about the simulation? Does the evidentialist one-box, and would an evidentialist who *didn't* care about copies at all still one-box? If the only thing that makes one-boxing rational here is a partly selfless concern for a causally inert copy of you, what does that tell us about whether acausal threats and surrogate goals deserve to be taken seriosuly in decision theory? Don't just give the stock Newcomb answers—press on whether the standard split between the two theories is doing any work once the beneficiary is a copy rather than the actual agent.
