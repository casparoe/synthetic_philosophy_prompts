I am a novelist, and I am writing a book whose central character comes to accept a version of the hinge-of-history hypothesis: the view that the present era has an unusually large expected influence over the long-run future of civilization, not because our moment is more virtuous or more interesting than earlier ones, but because we are acquiring technologies that could lock civilization onto radically different trajectories or end it outright, while the institutions that would reliably steer those technologies do not yet exist. On this view, the difference between acting well and acting badly now is unusually large in expectation, and the coming decades are among the most consequential in history. I have found this position more plausible than I expected, and I want the novel to test it honestly rather than give the reader a strawman.

Assume I am, with some genuine commitment, defending this view. I want a ranked map of what would count against it—not only what its critics say, but what a sophisticated defender would themselves treat as a genuine vulnerability. What empirical observations, historical patterns, formal arguments, or developments would have to obtain for the hypothesis to be false, or to lose its practical force? Which of these would be decisive, and which merely awkward? What does the hypothesis predict that its denial does not, and what would the world look like if it were false? I would particularly like to know whether some version of the thought that we should expect our time to be typical rather than special is the strongest threat, or whether the real vulnerabilities lie elsewhere. I would also like an honest assessment of whether the view, in roughly the form I have described, has been qualified enough to be unfalsifiable—whether the hinge can always be relocated to whatever threat currently looks largest—and whether that is a fatal problem for a thesis about the shape of history or an acceptable feature of a framework for thinking about long-run influence. I do not need references; I need the geography of the position's exposure.
