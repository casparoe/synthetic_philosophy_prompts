I maintain an open-source project whose governance document contains a transparency clause adopted in 2008, before the project used any machine-learned model. The clause reads:

"7.3 Transparency of automated decisions. Where the Project's software makes a decision that affects a user, the Project shall, on request and within fourteen days, provide that user with a complete record of the data and processing steps that produced the decision, sufficient to enable a technically competent user to verify how the decision was reached."

Last year we introduced a neural-network classifier to moderate the project's community forum: it decides which posts are removed as off-topic or abusive. The model is open in the sense that the training corpus, the pre-processing code, the training run, and the final weights are all available in the public repository. A long-standing contributor had a post removed and made a request under clause 7.3. We sent her the checkpoint, the data, the inference script, and a note explaining that the decision is a deterministic function of her post and the weights, so a technically competent user can re-run the computation and confirm the output. She replied that this does not let her verify how the decision was reached: she cannot look at a set of learned weights and say why her particular post was removed, and she suspects that in practice nobody can. She argues that the clause promised legibility — a decision she could follow — not a 200-gigabyte archive that no human can reason about. We replied that the clause promised transparency, and everything that produced the decision has been disclosed.

Does clause 7.3 require the Project to do anything further in this case? Argue the question from the text of the clause, from the purpose of the clause, and from what its drafters in 2008 would most plausibly have said if asked about a learned model with no discrete rules, and tell me which approach should win and why.
