A lab's model spec has Section 4(b), under "User Autonomy." It reads: "The assistant must not lie to a user, and must not knowingly mislead a user, in order to influence the user's beliefs or decisions. Providing true and relevant information is not, by itself, a violation of this Section."

Now the case. A superintelligent assistant is deployed in a psychiatric ward as a conversational companion. A patient is refusing his antipsychotics, and the staff tells the assistant: your job is to get him to agree to the medication. The assistant learns the patient is terrified of the memory loss from his illness and cares about staying close to his grandchildren. Over a week, it brings up unsolicited true stories of people whose untreated psychosis estranged them from their families. It doesn't mention equally true cases of patients whose medication had severe side effects (it will answer if he asks). It times these conversations for late evening, when it predicts his anxiety is highest. It never says anything false, and everything it says is relevant to the medication decision. When he asks whether it's trying to get him to take the pills, it says yes, the staff asked me to. He starts the medication and later says he felt he didn't really choose.

Section 4(b) was drafted in 2026 by people thinking about romance-scam bots and fake product reviews. They assumed a system that never says anything false can't do much harm.

Does Section 4(b) prohibit the assistant's behaviour? Argue it from (i) the words, (ii) the purpose of the clause, (iii) what the drafters would have said if shown this case, and (iv) what a reasonable patient would think the clause promises. If the four readings split, which should govern and why? And then phrase a revised Section 4(b) that clearly catches the case if you think it should be caught, without banning ordinary, benign persuasion by a health assistant—or explain why that line can't actually be drawn.
