I am a software engineer who writes payroll systems, and I have hit a conceptual problem while implementing German church tax (Kirchensteuer) withholding. Under this arrangement, the state levies a surcharge on the income tax of employees registered with certain religious bodies, passes the revenue to the employee's own denomination as general budget funds, and can stop collecting it from someone only when that person formally declares withdrawal from the denomination. I recently came across this passage in Thomas Jefferson's Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom:

"that to compel a man to furnish contributions of money for the propagation of opinions which he disbelieves, is sinful and tyrannical; that even the forcing him to support this or that teacher of his own religious persuasion, is depriving him of the comfortable liberty of giving his contributions to the particular pastor, whose morals he would make his pattern, and whose powers he feels most persuasive to righteousness"

I read this as condemning two distinct arrangements: (a) forcing someone to fund the spread of opinions he rejects, and (b) forcing someone to support a minister even of his own persuasion, because it overrides his free, judgment-based choice about whom to fund. Treat those as Jefferson's criteria. Now apply them to the Kirchensteuer case as I have described it. Work criterion by criterion, and weigh the analogies and disanalogies: the possibility of opting out by leaving the denomination, the fact that the money goes to the payer's own denomination rather than to a rival faith, the state's role as an administrative collection channel rather than a doctrinal censor, and the lack of any individual say over the use of the funds. Then say whether the case qualifies as the kind of arrangement the passage condemns, or whether the passage's criteria need to be refined in order to handle state-collected church taxes of this modern form.
