Here is roughly where I've landed on the gender-equality paradox, and I'd like a consistency audit before I write it up. I hold:

(1) The gender differences we see in occupational and educational choices are, in substantial part, socially constructed: cross-cultural variation and rapid historical change show that culture does real work.

(2) If a pattern is socially constructed in that way, then, other things equal, reducing gender inequality should shrink it rather than amplify it.

(3) The empirical observation behind the paradox — that several of the most gender-egalitarian countries show larger, not smaller, sex differences in fields such as STEM and healthcare — is roughly accurate in the relevant datasets.

(4) The egalitarian-country finding does not show that these differences are innate, biologically fixed, or beyond social influence.

(5) Gender-egalitarian policy remains justified whatever the source of the differences turns out to be.

Which of these commitments are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision? Please answer as exactly five numbered points, ordered by severity, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. In each point, name the pair of commitments at issue, say whether the tension is real or only apparent, and, for real tensions, state what would have to give.
