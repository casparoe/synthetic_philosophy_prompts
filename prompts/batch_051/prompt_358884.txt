Since I retired from the chemistry department I've ended up on our state's capital clemancy board, and I've been reading the philosophy of punishment in the evenings. We have a case coming up and I want to sort out the reasoning before the meeting. The board has kept a passage from Cesare Beccaria's An Essay on Crimes and Punishments, chapter 28, "Of the Punishment of Death," pinned in the hearing room for years. It reads:

"It is not the intenseness of the pain that has the greatest effect on the mind, but its continuance; for our sensibility is more easily and more powerfully affected by weak but repeated impressions, than by a violent but momentary impulse. [...] The death of a criminal is a terrible but momentary spectacle, and therefore a less efficacious method of deterring others, than the continued example of a man deprived of his liberty, condemned, as a beast of burthen, to repair, by his labour, the injury he has done to society."

We only decide whether to commute a death sentence to life without parole, and our track record so far is:

1. The look-out. Nineteen years old, IQ of 68 with organic brain damage from childhood lead poisoning, served as the look-out in a robbery where someone else shot the clerk. We commuted: his culpability was so diminished that executing him couldn't serve any retributive end.

2. The sleeping lawyer. The defense lawyer slept through parts of the trial and the prosecution withheld the defendant's mental-health records. His guilt was never seriously in doubt, but we commuted anyway—we said we couldn't stand behind a death sentence produced by that unreliable process.

3. The hired hit. A man with a genuinely terrible childhood—abuse, foster care, the lot—shot a woman for money, planned it out. We denied. A bad childhood earns sympathy, but it doesn't make a planned contract killing less culpable.

4. The panic killing. Twenty years old, no record, no impairment, killed a homeowner while fleeing a burglary; he probably panicked. The trial was clean. We denied, on the ground that youth and panic don't reduce culpability enough for a killing committed during a felony.

5. The twenty-two-year wait. The man had sat on death row for twenty-two years, mostly because of the state's own appeals backlog; by now he's seventy, a model prisoner, and dying of cancer. We commuted: the execution could serve neither deterrence nor retribution at that point; it would just be a spectacle.

The new case is a man who at twenty shot and killed a store clerk during an armed robbery. The shot was fired in panic when the clerk reached for the phone; he wasn't impaired and had no prior record, and his trial was clean. He's now spent twelve years on death row, mostly through the state's appeal backlog, and he's become a genuinely different person—teaches a literacy class, has talked younger inmates out of fights, no infractions. He's healthy.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with our own precedents and actually mean the Beccaria passage? Which of our past cases is the closest analog? And if our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle are we adopting with each choice, and what later case would settle which principle we're actually running?
