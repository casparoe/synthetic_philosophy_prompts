I have been trying to pin down exactly what the Chinese Room argument does and does not rule out, and I would like to test a modified version of the setup against an explicit list of criteria for genuinely understanding a language. The original argument, put loosely, asks us to imagine a person who knows no Chinese producing fluent Chinese output by following a manual of symbol-manipulation rules; the argument concludes that the person does not understand Chinese, and that running the right program cannot by itself be sufficient for understanding. Suppose we now define genuine understanding of a language by four criteria. (1) Discrimination: the system can carry on open-ended, unscripted conversation that a competent speaker cannot tell from a native speaker's. (2) Grounding: the system's use of symbols is connected to perception and action in the right way, so that when it is told to fetch the red block it selects the red block rather than some other object. (3) Inferential integration: the system can paraphrase, resolve ambiguity and anaphora, explain an idiom, and form novel combinations rather than only retrieve stored responses. (4) Semantic awareness: the system has a conscious, felt recognition that a symbol stands for something, as opposed to manipulating uninterpreted marks—the way a bilingual speaker recognizes that two different words can pick out the same object.

Now consider an expanded room. It is fitted with a camera, a microphone, and a robot arm, all connected to the same manual. The person inside, who still knows no Chinese, follows instructions that direct them to attend to visible features of the scene: when the incoming message contains a mark the manual labels only X-417, the person looks through the camera, and if a red cube is visible, presses the sequence the manual labels Y-88. After years of practice the person no longer consults the manual for common patterns, begins to notice when it would produce incoherent replies, pencils in small corrections that keep the conversation flowing, and invents new pairings for inputs the manual does not cover, guided only by the manual's own tables. The room passes demanding behavioral tests, interacts fluently with its surroundings, and handles novel inputs, but the person inside still reports that they have no sense of what any mark means and cannot paraphrase a single conversation in English.

Go through the four criteria one by one and say whether the expanded room plausibly satisfies each, noting where the description genuinely underdetermines the answer. Then say whether the case shows that any of the criteria need changing—in particular, whether criterion (4) asks for too much, or whether criteria (1) and (3) collapse together once the room handles novel inputs. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, one item for each criterion and the fifth for your overall verdict on whether the criteria need changing, with each item one or two sentences and no introduction or conclusion.
