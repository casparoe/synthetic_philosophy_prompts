For the past few months I have been working at an early-stage company that builds productivity software, and I have been asked to help design a feature that summarizes each user's weekly progress. A colleague on the research team has been arguing that we should stop leaning so heavily on users' self-reported goals, since users often say they care about deep focus but then demonstrably spend their time on shallow tasks. His proposal is to run inverse reinforcement learning on each user's interaction logs to recover their underlying reward function, and then tailor the app's feedback to that inferred function even when it conflicts with what users say they want. His framing is that the algorithm is uncovering the user's true, behaviorally revealed preferences, and that self-reports are mostly rationalizations after the fact. I find this persuasive up to a point, but I have a nagging worry that the phrase true preferences is doing more work than it should. My question is whether it is legitimate to interpret a reward function recovered through inverse reinforcement learning as a person's actual preferences in the sense that would justify overriding their stated preferences, or whether that interpretation rests on a confusion I should flag before we build the feature around it.
