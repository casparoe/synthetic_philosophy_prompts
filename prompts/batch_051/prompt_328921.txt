I recently helped two friends form a limited liability company, and I have been struck by how naturally the law talks about the firm as a person of its own: the company will hold the account, own the equipment, and be the party entering the contracts, while the three of us sign only as its agents. The same legal system, however, will sometimes set that personhood aside in an action to pierce the corporate veil and hold us personally liable when the firm was treated as a mere instrument, and in other contexts it denies the corporate person privileges that a natural person has, such as the right against self-incrimination. I have also read that some jurisdictions have declared rivers legal persons, which makes corporate personhood look less like an isolated convenience. I am trying to understand what the practice actually presupposes about what a legal person is. Does it presuppose that legal persons are real entities, useful fictions, bundles of human acts and relationships, or something else, and is it even consistent? What would have to change in the practice if one of those underlying views were replaced by another?
