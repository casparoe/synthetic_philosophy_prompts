I am a professor of philosophy, and I have been asked by a regional probation service to help design a short training module for probation officers who supervise offenders serving community orders. Each officer meets several offenders a week and must recommend conditions to the court, and those recommendations can affect whether an offender remains in the community or is returned to custody. The officers have no philosophical training, and they must be able to run whatever they are taught under real time pressure. The service has observed two recurring failures: some officers speak as though an offender's reasons for complying are simply whatever the offender already happens to want, while others moralise and speak as though reasons apply to an offender regardless of anything the offender cares about. I want the training to be built around the distinction between internal and external reasons as it is drawn in moral philosophy, in particular the internal reasons thesis associated with Bernard Williams: roughly, that a person has a reason to do something only if that reason can be reached by sound deliberation from what the person already cares about, as against the view that some reasons apply to a person independently of their present motivations.

Please write the centrepiece of the module: a decision procedure that an officer can run in about fifteen minutes before meeting an offender and again when deciding what to recommend to the court. The procedure should tell the officer how to elicit and record the offender's motivational set; how to test whether a candidate reason is internal by applying an explicit and teachable account of sound deliberation (for example, correcting errors of fact, making vivid what a proposed course of action would actually involve, and resolving conflicts among the offender's own aims); how to recognise when a consideration is merely the officer's external judgement rather than a reason the offender has; and what the officer should do when the procedure concludes that the offender has no internal reason to comply with a proposed condition. Be clear about which steps are matters of fact, which are matters of value, and which the procedure cannot settle. Then run the procedure on two cases: (1) an offender who wants to remain out of prison and to see his children but is using drugs, and (2) an offender whose only stable motivation is loyalty to a gang. Include at least one case in which the procedure gives an answer the officers will find unwelcome, and state plainly what the procedure cannot do.
