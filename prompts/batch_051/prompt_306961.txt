I recently became a citizen of a country whose constitution entrenches certain rights and requires a two-thirds majority in both houses, plus ratification by three-quarters of the member states, to amend them; ordinary electoral majorities cannot touch those provisions. The usual justification is that the constitution binds the people against their own future impulses, much as Ulysses had himself tied to the mast. But that analogy puzzles me: it treats the future electorate as a different agent from the present one, and it assumes that later majorities are less competent or less legitimate than earlier ones. What theory of political agency, identity over time, and rationality does this practice presuppose? Does it strictly require that the people be seen as a single enduring self that can rationally bind itself, or can it be defended under rival theories? And what would have to change in the design of amendment rules if we rejected the Ulysses picture?
