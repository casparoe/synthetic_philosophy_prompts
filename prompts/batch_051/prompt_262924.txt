I am a journalist covering science and ideas, and I am preparing a long-form piece on a live dispute among evolutionary biologists and theologians over whether the deep history of animal suffering undermines the doctrine that creation was originally good. A source in that dispute compressed his case into the argument below. I want to know whether it actually goes through.

He begins from the chapter on the struggle for existence in Darwin's On the Origin of Species, where Darwin describes how the apparent peace of nature rests on constant predation, parasitism, starvation, and death. Darwin later wrote to Asa Gray that he could not bring himself to believe that a beneficent and omnipotent God would have specifically designed parasitic wasps to feed inside the living bodies of caterpillars. My source treats that biological observation as the basis for a theological conclusion.

(1) The doctrine of a good creation teaches that before the Fall there was no animal suffering, predation, or death.
(2) The fossil and phylogenetic record shows that animal pain, predation, parasitism, and death predate the existence of humans by hundreds of millions of years.
(3) The Fall, understood as a moral event involving the first humans, cannot causally reach back into pre-human deep time to make earlier animal suffering possible.
(4) Therefore, either the doctrine as stated in (1) is false, or the doctrine must be revised to allow a good creation that already includes pre-human animal suffering and death.
(5) No revision that allows pre-human sentient suffering can preserve the core theological claim that the original creation was free from natural evil.
(6) Therefore, evolutionary biology gives strong reason to reject the doctrine of a good creation, unless one accepts a highly revisionary, perhaps non-historical, account of the Fall.

I want to know whether this argument is valid, and if not, exactly which step or inference fails. For each premise, specify the strongest reading under which it is true, and tell me whether the argument still goes through on that reading. In particular, is the move from (3) to (4) safe, or does it trade on an equivocation between "good creation" as an original historical state and "good creation" as a doctrine whose content is not exhausted by (1)? And is (5) defensible, or is it too strong? If the conclusion does not follow, give the minimal revision of the premises that would make the argument valid, and say whether the source's actual position needs that revision.
