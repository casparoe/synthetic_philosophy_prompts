I'm a novelist and I've got a home-made theory of consciousness I keep pitching to anyone who'll listen, so I'd like a proper honest assessment. I call it the 'second-draft self.' The idea is that the conscious 'I' isn't the author of our actions at all—it's a later editorial pass. The brain writes a rough first draft (decisions, impulses, words) faster than we can follow, and then consciousness comes in like a rewriter: it tightens the scene, cuts the digressions, and backdates everything so the final draft looks like the plan from the start. That's why our choices feel intended even when they weren't. Example: I reach for the water and a thought pops up, 'I must be thirsty,' and I read that as the cause of reaching, but really the thought is the blurb for a movement that had already begun. Or I blurt something, then say 'wait, that's not what I meant'—that second thought isn't catching the real first draft, it's the editor pasting in a cleaner line and marking the original as a typo. I know this is in the neighborhood of user-illusion and 'interpreter' views, but my particular claim is that the self is not merely an after-the-fact commentator; it's a revision that destroys the earlier draft and then insists it was the only draft ever written. Is that actually distinct from the standard views, or have I just relabled them? Reconstruct the strongest version of the idea, tell me what it gets right, and point me at the cases where it breaks.
