Here's a variation on Kavka's toxin puzzle that I can't quite get straight. In the standard version, someone offers you a million dollars if at midnight you genuinely intend to drink a mild toxin tomorrow at noon. You don't have to follow through; the money is payed as soon as the intention is verified, and you know all that. The usual point is that you can't just decide to have the intention, because you'd know you're going to back out tomorrow once the money is in hand, since the toxin only has costs and no later benefit.

Now change the payoff so it's not about intending. Instead, at noon today a brain scanner reads out your genuine subjective probability that you will drink the toxin tomorrow. You get the million if that probability is at least 0.9. Whether you actually drink tomorrow doesn't affect the money, and you'll suffer nothing worse than a day of harmless misery if you do drink. You want the million and you're initially undecided, so your odds of drinking are maybe around fifty-fifty.

Can you, without doing anything to physically bind yourself or change tomorrow's options, deliberately raise your probability of drinking up to 0.9? If you can, are you then in any way committed to drinking tomorrow, or could a rational person be 0.9 that she'll drink and then simply not drink? If you can't, is that just the familiar point that you can't voluntarily control your credences, or is it the toxin puzzle insight again in a new form?

Walk through what a deliberating agent should say here. In particular: does deliberation itself make it impossible to settle on a high probability for one of your own open choices, or is self-prediction possible after all and the original lesson about intention simply doesn't carry over to credence?
