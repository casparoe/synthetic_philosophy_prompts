So I had a long argument with a friend last night about something she called ascription universality — roughly, the idea that if a belief is rational for one person in an evidential situation, it has to be rational for anyone in that same situation. I'd never heard the phrase, but I realize I basically run my epistemic life by it. My rule is: when two people disagree and both say they have the same evidence, I don't let myself say theyre both rationally responding to it. I try to figure out which view is epistemically dominant — better supported, less dependent on a suspicious starting point — and I treat that person as the rational one and downgrade the other. If I can't tell, I treat both as unproven rather than granting both rationality. My friend says this is a shortcut disguised as a principle: she thinks two people with the same evidence can rationally diverge because of differences in cognitive character, stakes, or background frameworks. I think she's just protecting beliefs she'd never defend for a stranger. Is my rule defensible? What would have to be true for it to be a good policy rather than a bias? Is there a version that keeps the anti-special-pleading pressure without assuming a uniquely rational response?
