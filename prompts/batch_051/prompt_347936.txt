In my social choice seminar two people got into it after we went through Sen's liberal paradox, and they asked me to adjudicate but I honestly couldn't tell who'd won. Here's the back-and-forth from my notes.

The setup they both accepted: three states — P reads Lady Chatterley's Lover, L reads it, or nobody reads. P (the prude) ranks: nobody reads > P reads > L reads. L (the lewd) ranks: P reads > L reads > nobody reads. Minimal liberalism gives P a say over P-reads vs nobody (P picks nobody) and gives L a say over L-reads vs nobody (L picks L-reads). So society gets nobody > P-reads and L-reads > nobody. But weak Pareto says P-reads > L-reads, since both rank P-reads above L-reads. Around the loop: P-reads > L-reads > nobody > P-reads.

Sam said the paradox is a formal artifact, not a real constraint on liberalism. Calling a right a pairwise decisiveness condition is the whole problem: in this example the rights only generate a cycle because each person is also assumed to have a preference about what the other reads, and weak Pareto happily aggregates those nosy preferences as if they were ordinary welfares. The preference for P-reads over L-reads — the one Pareto uses — is exactly a pair of meddlesome preferences: L wants to corrupt P, and P would rather read it himself than let L be corrupted. A real liberal doesn't let those preferences determine the social outcome. Screen them out and the cycle never forms; you're left with the two personal verdicts (L reads, P doesn't), consistent and Pareto-respecting on the personal preferences.

Mara said that move gives away the store. Sen's point is precisely that you can't have personal decisiveness plus weak Pareto plus unrestricted preferences all at once. The screen-out-nosy-preferences response is just a way of saying you reject weak Pareto as it's standardly stated, which is one of the three things the theorem tells you to abandon. And the screen is itself a substantive moral restriction, not a value-neutral formal fix: you have to say which preferences about other people's choices count, and why P-reads > L-reads is disallowed while L-reads > nobody isn't. That's liberalism doing the work — which is fine, but then Sen hasn't been refuted, he's been confirmed: the tidy package of minimal rights and unrestricted Pareto was never coherent.

Sam came back: fine, the theorem forces liberals to reject one horn, but the way it's usually presented makes people think rights themselves cause cycles, when really the culprit is treating unanimous preferences as automatically respectable. That's an important diagnostic but not a paradox about liberalism. If you ask a liberal what she'd give up, she'd say unrestricted preference aggregation, not my right to decide what I read.

Mara: but a liberal who rejects unrestricted preference aggregation is rejecting the claim that everyone preferring x to y is by itself a reason for x over y. That's not a technical amendment; it's a real cost, and most people who aren't already liberals would not pay it. So the paradox isn't a red herring — it shows liberalism has to take on that cost upfront.

That's where they ended. Please adjudicate move by move: which of Sam's and Mara's specific claims are right, whether Sam's screening response actually dissolves the cycle, and what the overall cost of combining liberal rights with Pareto efficiency really is once the dust settles.
