We've been going in circles in my philosophy of science reading group about whether intuitions about particular cases or intuitions about general principles are more trustworthy, and I'm starting to think its a structural problem rather than a shortage of clever arguments. The case-first people say a widely shared judgment about a case—a lucky justified true belief that isn't knowledge, or a chain of pairwise improvements that leaves you worse off overall—is basic data, and when a principle offends it, the principle goes. The principle-first people say those case judgments are noisy, framing-sensitive, and culturally variable, so they'd rather keep a powerful simple principle like transitivity of value or a neat theory of knowledge and bite the bullet on the weird case. Both sides cite the same psych studies, nobody moves, and I've begun to suspect they aren't disagreeing about the same thing at all. What kind of disagreement is this: a hidden factual question about how reliable case intuitions are, a difference about what counts as evidence in philosophy, bedrock temperaments about simplicity and system, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—or should we expect none?
