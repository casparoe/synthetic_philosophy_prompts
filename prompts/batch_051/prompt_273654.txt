Two people in my early-modern political thought reading group have been arguing for a year about whether the state is basically a corporation or whether that is a category mistake, and its become clear they have never actually sat down and written out what they agree on. One side (corporate-form people) thinks a state just is a corporation with a territorial franchise: it has a charter of sorts, artificial legal personality, officers, and a bundle of powers to make law, tax, and wage war, and the East India Company is the clean proof that the line between firm and sovereign is only a matter of who gets to call the thing sovereign besides itself. The other side (public-authority people) thinks this is a seductive analogy: chartered companies only ever governed on sufferance because a real sovereign delegated power to them and could revoke the charter, and what sets a state apart is that its authority is held for a public, not for owners or shareholders, and in the final instance is answerable to no one outside itself. Write the joint statement they should have produced. It should include (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can sign without footnotes or weasel words, (2) a table where each remaining disagreement is stated once in neutral language and once in each side's preferred wording, (3) the crux sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, and (4) for each crux, a concrete test, case, or piece of evidence both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. My money says one crux is about revocability (whether a power you can lose at someone else's pleasure can still be sovereignty), and another is whether the East India Companys decades of minting coins and keeping an army were sovereign powers or just a very large delegated franchise, but do not let me fix the list in advance.
