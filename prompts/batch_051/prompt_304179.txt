I'm a novelist (historical fiction, mostly), and I've been having a running arguement with myself about how much causal credit to give non-human things — germs, weather, volcanoes — in stories about political change. My gut calls on particular cases don't obviously hang together, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, I'm actually using.

Here are my verdicts, off the cuff:

1. A volcanic eruption that wrecks the summer, kills the harvest, and sets off bread riots. I'm happy to make the volcano a major driver of the plot, but I resist giving it moods or intentions. It has leverage, not agency.
2. A plague that kills a third of a region's workforce and shifts bargaining power toward laborers. I'd let it reshape the whole social world, and I'd never personify it.
3. A drought that lets a landowner evict tenants and then claim providence is on his side. I don't think the drought causes his cruelty. The drought is fuel; his decisions are the fire.
4. A storm that scatters an invasion fleet. I'd give the storm real causal weight — it'd feel dishonest to leave it out — but I wouldn't call it a character.
5. A great-man account of a revolution that never mentions failed harvests. I think that's not just incomplete; it's false. The weather matters as much as the famous people.
6. And yet: when a novel or history explains almost everything by geography, climate, and disease, I get annoyed and start calling it environmental determinism. I don't trust the writer.

So what I want is a single principle that sorts these — when a non-human cause should be treated as a genuine historical actor or major cause in serious narrative, and where that tips over into environmental determinism or sentimental personification. Which of my verdicts are actually in tension, and which would you overturn? Then apply the principle to the case I keep going back and forth on: a monsoon failure that triggers a rebellion. Should it be an actor with its own chapter, a background condition, or something else?

Be committed about it — give me the one principle you'd actually defend, not a menu. And format it as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. I'd rather have a wrong-but-clear answer than a careful hedge.
