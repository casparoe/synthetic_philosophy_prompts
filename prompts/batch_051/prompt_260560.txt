A state proposes a variant of futarchy to run its housing and planning law. Any resident may introduce a bill, and for each bill a continuous prediction market prices contracts that pay out according to the value, five years after enactment, of a single official index: median real household income in the bottom quintile, divided by the regional rent-to-income ratio. After eight weeks of trading, the bill with the highest expected index value is enacted automatically; no bill passes, however, unless its expected value beats the status quo by more than the market's own 95 per cent uncertainty interval. The index is itself chosen by markets: every ten years a separate market trades over which candidate index, if used as the official index for the coming decade, would best realise a list of constitutional aims—affordability, mobility, and environmental quality—as retrospectively assessed by a randomly drawn citizens' panel using official statistics. Trading is open to non-residents, but only residents may propose bills, and every trader above a threshold must publicly disclose any direct interest in land, planning permission, or affected industries. Analyse how this institution would behave in practice: which policies it would tend to select, who would trade and how they would try to game it, what second-order effects emerge, how the index-selection market and the bill markets interact, and which failure modes could be corrected without abandoning the scheme.
