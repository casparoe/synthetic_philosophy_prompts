I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about whether Aristotle's account of money retains any grip on money as it exists today. My friend's position was that it cannot: Aristotle analyzes coined metal whose value bottoms out in a useful commodity, and modern money has left that world behind. I found myself resisting that conclusion, but I also do not trust myself to work through the text carefully enough to know whether I was defending Aristotle or just defending a mood. The passage we kept circling is from Aristotle, Politics, Book I, 1257a, in Benjamin Jowett's translation:

For when they had come to supply themselves more from abroad by importing things in which they were deficient and exporting those of which they had a surplus, the employment of money necessarily came to be devised. For the natural necessaries are not in every case readily portable; hence for the purpose of barter men made a mutual compact to give and accept some substance of such a sort as being itself a useful commodity was easy to handle in use for general life, iron for instance, silver and other metals, at the first stage defined merely by size and weight, but finally also by impressing on it a stamp in order that this might relieve them of having to measure it; for the stamp was put on as a token of the amount.

What I want is a careful prediction of what Aristotle, on the strength of this passage and the distinctions he draws around it in Politics I, chapters 8 through 10, would be committed to saying about institutions that postdate him: (a) state-issued fiat currency that is legal tender by decree, convertible into nothing, and has no significant non-monetary use; (b) Bitcoin and similar cryptocurrencies, which are accepted by no state, deliberately scarce, and likewise lack a useful underlying commodity; and (c) the largely digitized money of contemporary banking, in which most of what functions as money consists of ledger entries spent through payment networks rather than coined metal passed from hand to hand. I am not looking for generic speculation that Aristotle would have been a conservative about change. I want the prediction built from the actual conceptual machinery he uses: the distinction between natural and unnatural wealth-getting, the distinction between a thing's proper use and its use for exchange, and the account of how money arises from barter and then becomes the object of a further art of accumulation. Where would those distinctions extend cleanly to these modern arrangements, where would they force conclusions Aristotle would probably not have welcomed, and where would they simply stop engaging the phenomenon at all? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
