I have found myself following an epistemic rule that I want examined before it hardens into habit. When I am reading anonymous online comments, emails, or my students' papers (I am a teaching assistant in a non-humanities subject), I discount an argument substantially when the prose contains what I was taught to call bad grammar: subject–verb disagreement, dangling modifiers, comma splices, less for count nouns, or stigmatised dialect features such as habitual be or double negation. I do not merely notice the grammar; I treat it as evidence about the argument itself, often reducing my confidence almost to zero for anonymous comments and marking down otherwise coherent student work. My stated justification is that standard edited English is a visible trace of care, and that someone who has not checked their sentences has probably not checked their reasoning; my unstated suspicion is that I am also treating it as a proxy for formal schooling and for membership in my own speech community.

A linguist friend tells me this is prescriptivism wearing an epistemic costume: there is no grammar-independent "bad grammar," only dialects and registers, and every dialect has rules that its speakers follow with the same automatic precision as I follow mine. A colleague in the writing centre disagrees, insisting that standard edited English exists precisely because it reduces ambiguity and signals attention in particular genres, so competence in it can be a legitimate filter in those genres. I cannot evaluate every argument from scratch, so I need some cheap filters. What I want to know is whether this specific filter is defensible, and if so at what rate: is it reasonable to discount an argument nearly to zero on grammatical grounds alone, or is there a weaker version — say, treating nonstandard prose as a prompt to read more carefully rather than as a strike against the content — that keeps whatever diagnostic value the signal has without converting a sociolinguistic fact into an epistemic one?
