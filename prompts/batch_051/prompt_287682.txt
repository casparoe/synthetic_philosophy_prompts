Rousseau's doctrine of the general will carries the unsettling corollary that a person can be mistaken about what he truly wills, and that someone who refuses to obey the general will may rightly be constrained—made free rather than coerced. That corollary seems to license an asymmetry I notice everywhere in ordinary democratic judgment, including my own. When an election or referendum yields the outcome I believe is right, I call it the will of the people and treat it as something close to the general will. When the same procedure, with the same margin and the same quality of available information, yields the outcome I believe is wrong, I reach instead for debunking explanations—ignorance, manipulation, short-term interest—and conclude that the actual vote has diverged from what the people truly will. Only the content of the outcome decides which description I apply. Having spent my working life as a natural scientist, I am disposed to regard a distinction as suspect if it cannot be applied until after I know which result it is supposed to rationalize. So I want to know whether there is a principled, real-time criterion for judging that a particular vote expressed the general will rather than the will of all—or whether the general will is simply the name we give the will of all when we happen to like it. If you defend the asymmetry, show me how the criterion operates before the ballots are counted, not just afterward.
