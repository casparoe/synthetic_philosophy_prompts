I'm grading a midterm for a practical ethics course I'm TAing, and I'd like a second set of eyes on one answer. The exam question was: "Set aside the law and focus on the ethics. Some people argue that whistleblowing is a betrayal and that loyalty is weighty enough to normally settle the question. Reconstruct the strongest version of this loyalty objection to whistleblowing, then give the strongest reply to it, and then give your own verdict: is loyalty ever, on its own, a sufficient moral reason not to blow the whistle?" Here is the student's answer, left as submitted.

"Loyalty is not just blind obedience, since loyalty aims at some shared good, so it can't require going along with anything an institution does. The loyalty objection, at its strongest, says that by joining an institution we take on special obligations to those we work with, and that whistleblowing breaks the confidentially and trust those relationships depend on; it hurts people who trusted us. The main reply is that loyalty is thicker than the objection assumes: a nurse who reports unsafe staffing is loyal to her patients and arguably to her coworkers, so it isn't a betrayal unless we just define loyalty as loyalty to the institution as it currently is. So the objection only really sticks for a basically decent institution that has gone off course in one area. On the broader theory side, utilitarianism says leaking is right whenever it produces more overall happiness than staying quiet, which will usually be the case when the wrongdoing is serious. A Kantian, by contrast, is bound by the duty not to break promises or confidentiality and cannot make exceptions, so on that view whistleblowing would very often be ruled out. My own view is that loyalty always supplies at least a pro tanto reason not to blow the whistle, even in a corrupt institution, because loyalty is a virtue and a virtue always counts on the side of preserving the relationship or institution that currently exists; the only question is how heavily to weigh it against the harm."
