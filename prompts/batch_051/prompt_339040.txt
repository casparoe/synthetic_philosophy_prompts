I'm working through the fourth part of Kant's Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason, and I keep getting tangled up in the section where he makes the distinction between true religion and what he calls counterfeit service. The passage I'm stuck on comes right after he's been arguing that the only way to serve God is to live a morally good life, and that treating any non-moral activity—rites, sacrifices, private prayers, professions of faith—as if it could make us pleasing to God is a kind of illusion. He then says that this illusion involves treating God as a powerful being whose favor can be won by means other than becoming a better person, and that this is really a form of fetishism, even when the people doing it think of themselves as highly spiritual.

The thing I find confusing is why, exactly, Kant thinks the line falls where it does. He seems to distinguish between (a) doing your moral duty as a way of honoring God, and (b) doing some extra non-moral action in order to get closer to God or win his help, and he thinks (b) is idolatrous even if the action is perfectly innocent in itself. But I don't see why (a) isn't equally a way of trying to make oneself acceptable to God—if you do your duty partly because you want to be in God's good graces, isn't that also using your action as a means? And his response seems to be that in true religion you're not trying to buy anything, you just become worthy of whatever God decides, and you don't presume to know what else to do. Is that a stable distinction, or is he smuggling in a Protestant assumption about the unimportance of external forms? I'm taking a course on Kant's practical philosophy and we're reading this text in translation, so please don't assume I have the German; can you help me unpack what exactly he means by counterfeit service here, and whether the distinction he's drawing is more about the agent's intention or about the type of action itself?
