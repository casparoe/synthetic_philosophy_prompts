We're doing a unit on ancient historians in AP World History, and my friend keeps saying that history is just written by the victors, which sounds deep until you actually try to use it. We read a little Thucydides, who's supposed to be suspicious of hearsay, and Herodotus, who basically writes down whatever travelers told him, and I started keeping a list of my own instincts about which sources I trust. The problem is my list has a bunch of verdicts that don't seem to come from one rule, and one pair I can't reconcile at all. Im hoping you can either tell me the principle I'm already using, or tell me which instincts I have to give up.

Here's the list. A king's inscription says he crushed a rebellion; the only other account, from the rebels' descendants two generations later, says it was actually a negotiated truce, and a broken treaty stone survives but doesn't say who paid tribute. I lean toward the descendants on the outcome, even though they have plenty of motive to make themselves look good. Two enemy cities' propaganda disagree about who started the war, but both independently mention that the first battle happened near a village called Whitebridge; I think Whitebridge is basically certain. A tax roll from a conquering empire records 800 households resettled, while a nationalist historian of the conquered people writing later says it was 8,000; I trust the tax roll, since nobody falsifies paperwork to look mighty and the nationalist wanted the story to be worse than it was. A general's memoir from the winning side includes a chapter where he admits he was drunk when he ordered a disastrous charge; I end up trusting that memoir more overall, even its flattering parts. A colonial administrator produced the only records of a conquered people's religion; I'll use him for the names of gods and festivals, but I won't accept his claim that the people had no idea of a supreme being, since that sounds like his own theory rather than anything he observed. And here's the one I keep flip-flopping on: a regime that everyone now agrees lied constantly about its own crimes did issue one internal report saying the harvest failed and the population declined a little. My instinct that self-damaging admissions are reliable says trust the report, but my instinct never to trust official numbers about a regime's own wrongs says the opposite. I honestly dont know which one I'd bet on.

So is there a single principle of source criticism that captures all of these verdicts? If there is, state it precisely, tell me which of my cases each part of it is doing work for, and say what it predicts about cases I haven't listed, like a loser's diary written the same year, or two court histories that both flatter the same king but disagree on dates. If there isn't one, which verdicts should I drop, and what's the simplest principle that keeps the rest? And after all this, what should I say to my friend about the line that history is written by the victors: is it a rule, a bias, or just something people say before looking at the evidence?
