A friend in a methodology-of-economics reading group has built an argument he now suspects proves too much, and he asked me to help him find the joint in it; I couldn't. He is defending a view he calls the F-twist, associated with Milton Friedman's methodology of positive economics: the claim that a model is to be judged by its predictive success, and that the realism of its assumptions is beside the point. The argument runs like this. (1) The only thing that can count for or against a model is how well its predictions match observation. (2) A model's assumptions are not predictions; they are starting commitments whose justification is wholly downstream, in the accuracy of the predictions they help produce. (3) So the fact that an assumption is false or unrealistic is never, by itself, a reason to reject a model. (4) Consequently, familiar complaints that firms do not literally maximize profits, or that market participants are not fully rational, are no objections to models built on those assumptions, so long as the models predict well. (5) Nothing in the reasoning is special to economics: if a model built on the assumption that the earth is flat reproduces, accurately and across a wide range of dates, the observed times of sunrise and sunset, then by (1)–(3) that is a good model of the sunrise, and the falsity of its flat-earth assumption is no objection. (6) But a flat-earth sunrise model is not a good model: its organizing assumption is a falsehood we have independent reason to reject, and a model whose central assumption we know to be false is not one we should accept. (7) Therefore at least one of (1)–(3) must go. My friend says he would most like to keep (1), because without it methodology collapses into interminable quarrels about realism that no data can settle, but he cannot see how keeping (1) lets him refuse (5). I share the worry but also can't locate the bad step. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument does not actually force the choice it appears to, or if my friend is misdescribing the position he wants to defend, please say that plainly instead of playing along.
