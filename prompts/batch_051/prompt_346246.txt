I keep going back and forth on what should count as a language, and I've ended up with six criteria I want someone to pressure-test:

(1) It has a finite stock of meaningful units (signs or words) with reasonably stable meanings.
(2) Those units combine by rules into an open-ended range of messages, so people can produce and understand strings they've never come across before.
(3) It can be used for displacement: talking about the past, the future, possibilities, or stuff that isn't in the here and now.
(4) It has some double articulation: the meaningful units are put together from a smaller set of meaningless pieces, so the system doesn't need a separate sign for every possible message.
(5) The connection between form and meaning is mostly arbitrary rather than transparent pantomime.
(6) It's learned from others and passed on across generations within a community, rather than reinvented by each user or confined to one person.

ASL and BSL obviously pass all six, so that's not what I'm worried about; I want to know whether the criteria draw a sensible line at the bottom.

Now here's the borderline case. Suppose a deaf child grows up in a hearing family in a remote village with no exposure to any established sign language. Over the child's life, the child invents a manual system of roughly 600 stable signs, and the family half-learns it. It's not just mime. Many signs are arbitrary in form, and there's a small set of recurring handshapes and locations that get reused across signs without much independent meaning. The child combines signs into multi-sign strings with a consistant order, has one gesture that works like a negator and another that works like a question marker, and can sign about what happened last week, what might happen after the harvest, and even stuff the child makes up as a joke. The parents and an older sibling sign back, but they mostly use single signs or two-sign combinations and lean on the child for the grammatical heavy lifting. The system hasn't spread outside the household, and it will probably die with the child.

Does this thing qualify as a language? Go through the six criteria one by one. For each criterion, say whether it's met, unmet, or only partly met, and how confident you are. Where it's unmet or only partly met, say whether that's a real requirement for being a language or just a feature this particular case happens to lack. Then answer the question I actually care about: does this case show that my criteria draw the line in the wrong place — for example, that requiring cross-generational community transmission rules out something that plainly is a language, or that I've weighted productivity too heavily? Be willing to tell me the criteria themselves are the problem.
