A national constitution adopted in 1990 contains the following provision, drafted when the public feared military coups and ballot-box fraud:

"All public authority shall derive from the will of the people. The people shall express that will in genuine, free, and fair elections held at regular intervals, and the government so elected shall be accountable to the people through those elections alone."

Since then, a party elected with 51 percent of the vote has, without amending this provision, enacted a series of laws that do the following: place the election commission under the control of a committee appointed by the prime minister; redraw electoral districts so that the party's 51 percent of votes reliably yields 60 percent of the seats; require all civic associations receiving foreign funding to dissolve, which eliminates most independent election-monitoring groups; and replace the public broadcaster's board with party loyalists. Elections continue to occur on schedule, opposition parties may campaign, and votes are counted without systematic fraud.

Does the provision, read as its drafters would have read it, reach these measures? Does it reach them if read as a general principle about what it means for public authority to derive from the will of the people? If those two readings diverge, which should a constitutional court follow, and what does that imply about whether "democratic backsliding" names a coherent condition rather than a partisan label for policies one dislikes?
