In the philosophy of photography, Kendall Walton argued that photographs are transparent. On his view, looking at a photograph is a way of seeing the photographed object itself, in roughly the way that looking through a telescope or a mirror is a way of seeing the object at the other end of the optical chain. The ground of the claim is the production process: a photograph is formed by a mechanical sequence of light, a lens, and a sensor or photosensitive surface, so its content is counterfactually dependent on what stood in front of the camera rather than on what anyone believed or intended to depict. That, Walton held, is what separates photographs from hand-made pictures such as paintings, and it is also what lets a photograph function as an epistemic backstop — a check that is not mediated by a reporter's possibly false or biased beliefs.

Walton's argument presupposes that a photorealistic image before you was produced by just such a light-to-surface causal chain. Now suppose a generative model produces an image, of the kind often called a deepfake, that is visually indistinguishable from a photograph of an event even though no such event occurred and there is no scene-to-pixel counterfactual dependence. Had the nonexistent scene differed, the generated image would not track that difference, because there was no scene to track.

Does Walton's transparency argument survive this development, or does it need repair, or is it dead? Be specific about which premise the development bears on, and about the difference between a claim about how an image was produced and a claim about what a viewer can know from looking at it. If the transparency thesis can still be saved for images whose provenance has been verified, show how; if its epistemic value collapses once some images are synthesized, say what the remaining problem should be called instead.
