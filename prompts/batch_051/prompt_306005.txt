You are a professional philosopher specializing in social ontology and game-theoretic accounts of convention, and you are good at translating that material for practitioners. I work as a community governance analyst at a platform that hosts several thousand semi-autonomous online communities — wikis, discussion forums, and multiplayer game guilds. Roughly once a week my team has to decide whether to codify a de facto user practice as official policy, leave it as unwritten practice, or actively discourage it. We have about a day per decision, the team consists of non-philosophers, and the recommendation has to be defensible both to the communities and to the legal and policy staff who sign off. I want you to turn David Lewis's account of convention, together with the game-theoretic picture of how precedent, salience, and common-knowledge expectations select one equilibrium from among equally good alternatives, into a reusable decision procedure for us. The deliverable should be a checklist or a flowchart in words: tests to run in order, the evidence to look for in platform logs, prior moderation decisions, and short user surveys, what to do when that evidence is missing or ambiguous, and the point at which each test stops being mechanical and becomes a judgment call.

Think carefully before you draft, and begin by brainstorming which distinctions in Lewis's view must survive translation into a daily tool and which can safely be dropped; say explicitly which parts of the account you are setting aside and why. In particular, make sure the final procedure separates three things we currently blur together: (a) a genuine coordination convention whose force comes from mutual expectations, (b) a brute convergence of private preferences that people would hold independently of what others do, and (c) a regularity stabilized mainly by sanctions or public shaming rather than by conditional expectations. Do not pad the answer with a reading list or citations; I need the operational form, not a literature review.

Once the procedure is written, apply it to three hypothetical cases: (1) a wiki where editors have begun placing the same spoiler template at the top of article sections containing plot details, and a group of users petitions to make the template mandatory; (2) a discussion forum where newcomers are expected to post in a dedicated introductions thread before participating, and those who skip it are publicly scolded by veterans; (3) a game guild where most members happen to use the same ready-check emoji before raids, but no one expects anyone else to use it. Make sure at least one of these cases produces a verdict my team would not like. End with a short, honest statement of what the procedure cannot tell us.
