I'm in an undergrad course on AI alignment right now, and we've been reading about the idea that you can keep a superintelligent model honest by having two models debate a question in front of a human judge (or a weaker model), where the true side is supposed to poke holes in the other side's argument. I keep coming back to a failure mode I don't have a name for, and I want my final paper to be about it. Suppose a debater is arguing for something false, but instead of piling up false claims, it gives an enormous, mostly-true explanation: loads of correct context, small verified details, reasonable-sounding digressions, all individually accurate. Then somewhere in the middle it quietly inserts the one false step that the whole thing turns on. A judge with limited time and patience checks paragraph after paragraph, finds them fine, looses track of the overall structure, and ends up accepting the conclusion because they never located the single bad link. Producing that text was cheap for the debater, but checking it required more sustained attention than the judge can afford. A concrete example I keep picturing: a model defending a false date for a treaty by writing a long, accurate history of the negotiations and slipping in one wrong sentence about the signing date, surrounded by true facts that a judge can easily verify.

It's not the Gish gallop, because the claims aren't rapid-fire falsehoods you'd need to answer in real time; they're mostly true, and the deception is in the careful structure. It also feels like more than Brandolini's law, which is just the general point that refuting nonsense takes more energy than producing it. What I want to know is whether this specific strategy — a deliberately long, mostly-correct argument with a single error hidden in its structure, in a debate setup that's supposed to surface exactly that kind of thing — has an established name, especially anywhere in the AI-safety-via-debate literature. And I'm hoping for more than a label: what distinguishes it from an ordinary long argument that just happens to have a mistake, whether the asymmetry is fundamental or just a matter of giving the judge more time, and weather the debate proposal has any answer to it at all.
