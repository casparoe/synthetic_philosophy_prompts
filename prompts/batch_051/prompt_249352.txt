Take the canonical grudge-informer case from the Hart–Fuller debate, leaving aside its contested historical details: a woman denounces her husband to the authorities for private remarks against the dictator; he is convicted under the law then in force and imprisoned; after the regime falls, she is prosecuted for the denunciation. Now change exactly one feature: the statute under which the denunciation is brought is not a Nazi innovation or wartime decree, but a long-standing sedition provision of the ordinary criminal code, enacted decades earlier by a democratic legislature, formally valid by any account, and routinely enforced by ordinary courts before the dictatorship. The remark, the report, the conviction, and the postwar prosecution are otherwise unchanged.

Work through how this variation changes the dispute between the Hartian legal positivist and the Fullerian natural lawyer, using those labels only as shorthand. Respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Your five items should address, in order: (1) the point on which Hart and Fuller agree in the original case and the precise question at which their analyses part ways; (2) whether the variation affects Hart’s claim that the statute is valid law and that condemning the informer honestly requires retrospective legislation; (3) whether the variation deprives Fuller of his procedural or internal-morality argument, or whether that argument still has purchase, and whether he must fall back on a substantive natural-law claim; (4) whether the two positions now converge on a verdict for different reasons or still disagree about the proper ground of decision; and (5) what the variation reveals that the original case obscured about whether the debate concerns legal validity, obedience, or judicial duty.
