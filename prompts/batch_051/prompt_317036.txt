I keep tying myself in knots about what actually binds me when I promise. Here's where my gut lands on a batch of cases.

I promised my brother I'd pick him up from the airport at midnight Tuesday. By 10pm I'm in bed, it's raining, and I really don't want to go. Gut: I go; I said I'd do it.

I promised a neighbor I'd water her tomatoes while she was away, then her trip got cancelled and she texted me not to bother. Gut: nothing is owed anymore. The promise just evaporates.

I promised my mother near the end that I'd fix up the old house and sell it to my cousin rather than to a developer, even though I knew she'd be gone by then. A developer now comes in at nearly double the money. Gut: I can't take it; the promise binds even though she'll never know.

A guy once cornered me and made me promise, with a gun in my face, to bring a package across town the next day. Far as I'm concerned that just doesn't bind; the "promise" doesn't count.

I promised my kid I'd be at her school play Thursday, and Wednesday night my boss tells me the client walks if I'm not in the office Thursday. Gut: the promise still binds — I just break a binding promise and owe her something. That's different from discovering it was never binding.

I told someone at a party "I promise I'll think about your idea" mainly to end the conversation. Gut: this is barely a promise at all. If anything I owe him a polite hearing, not a real deliberation.

I promised a friend I'd read his job application by Friday, and by Friday I'm wrecked. Gut: I still owe him something, but I can text and ask for two more days. Renegotiating isn't the same as breaking.

My working theory was that a promise binds exactly when breaking it would let someone down or leave them worse off than if I'd never made it. But that won't explain the house promise to my mother — nobody's let down, nobody's worse off — and it won't explain the gunpoint case either, where the guy would be worse off but I don't owe him a thing.

I was reading David Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature, Book III, Part II, Section V ("Of the obligation of promises"), and this passage caught me:

"The act of the mind, exprest by a promise, is not a resolution to perform any thing: For that alone never imposes any obligation. Nor is it a desire of such a performance: For we may bind ourselves without such a desire, or even with an aversion, declar'd and avow'd. Neither is it the willing of that action, which we promise to perform: For a promise always regards some future time, and the will has an influence only on present actions."

That seems to be saying my real resolution to pick up my brother isn't what does the work either — Hume would say the mental act alone can't generate any obligation, because I also promise things with outright aversion. So: is there a principle that gets my verdicts, or most of them? And does Hume's story — that promises are artificial conventions, not natural facts about my feelings — supply the missing piece, or does it force me to give up one of my verdicts (probably the deathbed one, or maybe the party one)? I want the principle stated precisely, with which case motivates each clause, what it says about cases I haven't listed, and an honest list of the verdicts it can't accommodate. Also please seperate what's actually settled in this area from what people who work on promising genuinely still fight about, and don't make a contested reading of Hume sound like the consensus.
