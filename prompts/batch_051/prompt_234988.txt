I keep going back and forth on loyalty and when exposing someone is a betrayal, so I’m going to write down my actual gut calls on some cases and see whether there’s a principle behind them.

Case A: A friend from years ago tells me he once skimmed a few thousand dollars from his employer by padding expense reports. It’s long over, he’s ashamed, and it never happens again. I stay quiet. I don’t feel disloyal to anyone for staying quiet.

Case B: A co-worker is falsifying safety-test numbers on a product that could hurt people. I report him internally. I’d feel bad for him, but not like I betrayed him, and I’d still do it if he were a friend.

Case C: My sister-in-law is having an affair. My wife asks me point-blank whether I know anything. I tell her. It feels like a small betrayal of the sister-in-law, but I’d still do it.

Case D: A grad student finds out her advisor fudged some statistics in a well-known paper, but the fudge doesn’t change the main conclusion. I think she should confront him privately and only escalate if he refuses. If she stays silent after he refuses, that’s wrong; if she quietly fixes things in her own work without telling anyone, that’s not a betrayal of him, just maybe spineless.

Case E: A soldier saw his unit kill a civilian and cover it up. Years later a reporter asks him. I think he should tell the truth, and I don’t think it’s a betrayal of the unit; they had already broken the code they were supposed to be loyal to.

Case F: I’m on a hiring committee and my best friend is a bad candidate. I vote against him. I think this is only a betrayal if I’d let him believe I was going to support him and didn’t warn him. Otherwise it isn’t.

Case G: A politician publicly criticizes his own party’s leader during an election campaign while still calling himself a party member. I think that’s disloyal and he probably shouldn’t do it, but “betrayal” feels too strong. If he quit and immediately campaigned for the other side, that would feel like a betrayal.

My tentative principle, which I only half believe: exposing someone is okay—or at least not a betrayal—when the wrongdoing is serious, ongoing or likely to recur, harms identifiable people, and you’ve tried private pressure first or the situation makes that impossible; how close you are to the person raises the bar but doesn’t give an absolute veto. What I like is that this handles A (over, he’s ashamed), B (ongoing, serious), D (private first), and E (private pressure impossible). What it does not handle is C, since revealing the affair feels like a betrayal even though it maybe doesn’t meet several of the conditions. And I’m not sure it explains why G is wrong-but-not-betrayal, while F only becomes betrayal if I’d misled my friend.

So: is there one principle that get's all of these, or close? Which of my verdicts should it make me give up? And what does the principle you settle on say about a case I keep avoiding: a man finds out his brother drove drunk years ago and never got caught, and now the brother is about to marry someone who doesn’t know about it. Does he have to tell her?
