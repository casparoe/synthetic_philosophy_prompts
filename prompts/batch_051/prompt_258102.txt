I have been working through decision theory on my own and ran into something in risk-weighted expected utility theory (RWEU) that I cannot sort out, and I would be grateful for an outside diagnosis. The theory represents a person's attitude to risk with a risk function r, increasing from r(0)=0 to r(1)=1, where a risk-avoidant agent has r(p)<p for every p strictly between 0 and 1. A gamble is evaluated from its worst outcome upward: one begins with the utility of the worst outcome and then, for each successively better outcome, adds r(the probability of doing at least that well) times the utility increment attached to that outcome. So an agent with utility linear in dollars and r(p)=p² values a fair-coin gamble paying $3 on heads and $0 on tails at r(1/2)·3 = 0.75, and therefore prefers a sure $1 to that gamble even though the gamble's expected value is $1.50. I like this because it seems to capture the idea that a rational person may decline some positive-expectation gambles without being irrational.

A friend of mine has built the following argument and says it shows the theory is self-defeating; I cannot find the exit. (1) Take an agent with u(x)=x and r(p)=p² who is offered, on each of three days, a choice between a sure $1 that day and an independent fair-coin gamble paying $3 on heads and $0 on tails. By the calculation above, RWEU gives each daily gamble value 0.75 against value 1 for the sure dollar, so the theory tells the agent to take the sure dollar each day, ending with $3. (2) Now suppose the same agent is instead offered a single choice today between a sure $3 and the compound plan of taking all three such gambles. Treated as one act, the compound has outcomes $0, $3, $6, and $9 with probabilities 1/8, 3/8, 3/8, and 1/8, so its RWEU is r(7/8)·3 + r(1/2)·3 + r(1/8)·3 = (49/64 + 16/64 + 1/64)·3 = 3.09..., greater than the sure 3. So the theory tells the very same agent to prefer the three-gamble plan. (3) But the three one-dollar sureties taken day by day amount to exactly the same dollars as the single sure $3, and the sequence of three gambles, if the agent takes the gamble each time, has exactly the same final outcomes and probabilities as the compound plan; an account of rational preference should not flip its advice merely because identical options are packaged as three small choices rather than one large one. (4) Therefore RWEU gives contradictory advice about the same options and cannot be an adequate theory of rational preference. I would like to keep both my attraction to risk-aversion of the RWEU kind and the principle that packaging should not matter, but my friend says I cannot have both. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
