I spent thirty years in population genetics and have only recently started reading philosophy seriously, so forgive me if I'm retracing old ground. I keep running into the "meme" literature — Dawkins, Dennett, and the anthropologists who hate both of them — and it strikes me that people argue past each other because they never say up front what a Darwinian theory of culture is actually supposed to do. I don't want a survey and I don't want a reading list. I want the desiderata. Any adequate theory of cultural evolution has to deliver certain things, and for each I want the reasoning, ideally with a concrete toy case: the phenomena it must explain (why some cultural variants spread and others go extinct, how a lineage can persist when every transmission introduces noise, how cumulative adaptation is possible without a genome or a clean germ line/soma split), and the distinctions it must respect (imitation versus mere contagion versus independent reinvention; a variant spreading because it helps its human hosts versus because it is just good at getting itself copied). It also can't contradict what we already know from evolutionary biology — for instance, blending inheritance eats variation and makes selection weak, so a cultural system needs some story about how variants stay seperate enough to be selected. And I want you to flag which desiderata are non-negotiable requirements and which are just habits of one camp. Having spent decades watching real populations evolve, I strongly suspect one of the usual demands in these debates is not a demand on a theory at all but a preference; tell me which requirement does the most work in ruling out candidate theories, and whether it has actually earned that work.
