Practice question: Is it a problem for the rule of law when a legal system lets old statutes fall into long-term non-enforcement instead of formally repealing them? Explain the best case for saying it is a problem and the best case for saying it is not, then take a side.

Student's answer: Long-term non-enforcement, sometimes called desuetude, is when officials simply stop applying a statute for so long that everyone treats it as dead even though it remains formally on the books. The rule-of-law worry is that the law's content then changes without any legislator deciding to change it; the community's non-use acts almost like a retroactive repeal. A second worry is notice: someone who reads the statute book sees a rule that looks current, but if officials suddenly revive it, she can be punished under a rule she reasonably believed was no longer in force. On the other side, you could say that forcing officials to dust off every forgotten law would be absurd and would itself be arbitrary, and that the rule of law only really requires consistent application of the laws a society actually lives under. I mostly think the practice is harmless, because a law that nobody enforces is not really a law in the operative sense. The harder problem, in my view, is selective enforcement, which is basically desuetude in miniature: a prosecutor choosing not to charge some people under a live law. Both make the law less predictable, but selective enforcement is worse because it is hidden, whereas desuetude is at least out in the open.
