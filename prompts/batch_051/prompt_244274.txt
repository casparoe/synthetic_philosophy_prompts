I recently returned to the church I grew up in and was asked to lead a small group on how we decide which commands in Scripture still bind us and which were for their original setting. The question stopped being abstract when my daughter asked why we eat shellfish but treat other Levitical rules as serious, and I could not give her a principled answer. I want to know what principle, if any, captures my own judgments, where it breaks down, and which judgments I should revise.

My current, largely unreflective verdicts, case by case:

- The dietary rules in Leviticus, such as the bans on pork and shellfish: not binding now. They marked Israel off from its neighbors, and the New Testament seems to set them aside.
- The ban on charging interest on loans to the poor: the specific ban feels tied to an ancient agrarian setting, but the underlying principle against exploiting the desperate feels standing.
- The instruction in Paul's letters that women cover their heads when praying or prophesying: the covering itself feels cultural, but the concern not to bring shame on the gathered community feels standing.
- The household-code instructions that slaves obey their masters: I read these as accommodations to an ancient institution rather than a standing endorsement of slavery, and the deeper arc of the text seems to condemn the institution itself.
- The command to greet one another with a kiss: the form is cultural; the point about warm and appropriate greeting is standing.
- The prohibitions on murder, theft, and false testimony: binding outright.
- The sexual prohibitions found in the same book of Leviticus as the dietary rules: I find myself treating these as standing rather than cultural.

The difficulty is the last item against the first. I treat the sexual rules of Leviticus as transcultural while treating the dietary rules from the same law code as cultural, and every distinction I reach for — moral versus ceremonial, repeated in the New Testament versus not, rooted in creation versus not — either breaks down under inspection or seems reverse-engineered from the conclusion I already hold. Is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that gets all of these verdicts, or nearly all? What does it say about cases I have not settled: the rules about mixed fabrics, the prohibition on tattoos, the instruction that women not teach or exercise authority, the Jubilee debt-release rules, and the command to be subject to governing authorities? And which of my verdicts, if any, should I give up?

Please state the candidate principle or principles precisely, say which clause of the principle is doing the work for each of my verdicts, and be honest about which of my judgments it cannot accommodate. Separate what is settled among serious interpreters from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
