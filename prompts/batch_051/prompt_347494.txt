You're a philosopher of technology who's especially good at forcing a position to say what it would have to give up. Think carefully, and maybe brainstorm a little before you answer.

I keep coming back to the old debate about whether artifacts have politics, and on reflection I'm drawn to a tidy instrumentalist view: an artifact is just a stabilized capacity for doing things, and whatever looks political about it comes from the intentions of designers and the social arrangements around its use. A kitchen knife isn't political; a law about carrying knives is. I like how clean that is, and how it seperates facts about objects from facts about choices.

But I have an intuition I can't seem to shake. Picture a city rebuilding a pedestrian underpass with a ceiling too low for a tall adult to walk through upright, and the only accessible-looking ramp dead-ends at a locked maintenance door. No one can point to any policy excluding wheelchair users or parents with strollers; the engineers made the choice as a cost compromise. It still feels false to say the underpass itself is neutral and only later uses are political. The exclusion seems to be built into the thing.

I don't want to be told which side wins. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and is there a reading on which both survive? Is the intuition tracking a property of artifacts, or of design processes and stable social functions? And how would I tell the difference in a real case where intent is genuinely ambigious?
