I teach an upper-level formal epistemology seminar and I'm putting together the final. I want one essay question on self-sampling vs self-indication that can't be answered by just restating SSA and SIA from lecture. It should force students to work with the presumptuous philosopher and say whether the total number of observers is the kind of thing their evidence can bear on. Please write the question, a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme that seperates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain.

Exam question (50 minutes; roughly 700 words)

Two cosmological theories, T1 and T2, make exactly the same predictions for everything any observer will ever see: same physics, same history, same data. They differ on one point only. T1 says the universe will contain exactly 10^12 observers in total; T2 says it will contain exactly 10^24. You assign each theory a prior of 0.5 and you have no other relevant evidence.

Sam: SSA gives 50/50. SSA says reason as if you're a random sample of observers in the actual world. Both theories produce observers with the same observations, so your evidence is identical under T1 and T2 and you don't update. SIA, by contrast, tells you to believe T2 just because it has more observers and that makes your existence more probable. That's the presumptuous philosopher's mistake: a sheer increase in head count isn't evidence.

Ira: SSA is the one throwing evidence away. The evidence isn't the bland fact that "some observer exists." The evidence is that I exist, this specific observer with this specific life. If T2 contains a trillion times more observers, it was a trillion times more likely in advance that I'd turn out to be one of the ones who exists. SIA is just ordinary probabilistic reasoning about indexical facts. SSA's neutrality is bought by replacing "I exist" with "someone exists," and that isn't my evidence.

Dana: Both rules are answering a question that shouldn't be asked. T1 and T2 are observationally equivalent, full stop. The number of observers is not something my evidence can discriminate, so my posterior should just equal my prior.

Evaluate. Defend one of these three views, or develop and defend a fourth. Your answer must: (a) state precisely what your preferred rule says about self-locating evidence; (b) identify where at least one rival view goes wrong; and (c) spell out one concrete consequence of your position for either the doomsday argument or the reference class problem.

Model answer

I'll defend a sharpened version of Ira's view. The real question is whether self-locating facts, facts about which observer you are, count as evidence. They do. Once that's granted, Sam's neutrality and Dana's quietism both fail, and the presumptuous conclusion, while uncomfortable, is the price of taking indexical evidence seriously.

The rule I accept is SIA: reason as if you are randomly sampled from the set of all possible observers, weighted by each observer's probability of existing under the hypothesis in question. Formally, the probability that I exist given Ti is proportional to the expected number of observers under Ti. With equal priors, P(T2 | I exist) = 10^24 / (10^12 + 10^24), which is about 1 minus 10^-12. So the fact that I exist overwhelmingly supports T2.

Why isn't this the presumptuous philosopher's absurd mistake? It only looks absurd if you describe the evidence as "some observer exists" rather than "I exist." Sam's SSA treatment asks how likely each theory makes it that some generic observer has my observations. Since both theories are full of such observers, the likelihoods are equal. But that replaces my actual evidence with something strictly weaker. More draws from the space of possible observers raise the chance of this particular draw, namely me. The indexical fact does discriminate.

Dana has a grain of truth: the theories are equivalent with respect to all third-person, non-indexical observations. But they are not equivalent with respect to the observation "I am one of the observers." Denying that this is evidence commits Dana to throwing out all self-locating evidence, including the evidence that drives the doomsday argument. That's a radical restriction on evidence and she gives no argument for it.

There is a real cost, and I accept it. SIA produces a massive shift in favor of T2 on the basis of a bare difference in head count. But the shift is massive because the likelihood ratio is massive: T2 has 10^12 times as many possible observer slots that could have been me. Counterintuitiveness is not a fallacy. If someone wants the posterior to stay at 50/50, they need a prior that cancels the likelihoods, which means they were never treating T1 and T2 as symmetric after all. That's fine, but it's a prior, not an argument against SIA.

Sam and Dana both end up at 50/50 but for opposite reasons, and that matters. Sam needs SSA to be a genuine rule of reasoning; Dana needs the question to be out of bounds. Sam's problem is that SSA doesn't deliver neutrality on its own. SSA requires a reference class, the set of observers I should regard myself as sampled from. If the class is actual observers, you get Sam's result. If the class is possible observers weighted by existence probability, you get SIA. So SSA doesn't settle the dispute; it relocates it into the choice of reference class. Without a principled reason to pick the actual set over the possible set, Sam can't claim SSA as a default.

The concrete consequence I'll flag concerns the doomsday argument. Under SSA, learning that you are roughly the 10^11th human favors a small total human population, because a random sample from a long-lived species shouldn't find itself near the beginning. SIA cancels this. If N is total humans and r is my birth rank, SSA says P(I have rank r | N) is 1/N for N at least r, which favors small N. SIA multiplies this by the fact that a world with N observers is N times more likely to contain me, so the product N times 1/N is independent of N. The doomsday shift disappears. A long-lived species is more likely to contain you in the first place, which exactly offsets the unlikeliness of your early rank. That a single rule kills the doomsday conclusion without gerrymandering is a point in SIA's favor.

Marking scheme (out of 25)

A answer (21-25): States a specific rule precisely enough to calculate with it. Identifies the crux as whether self-locating facts are evidence, not which label to apply. Does the arithmetic under the preferred rule (e.g., a factor of 10^12 for T2 under SIA, or the corresponding claims under SSA or a no-evidence view, stated as likelihoods rather than vibes). Gives a concrete failure in at least one rival: Sam replaces "I exist" with "someone exists"; Dana's observational equivalence applies only to third-person evidence; SSA's result depends on an undefended reference class; Sam and Dana reach the same verdict by different routes and cannot both be right for both reasons. Draws a consequence for the doomsday argument or the reference class problem and uses it as a reason, not as decoration. Acknowledges a cost of the favored view (the presumptuous conclusion, the loss of self-locating evidence, etc.) and absorbs it rather than ignoring it.

B answer (16-20): Correctly states SSA and SIA and correctly says they diverge in this case. Gives a reason for preferring one, such as "SIA is presumptuous" or "SSA is neutral," and does the arithmetic. But it stays at the level of the positions from lecture: it does not isolate the underlying dispute about whether "I exist" is evidence, does not diagnose a rival in a non-question-begging way, and mentions doomsday or reference class only by name without connecting them to the verdict.

C answer (10-15): Restates SSA and SIA but misapplies one of them, e.g., says SSA favors T2 because more observers. Treats the presumptuous philosopher as a refutation of SIA by label alone. Conflates priors and posteriors, or treats equal priors as by themselves decisive.

Errors that cost marks at any level: claiming SSA favors the larger population; saying SSA and SIA give the same answer here; ruling out self-locating evidence with "observationally equivalent" and no argument; wielding the doomsday argument as a knock-down against SSA without stating its premises; writing a general essay on anthropic reasoning without committing to a position on this case.
