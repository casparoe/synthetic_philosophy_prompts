I write about science and ideas for a living, and I'm putting together a piece about why some philosophical arguments never end. My case study is what holds a proposition, or a fact, together — the puzzle philosophers call Bradley's regress. I've read enough to get the rough shape of the two sides, but the debate seems to have been running on the spot for over a century, and I'd like a diagnosis rather then a verdict.

Here's the puzzle as I understand it. Take the fact that the mug is on the table. You seem to need three ingredients: the mug, the table, and the relation being-on. But those three ingredients could all be real, and yet the fact could still fail to obtain — the mug, the table, and the relation could all be there without the mug's actually standing in that relation to the table. So you need something to bind the relation to its two terms. If you posit a second relation, instantiation, to do the binding, you face the same problem one level up: what binds instantiation to the mug, the table, and being-on? And so on without end. The Bradleyan side concludes that relational thought can't ultimately capture reality, and that the copula can't be treated as just another object on the list. The other side — call them the relation realists — says the regress is a paper tiger. Either facts are basic and shouldn't be decomposed into ingredients in the first place, or relating is its own kind of thing that doesn't need a further relation to do its work, or (the Fregean move) the relation was never an object at all but an unsaturated thing that completes itself in its terms.

My working theory is that the debate hasn't moved because it's really three arguments in one coat: a formal argument about whether standard predicate logic can represent the unity of a proposition, a psychological argument about what kind of explanation feels satisfying rather than ad hoc, and a metaphysical argument about whether there are relation-like constituents in the world at all. But I can't tell whether that's right, or whether the two sides just differ at bedrock about what counts as a vicious regress, or whether they're answering seperate questions while sharing a vocabulary.

I'd like you to take the role of a professional metaphysician who works on the unity of the proposition and is comfortable diagnosing the structure of a disagreement rather then just picking a side. Please think carefully: begin by brainstorming three or four candidate diagnoses (a verbal disagreement, a substantive factual issue, a difference about standards of explanation, two different questions under one heading, bedrock starting intuitions, an ill-posed question), weigh them against what each side actually says, and then settle on the one that best explains the stall. Then tell me honestly what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a reframing of the question — or whether no progress should be expected and why. Write the entire answer in British English.
