In the dedication to Leviathan, Thomas Hobbes writes: "I speak not of the men, but (in the Abstract) of the Seat of Power." I have been turning this over as a clue for a view about corporate blame. The view, which I have been calling the seat-of-power theory, is that blaming a company is not a disguised claim about the mental states of its employees, shareholders, or executives. It is a claim directed at the authorized decision structure itself: the roles, procedures, and lines of authority that Hobbes might call an artificial seat of power. When we blame a company for, say, hiding known product defects or funding a disinformation campaign, we are saying that the seat of power was defective, not that any natural person had a guilty mind. I take it this is why a corporation can be sanctioned, or even forced to reorganize, even when no single individual meets the usual conditions for blame.

I am aware this sounds close to Peter French's corporate internal decision structure and maybe to Philip Pettit's group-agent account, and I am also aware it might be just a metaphor dressed up in early modern language. Please act as an expert in collective responsibility and moral philosophy. Start by steelmanning the seat-of-power theory in your own words, then give me the three strongest objections you can find. For each objection, say whether it is fatal or merely uncomfortable, and tell me whether the view is really distinct from French or Pettit, or whether it collapses into a systems-improvement claim that loses what is distinctively moral about blame. Also, say which sort of case it handles worst: a tightly hierarchical company, a decentralized supply chain, or an organization whose harmful act came from a single rogue employee acting against policy.
