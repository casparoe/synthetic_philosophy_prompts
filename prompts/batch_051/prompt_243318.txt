I have been developing a notion I call attentional inflation, and I want an honest assessment of whether it is a real explanatory concept or merely an economic metaphor with a new name.

The idea begins from a parallel with monetary inflation. When the money supply grows faster than the supply of goods and services it can buy, each unit of currency buys less. I want to say that something similar is happening to attention. The supply of engineered claims on attention — notifications, autoplay, infinite scroll, streaks, recommendation feeds optimized for interruption, variable reward schedules — has grown enormously, while the total stock of human attention available in a day is roughly fixed. On my account, attention has therefore been debased: each unit of it buys less.

By "buys less" I mean two things. First, the experiential yield of a minute of ordinary attention has fallen: quiet or unrewarded activities feel flatter than they used to, and people increasingly need background stimulation to get through routine tasks. Second, the baseline intensity required to hold a person's attention has risen, which forces platforms to issue ever more aggressive claims on it; that is an attentional arms race, and the arms race feeds the inflation. The prediction is that unengineered attention loses felt value not because the underlying activities have changed, but because the attentional currency circulating around them has been debased. I take the mechanism to be systemic, not merely individual: even a person who abstains from heavy use enters an environment — interfaces, pacing, social expectations — already calibrated for an inflated baseline.

I am aware of adjacent ideas: the standard observation that information abundance makes attention scarce, the hedonic treadmill, tolerance in addiction, and the literature on dark patterns and persuasive design. My question is whether attentional inflation does any work beyond those. Is there an existing account, under a better name, that already captures whatever is right in it? Treat the idea as charitably as you can: give its strongest formulation, identify where the monetary analogy breaks, and say whether the breaks are cosmetic or fatal. Then give me the strongest objections you can, and end with a verdict on whether the concept is worth keeping.
