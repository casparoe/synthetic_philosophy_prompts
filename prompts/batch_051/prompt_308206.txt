I keep returning to a structural parallel between a decision-theoretic result and a rationale for free expression, and I want to know whether the parallel is deep or superficial.

The first side is Good's theorem on the value of information. For a single expected-utility maximizer with fixed payoffs and priors, a costless signal that arrives before the choice of action can never reduce expected utility. The intuitive proof is that the agent can always ignore the signal and act exactly as she would have without it; conditioning her action on the signal only adds a strategy she previously did not have. The theorem therefore gives formal content to the idea that free information cannot hurt a rational decision-maker.

The second side is the marketplace-of-ideas argument in free speech thought: that open exchange of views improves the community's beliefs and decisions because suppressed ideas are, in effect, information the community is denied; and that the right answer to a bad or peculiar idea is more argument rather than prohibition. Framed this way, censorship looks like a standing commitment to refuse a free signal, and the comparison with Good's theorem seems to supply the formal backbone the marketplace argument has always lacked.

The mapping I have in mind: the agent is an individual reasoner or a polity; speech acts are signals; the choice of action is the decision about what to believe or do; and censorship is the refusal of cost-free information. If the mapping worked, good speech environments would dominate censored ones in the same sense that an informed choice dominates an uninformed one.

But the analogy appears to strain at several points. Good's theorem assumes a single agent with a fixed prior and fixed preferences, updating by conditionalization, and payoff-relevant states unaffected by the signal itself; a public sphere contains many strategically motivated speakers, and speech can itself change preferences, impose direct harms, or capture scarce attention before it can be ignored. The theorem is also a claim about expected utility, not about convergence on truth.

Please evaluate the parallel precisely. Give the strongest mapping under which something like Good's theorem could hold for a community exchanging speech, state the assumptions that would have to be true on both sides for the theorem to transfer, and identify at least one real-world feature of free expression that the transferred theorem would predict or justify but that a sound account of free speech should reject. Is this a genuine identity of structure under the right abstraction, a loose analogy, or a confusion?
