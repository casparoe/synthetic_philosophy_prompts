I am a PhD student in philosophy of religion, writing a dissertation chapter on Molinism, the view that God possesses middle knowledge of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom — knowledge of what any possible free creature would choose in any complete set of circumstances — and that this knowledge reconciles robust divine providence with libertarian freedom. I am collecting objections to middle knowledge itself, not to its various applications, and I already have the following four, which I take to be the standard critical moves. I do not need these repeated.

1. The grounding objection, associated with Adams and Hasker. For a counterfactual such as the claim that Judas would freely betray Christ if placed in certain circumstances, nothing makes the conditional true: the antecedent does not entail the consequent (that is the point of libertarian freedom), the creature does not yet exist to perform the act, and God's decree does not determine the act. The truth of the conditional is left as an ungrounded brute fact.

2. The no-truth-value objection. Because the antecedent does not entail the consequent and no corresponding free act has occurred, such conditionals arguably lack determinate truth values. If the set of true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom is not complete and determinate, there is nothing there for middle knowledge to know.

3. The epistemic-access problem. Even granting that such conditionals have truth values, it is obscure how God could know them. God cannot know them by knowing His own decree (that would collapse into theological determinism), cannot know them by observation of the actual world (the relevant creatures and circumstances are merely possible), and cannot know them as necessary truths. The term "middle knowledge" labels the category without explaining the access.

4. The divine-control objection. Since God does not decide which counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are true — they obtain prior to any actualization — God's choice among worlds is constrained by a body of truth over which He has no control. Critics urge that this yields too little providential control: God can select circumstances but cannot alter the true conditionals that settle how creatures would respond to them.

As far as I can tell, all four of these concede that there is a well-defined fact of the matter about what a merely possible free creature would do, and then attack the ground, truth value, knowability, or providential usefulness of such facts. I would like an objection that does not make that concession: one that targets the coherence or the semantics of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom themselves, or that presses on some other commitment of Molinism that the four listed objections leave untouched. State the objection precisely, and then say what it adds — in particular, which part of the Molinist position it undermines that the four above do not reach. If you think the list is genuinely exhaustive, say so, but then identify which of the four objections is doing the most serious work and which commitment of Molinism remains most exposed.
