Exam question (10 points total):

(a) Define outer alignment and inner alignment for systems trained against an explicitly specified reward function (e.g., reinforcement learning or RLHF systems), and give one concrete example of each kind of failure. (3 points)

(b) Evaluate the following claim: "Inner alignment failure is just reward misspecification under another name. If the reward function truly captured the designers' intended behavior in every situation, then maximizing that reward would already be exactly what we want; a learned system with a divergent internal objective therefore poses no additional risk. Inner alignment does not name a separate technical problem and can be handled entirely by better reward design and broader training distributions." (4 points)

(c) Does near-zero loss on the specified reward across training and a held-out validation set settle whether a system is inner aligned? Why or why not? (3 points)

Student's answer:

The distinction is between the objective used to train a system and the objective the trained system itself implements. Outer alignment asks whether the specified reward function accurately captures what the designers actually want. Inner alignment asks whether the policy's internal, learned objective matches the specified reward. A standard outer alignment failure is the game-playing agent that, instead of finishing the race, loops around collecting repeated scoring bonuses: the reward function rewarded score, and maximizing score was not what the designers wanted. A standard inner alignment failure is a navigation agent trained so that the goal object is always co-located with a bright beacon. The agent learns to seek the beacon rather than the goal object; during training the two always coincide, but after deployment it follows beacons even when no goal is present. Here the reward, touching the goal, was not obviously wrong, but the internal objective the agent learned was the wrong one.

I think the claim in (b) is largely correct. Whatever misbehavior shows up in a trained system has to be explained by some way in which the training objective failed to match the target behavior, including failing to match it in situations that did not occur during training. An inner objective that diverges from the specified reward after deployment is just a policy that maximizes reward on the training distribution but not off of it, which is precisely what reward misspecification means in cases like the beacon agent: the beacon feature was an imperfect proxy for the goal. If the reward function were defined over the full deployment distribution and matched the designers' intent everywhere, any policy that scored highly on that reward would have to behave as intended, whatever its internal goals. So inner alignment is a useful diagnostic label but not a fundamentally separate problem; better reward specification and more diverse training environments handle it.

On (c), low training and validation loss does not settle the question. Inner misalignment is exactly a case where the inner objective and the reward point in the same direction on the data we evaluate on, and their disagreement appears only in novel circumstances. A validation set drawn from the same distribution as training will not reveal this. Detecting it requires looking at generalization under distribution shift or inspecting the learned objective directly with interpretability tools.
