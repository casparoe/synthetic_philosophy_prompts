I sit on the small-grants committee of a foundation that funds field investigations of reports of apparently parapsychological phenomena—apparitions, precognitive dreams, spontaneous psychokinetic events, mediumistic communications, and the like. We receive dozens of reports every month, many from sincere and articulate witnesses, but we fund only about four investigations a year. The director wants a written triage procedure that intake staff can apply to decide which reports go to the committee for possible funding, and which are declined without further review. The procedure should be usable by non-philosophers: a sequence of questions in order, criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, points where the procedure stops with a no, and points where the decision is handed to a person because the question is one of judgment rather than rule-following.

The procedure must be grounded in the methodological position Henry Sidgwick worked out in his 1882 presidential address to the Society for Psychical Research, not in generic debunking or generic open-mindedness. In that address Sidgwick argued, among other things, that the bare fact that a report conflicts with the established order of nature is not a sufficient reason to dismiss it; whether any such phenomena occur is a factual question, and factual questions are settled by examining reports under conditions designed to exclude chance coincidence, malobservation, illusion, and fraud. But he also insisted that the uncritical accumulation of weak testimony cannot amount to strong evidence, and that an inquirer must not begin with a predetermined skeptical or credulous conclusion. I want you to translate that position into a procedure.

The procedure should handle these difficulties honestly:

- How to weigh the prior plausibility of a report without letting it settle the matter in advance.
- What counts as evidence that the witness had a real experience as opposed to merely a belief about an experience.
- How to test whether an apparent anomaly could be explained by chance coincidence, selective memory, poor observation, deliberate fraud, or known psychological mechanisms.
- When it is appropriate to say that a report, even if sincere, is too weak to justify spending one of our four investigation slots.
- Where the procedure itself will break down or produce arbitrary judgments unless a person steps in.

Then run the procedure on three sample reports:

1. A single adult witness reports a detailed dream in which a distant relative died in a specific way; the death occurred within the same hour and matched the dream in several particulars, and the witness wrote the dream down before learning of the death.
2. A household reports repeated night-time noises and small objects displaced in a house with multiple residents, including a teenager and a recently bereaved adult.
3. A professional medium claims to relay verifiable private facts about deceased relatives of a sitter, supported by signed testimonials from several clients but no independent controls.

At least one of the three should be a case where the procedure yields a no that the committee will find emotionally or institutionally unwelcome. Conclude with a paragraph on what the procedure cannot do and where Sidgwick's own position leaves the foundation without a mechanical rule.

Keep the response to no more than 1,200 words.
