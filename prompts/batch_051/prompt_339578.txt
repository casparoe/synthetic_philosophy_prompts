I'm drafting a short position statement for a graduate seminar in epistemology, and I'd like a consistency check before I circulate it. The seminar has mostly been about whether the standards for knowledge shift with context, and we've spent several weeks on DeRose's bank cases, Cohen's airport case, and Stanley's interest-relative invariantism. Here is roughly where I've landed: (1) Knowledge ascriptions are context-sensitive: whether 'S knows that p' is true can vary with the conversational context even while S's evidence, belief, and the truth of p stay fixed. (2) Whether S knows p, in the underlying non-linguistic sense, depends only on truth, belief, and evidence; practical stakes never make a difference to whether the knowledge relation obtains. (3) The correct treatment of high-stakes cases is to say that the conversational context raises the epistemic standard: that is why the subject should not claim to know, not because the subject has lost any knowledge. (4) There is nonetheless a single epistemic relation picked out by the word 'knows'; contextualism is a view about the word and its content, not about there being different knowledge relations for different purposes. (5) I reject subject-sensitive invariantism because it lets the subject's practical situation infect whether they know, and that seems to mix the theoretical and the practical in the wrong way. Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be; then tell me whether there is a single revision that would clean up the most.
