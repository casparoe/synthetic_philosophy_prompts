I've been reading Nāgārjuna and have arrived at a reading I want pressure-tested. I call it 'spent presupposition.' The tetralemma's four alternatives — P, not-P, both, and neither — are not, I think, a four-valued logic; each is an attempt to say how a thing stands on its own, and each borrows in advance the very intrinsic nature (svabhāva) it then either attributes or denies. Emptiness, on this reading, is not a property of things or a higher-order fact about them; it is what shows up when a claim has exhausted a presupposition no phenomenon could satisfy. That is why the fourth alternative ('neither P nor not-P') is not dialetheism, a truth-value gap, or mere uncertainty: it is where the indispensable presupposition finally becomes visible as unsatisfiable. It also lets Nāgārjuna dodge the self-refutation charge, because his rejections do not add a fifth position — they register the default on a loan every positive and negative formulation has already taken out. Is this coherent, or does it collapse into ordinary presupposition failure of the sort Strawson made familiar — sentences that end up neither true nor false? If a distinct idea survives, what is its strongest form and what does it get right? And where does it break: does it turn Madhyamaka into a doctrine about language rather than about things, or reduce it to quietism? Please respond in American English.
