I am a retired natural scientist who has recently begun reading Plato's Laws, and I find myself returning to Book One, where the Athenian Stranger defends the supervised use of wine as a test of character. The passage that most interests me, in R.G. Bury's translation, is the description of what wine does:

"he becomes filled with high hopes and a sense of power, till finally, puffed up with conceit, he abounds in every kind of licence of speech and action and every kind of audacity, without a scruple as to what he says or what he does."

I want to think carefully about what follows from this. If Plato is right that intoxication chiefly removes the habituated restraints that normally keep preexisting dispositions in check, rather than manufacturing genuinely new ones, what are the most important implications for how a secular society should think about alcohol-related wrongdoing? In particular, what are the implications for the contrast between excusing bad behavior on grounds of intoxication and treating the behavior as evidence about the wrongdoer's stable character? Please give the implications that bear most directly on how one should structure alcohol policy in a workplace or university setting, and include the strongest objection to drawing policy conclusions from Plato's picture in this way.
