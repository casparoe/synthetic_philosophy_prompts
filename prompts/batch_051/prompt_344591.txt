I've been going back and forth with a housemate about direct democracy, specifically how California's initiative process compares to the Swiss device where parliament can put its own counter-proposal on the ballot alongside a citizens' initiative, and voters can vote yes on both, with a tiebreaker question deciding which one actually wins if both pass. We ended up designing a frankenstein version for California and now I want someone to stress-test it.

Here's the scheme. When a citizen initiative qualifies for the ballot, each house of the legislature has 60 days to pass a rival measure on the same subject, by simple majority, no governor signature needed; if the two houses can't agree on a rival text, no rival appears. The ballot then asks three questions: yes/no on the initiative, yes/no on the rival, and a tiebreaker question, counted only if both measures pass, asking which of the two should become law. A measure passes only if it gets a majority of the votes cast on its own question and its total yes votes equal at least 30% of all registered voters in the state, so skipping that item or staying home counts against clearing the floor. If exactly one measure clears that bar, it becomes law. If both clear it, the tiebreaker picks the winner. There's one asymetric twist: a rival that wins becomes an ordinary statute the legislature can amend later by majority vote, but an initiative that wins is locked for ten years and can only be changed by another ballot measure.

So that's the mechanism. Which actors would start using the initiative mostly as a bargaining chip with the legislature, and which would rather back the legislative rival from the start? How do campaigns spend and how do voters use their two yes votes, especially once people realize a yes on the weaker version can hurt the stronger one? How does the 30%-of-registered-voters floor change opponent strategy, and is that a bug or a feature? Where does the whole thing get captured first? I'd rather you map the incentives and failure modes than hear whether it's a good reform.
