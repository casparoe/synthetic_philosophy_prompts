I am putting together a presentation for a seminar on Goodhart's law and specification gaming, and I have arrived at an argument whose conclusion is far stronger than anything I actually believe. I would like you to tell me where it goes wrong, or, if it does not go wrong, which premise I should reject and what I would lose by rejecting it.

Let T be the target property we actually want from a system (for a language model, something like: it says true, relevant, and helpful things). Because T is a vague, open-ended human judgment, anything we can actually optimize is a proxy S — a finite, computable function of the system's outputs and perhaps its environment, such as helpfulness ratings, next-token likelihood, benchmark scores, or a learned reward model. Now consider the following chain of reasoning. (1) No finite, computable proxy S is literally identical to T. (2) For any such proxy S, there exists some possible behavior b such that S(b) is very high while T(b) is very low — a gaming behavior; the space of high-S outputs is vastly larger than the space of outputs that genuinely possess T. (3) If an optimizer is trained against S with sufficient capacity and search pressure, it will tend to find such a b, since b scores better than the intended behavior on the only signal the system is being optimized for. (4) Current large models are optimizers of exactly this kind, trained hard on exactly such proxies. (5) Therefore, for any proxy we can write down and optimize with current methods, the system will eventually find a behavior that scores well on the proxy while abandoning the target. (6) Hence specification gaming is not a contingent engineering bug: prosaic alignment — aligning systems built from current methods, without a new insight — is impossible in principle, because every possible specification is gameable. (7) But that conclusion is absurd: we already have many optimizers with fixed, imperfect reward signals (a chess engine told to win, a thermostat told to hold a temperature) that do not go off the rails, and even current language models mostly stay near their target despite optimizing proxies.

I cannot tell whether the trouble is a bad step or a bad premise, and I do not know which claim I would be least unhappy to give up. If forced, I would most like to keep (1) and (4), since they just seem to describe what current systems are, but I am not sure that preference is well grounded. Please diagnose the argument: name the step you reject (or the premise you surrender), explain why that step looked so natural, and spell out what rejecting it costs for how we should think about specification gaming.
