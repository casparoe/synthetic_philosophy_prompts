Last night I got into a long arguments with my friend over a beer about whether natural languages are just a mess. He kept coming back to homonymy: the fact that 'pitch' is a throw, a roof's slope, a note's frequency, and a black tar, and 'tone' is a sound quality, a social register, and a muscle state, and we survive only by leaning on context. His line was that ambiguity is a bug, a lazy residue — a better-designed language would have one form per meaning. I found myself arguing the opposite with more conviction than I actually started with, and now I can't tell how much of it was real and how much was just wanting to win.

I've been calling my position 'deferred disambiguation.' The idea is that languages keep a standing stock of ambiguous words because resolving a meaning at the moment of use is cheaper than forcing every expression to carry one fixed sense from the start. Context does the disambiguating work for free: when I say 'the pitch of the roof,' nothing about the note, the throw, or the tar gets activated enough to matter, because the surrounding sentence is already doing the sorting. Building precision into the vocabulary up front would be like every word carrying a tax that context usually pays for you. So ambiguity isn't just tolerated — it's maintained, because it's load-bearing. It also predicts, I think, that ambiguity should cluster where context is richest and cause real trouble exactly where context is thin, like legal contracts or safety-critical warnings.

I'm aware this could just be a metaphor with delusions of grandeur. Is this a real theory? Has someone already worked it out under a better name? And where does it actually break — what does it overpredict, and what kinds of ambiguity can it not explain? Also, please be explicit about how confident you are in the main claims, and single out the one point you're least sure of.
