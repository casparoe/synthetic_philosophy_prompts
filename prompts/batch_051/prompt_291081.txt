I was diagnosed with adult ADHD last spring, and the whole thing has been harder to think about than I expected. A friend of mine is doing a philosophy degree and has been reading Ian Hacking's stuff about looping effects, and he keeps insisting my diagnosis is less like discovering a broken bone and more like being drafted into a social role. I tried to write out his argument in numbered steps, because I want to see if it actually goes through:

(1) A condition is a real feature of a person only if it would be there, in more or less the same form, even if nobody had a name or diagnostic category for it.

(2) But when someone gets a psychiatric label, the label enters their self-understanding and changes what they do: they reinterpret their past in its vocabulary, seek out treatment and communities organized around it, ask for accomodations, and explain themselves to others in its terms.

(3) Those changes feed back into what clinicians mean by the category, since the criteria get revised partly in response to how classified people behave and how the diagnosis is lived — and that loops back into how the next generation of diagnosed people sees itself.

(4) So for psychiatric kinds, the phenomenon as it presently exists depends on the ongoing activity of classifying; it would not be there in anything like its present form without the loop.

(5) Therefore the diagnosis is not picking out a real condition the way a viral infection or a broken bone is. At best it is an interactive kind, at worst it is made up.

Step (2) just seems like a description of what a diagnosis does, and step (3) is meant to be the empirical bit, so I can't tell whether step (1) is secretly doing all the work. I also can't tell whether step (4) really entitles him to step (5), or whether there is a shift in what counts as being there. And honestly I might just be resisting the conclusion because it is my own diagnosis. Is the argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and what would the strongest corrected version of that step say?
