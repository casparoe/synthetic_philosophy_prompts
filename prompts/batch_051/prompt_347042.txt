I'm in a grad seminar on formal epistemology, and we've spend a couple of weeks on diachronic coherence. One idea has gotten under my skin: van Fraassen's Reflection principle. As I understand it, the principle says a rational person's current credence in a proposition should line up with the credences she expects to have later. If I'm certain now that tomorrow I'll be 90% confident the defendant is guilty, then I should already be 90% confident today — or, more carefully, my current probability should be a weighted average of the probabilities I think I might assign later, conditional on my actually assigning them. The diachronic Dutch book argument makes it look like a requirement of rationality, not just a neat heuristic, because violating it supposedly leaves you open to a sure-loss series of bets across time.

I'm provisionally on board, but I want to know what would genuinely count against it, because I keep running into cases that feel like trouble: taking a drug that will scramble my memory tonight, getting drunk, deciding ahead of time that I'll ignore evidence, or deferring to a future self I already know will be biased. Are these real counterexamples, and if so, how deep do they cut? Which ones would a defender of Reflection concede as counting against the principle, and which would they dismiss as changing the subject? I'd like the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward. I'd also like an honest answer to whether the principle has been qualified so much that it can't be falsified — and whether that insulation is a problem in a distinctive way for a norm of rationality, as opposed to an ordinary empirical claim. The thing I'd personally find most persuasive is a case where Reflection seems to demand updating without any evidence at all, not just a case where it stays silent.
