Here is an argument I have never been able to diagnose, and it seems to prove too much. (1) Chalcedonian Christology holds that Christ is one person — a single subject of thought and action — in two complete natures, divine and human. (2) So one and the same subject would be omniscient and not omniscient (as the divine Word he knows all things, while the Gospels present him as ignorant of the time of the last day); impassible and passible; immortal and mortal. (3) If a coherent position can allow a single subject to be F and not-F in this way, it has suspended either the law of non-contradiction or the indiscernibility of identicals for that subject. (4) The standard repair is to relativize the predicates: the one subject is omniscient-qua-divine and ignorant-qua-human. But this device is portable — any contradiction whatever can be defused by positing two aspects or respects and refusing to say the thing is F or not-F without qualification. (5) A move that can neutralize every counterexample is a blank check, not an explanation; so the qua move cannot be confined to Christology. (6) Therefore the Incarnation, understood as literally true rather than as metaphor, either asserts a true contradiction or licenses a dissolves-anything device. I want a committed diagnosis, not a survey: which step is the weak one, and what exactly does it cost to give it up? Can the qua move be disciplined so that it does genuine metaphysical work without becoming a universal solvent, or is that hope itself the step that should be abandoned?
