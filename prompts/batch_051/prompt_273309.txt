An asymmetry I keep noticing in how people do history. If someone writes about ancient medicine or early modern physics by sifting past thinkers for how close they got to what we now believe—credit for anticipating germs, marks off for believing in phlogiston—that gets written off as Whig history and nobody really argues about it. But the same people will happilly write the history of abolition or women's suffrage as a story that leads to us: the point of the story is that the movement made things better, and past actors get judged mainly by how far along the road to us they saw. A historian who insisted on writing the suffrage movement on it's own terms and refused to structure it as progress would be read as making a point rather than doing her job; the same insistence in the history of physics is exactly what separates careful scholars from popularizers. So why is progress-toward-the-present a sin in one kind of history and the default, almost compulsory framing in the other?

I want the strongest candidate principle you can formulate to justify treating the two subject matters differently, stated crisply. Then beat it up with a matched pair of cases—one scientific and one moral-political—chosen to be as similar as possible, so the principle has to do the work rather than the choice of example. Tell me honestly whether the asymmetry survives, and if not, which side you'd keep and which you'd reverse.
