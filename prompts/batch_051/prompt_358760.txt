In The Ethics of Belief, William Kingdon Clifford writes: "The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him."

Now consider a development Clifford did not anticipate. A loan officer must decide whether an applicant will repay, and the belief that guides her decision is supplied by an opaque credit-scoring model: its predictions are well calibrated on historical data, but the evidential basis for any particular score cannot be recovered by the officer, or even by the model's builders. The officer checks the model's aggregate error rate and believes the score on that basis. Suppose the prediction about this applicant turns out true; on Clifford's view, that should not settle the moral question. Does Clifford's argument survive this case? On his criterion, has the officer acquired the belief in a way that entitles her to it, or has she believed on evidence before her that she had no right to treat as sufficient? If the criterion condemns her, who bears the wrong: the officer, the people who built and deployed the model, or no one? If it acquits her, does the wrong move or simply disappear? Identify which premise the opacity of the system puts pressure on, and say whether Clifford's criterion can be restated so that it reaches the case while keeping its core claim that what matters is the origin of the belief, not its truth.
