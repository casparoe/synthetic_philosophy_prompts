Give me a working definition of integrity—the kind Bernard Williams's critique of utilitarianism is trying to protect—that gets the following cases right. By integrity I do not mean mere honesty or keeping one's word; I mean something closer to the way a wholehearted person's choices flow from commitments, attachments, and projects that are deeply their own, rather than from a standing readiness to revise or override them whenever an impartial calculation suggests something else. Here are the cases:

1. A sculptor turns down a highly lucrative commission that would require her to work in a style she regards as empty, and instead spends two years on a demanding project she genuinely believes in.
2. A man must choose which of two people to rescue from a river, and one of them is his wife. He saves her without first considering what an impartial agent would have done, and only later realizes he never did.
3. The same man, in the same situation, pauses before acting to make sure that saving his wife would be at least as justifiable, from an impartial standpoint, as saving the stranger, and only then saves her.
4. A lifelong pacifist in a besieged community takes up arms because an impartial calculation tells him more lives will be saved if he fights, even though doing so goes against what he has always been.
5. A person who has campaigned for a cause for years drops it within a month of finding that it costs him friendships, and says he never really believed in it anyway.
6. An activist refuses a compromise that would secure nearly all her movement's aims, because she regards the one omitted plank as the distinctive thing the movement stands for, even though refusing may lose everything.

I am fairly confident that 1 and 2 are cases of integrity and that 5 is not. I do not know what to say about 3, 4, and 6. Propose the definition, run it through all six cases, and say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer at all. Then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts.
