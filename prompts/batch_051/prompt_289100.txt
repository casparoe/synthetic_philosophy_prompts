I have been trying to work out how we decide whether a claim about hidden coordination and deception should be taken seriously or dismissed as a conspiracy theory, and I keep returning to the lead-up to the 2003 invasion of Iraq as a test case. Two rival stories were in play, and both had the same underlying shape. The official story, advanced by the United States and British governments, was that Saddam Hussein's regime was hiding an active programme of weapons of mass destruction and deceiving the weapons inspectors. The dissenting story, widely treated as a conspiracy theory, was that officials were overstating the intelligence to build public support for a war. After the invasion, no stockpiles of the kind alleged were found, and later inquiries concluded that the intelligence had been presented with more certainty than the underlying material justified.

Please analyse this episode through the lens of a distinction I have been thinking about. One view says there is something about the very form of a conspiracy explanation that makes it epistemically suspect—secrecy, hidden motive, and the way such explanations reinterpret every inconvenient fact as further evidence of the cover-up—so that any explanation with that form should carry a heavier burden of proof. The other view says this is prejudice by category: the form of an explanation tells you nothing about whether it is true, so the only defensible approach is to judge each explanation on its evidence, no matter who is advancing it or how it sounds.

I am not asking for a history lesson, and the answer should not depend on disputed details. Use only the facts I have supplied. What does the judge-by-form view explain, and what does it misdescribe, about the way the two stories were treated before the war? In particular, why did one count as an intelligence assessment while the other counted as a conspiracy theory, even though both rested on claims about concealment and deception? And what does that asymmetry show about the judge-by-form view—does it confirm that we need extra caution before accepting stories of hidden coordination, or does it suggest that the extra caution tends to be aimed wherever the stigma has already been attached?
