Consider an ordinary asymmetry about what inheritance does and does not transmit. In cases within living memory, we treat inheritance as strong enough to carry a wrong forward. If someone steals a painting and dies, and the painting passes to the thief's innocent child, ordinary judgment says the victim's family may still claim it, or may follow the stolen thing and its traceable proceeds into the heir's hands; the innocence of the heir does not launder the theft. The same holds for an embezzled fortune: we would not say that the wrongdoer's heirs may quietly keep the stolen portion simply because they personally did nothing wrong. But when the wrong lies several generations back — land taken by force, labor extracted under slavery, wealth built on a dispossession — many of the same people treat the matter differently. The present-day descendants of the wrongdoers are regarded as blameless owners of what they inherited, and the present-day descendants of the victims are told that the claim expired with the original wrong. So inheritance appears strong enough to carry the benefit of a wrong across many generations, but too weak to carry the liability across those same generations.

Is there a principle that generates both judgments, or is the difference only the psychological distance of time and unfamiliar faces?
