I've somehow ended up on a committee that's trying to kill off a daft unwritten convention in my profession (roughly: junior people are expected to work through the weekend and pretend they don't), and it's made me realise I've accumulated a whole set of views about how norms actually die that I've never checked against one another. I'd be grateful for an audit rather than a lecture. Here's where I've landed.

(1) I'm interests-first about this stuff. Big structural practices – think slavery or the slave trade – survive exactly as long as they serve the powerful, and they fall when they stop serving them. The moral arguments that had been lying around for decades then get the credit after the fact, for a change that was going to happen anyway. This is basically what people call the Williams thesis.

(2) But I'm also quite persuaded by the anti-Williams evidence: the British slave trade was still booming and profitable when it was abolished in 1807, and I do think the mass petitions, the sermons, and the explicitly moral arguments did actual causal work there. I can't see how you get that case out of the interest story alone.

(3) On duelling, by contrast, I'm an honour-story person all the way: it ended in Britain because it became faintly absurd in the circles that mattered, not because someone finally won the argument about the sanctity of life. Those arguments had been around for two centures and had done roughly nothing.

(4) Footbinding is, for me, the purest case of the same mechanism: it collapsed once it got redefined as a mark of national backwardness, rather than because a feminist argument triumphed on the merits.

(5) So the position I've drifted into is a division of labour: economic practices die by interest, status practices die by honour, and explicit moral argument is mostly a lagging indicator in both kinds of case.

(6) But when I'm actually advising people about our stupid little workplace norm, I catch myself saying something stronger: making a norm unprofitable or ridiculous is the only thing that ever works, and arguing about the principle is basically useless.

(7) And then I also want to say that honour just is a kind of interest – an interest in being well-regarded – so putting "interest" and "honour" against each other is a false opposition.

I honestly can't tell which of these are in real tension, which only look like they are, and which I'd need to revise first. Could you go through them, rank the tensions by severity, say what exactly would have to give in each case, and then tell me whether there's a single revision that would clean up the most with the least damage?
