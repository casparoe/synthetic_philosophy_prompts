I was prepping a lecture on interpretation the other day and realized I don't have a clean answer to a split I keep seeing. In any discussion of biblical symbolism, people will say without hesitation that seven is completeness, forty is a period of testing or preparation, twelve is the covenant people—and nobody finds this embarrassing. But the moment someone points out that the 153 fish in John 21 is the 17th triangular number, or that 666 encodes Nero by gematria, a lot of the same people roll their eyes and call it numerology. So we have an approved category (symbolic numbers: 7, 40, 12) and a disapproved one (pattern-hunting: 153, 666, sums of letters, multiples and reversals), and I want to know whether the line between them can be justified, or whether it's just a way of sorting numbers into ones we've already agreed to let in. Give the strongest candidate principle you can find that separates interpretation from pattern-hunting. Is it that the approved numbers are structured into the narratives themselves (seven days of creation, forty years in the wilderness, twelve tribes and twelve apostles) while the disapproved ones are read off afterward? That the approved meanings are stable across contexts? That they're simple enough to be seen rather than derived? That they come from the stories rather than from arithmetic on the page? Then stress-test your principle against the hard cases: 666 is in the text and practically begs to be decoded, but plenty of people still treat decoding it as numerology; 153 has a genuine mathematical property but the text gives no hint the author cared; and 40 may just be a round number for a generation. At the end I want a honest verdict: is there a real distinction here, or is 'symbolism vs numerology' just a polite name for a sociological fact about whose readings got canonized?
