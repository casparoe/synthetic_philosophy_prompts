I am preparing a philosophy department colloquium defending the thesis that intuitive judgments about particular hypothetical cases carry little or no independent evidential weight in philosophy, and that the real evidential work is done by fit with general principles plus theoretical virtues such as transitivity, simplicity, and explanatory coherence. On my view, case intuitions are revisable, defeasible guesses rather than fixed data to be accommodated. The audience will be mainstream analytic philosophers, mostly moral philosophers and epistemologists trained in the method of cases, along with two experimental philosophers who will press the empirical instability of case judgments. I want a Q&A preparation sheet, not a restatement of the view: the objections I will actually get, in roughly their likely order and phrased as this audience would put them, each with an assessment of how much damage it does, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and a short reply. Also identify the one objection I should most hope nobody raises, and the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Conclude with a one-sentence summary of the overall Q&A strategy, formatted as \answer{...}.
