Below is the opening of John Maynard Keynes’s 1930 essay *Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren*:

We are suffering just now from a bad attack of economic pessimism. It is common to hear people say that the epoch of enormous economic progress which characterised the nineteenth century is over; that the rapid improvement in the standard of life is now going to slow down – at any rate in Great Britain; that a decline in prosperity is more likely than an improvement in the decade which lies ahead of us.
I believe that this is a wildly mistaken interpretation of what is happening to us.

Make an argument for the following position: this passage is not best read as a piece of short-term economic forecasting, but as the opening move in an argument about how to live well once the pressure of economic necessity has been reduced. In defending that position, you should consider what exactly the phrase “the standard of life” is doing in the passage; whether Keynes’s diagnosis would differ if the relevant object were not an improved standard of life but a better form of life; and whether the passage invites a purely economic test of its claims or an essentially moral one. Argue for the reading you take to be most defensible, state explicitly what could make you abandon it, and distinguish your reading from the view that Keynes is merely correcting a mistaken growth forecast.
