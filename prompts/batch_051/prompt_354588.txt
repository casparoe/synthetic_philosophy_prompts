A friend of mine works in machine learning, and he has become convinced that the no-free-lunch theorems settle the problem of induction in a way that philosophers have mostly missed. He walked me through the following argument, and although every step seems plausible to me, the conclusion seems impossible to accept.

(1) An inductive rule is rationally justified only if it can be shown, before we adopt it, to be at least as reliable as any rival rule.
(2) The no-free-lunch theorems show that when performance is averaged uniformly over all possible data-generating environments, every learning algorithm has exactly the same expected performance as every other.
(3) So no inductive rule can be shown, before we adopt it, to be more reliable than any rival: for every environment in which a rule does well, there is another in which it does correspondingly badly, and the averaging in (2) counts all of them equally.
(4) Therefore no inductive rule is rationally justified. Induction is not merely fallible; it is groundless.
(5) Yet some inductions plainly are better supported than others. Preferring a smooth curve to a wildly oscillating curve that fits the same past data is rational, and expecting the sun to rise tomorrow is rationally preferable to expecting it not to rise, even though both are consistent with all past observations.

I most want to keep (2), because it is a mathematical result, and (5), because giving it up would amount to global skepticism about ordinary and scientific learning. Which step should I reject, and what does that rejection cost? In particular, what should rational justification of an inductive rule amount to if it is not the guarantee demanded by (1), and would that alternative notion still explain why the smooth curve is rationally preferable to the gerrymandered one?
