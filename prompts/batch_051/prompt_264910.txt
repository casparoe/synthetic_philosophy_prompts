At a dinner last week two friends of mine got into it over whether the 'man of his time' defence actually works, and I spent the whole argument wishing someone would just lay out the moves, because I couldn't referee it and I still cant now. The test cases on the table were Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus. Here's the exchange as best I remember it.

(1) Maya opened by saying the defence is usually bad history before it's even bad ethics. The men we trot it out for were not surrounded by unanimous agreement. Jefferson wrote about slavery as a moral evil and a threat to his country while continuing to enslave people; Churchill's empire talk was challenged by plenty of his own contemporaries; and Columbus was so brutal that the Spanish authorities themselves eventually hauled him back in chains. So saying 'they couldn't have known better' is often just false.

(2) Eli said Maya was attacking a strawman. The point isn't that they literally couldn't have known. It's that a person's social world sets the baseline for what it's reasonable to expect them to overcome. Condemning an 1800 slaveholder is easy for us because it costs us nothing; for him, giving slavery up meant losing wealth, status, and the respect of everyone he knew. Context doesn't make the act right, but it should make us less confident we'd have done better, and less eager to write off a whole life.

(3) Maya answered that this is a much weaker claim than the one people actually use. If all the defence amounts to is 'context can mitigate blame somewhat,' fine — but then it can't do the job it's asked to do, which is to say Jefferson should stay on the pedestal because he was a man of his time. Mitigation isn't exoneration, and the size of the mitigation is exactly what's at issue.

(4) Eli came back with the future-judgment point. If she insists on judging 18th-century figures by today's standards, she has to accept that people in 2300 will judge us by theirs — for factory farming, say, or consumer waste, or whatever they'll find unforgivable that we currently find normal. None of us can say which of our settled practices will look monstrous. So there's a reason for historical humility that her side doesn't have a good place for.

(5) Maya replied that this doesn't scare her: the future might be right about us, and our descendants condemning us wouldn't be a category mistake. Humility ought to mean accepting that we're probably wrong about something we can't yet see, not suspending judgement about what we can already see. And saying 'what these men did was wrong and they bear responsibility' is consistent with saying they also did great things; the defence tends to get used to block the first part, not to add the second.

(6) Eli's last move was a partial concession. He admitted the strong version — 'they can't be judged at all' — is hard to defend. But he said the defence still tracks something real if you apply it case by case: there's a difference between violating the norms of your own time (Columbus, whom contemporaries protested) and failing to transcend them (Jefferson, who saw the contradiction and couldn't live it). So 'man of his time' does real work for ordinary people and for telling those two cases apart, even if it gets abused for hagiography.

I'd like you to adjudicate this move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and then say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
