At my company the annual review has a step I can't get my head around. Every employee has to fill out a long self-evaluation first: you rate yourself on a bunch of competencies, list your biggest wins and contributions, and describe where you fell short over the year. Then your manager and three peers fill out the same grid about you, and in the calibration meeting the manager will often say the self-ratings are unreliable because people systematically overestimate themselves — apperently that's just accepted as fact. But the same process also gives you a rating on something like self-awareness or accurate self-assesment, and a manager has to judge that mostly from how far your self-eval matches everyone else's. That seems to require you to have genuine access to your own skills and motives, at least enough to report them under friendly conditions, while at the same time the process treats you as a biased witness to yourself.

So what model of introspection does this practice actually presuppose, if any? Does it assume self-knowledge is a real perception-like faculty that is just noisy, or that people only have reliable access to some things — say, what they did, but not how well they did it — or is the self-eval not really treated as evidence at all but as something else, like a commitment device, a ritual, or a negotiation opener? Is the practice internally consistent, and would it have to change much if the view that introspection is radically unreliable turned out to be true?
