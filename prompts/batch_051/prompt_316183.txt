I’ve got a four-year-old, and over Christmas I found myself answering endless questions about how Father Christmas gets into the house without a chimney, whether the tooth fairy can find us if we move, that sort of thing. It’s clearly lying, but it’s lying that feels almost like an institution of early childhood rather than an exception. At the same time I’ve been re-reading Kant’s On a Supposed Right to Lie from Benevolent Motives (1797). As I remember the essay, Kant’s argument is roughly this: truthfulness is a strict, unconditional duty, because if you allow a lie when it seems likely to produce a good result, you make the very source of right depend on your private calculation of consequences; and if your calculation turns out badly, then you bear responsibility for the result, whereas the truthful person doesn’t. He’s thinking mainly about the case of lying to a murderer at the door to save a friend, but the principle looks general enough to cover benevolent lies to children as well.

Now the white-lie-to-children case doesn’t fit the murderer-at-the-door model neatly. The lies about Santa and the tooth fairy are not one-off emergencies; they’re repeated, socially sanctioned, developmentally expected, and widely thought to do something good — not just give the child a belief, but create a temporary, shared world of magic that the child is supposed to grow out of. The child often isn’t angry when they find out, and some people say the discovery teaches something about trust: that adults can mislead you playfully or generously and still be reliable in ways that matter. If that’s right, then Kant’s argument is under pressure in a way he never considered, because the deception is aimed at someone who is not yet being treated as a fully competent moral agent.

Does Kant’s argument survive this development? Go through the argument carefully, identify which premise is actually threatened by the Santa case, and tell me whether the absolute ban on lying can be repaired into a weaker but still important claim — for instance, that truthfulness is owed to persons as competent reasoners, but that children occupy a different status — or whether the case shows that the argument was overbroad from the start. I’m less interested in the usual “what if the murderer is at the door” debate and more interested in whether lying to children from benevolent motives is a different kind of case entirely.
