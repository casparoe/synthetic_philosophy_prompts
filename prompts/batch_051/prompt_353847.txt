Last week, in a seminar on philosophy of language, two of my classmates got into an argument none of us could settle, and I've been turning it over since. The question was whether a language could, in principle, lack pronouns and indexicals — words and morphemes like "I," "you," "here," "now," "this," and "tomorrow," whose reference shifts with the context of utterance. One of them, Dev, argued it clearly could. Imagine, he said, a community that speaks only in proper names and eternal descriptions keyed to a public coordinate grid and a standard calendar: instead of "I'm hungry," they say something like "Dev at node P-7 at noonplus-0 has an empty stomach third-degree"; instead of "Meet me here tomorrow," they say "At node P-8 at noonplus-1, Dev's body and Ana's body will be present." Dev's claim was that indexicals are only a compression device, and that any proposition expressible with them can be expressed, at least in principle, by sufficiently specific descriptions and names — so nothing essential is lost.

Ana disagreed on two fronts. First, she said the coordinate frame itself has to be anchored: to introduce the grid and calendar, the community must have done something like fixing an origin and a starting point in a deictic act, and later users must still find themselves in the frame. Dev answered that establishing a frame requires only pointing and naming, not indexicals, and that in any case a point about how a language is introduced is not a point about what it can express. Ana then pressed a sharper objection: even if the frame is public and stable, a speaker who merely knows "Dev at node P-7 now has an empty stomach" isn't thereby moved to eat unless they also grasp "I am Dev, at node P-7, now." That self-locating thought is exactly what Dev's purified language cannot say, yet it seems required for any speaker to use the language as a language — to know which name is theirs, which node they occupy, and which time is the present one. Dev replied that she was now smuggling psychology into semantics: the language states all the facts; that its users need an extra-linguistic self-locating belief in order to act is no more an objection to the language than the fact that drivers need to know which car is theirs is an objection to the statement "the car at spot 5 needs fuel." Ana answered that if every actual use of the language depends on indexical cognition the language refuses to represent, then what Dev has described is at best a notation, not a language anyone could speak — and the original question, she said, was about a speakable language. By the end, they were also accusing each other of blurring the distinction between pronouns as a word class and indexical reference as a function, since pronouns can be non-indexical and indexicality can occur without pronouns, but neither would budge.

Please adjudicate this as though you were hearing both sides: who is right about which specific points, where does each argument overreach, and can the original question be disambiguated in a way that would let them converge on an answer?
