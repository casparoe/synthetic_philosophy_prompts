I'm a PhD student in an NLP/interpretability group, and I keep having the same argument with my advisor about how we talk about model failures. In every paper draft and incident report, false outputs get called hallucinations — invented citations, made-up dates, fake people — and reviewers correct us if we call them lies or bullshit. The standard response is to add a disclaimer warning that outputs can be inaccurate, maybe plus some retrieval, and move on. No one on my team would say the model was bullshitting; that word gets treated as unserious. But I've read Frankfurt's distinction between the liar, who is trying to get you to believe something false, and the bullshitter, who just does not care whether what he says is true. A language model sure looks closer to the second than the first, if it fits either category at all.

So what does the practice of calling these failures hallucinations — and refusing to call them lies or bullshit — actually presuppose about the model, about whether it is asserting or reporting anything, and about what kind of failure we think this is? Does the word hallucination smuggle in a perceptual picture, as if the model were mis-seeing a world, that doesn't really apply to something that only predicts text? And if we took the bullshit reading seriously instead, what would change concretely: would we write the disclaimers differently, alter the training objective, treat the outputs as not being assertions at all, or something else? I want the answer to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sounds like, and to say where it's underdetermined. oh and answer in American English.
