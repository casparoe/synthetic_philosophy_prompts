I'm a philosophy professor, and I've been going in circles with a colleague in biology over the same old argument, so I want to turn it into a design problem. Every spring she brings up periodical cicadas: thirteen- and seventeen-year cycles, and she says the fact that these are prime is doing real explanatory work — being prime minimizes overlap with predator cycles, and the cicadas' life history is explained by a mathematical fact, not just by selection pressures. Same with honeycomb: the hexagonal tiling is the least-wax way to partition a plane, and she says the bees are, in effect, exploiting a theorem. Her deeper claim (she's read the indispensibility stuff) is that mathematical facts are part of the furniture of the world because our best science can't state its explanations without them. I think the mathematics is bookkeeping: a compact way of summarizing facts about predators, wax, and surface tension that could, in principle, be restated without numbers.

What I'd like from you: treat her claim as a testable empirical hypothesis rather than a metaphysical standoff. Design the best test you can of the claim that a mathematical fact explains one of these empirical regularities (cicadas, honeycomb, or a better case if you have one). Be concrete: what would you measure or manipulate, what contrast are you using (primes vs. nearby non-primes, hexagons vs. other tilings, or something else), what pattern in the data would favor "the math is doing genuine explanatory work," and what pattern would favor "the math is just an after-the-fact summary." Then say what a positive result would and wouldn't show. In particular, mark the residue honestly: once the best feasible experiment comes back, what part of this disagreement is left for observation to settle in principle, and what part is just not the kind of thing any observation could decide? Keep the whole thing under 800 words, and skip the bibliography; I want the experimental logic and the boundary of what it can reach, not the literature.
