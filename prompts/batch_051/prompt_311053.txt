Consider a self-contained constitutional counterfactual, and reason through how it resolves.

The Republic of Kestria is a unitary parliamentary democracy governed by a single written constitution. Parliament is elected for four years under an ordinary Electoral Code; every election requires a writ that the President is constitutionally obliged to issue at the proposal of the Prime Minister, or, after a loss of confidence, of the caretaker Prime Minister. The President, elected by Parliament, has three years left in office. The Prime Minister is appointed by the President and must be able to command Parliament's confidence; a government that loses that confidence continues as a caretaker until a successor is appointed. New elections must be held within forty days after Parliament's term expires. The constitution has no state-of-emergency clause and no decree power. The Constitutional Court may review statutes but may not issue orders to the President or Parliament.

In the final sitting of a deeply fragmented Parliament, the government loses a motion of no confidence. During the ensuing caretaker period, while the chamber is still formally in being, a sunset clause in the Electoral Code is allowed to take effect: the Code lapses two days before the term ends, and no replacement is passed. Parliament's term then expires by operation of law. The caretaker Prime Minister asks the President to issue the election writ. The President refuses, on the ground that an election with no lawful procedure would itself be unconstitutional, and announces that the presidency is now the only continuing constitutional organ. The Prime Minister replies that the caretaker government alone remains democratically accountable and that the President has become a partisan obstruction. The Constitutional Court calls the situation a constitutional vacuum and says it has no enforceable remedy. The police, the military, the tax authority, the ordinary courts, and the municipal governments have so far obeyed their last instructions and no one else's.

Predict, with an argued chain of reasoning, who governs Kestria over the next twenty-four months and how the gap is closed or stabilized. I am less interested in a single verdict than in the mechanism: what makes any group of officials, soldiers, or creditors treat one claim as authoritative rather than another; which actors face the tightest constraints; where coordination and focal points do more work than law; and whether the resolution is more likely to come from a court-made doctrine of necessity, a negotiated interim council, a military intervention, a de facto presidential or prime ministerial government, or something else. Be explicit about confidence: which predictions are robust, which are guesses, and which load-bearing empirical assumptions about how institutions behave would, if false, reverse them. Treat the scenario as self-contained; general concepts such as necessity or caretaker government are fair game, but do not rest the answer on the details of any actual jurisdiction.
