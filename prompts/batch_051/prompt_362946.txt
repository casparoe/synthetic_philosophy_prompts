I'm drafting a short position paper for a democratic-theory seminar on who should get to vote, and I want to check my commitments hang together before I send it around. I've ended up with a bundle of views that all feel right to me individually, but I'm unsure wether they add up, so I'd like an audit rather than a defense of any one of them.

Here they are, in no particular order:

(a) Voting in general elections should be compulsory, roughly Australian-style, with small fines for unexcused non-voting. I don't just think low turnout is bad; I think eligible people who abstain are doing something mildly wrong.
(b) The voting age should be lowered from 18 to 16.
(c) Non-citizens who have been legal residents for five years or more should be able to vote at every level, national as well as local.
(d) The franchise should be grounded in the all-affected interests principle: people whose lives are systematically shaped by a government's decisions should have a say in choosing it.
(e) Children under 16 should not have the vote. I don't think merely having interests at stake is enough; voting requires a baseline of electoral competence, and below 16 that baseline generally isnt met.

I keep telling people all five are just about making democracy actually count everyone's interests equally, but I can't tell whether that slogan papers over cracks.

Go through the pairs and tell me which genuinely pull against each other and which only seem to. For each genuine tension, rank them by severity, say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix would be, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I want the smallest changes that restore coherence, even if I won't like them.

At the very end, on its own line, write \answer{...} containing your ranked list of the genuine tension-pairs only, most severe first, using the letter labels, like \answer{(x)-(y) > (w)-(z)}.
