I retired a few years ago from a career in molecular biology and have been slowly working my way threw some philosophy outside of science. I've been reading Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, the one usually translated as On the Use and Disadvantage of History for Life. A passage struck me, and I'm trying to connect it to something modern.

He describes a capacity he calls plastic power (I don't have the text in front of me, so I'll paraphrase). For an individual, a people, or a culture, this is the strength to absorb, digest, and reshape past events—including painful or alien ones—into one's own growth, rather than being burdened or defined by them. He suggests that this power has limits, and that beyond a certain point the past swamps the present.

Now here's what I'm wondering. In the last decade or so we've built tools for exactly the opposite: lifelogging, automatic photo backups, searchable chat archives, fitness trackers, even people who record every conversation. The idea is that forgetting is a bug, and perfect memory is a feature. If you were to predict Nietzsche's view of this, based on that passage and the rest of the essay, do you think he would see it as a manifestation of a healthy or a sickly relation to the past? Would he maybe classify lifelogging as a new extreme of monumental, antiquarian, or critical history, or would it be something outside his categories? I'm less interested in a summary of the essay and more in a reasoned prediction on this specific point.
