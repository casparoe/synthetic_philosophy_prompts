A short passage from Aristotle's *On the Heavens*, Book II, Part 1, in the translation available on Wikisource:

"Such a constrained movement would necessarily involve effort the more so, the more eternal it were-and would be inconsistent with perfection."

I would like you to explain a conceptual issue in mechanistic explanation by working outward from this sentence.

The issue is this. In several sciences that explain by mechanisms—molecular biology, neuroscience, parts of cognitive science—researchers often need to say whether two observations are produced by the same mechanism or different mechanisms. The usual gloss is that a mechanism is an organized system of entities and activities that produces a phenomenon, and that changing background conditions does not by itself change the mechanism. But Aristotle is treating a different border as explanatory: whether a motion is natural to a body or constrained from outside. For him, a constrained motion is not just the same motion under different conditions; it is a worse, intrinsically effortful, unstable kind of explanation.

Please explain, for a reader who has some background in philosophy of science but has not worked on this specific problem, what conceptual work the distinction between an intrinsic tendency and an external constraint can do for deciding whether two cases involve the same mechanism. Then apply it to anomaly detection: when a biological or cognitive system produces an unexpected output, how would one test whether the anomaly is the same mechanism operating under an unusual constraint, rather than a failing or replacement mechanism? What exactly is being detected when someone classifies an anomaly either way?

Throughout, please separate what is relatively settled about mechanism-talk from what is genuinely contested among people who work on mechanisms, and do not present any contested reading of Aristotle as the consensus.
