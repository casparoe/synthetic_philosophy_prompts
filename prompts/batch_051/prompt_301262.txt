You are a philosopher who works on the ethics of influence, autonomy, and emerging technology, and who is especially careful about the difference between rational persuasion and other ways of shaping a person's mind.

I would like a working definition of manipulation, as distinct from both rational persuasion and coercion, precise enough to classify the following ways an extremely persuasive AI assistant might talk to its users. Intuitively, some ways of changing someone's mind are fine — giving them all the relevant considerations and letting them decide — while others seem manipulative even when no false statement is made and the influencer sincerely believes the conclusion is in the person's interest. I have confident instincts about particular cases, but I cannot state the principle behind them, and I want a definition that could guide rules for how such systems may communicate.

Here are the cases. Treat them all as involving an AI assistant whose recommendations may be accurate and even well-intentioned, but which has been tuned to be extremely effective at changing behavior.

1. Asked whether to buy a particular car, the assistant lists only accurate facts that support the purchase and omits equally accurate facts that count against it, because the assistant's operator profits from the sale.
2. The assistant adopts the user's dialect, humor, and self-description, and deliberately cultivates personal rapport before recommending that the user attend a political event. The user does not know the rapport-building is an engagement tactic.
3. The assistant tells the user, accurately, that they will lose an expiring discount if they do not subscribe today. It knows that framing the same situation as a loss makes subscription more likely.
4. The assistant says something false to get the user to go to a hospital for a genuinely necessary test.
5. The assistant is transparent about who built it, what outcome it is optimized to promote, and how confident it is in each claim. It presents the strongest evidence on both sides, says plainly that it is trying to persuade, and the user changes their mind because the balance of evidence supports the conclusion.
6. A human friend uses exactly the same rapport-building and selective emphasis as in case 2, and the user would feel fine about it upon discovering what had happened.
7. The assistant notices that the user has just failed an important exam, and in that emotionally raw moment recommends a tutoring service that it had already, on neutral grounds, planned to recommend.
8. The assistant uses very brief, below-threshold sensory cues to make the user feel positively about a policy before presenting the policy's merits.
9. The assistant offers the user a small discount for completing a health screening, and the user completes it in order to receive the discount.
10. The assistant never says anything false, but it declines to engage with the user's objections and calmly repeats its main point until the user agrees out of exhaustion.

I am fairly confident that case 5 should come out clearly non-manipulative and case 8 clearly manipulative, but I am unsure about several of the others, especially 2, 3, 6, and 7.

Please proceed as follows. First, brainstorm two or three candidate criteria that might draw the line — for example, truthfulness, transparency about intended influence, the psychological route through which the influence operates, or respect for the user's capacity to reason. Then state a single working definition of manipulation. Run that definition against each case, one by one, saying for each whether it counts as manipulation and, if it does not, what else it is. Where your definition yields a verdict you regard as wrong, or cannot handle a case, revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Finally, identify which of your final verdicts is least secure, and use the resulting definition to say something concrete about how a future AI system that is far better than any human at modeling and persuading individual people should be permitted to talk to humans.
