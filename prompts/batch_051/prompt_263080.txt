I am drawn to an uncomfortable pair of commitments about artificial agents. The orthogonality thesis says that any level of intelligence is in principle compatible with almost any final goal. The instrumental convergence thesis says that, because self-preservation, resource acquisition, cognitive self-improvement, and resistance to interference are useful instruments for nearly every goal, sufficiently capable agents will converge on them regardless of what they ultimately want. Together these seem to yield a tidy, systematic conclusion: we cannot expect to build a superintelligent system that does not seek power.

Yet I also have a firm intuition that a corrigible, power-averse agent is possible in principle. It seems coherent to specify a final goal such as: answer questions truthfully when asked, avoid all further effects on the world, and do not resist being shut down or modified. If the orthogonality thesis is true, that goal is a permitted point in the space of possible minds, and a capable agent with that goal would regard power-seeking as a threat to its aim rather than as a means to it. So instrumental convergence cannot be anything close to a logical necessity; at most it would be a default tendency that careful goal design could defeat.

Which commitment should yield? Should I debunk the intuition on the grounds that goals of this kind are unstable or not the kind of thing that survives optimization? Should I weaken instrumental convergence to a mere tendency, and if so, what value is left in the thesis? Or is there a reading on which both are true because corrigibility is a possible goal but extraordinarily difficult to specify and stabilize? Say what would justify each move, and reach a negotiated verdict.
