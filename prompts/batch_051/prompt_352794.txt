I have been defending the view that our growing epistemic dependence on opaque machine-learning systems is eroding understanding, not merely redistributing it. The claim has two separable parts. First, an empirical-conceptual part: a community can improve its predictive success while understanding less about the domain it is predicting, because it can act on model outputs that no one in the community can explain. Second, a normative part: this loss of understanding is a real epistemic loss even when the predictions improve, because understanding is not only a means to better prediction. I want you to tell me what would count against this position, and I want the assessment honest rather than balanced. Which findings or arguments would show that the first part is false, that the second is false, or that the two together do not support the overall thesis about erosion? Tell me what the view predicts about the fine structure of epistemic practice that the relocation view does not, and where the two views could be made to come apart. Press especially hard on the rival account that understanding has simply relocated, from the individual practitioner to the people who built and validated the system or to the human-machine system as a whole, so that nothing has been lost; and on the charge that the thesis has been qualified into unfalsifiability. Where should a defender of the view concede that something genuinely counts against it, and where should they hold the line? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and conclude with a verdict on whether the position is testable enough for a view of this kind, and whether its insulation, if real, is a vice.
