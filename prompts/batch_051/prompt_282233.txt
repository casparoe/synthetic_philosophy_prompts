I'm a programmer, not a philosophy person, but I keep reading about this expressivist idea that moral sentences don't have truth values -- "lying is wrong" just expresses disapproval of lying, kind of like a void function whose whole point is its side effect, not its return value. So I tried to turn that into an argument in code terms, and I can't tell whether the analogy is doing real work or just sneaking in the conclusion.

(1) If expressivism is right, "stealing is wrong" has no truth value; it just performs a speech act (expresses disapproval), like a side effect.
(2) The very same sentence can sit in a conditional unasserted: "if stealing is wrong, then paying someone to steal for you is wrong." There the speaker isn't disapproving of stealing, so the side effect doesn't fire, but the clause still has to contribute something to the conditional.
(3) For a conditional to do its job, its antecedent has to yield a truth value, or at least something close enough. A side-effect-only expression can't sit in the condition slot without making the branch undefined.
(4) But "if stealing is wrong" does pick out a branch; we know how to go on from it and draw the obvious conclusion, so the clause must be returning a truth value after all.
(5) So simple expressivism can't be right: moral sentences behave like bools, not void functions.

I suspect this is just the Frege-Geach problem with the serial numbers filed off, and maybe (3) is the load-bearing move. But I also half think the argument actually goes through. Step through it: is it valid, and if not, where exactly does it break? Can an expressivist grant everything up to (4) and still deny (5)?
