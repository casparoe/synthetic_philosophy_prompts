I'm in a small clinical ethics study group with a bunch of medical residents, and last week we got stuck on the ethics of randomized trials: the instructor kept saying "equipoise" as if it settled the argument, but half of us used it to mean "this particular doctor honestly doesn't know," and the other half meant "the field as a whole is genuinely split." I'm making a handout for our group and I want it to sort people by what they'd actually do, not by which label they happen to like. Could you write me a decision tree with maybe seven or eight nodes that takes someone from a concrete case to the view they actually hold about when a physician may enroll her own patient in an RCT comparing two treatments A and B?

Start everyone from the same seed case and make each branch a small variation on it, phrased in plain clinical language a tired resident can answer without already knowing the jargon. Here's a seed case you can tweak: a patient with a serious but stable condition sees her own physician; the physician has a slight private hunch that A is better for this specific patient, based on two case reports and her own expereince, but the specialty as a whole is truly divided and a well-designed RCT is recruiting; the patient says she honestly doesn't mind which arm she gets and wants to help build the evidence. I want the branches to probe the distinctions that actually do the work: whether the doctor's private lean matters on its own, whether aggregate community uncertainty can substitute for the individual doctor's uncertainty, whether informed consent can waive or alter the duty to recommend a single best option, whether becoming an investigator changes the role enough that bedside duties no longer apply in their usual form, and whether a placebo or non-standard-dose control arm changes the answer compared with a clean A-versus-B. Make each terminal leaf carry a name and one sentence on its most awkward practical consequence. Include at least these leaves: a strict personal-indifference view, an individual uncertainty view (may randomize only when the physician sees A and B as genuine toss-ups for this patient), the standard community-level clinical equipoise view, a "both arms are reasonable standard of care plus valid consent" view that requires no deep uncertainty at all, and a role-separation view where the ethics are set by the research context, not the bedside. For any combination of answers that has no occupant, flag it and say whether the combination is empty because the view is incoherent, too unattractive for anyone to defend, or just unexplored. Finally, tell me which pair of leaves your tree cant cleanly separate and why.
