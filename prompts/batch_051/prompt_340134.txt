A friend of mine and I spent most of last evening arguing about the Knobe effect — the finding that people are substantially more willing to say that a chairman intentionally harmed the environment as a side effect of a profit-seeking decision than to say that a chairman in a parallel case intentionally helped the environment. She maintained that the effect is ultimately about norm violation: people call a foreseen side effect intentional when it contravenes a standing norm, and withhold the label when it conforms to one. I argued for a blame-based account instead: we reach for intentionally when the agent is rightly on the hook for the side effect, and we withhold it when the side effect is unwelcome but there is nothing to hold against the agent.

Going through old handouts afterward, I found the track record of a professor who taught a course on experimental philosophy and recorded her own verdicts on a series of side-effect vignettes: the standard harm chairman (profits up, environment harmed), intentional; the standard help chairman (profits up, environment helped), not intentional; a general who bombs a munitions factory, knowing the blast will also destroy a neighboring apartment building and kill civilians, intentional; a surgeon who administers a standard anaesthetic, knowing the patient will have routine post-operative nausea, not intentional; and a planner who widens a dangerous road to prevent fatal crashes, knowing some residents' commutes will get slightly longer, not intentional.

The professor has now circulated one further vignette for next week. A coast guard pilot, in worsening weather, lands his helicopter on a protected dune to evacuate two stranded climbers. He knows the landing is prohibited by the reserve's conservation rules, and he knows it will crush a roped-off nesting area and kill a number of hatchlings; he says he would have avoided the damage had an alternative been available, but landing elsewhere would have delayed the rescue and risked the climbers' lives. Afterwards he reports the damage and offers to fund restoration. Did the pilot intentionally destroy the nesting area? I am trying to work out what the professor will say, and what she should say if she wants to be consistent with her own record. Which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog, and which rule — norm violation, blame, justified trade-off, or something else — best accounts for the record? If the earlier cases genuinely underdetermine her verdict, please lay out what each live rule predicts for the pilot case, give your best prediction with reasons, and describe one further vignette that would cleanly separate the remaining candidates.
