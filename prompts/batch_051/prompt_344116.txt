I'm working through Turing's 1950 Mind paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence, and the short section on extra-sensory perception near the end has me stuck. Early in the paper he proposes that we should drop the question whether machines think and replace it with the imitation game: an interrogator talks to a human and a machine by text, and if the interrogator can't reliably tell which is which, that counts as success for the machine. Then in section 9 he says the statistical evidence for telepathy is overwhelming, and that if telepathy is real then a telepathic contestant could make the interrogator's judgment unreliable, so the test would become unfair. He says the rooms would have to be made telepathy-proof, but if telepathy is real we could never be sure a room actually is telepathy-proof. That last section seems to pull in the opposite direction from the confident setup. Is Turing genuinely admitting that ESP could wreck his proposed criterion, or is the section doing something else? I want the candidate readings that reconcile the early confidence with the late worry: e.g., he's only granting telepathy for the sake of argument, or he's making a deeper point about the conditions under which any empirical test can work, or he changed his mind, or the section is a joke. Which reading requires the least special pleading, and is there a reading on which the ESP section isn't a weird footnote but actually strengthens the argument of the paper?
