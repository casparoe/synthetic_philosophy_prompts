I'm teaching a graduate seminar on self-locating belief and observer-selection effects, and I want to open the unit with a diagnostic instrument rather than a lecture, so that students discover their own commitments before they learn the standard labels. My students have a solid background in formal epistemology and decision theory, but I will have deliberately withheld the anthropics vocabulary, so the quiz must sort people by what they say about cases, not by what they call themselves. Please write an eight-to-ten-question "which position on observer selection do you actually hold?" quiz. Each question should be a concrete vignette and should include at least these cases: a God's-coin-toss case where a fair coin determines whether one observer or many are created; an incubator-style sequential-creation case; a presumptuous-philosopher case where two otherwise equivalent cosmological theories differ only in total observer number; a doomsday-argument case where the respondent samples their birth rank among all humans who will ever live; a Sleeping Beauty case; and one many-worlds-style case where two branches of unequal amplitude both contain a version of the respondent. Every question should offer two or three forced-choice answers phrased as bets, credences, or prospective actions in the vignette, with no mention of self-sampling, self-indication, or any other party label, and no leaf should be defined by a slogan a respondent could recognize as a camp.

The scoring key should not merely tally one point per camp. Instead, sort respondents along two independent axes: (1) how much evidential weight, if any, the sheer number of observers in a hypothesis carries for them—none, full proportionality, or a capped or prior-tempered middle; and (2) what they implicitly treat as the reference class—actual observers, possible observers, observer-moments, or a pragmatic class that shifts with the question asked. Locate the leaves in that two-dimensional space, with SSA and SIA at their standard locations and at least one intermediate position that reasons like the presumptuous philosopher in local cases but refuses the full extrapolation to enormous observer counts, labeling any such leaf "unnamed" if no one defends it under a standard name. Explicitly mark cells that are unoccupied and say briefly whether they are incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored, and flag any two leaves your instrument genuinely cannot distinguish. Finally, add a short "how to read your result" paragraph, and then end with a single-sentence summary of the instrument as the very last line, beginning "Summary:".
