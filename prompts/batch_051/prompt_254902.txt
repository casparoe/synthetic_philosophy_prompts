I run a 75-minute undergraduate seminar on Confucian role ethics with 16 students. Design a structured classroom activity — role cards, three timed rounds, and a debrief — that makes participants feel the force of the thesis that the self is constituted by its roles before anyone names that thesis. The phrase "role ethics" should not be spoken until the debrief.

The activity should be built around a single dilemma in which two genuine role-obligations conflict, such as choosing between protecting a family member's misconduct and being truthful to an institution or a friend. Write the dilemma with enough texture that neither obligation is obviously defeating. Provide exact card text for at least six roles — for example, parent, adult child, close friend, teacher, junior colleague, neighbor — with each card stating one or two concrete relationship-specific expectations and the relevant history the role-holder shares with the other parties.

Then specify the three rounds:

1. Role-dense deliberation: in small groups, each student argues and votes from the card they were dealt, under a rule that they may only appeal to what their role gives them reason to do, not to abstract principles or rights.
2. Role-swap: redistribute the same cards, rerun the same dilemma, and require each student to state honestly whether and why their judgment moved when their role changed.
3. The leftover self: ask each student to write four sentences resolving the same dilemma "just as a person, not as any of these roles," then have them underline every phrase that smuggles in a role-like relationship — promises, debts, care, authority, membership. Push the groups to see how much remains once the underlined phrases are removed.

Provide a minute-by-minute timeline for the full 75 minutes, including setup and cleanup. Then give a debrief script in which each round is mapped onto a specific role-ethical claim: persons are made rather than found through their roles; ren is achieved through virtuosity within roles rather than through universal principle; li is role-sensitive propriety rather than a context-free decision rule; and the "leftover self" is an abstraction that cannot do real moral work. Use the early Confucian father-and-son case only as a debrief comparison, paraphrased rather than quoted, to show how the activity recovers a classic role-ethical verdict.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it — for instance, students resolving Round 1 by appeal to a universal harm principle despite the role-appeal rule; one role being seen by everyone as obviously stronger so the trade-off collapses; or Round 3 producing confident appeals to "authenticity" or individual conscience. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce: the felt authority of real lived roles developed over years, the cumulative self-cultivation that role ethics prescribes, and any thicker cosmological or religious grounding that some versions of the view include. The goal is that students leave feeling tempted by the role-ethical picture but also aware of where a classroom simulation runs out.
