I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a piece about the moral weight of small commitments: arrival times, RSVPs, promises to send comments by Friday. In my reporting I have found that my verdicts on particular cases are more definite than any principle I can state. I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here are the cases.

(1) A friend asks me to help load a moving truck at 3:00 sharp. I arrive at 3:25 without warning because I stayed on a phone call I could have returned later. I think this is a real wrong, and not a small one.

(2) The same friend, the same truck, the same 25 minutes, but the delay is a subway failure and I had no signal to warn them. I do not think I did anything wrong, though I half-believe I should have left more margin.

(3) I agree to meet a source for coffee at 3:00. At 2:40 I text that I am running 20 minutes late and propose 3:20. The source sees the message before leaving. I do not think I have broken a promise at all; I have rescheduled.

(4) A colleague promises comments on a draft by Friday. Friday passes in silence and the comments arrive Tuesday morning without a note. I think this is a clear breach.

(5) A different colleague makes the same promise. On the Friday, she writes that she needs until Sunday and apologizes for the delay; the comments arrive then. I think this is still a breach, but only barely, and almost fully repaired by the same-day warning.

(6) A friend is habitually 10 or 15 minutes late whenever we meet, and unfailingly texts when about ten minutes away. I cannot bring myself to condemn any single instance as a wrong. Yet I am convinced the pattern itself is a moral defect, as though the habit makes promises different from the ones actually uttered each week. This pair of verdicts, (1) and (6), is the one that bothers me most.

(7) I accept an invitation to a dinner party of eight, then decide that afternoon that I am too socially drained to go, and text my apology while the meal is being served. I think this is a clear wrong even though the evening is not ruined and nobody has to change plans.

(8) A repair person says he will arrive between 9:00 and 1:00 and shows up at 1:20 without calling. I think this is a minor breach. But if he had said only "midday-ish," I do not think a 1:20 arrival would breach anything, though it would be mildly irritating.

My working principle is roughly this: a small commitment is violated when I disappoint a reasonable expectation I have knowingly invited, and its seriousness depends on the cost imposed, how far the other party was relying on it, and whether I gave them a real chance to revise. That principle reads (3), (5), and (6) differently from how my verdicts do, so it needs repair or replacement.

Is there one defensible principle that captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them? What does the best principle say about the pattern case (6), and which of my verdicts does it force me to revise? And how does it come down on the case I return to most often: I promise to call a friend on their birthday, forget, and call three days later with an apology but no excuse—is the apology the end of the matter, or does something remain uncorrected?
