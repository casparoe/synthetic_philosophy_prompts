I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about everyday conventions and how far they bind visitors, using my unreflective verdicts on a set of concrete cases. My responses pull in different directions, and I would like you to find the principle — if there is one — that captures them.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A visitor from a country where tipping does not exist eats at a sit-down restaurant in the United States. I think leaving nothing is wrong, even on a first visit, once the visitor has been told how service wages work, because the server's income depends on the tip. A genuinely ignorant first-timer should be informed rather than blamed.

2. The same visitor buys a coffee at a counter, and the payment screen suggests a 22% tip with a small no-tip option. I think selecting no tip is permissible, though I would feel social pressure. This feels different from table service, but I cannot say exactly why the obligation attaches in one case and not the other.

3. A visitor to a country with strict queueing norms walks to the front of a bus-stop queue. I think this is wrong for visitors too, but fairly minor: the wrongness seems to be about disrespect and fairness rather than harm.

4. In my own city, jaywalking is illegal on paper but everyone does it when the street is empty. I think crossing against the light on an empty street at night is not wrong.

5. As a visitor in a city where pedestrians consistently wait for the signal, I would wait, and I think a visitor who regularly crosses against the light there is doing something wrong — but the wrongness is social rather than about safety, and a local who became indignant at a single crossing would be overreacting.

6. At a customs post in a country where travelers are expected to slip the official a small payment to have their papers processed without invented delays, I refuse, and I think the convention itself is wrong and ought not to be followed either by visitors or by locals.

7. When visiting a religious site that asks visitors to cover their heads, I comply even though I do not share the belief. The cost is low, complying is a sign of respect, and refusing would communicate contempt.

Cases 1 and 6 are the ones that trouble me most: in both, a local person's income or workload depends on a customary payment, and in both the visitor knows about the convention, yet I want to say the visitor must comply in one case and must refuse in the other. Cases 1 and 2 are also hard for me: both are tips in the same country, but I treat one as morally obligatory and the other as optional.

My provisional principle is something like this: a visitor should follow a local convention when breaking it would impose a real cost on an identifiable person, or when breaking it would express contempt for the people one is among; but the visitor should not follow a convention whose content is itself wrongful. That principle, however, looks like a patchwork, and it does not tell me why ignorance excuses fully in some cases and only partially in others, or why the counter-service screen is optional.

Please tell me what principle, if any, captures these verdicts, stated precisely and with the feature of each case that motivates each clause. If the best principle cannot capture all of them, tell me which of my verdicts I should revise and why. Then apply your principle to two cases I am still unsure about:

A. I visit a country where haggling in markets is expected, and the opening asking price is several times the price locals pay. I think paying the asking price without haggling is permissible, since I would rather pay more than bargain. Is that right, or does the principle require me to haggle?

B. In my home country the tip norm for table service is 10%. As a visitor to the United States, am I obligated to tip 20%, or would 10% satisfy whatever principle governs case 1? Does the principle track the local percentage, or the underlying role the payment plays in the worker's wage?
