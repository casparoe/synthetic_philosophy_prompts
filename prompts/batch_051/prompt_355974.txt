I hold a version of what is sometimes called the New Hume reading of causation. On this reading, when Hume says in Section VII of An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, "Of the Idea of Necessary Connexion," that we never discover any power or necessary connexion in objects, he is making an epistemological claim, not an ontological one: the gap is in us and in our cognitive access, not necessarily in the world itself. I read the following passage as carrying much of the weight of my view:

"When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other."

The phrase "never able, in a single instance, to discover" already sounds to me like a limitation of our powers of discovery, not a proof that nothing is there. On my reading, Hume can consistently hold that there may be mind-independent necessary connexions — real, hidden powers in objects — while also holding that our idea of necessity comes entirely from constant conjunction and a habit or determination of the mind.

I want a proper map of this position's exposure, not just the standard objections. What would count against it? Please distinguish evidence and arguments that would be decisive from those that would be merely awkward. In particular: (1) what textual evidence from the Enquiry or the Treatise would most decisively push toward the regularity reading, under which there is no real hidden necessity at all and necessity is only a projection of the mind? (2) where do defenders of the New Hume reading concede real weakness — for example, passages where talk of powers in objects looks hard to square with Hume's Copy Principle or with his claim that we have no idea of necessity beyond what custom supplies? (3) should someone argue that the New Hume reading is insulated from refutation — that every apparently reductionist statement is recoverable as "merely epistemological" — and if so, is that insulation a genuine problem for an interpretive position of this kind?

Please end with an honest statement of which consideration or finding would most likely change your verdict, and where your reasoning is most fragile.
