Good's theorem on the value of information is often summarized with the slogan that a rational agent should never turn down information that costs them nothing. Please give an intuitive explanation of why the theorem holds for an expected-utility maximizer—roughly, why a decision maker who is free to ignore a signal cannot be made worse off by receiving it—and then explain the assumptions this proof quietly relies on. In particular, describe at least two distinct kinds of case in which costless information could nonetheless be worthless or harmful, and in each case identify exactly which assumption of the theorem fails; where possible, separate cases that reduce to psychological or computational costs from cases that remain troubling even for an otherwise ideal agent.
