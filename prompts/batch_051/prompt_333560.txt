I am giving a research talk next month at a departmental seminar, and I want a question-period prep sheet, not a restatement of the paper. The talk turns on the passage in John Stuart Mill's On Liberty, Chapter III, "Of Individuality, as One of the Elements of Well-Being," where Mill introduces the idea of experiments of living:

As it is useful that while mankind are imperfect there should be different opinions, so is it that there should be different experiments of living; that free scope should be given to varieties of character, short of injury to others; and that the worth of different modes of life should be proved practically, when any one thinks fit to try them. It is desirable, in short, that in things which do not primarily concern others, individuality should assert itself. Where, not the person's own character, but the traditions or customs of other people are the rule of conduct, there is wanting one of the principal ingredients of human happiness, and quite the chief ingredient of individual and social progress.

My thesis is that Mill runs together two different arguments in this paragraph, and they are not equal in status. One concerns the agent's own faculties: a life chosen from within exercises the full range of a person's powers, while imitation leaves them unused. The other concerns what a society can know: that "the worth of different modes of life should be proved practically." I argue that the second is the load-bearing one, and that the value of individuality is best understood as a condition of collective learning, not as a freestanding ideal of self-development. The applied conclusion is that informal conformity pressure — reputational penalty, derision of unconventional lives, the quiet assumption that there is one acceptable path in a profession or a school — is objectionable on Millian grounds not because it is near-coercion that narrowly fails a threshold, but because it forecloses experiments whose results society, by Mill's own premises, cannot know in advance.

The audience is a philosophy department of roughly twenty people. Most are political philosophers in the contemporary liberal tradition — two work primarily on Rawls, one on Raz, and one teaches Mill regularly — and there are two historians of nineteenth-century political thought. There is also a cluster of three social epistemologists who work on testimony, disagreement, and the division of cognitive labor. These groups will not raise the same objections, and I need the objections in their vocabularies, not a generic list from the Mill literature.

The argument they will have heard before question time runs: first, the passage commits Mill to the view that the worth of a mode of life is knowable only by being lived; second, the more realistic threat to such trials is custom rather than law, since most people's conduct follows "the traditions or customs of other people" without any statute requiring it; third, if the epistemic reading is correct, then a society that forecloses experiments by informal enforcement does not merely burden individual choice but damages its own inquiry into ways of living; and fourth, a Millian objection to conformity therefore need not wait on the harm principle or on finding a violated right.

This has recently become personal for me: I left a profession last year whose enforcement of what counted as a "serious" life was almost entirely informal, and I want to be sure the paper can withstand people who doubt whether Mill would have objected to that at all, or who think the reading is anachronistic.

Please give me the objections in the order they are likely to arise, and within that order ranked by how much damage they would do, each phrased as the relevant member of this audience would phrase it and tagged with who is most likely to raise it. For each objection, give a two-minute reply, state what I should concede immediately and what I should hold, and note where the reply is soft. End with the question I am most hoping nobody asks, and explain why it would hurt.
