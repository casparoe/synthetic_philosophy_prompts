I keep coming back to L.A. Paul's argument about transformative experiences, and a friend who works in VR keeps telling me it's about to be obsolete. As I understand Paul, for a big class of decisions—having a kid, getting a cochlear implant, enlisting, moving to a new country—the usual calculate-expected-value approach breaks down for two reasons. First, the experience is epistemically transformative: you can't know what it's like from the inside before you've had it, so reports, videos, and other people's testimony underdetermine the choice. Second, it's personally transformative: it changes the very values and preferences you'd use to evaluate it afterward, so the person doing the choosing won't be around to cash the check, so to speak.

My friend says: fine, but suppose in ten years there's a cheap, safe, perfectly reversible preview cap. You put it on for a week, and you actually undergo the whole thing—including the reordering of your priorities—and then you come back to your current self with the memory of what it was like. You'd know exactly what the outcome would be like and exactly how it would change what you care about, so you could finally make an informed decision. He claims this kills Paul's argument entirley; I can't articulate why, but I think he's too quick.

So: does Paul's argument survive this development? If yes, say precisely which part survives and what the remaining obstacle to rational choice is. If no, say which premise the preview cap destroys and whether the argument is merely wounded or actually dead.
