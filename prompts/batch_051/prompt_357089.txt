A philosophical puzzle about empty names is this: sentences like "Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street" can be true in Arthur Conan Doyle's stories even though, outside the stories, the name "Sherlock Holmes" has no bearer. I would like a working definition of truth in fiction — of what it is for a statement to hold within a story, as distinct from being true about the story from outside — tested against these cases, all from the original Holmes stories, with my pre-theoretic verdicts attached:

(1) "Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street" — true in the stories.
(2) "Sherlock Holmes is a consulting detective" — true in the stories.
(3) "Sherlock Holmes is a woman" — false in the stories.
(4) "Holmes owned at least one pair of shoes" — true in the stories, I think, although no story says so.
(5) "Sherlock Holmes is a fictional character" — true of the stories, but I think not true in them.
(6) "Sherlock Holmes had an even number of hairs on his head when he first met Watson" — I cannot decide between false and neither true nor false.
(7) "Sherlock Holmes exists" — true in the stories, false of the real world.

Propose the definition, run it through all seven, and say where it forces you to correct or defend my verdicts. Then tell me what the definition commits you to about what, if anything, the empty name contributes to these sentences.
