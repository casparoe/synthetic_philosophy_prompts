In the course of researching a novel about a mathematician who comes to distrust a celebrated machine-assisted proof, I have had to think harder than I expected about what a proof is. The position I find myself drawn to is that a proof is at bottom a finite object that can be checked mechanically: a derivation in which each step is licensed by fixed rules and whose correctness does not depend on anyone's ability to hold the whole argument in view at once. This view strikes me as having real systematic virtues. It supplies a definite, non-arbitrary criterion for proofhood; it removes appeals to authority, reputation, and communal judgment; it treats human mathematical reasoning and machine computation as instances of the same underlying activity; and it explains why there can literally be proofs too long for any person to read in a lifetime.

Yet I cannot shake the contrary intuition that a proof no human being can survey is not, in the sense that matters to mathematics, a proof at all. The four-colour theorem, and the later computer-assisted proof of the Kepler conjecture, are my test cases. The machine exhaustively checked the cases and certified the result; but, my intuition insists, no one was shown the proof. A proof should be something that can be followed, and it should yield understanding of why the theorem is true rather than merely grounds for deferring to a black box. What we have in such cases, the intuition says, may be an excellent certificate that a formal derivation exists, but not mathematical knowledge.

I am not looking for a survey of positions, and I do not want to be told which side wins. I want you to give me the strongest version of each commitment: the strongest case that mechanical checkability is sufficient for proofhood, and the strongest case that surveyability and understanding are essential to it. Then offer a negotiated verdict. Should the intuition be discarded as a pre-computational prejudice, should the theory be amended so that a proof must be surveyable by some human or community, or is there a reading on which both survive, perhaps by distinguishing proof as objective certification from proof as human understanding? Explain what would justify each of those moves.
