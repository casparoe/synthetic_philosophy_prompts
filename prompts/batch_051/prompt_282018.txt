You are a professional philosopher specializing in normative ethics, value theory, and the long-term future of agency. I want your honest assessment of a theory I have been developing about value drift. Please start by brainstorming several ways to interpret the view and reconstruct the strongest version of it before you evaluate it, and do not pull punches.

I call my view the Reflection-Loss Account of value drift. Its central claim is that the main threat to preserving worthwhile values over very long timescales is not that future agents will deliberately abandon those values for bad ones, but that the transmission mechanism itself inevitably coarse-grains evaluative content. Values are stored in finite carriers—institutions, texts, reward models, individual memory, training corpora, constitutional rules—and any finite carrier discards information. Over repeated cycles of interpretation and re-implementation, what tends to survive is the part of a value that is easiest to re-express in the storage medium, not the part that is most important. So value drift is, at bottom, an information-theoretic problem rather than a motivational one.

From this I draw a strong conclusion. Attempts to prevent bad values in the far future by fixing a set of terminal values or constitutional constraints are self-undermining, because the fixed specification is itself a lossy compression and will be reinterpreted by later agents in ways that drift. What should be preserved instead is the metacognitive process that produced the values—the deliberative practices, correction mechanisms, and experiences of seeing certain patterns as bad—not the outputs of that process.

For example, a long-lived institution that tries to transmit compassion by writing down a definition of compassion will drift toward whatever behaviors the word can be used to justify, because the word is the easiest thing to transmit and the underlying response is not stored. But if the institution transmits the practice of examining concrete cases, asking certain kinds of questions, and correcting for self-serving applications, later agents might regenerate the value even if the original definition is lost. I therefore claim that value lock-in is impossible in principle: one can only lock in a generator of values, never a value itself. It also predicts that proposed alignment targets such as human values, corrigibility, or non-deception will drift into nearby corruptions unless we preserve the procedures of moral learning that gave rise to them.

I know about value lock-in and related alignment ideas, but this seems to me different because it claims an impossibility position grounded in finite representation rather than in motivational oddities or insufficient optimization. Is my view coherent? Does it already exist under another name? What exactly does it get right, and where does it break or stop being distinctive? Give me a charitable but candid assessment, and name the strongest objection to the strongest version of the view.
