I recently read a short article about the ethics of griefbots—chatbots trained on a dead person's messages and speech so that the bereaved can keep talking to them—and I am not sure how much of it to trust. The author's position, as I understood it, came in three moves. First, because the dead no longer exist, a griefbot cannot harm them; talk of posthumous interests is at best a metaphor, since there is no subject to whom anything could matter. Second, the morally weighty facts are all on the side of the living: whether a grieving person freely consents, and whether the bot actually helps them rather than locking them into denial. Third, because only the user's welfare carries moral weight, the only defensible restrictions are ones that prevent deception or psychological damage to the user; beyond that, worries about what the bot says, how it is marketed, or whose data it uses lack any subject to be protected. The author concluded that for a consenting adult a griefbot is permissible in roughly the way a private memento is, and that refusing to facilitate one is squeamishness dressed up as principle.

I find the first move compelling, but the step from having no experiences to having no posthumous interests struck me as too quick, and I suspect the conclusion about squeamishness rides on it.
