I am a high school student working on a philosophy paper about the way thought experiments, sometimes called intuition pumps, can either clarify a dispute or quietly settle it before the argument really begins. The paper has to engage with this passage from William James's Pragmatism (1907), from the lecture called "What Pragmatism Means":

"Some years ago, being with a camping party in the mountains, I returned from a solitary ramble to find every one engaged in a ferocious metaphysical dispute. The corpus of the dispute was a squirrel—a live squirrel supposed to be clinging to one side of a tree-trunk; while over against the tree's opposite side a human being was imagined to stand. This human witness tries to get sight of the squirrel by moving rapidly round the tree, but no matter how fast he goes, the squirrel moves as fast in the opposite direction, and always keeps the tree between himself and the man, so that never a glimpse of him is caught. The resultant metaphysical problem now is this: Does the man go round the squirrel or not?"

James goes on to argue that the dispute dissolves once you make a distinction about what "going round" practically means: the man circles the squirrel in the north-east-south-west sense, but never gets from its front to its side and behind it, since the squirrel keeps its belly turned toward him. He uses the story as a model of how a pragmatic distinction can end a philosophical quarrel.

Write an essay of about 900 words on whether thought experiments of this kind are more likely to clarify philosophical questions or to rig them in advance. Organize it under exactly these headings, in this order, with these approximate lengths: "The example" (100 words), "What James thinks it shows" (150 words), "The case that such intuition pumps clarify" (250 words), "The case that they persuade before they prove" (250 words), and "My verdict" (150 words). In "My verdict," do not end neutral; defend a definite conclusion about how much we should trust this method.
