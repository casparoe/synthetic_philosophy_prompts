I am drafting a novel in which the protagonist wears a device that continuously records what she sees and hears, and she has come to rely on it exactly as one relies on memory: she consults it without effort, trusts its entries without checking them, and is briefly disoriented when it is removed. A subplot turns on whether the device is, in any strict sense, part of her mind, and I want the dispute in that subplot to be more precise than the usual talk about phones becoming extensions of ourselves.

Toward that end I have collected the standard objections to the extended mind thesis as I understand them. First, the coupling-constitution fallacy: a tight causal loop between an organism and an artifact, even a mutually dependent one, does not make the artifact a constituent of the cognitive process itself. Second, cognitive bloat: if a notebook counts as part of the mind, so do address books, calculators, and perhaps one's colleagues, and the boundaries of the mind dissolve. Third, the mark-of-the-cognitive objection: the entries in an external store have derived content fixed by convention, whereas genuine mental states have intrinsic, nonderived content. Fourth, the fragility objection: the parity with biological memory fails because an external store can be lost, damaged, or consulted only intermittently, rather than being constantly available and automatically relied upon. Fifth, the ownership objection: one relates to one's own memories in a direct first-personal way, but to notebook entries only through perception and inference.

I am looking for one more objection to the extended mind thesis that differs in kind from these five — not a restatement, a special case, or a combination of them. Then tell me honestly whether this further objection would, in a novel, cut more deeply than the five I already have, or whether it would only slow the scene down.
