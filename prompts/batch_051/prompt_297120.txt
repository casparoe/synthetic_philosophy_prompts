I've been mulling over an argument that's been making the rounds in a course I'm taking on philosophy of computation, and I'd like a professional philosopher of mathematics who also knows their way around computational complexity to help me sort it out. The argument is meant to show that complexity theory places a hard limit on what we can come to know in mathematics, and it goes like this.

(1) Deciding whether a propositional formula is satisfiable (SAT) is NP-complete: checking a proposed assignment is fast, but finding one is the canonical hard search problem. (2) Assume P ≠ NP, as most complexity theorists do. Then any algorithm that runs in feasible time — say, polynomial in the input size — gets at least some SAT instances wrong: for every such algorithm, there is a formula it misclassifies. (3) So there is a specific, finitely describable SAT instance on which every feasible algorithm misclassifies. (4) Whatever a human mathematician does when assessing whether such a formula is satisfiable is just the running of some feasible algorithm: the brain is finit, made of ordinary physics, and works within polynomial time and space bounds on any realistic account. (5) Therefore, if P ≠ NP, there is a concrete mathematical truth — of the form 'this formula is satisfiable' or 'this formula is unsatisfiable' — whose truth no human mind, however brilliant, could ever come to know. (6) So the usual picture of mathematics as open-ended enquiry is false: there are perfectly definite statements that are permanently cognitively closed to us.

I find the conclusion genuinely unsettling, but I half-suspect step (3) and step (4) both. The question is whether the argument is valid, and if not, exactly which step is the first to fail and what the corrected step would actually entitle us to say. Could you start by rewriting steps (2) and (3) in quantifier form, then work through the argument carefully and tell me whether the move from 'every algorithm has some blind spot' to 'there is a particular truth that is hidden from everyone' is legitimate? I'd also like to know whether there is an equivocation somewhere between worst-case hardness and what is hard in practice, and whether step (4) is doing more work than it looks.
