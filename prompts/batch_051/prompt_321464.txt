I have a colleague who defends a picture of philosophical method in which thought experiments are to philosophy what controlled experiments are to science. On this analogy, the description of the case corresponds to the experimental apparatus; the intuitive verdict the case elicits corresponds to the observation; and the general principle that systematizes a range of such verdicts corresponds to the theory. From this he draws a strong methodological conclusion: since observations are supposed to overturn theories rather than the reverse, we should always allow a clear intuition about a case to overturn a general principle, and we should never allow a general principle to override a clear intuition about a case.

I would like to know how far this analogy actually holds. Please give me an explicit correspondence table, covering at least the following: the case description, the elicited intuition, the covering principle, the analogue of controlled conditions, the analogue of replicability across observers, and the analogue of experimental error. Then identify where the mapping breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that matter for my colleague's conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. I am especially puzzled about where transitivity and simplicity fit into this picture: transitivity looks like a principle we would not abandon merely because some case intuition appeared to violate it, and simplicity looks like a virtue of the theory side rather than something observed in any particular case. Once these disanalogies are on the table, does the colleague's conclusion survive? If so, in what weakened or qualified form? And is there a better analogy for the relation between intuitions about cases and general principles?
