For a while I have been arguing with friends about whether it is wrong to hurt characters in video games. Some of us will reload a save rather than let a non-player character die, even when it costs us progress or loot; others say that is just an aversion to gore, or a wish not to feel like a monster, rather than real moral concern for the character. We agree about the behavior, but we disagree sharply about what the behavior shows. I am a high school student putting together an independent research project, and I would like to see whether this argument can be turned into a testable question rather than an endless exchange of intuitions.

The claim I want to investigate is this: when players treat video-game characters as mattering—hesitating to harm them, reporting guilt afterward, or spending in-game resources to protect them—they are doing something closer to genuine moral concern than to managing their own discomfort or self-image. I can run simple behavioral tasks on classmates using a modified game scenario, questionnaires, and small points-based or candy-based incentives, but I have no physiological equipment, no advanced programming ability, and no access to an ethics board beyond ordinary parental-consent procedures. Please design the best test of this claim that I could actually run under those constraints, with conditions and controls, and specify which pattern of results would support the moral-concern reading and which would support the discomfort/self-image reading.

Finally, say what the test would fail to settle even if the results came out strongly one way—in particular, whether a positive result would show that the characters themselves have moral standing, or only that players have moralized their own reactions.
