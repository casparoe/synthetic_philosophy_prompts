I run an undergrad philosophy of math club, about 15 people, mostly non-majors, and Ive got 90 minutes. I want a session built around the MU-puzzle, the little formal-system exercise: start with the string MI, and you have four rules: (1) if a string ends in I you may append U; (2) from Mx you may derive Mxx, i.e. double everything after the M; (3) any III can be replaced by U; (4) any UU can be dropped. The goal is to reach MU. The point I'm after is the shift between working inside the rules and the move of stepping outside the system to see why MU is unreachable (the I-count is never a multiple of 3). I don't want to lecture that; I want them to feel the stuckness first, and actually struggle long enough that the outside view lands as a relief rather than a trick. Design the session as a small-group challenge: give me the rules as the groups would see them, what materials I need, how to keep them grinding without subtle hints, when and how to introduce the outside view—maybe as a mid-session prompt or a competing group—and then a debrief that maps the moment of release onto the general idea that some facts about a system are only visible from a vantage the system itself cant express. Be honest in the debrief design about what the puzzle does and doesn't show—it's a toy, and it isn't by itself an argument about Gödel or about minds being able to jump out of any system. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (groups finding the mod-3 trick early, people disengaging, the reveal falling flat) and how to handle it. Finally, keep separate what is mathematically settled here from what is genuinely contested in the philosophical gloss, and flag anywhere the session would be tempted to present a contested view as settled.
