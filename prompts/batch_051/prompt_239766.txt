Hey, I'm organizing a small workshop on self-amending rules — mostly game designers who've played a few rounds of Nomic (the game where changing the rules is itself a legal move) and found them either delightful or broken. I don't want a history of famous games or a list of examples from actual rulesets; I want a classification instrument: a decision tree or taxonomy that sorts the ways a Nomic-style ruleset can get itself into legal trouble when a rule applies to itself or to the amendment process that changes it. The tree should be built from structural features first. Each branch node should be a question a designer could answer by looking at the rule's form before seeing how it plays out — e.g., does the rule name itself or its own number? does it say it can or can't be amended? does changing it require a procedure that the rule itself also governs? — rather than a question about which historical paradox it resembles.

Make sure the leaves include at least these trouble-types: self-repeal, self-entrenchment, circular priority between two rules, and scope confusion between a rule and an amendment. But don't organize by that list; organize by the logical space of what a self-referential rule can do, and locate these positions in it. If a logically possible combination is empty, say so and give a sentence on whether it's empty because it's incoherent, unstable, or just unexplored. Also flag any two trouble-types your tree can't cleanly seperate.

The audience is smart but not academic; six to nine leaves is fine. For each leaf give a one-line cleanest minimal example (invent it, no need for real game history) plus a short name. Then make the very last line a single sentence, beginning with 'Summary:', saying what your taxonomy treats as the deepest divide between the harmless self-referential rules and the genuinely paradoxical ones.
