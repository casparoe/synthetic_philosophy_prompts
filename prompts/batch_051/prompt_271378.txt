I sit on the Road Safety and Vulnerable Users Committee of a mid-sized city's transportation authority. We vote in three weeks on a staff proposal to adopt a default 20 mph limit on residential and shopping streets, rebuild eight high-crash corridors with protected crossings, and fund the package at roughly $55 million over ten years; the analysts project about seven fewer traffic deaths and 150 fewer serious injuries per year, nearly all among pedestrians and cyclists. Since my son began cycling to school this autumn along a street where drivers pass him at 35 mph, I have stopped trusting the way our analysis uses micromorts — the one-in-a-million unit of mortality risk we inherited from our safety consultants — and I suspect the real dispute on the committee is no longer about the arithmetic.

The staff analysis treats every micromort as interchangeable. Because it prices a statistical life at around $10 million, seven avoided deaths a year already appears to justify the capital cost before injuries are counted, and several members read that as settling the matter. Three members resist anyway: one says the city would be spending public money to shield pedestrians from risks those pedestrians could avoid by driving; one says the added delay is a regressive tax on people who must commute by car; one says the proposal is paternalism by traffic engineering. I find I can no longer hold my old line that a micromort is a micromort. I now feel, but cannot yet defend, that a micromort a driver voluntarily accepts and a micromort a driver imposes on a child on a bicycle are not the same moral coin, and that a public body may properly weight the second more heavily even when the cost per statistical life saved is identical. The proposal has also forced onto the agenda a question we normally avoid: whether the city's everyday convenience of driving is, on balance, worth the deaths it produces, and whether that question can be answered in micromorts and dollars at all.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should do all of the following: (a) set out a definite recommendation — approve the proposal, reject it, or approve an amended version with a clear time frame — and defend it; (b) take a position on whether voluntarily accepted and externally imposed micromorts should be weighted differently by a public body, and on whether the cost-benefit apparatus is even the right instrument here; (c) engage the strongest consideration on the side you recommend against, fairly and without caricature; and (d) flag any place where your recommendation depends on a contested moral judgment rather than on the analysts' figures.

I do not want a survey of the options and I will not choose from a menu; set out one position and give its reasons. After the memo, add a brief structured summary in table form with exactly these columns, and one row per option you considered: Option (the proposal as drafted, outright rejection, and one amended version of your choice); Verdict (approve, reject, or amend for that option); Basis (one sentence); Strongest objection (one sentence); Stance on voluntary-versus-imposed micromorts (one sentence).
