Argue for the following position. Good's theorem on the value of information is often glossed as showing that a rational agent should never refuse free information and that information cannot hurt a rational agent. That gloss overstates the result. Properly understood, the theorem shows only that for an ideal expected-utility maximizer facing a fixed decision problem, costless information cannot lower the agent's expected utility ex ante, because the agent is always free to disregard the information and choose whatever action would have been optimal without it. Develop a committed argument that Good's theorem does not by itself vindicate general information-seeking for real, bounded agents. In particular, identify the assumptions that carry the proof—costless acquisition and processing, freely ignorable information, Bayesian updating, and a fixed choice set and utility function—and explain how the conclusion fails to transfer when those assumptions fail: for example, when information cannot be ignored once received because it alters attention, mood, or self-conception, or when learning changes a person's preferences or the set of actions available to her. Draw a precise line between what the theorem does and does not establish, and defend that line against the objection that anyone who declines costless information is for that reason irrational. Take a definite position throughout, and do not merely survey interpretations.
