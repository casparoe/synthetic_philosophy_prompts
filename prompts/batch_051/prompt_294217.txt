Here's something I keep chewing on when I teach the history of infinity. In Physics III, Aristotle seems to allow a kind of infinite — the infinite in division, or in adding on — but he refuses to let it exist all at once; the infinite is more like a day or the Olympic games, he says, something that goes on coming to be rather than a completed whole. Then in Hilbert's essay On the Infinite (1926) you meet the famous closing thought, roughly that no one is going to drive us out of the paradise Cantor created for us. On the face of it that's a flat contradiction: Aristotle says the completed infinite cannot exist, Hilbert says it's a paradise and we're living in it. Are they really contradicting one another, though? Lay out the strongest reconciliations. One candidate is that they mean different things by 'actual': Aristotle is talking about completed physical or substantial actuality, Hilbert about an actual infinite inside a formal theory. Another is that Hilbert was actually a finitist about honest, contentful mathematics, and the transfinite was an 'ideal element' that earned its keep only if a finitary consistency proof could be supplied — in which case his paradise is rented, not owned. Work through those and any others, and say where each reading is honest and where it starts to be special pleading. Do we end up with a genuine agreement about the infinite, or two different projects that happen to use the same word? And wether it's a reconciliation at all if it turns Aristotle into Hilbert's warm-up act.
