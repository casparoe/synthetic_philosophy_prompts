I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a long essay on the epistemology of legal fact-finding — specifically, on how courts reason when the decisive evidence is a probability. In my research I keep returning to an argument that seems to prove far too much, and I want help locating the step that should be rejected.

The two cases I have in mind are familiar. In the gatecrasher case, a promoter sues one spectator chosen at random from the stands of a rodeo, and the only evidence is that 999 of the 1,000 people in attendance did not buy tickets; nothing ties this spectator to the missed admission fee except his presence in the stands. In the blue bus case, a pedestrian is struck by a bus and sues the Blue Bus Company on the sole ground that Blue Bus operates 80 percent of the buses on that route. Each claim seems, on a purely probabilistic reading, to satisfy the civil preponderance standard — and a criminal version of the gatecrasher would seem to clear any quantitative rendering of proof beyond a reasonable doubt — yet the standard intuition is that neither finding should be made: naked class membership is not proof of what any particular person did.

Here is the argument that worries me. (1) Naked statistical evidence — evidence that a defendant falls into a class most of whose members did the act or failed in the obligation — cannot by itself warrant a finding about that defendant's conduct. (2) But every piece of evidence courts do accept works by licensing a probabilistic inference from an observed fact to a conclusion about the defendant: eyewitness identifications are credited because witnesses of the relevant kind are reliable at some base rate; DNA matches are presented through random-match probabilities; documents and alibis are weighed against background frequencies of lying, forgery, and coincidence. (3) So the distinction drawn in (1) cannot be maintained: all evidence is, at bottom, a statistic about how often evidence of that kind points to the truth. (4) Therefore, either courts must accept naked statistical evidence — and find against the gatecrasher and the blue bus company — or courts must exclude eyewitness testimony, DNA evidence, documents, and every other form of evidence, since none of it is anything more than a statistic. (5) The second option is absurd: a legal system that could not find facts on any evidence would be no legal system at all. (6) Hence courts should, after all, find liability on the bare 99.9 percent and 80 percent facts.

I cannot accept (6), but I also cannot see which step I am entitled to deny. I would most like to keep (1) — that a person should not face a verdict solely because he falls in a mostly-culpable class — and (5) — that courts can reach factual findings on evidence. Where does the argument go wrong? Since each move looks individually forced, I would especially value a diagnosis that says what a theory of legal proof must give up, and with what consequence, to block the step doing the real work.
