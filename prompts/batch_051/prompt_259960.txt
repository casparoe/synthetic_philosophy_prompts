I am a novelist, and I am wrestling with a sentence a copyeditor wrote in the margin of my manuscript. A character in the novel, a bigot, uses a common racial slur. The word appears in three places: first, as direct speech, when the man hurls it at a neighbor during an argument; second, in free indirect discourse, where the narration slides into the man's inner voice and the word sits there unquoted; and third, later, when the neighbor reports what was said, in a sentence like "He called me —" and the word is repeated. The copyeditor's margin note beside the first occurrence reads: "It's just a word." I take the note to be an argument for leaving the word intact in all three places, and I suspect it is doing more work than it admits, but I cannot say exactly what the claims are.

I want you to take the one-liner "It's just a word" apart. What distinct, precise theses could it be expressing — about whether a slur means the same as its neutral counterpart, about where the harm of a slur is located, about whether a sentence that prints a word thereby does the deed the word normally does, about whether an author's relation to a character's words changes the word's force? For each candidate reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. Then commit: state the reading you believe the copyeditor is actually relying on, whether that reading survives the three occurrences I listed, and whether "It's just a word" actually supports keeping the slur in all three. If the right treatment differs across the three occurrences, give me the principle that sorts them, and be explicit about which of my own instincts it would overturn. I am asking for a definite position and reasons, not a list of possibilities for me to choose among.
