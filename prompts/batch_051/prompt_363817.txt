Consider a claim that divides decision theorists: while a person is genuinely deliberating whether to do A or B, they cannot at that same time rationally hold a precise probability that they will do A. On one side, assigning a probability to one's own open action abandons the stance of a chooser for that of a forecaster, so the probability is either unavailable, incoherent, or rational only because the decision has already been made. On the other side, such probabilities are rational and ordinary: we can use evidence about our dispositions, incentives, and past behavior to form precise credences about what we will do, and any oddity in reporting them while deciding is a performance effect rather than a deep rational limitation. Call this the crowding-out thesis.

I would like you to design the strongest behavioral or experimental test you can of the crowding-out thesis, concrete enough that someone could run it. The test should try to distinguish a genuine rational limitation from the weaker claim that people are merely bad at or uncomfortable with reporting probabilities about their own choices while deciding. In particular, separate the thesis from the confound that paying people for accurate self-prediction may itself change the expected utility of the act. Then be explicit about what a positive or negative result would and would not show: which parts of the philosophical claim get operationalized, and which parts no experiment could reach. As you go, mark which parts are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.

Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, covering this sequence: the test design; the result pattern that would favor the crowding-out thesis; the result pattern that would favor the permissive view; the strongest alternative explanation for the result a crowding-out advocate would cite; and what no test of this kind could settle in principle.
