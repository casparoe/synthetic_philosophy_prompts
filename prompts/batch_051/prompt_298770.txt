I'm a judge on an ordinary court in a country that outside observers now routinely describe as a case of democratic backsliding, and I have to rule on something that's become painfully familar ground. I want help with the actual constitutional reasoning, not with predictions about what the higher courts will do to me afterwards. The provision at issue is Article 71 of our constitution, which was written in the early 1990s after a coup and a series of floods and earthquakes, and it reads:

" If the safety or functioning of the Republic is endangered by a grave external threat, by an insurrection, or by a natural calamity that the ordinary authorities are unable to manage, the President may proclaim a state of danger by decree. The proclamation must state which of these grounds is being invoked. The National Assembly shall convene without delay and may at any time terminate the state of danger by a majority vote of its members. Unless the National Assembly authorizes an extension by law, the state of danger lapses after thirty days. While the state of danger is in force, the President may issue such ordinances as are necessary to restore normal conditions; these ordinances may not amend this Constitution, dissolve the National Assembly, or suspend the activity of the constitutional court."

The facts are these. The president, eight years in office, has already captured the prosecution service, the public broadcaster, and most of the constitutional court's new appointments. He is behind in the polls before an election in three months. Two weeks ago he proclaimed a state of danger invoking "grave external threat," describing a foreign influence operation that he says is trying to rig the election. He then issued ordinances that (a) transfer administration of the election from the independent electoral commission to the interior ministry, (b) order the public broadcaster to give candidates "corrective" coverage, which in practice means the government daily, and (c) bar foreign-funded nongovernmental organisations from monitoring the vote. None of these ordinances amend the constitution, dissolve the assembly, or suspend the constitutional court, so the literal prohibitions in Article 71 don't reach them. The assembly, where his coalition has a narrow majority, voted to authorize an extension. On paper, he stayed inside the clause.

The lawsuit before me was filed by an opposition candidate and an election commissioner who was removed from her post by the first ordinance. The government's position is that Article 71 commits the existence of a "grave external threat" to the president, and that courts may not second-guess that determination or the measures he says are "necessary." My own chief judge has reminded me that judges should "show restraint" during emergencies — we have both seen what happens to judges who don't.

Here's what I actually need from you. Walk me through how the different approaches to interpretation would handle this: the plain words, the purpose of the provision, what the drafters would have said if they'd been asked about a president using the clause against elections he might lose, and what a reasonable member of the public would understand Article 71 to allow. Then give me your own view of whether these ordinances are lawful under Article 71. And, importantly, tell me what would change your mind — which factual claim or interpretive step would flip your verdict, and where your reasoning is weakest. I want to know if I've talked myself into an answer because I'm afraid of the alternative.
