I have been trying to get clear about something, and I would like a careful reconstruction of the ideas behind two ordinary practices rather than a general essay on gratitude.

Consider, first, the thank-you note. In my family, whenever I receive a gift, I am expected to write a note that names the giver, names the gift, and says something about how I will use it, and I am expected to send it soon after the gift arrives. This expectation holds even when I did not want the gift, and even when the giver appears to have given it only out of a sense of obligation. The note is addressed to a particular person, not to the world at large, and it is backward-looking: it responds to what that person did.

Consider, second, the gratitude journal. Many people I know keep one, and they follow the advice to write down three things they are grateful for each day. The items people actually record are often things like: the rain finally came, I woke up healthy, a stranger ahead of me paid for my coffee. Here there is often no specific benefactor, no deliberate act, and no particular person to whom the gratitude is addressed. And the practice is usually praised for what it does to the writer going forward: it is said to make a person happier or more resilient.

These two practices seem to contain very different pictures of what gratitude is. The thank-you note appears to assume that gratitude is a debt owed to a benefactor who acted on purpose, a debt that points back at one past act. The gratitude journal appears to assume that gratitude can be directed at good luck or at background conditions, and that its real value lies in the future state of the grateful person.

I would like you to reconstruct, carefully, what each practice presupposes about gratitude: about benefactors, about luck, and about the past. Which of these assumptions does each practice strictly require, and which ones merely fit it without being forced? Are the two pictures consistent with each other, or does each quietly rule the other out? Where do the practices leave things open, so that more than one account of gratitude could explain why we act this way? Finally, if the view implicit in one practice were carried over into the other, what would have to change in how we write thank-you notes or keep gratitude journals?
