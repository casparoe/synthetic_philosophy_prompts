I recently joined my city's planning commission, and I have found myself relying on a rule I never articulated before. When a development proposal comes before us, I discount the testimony of developers, planning staff, and outside researchers on anything having to do with how the project will affect the neighborhood, and I give near-decisive weight to long-term residents who would have to live with the consequences. My reasoning is that residents possess local knowledge that outside experts simply cannot have — which intersection floods, where the school drop-off line already backs up, how winter light falls on a particular block — and that residents, unlike developers or paid consultants, are not being paid to reach a conclusion; their stake is just the ordinary stake of living with the outcome. Last month this rule led me to vote against a mid-rise mixed-income building near a transit stop. The staff report and two outside economists argued that the project would put downward pressure on rents and add ridership to an underused bus line, but the residents who spoke were nearly unanimous that it would destroy the character of the neighborhood and overwhelm the street.

I can see that I am really applying two different standards within this one rule. On questions of on-the-ground impact — parking, noise, shadows, drainage, traffic — I treat residents as authoritative. On questions of citywide or regional effect — whether new supply slows rent growth, whether upzoning displaces or retains lower-income renters, whether the building changes school enrollment or transit use — I am less sure that local voices should count at all, and yet I notice that I still discount the experts because their models feel abstract to me.

I want to know whether this habit, or the tidier two-tier version of it, is defensible as an epistemic policy rather than a status-quo bias wearing the language of local knowledge. Is there a principled rate of deference to long-term residents versus outside experts, and does the right rate depend on which kind of claim is at stake — lived impact versus market-level effect? How should I weigh the fact that residents will bear the daily costs of the project against the fact that homeowners among them also stand to benefit from restricted supply? I also worry that a prior belief about who has a right to the neighborhood — that its current residents do — is quietly selecting which testimony I count as evidence. How could I tell, from the inside, whether I am applying the principle or merely rationalizing exclusion in cases where the same principle would lead me to trust residents who support a project I happen to favor?
