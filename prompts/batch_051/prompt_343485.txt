A few months ago someone I used to be close with threatened to forward some old messages to my employer unless I gave him money. I didn't pay, he sent them, and the whole fallout has got me reading about blackmail nonstop. The thing I can't get past is the basic paradox: the two pieces are each legal on their own — you're allowed to tell the truth about someone, and you're allowed to ask for money — but when you hook them together as "pay me or I'll tell," it's a crime. I mostly care about the moral wrong of it, not the precise legal elements, becuase the law is just one place where people had to draw a line.

So before I read any more theories, write me the desiderata: what does a theory of the wrongness of blackmail actually have to get right? I want the full spec, not the scorecard. Give me the requirements list — the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse, the facts it can't contradict — with why each one is a requirement. Seed it with the hard cases I keep tripping on: the threat to report a real crime the victim committed, the threat to publish true but old information, the stranger who stumbles on a secret versus the ex who was told it in confidence, demands that aren't money, the case where the threatened disclosure also embarrasses the blackmailer, and the fact that threats like "pay me or I'll sue" or "lower the price or I'll buy from your competitor" are not blackmail. Then tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of particular camps (exploitation, third-party harm, breach of confidence, coercion, privacy, whatever else), wether any two requirements on the list can't be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement knocks out the most popular theories.
