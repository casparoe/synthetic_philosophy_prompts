Observers of democratic erosion seem to me to have been stuck in the same standoff for years. One camp treats democracy as effectively binary: if elections remain competitive, incumbents can lose, and opposition parties can take office, the regime is still a democracy, so talk of backsliding is little more than a partisan way of disparaging policies one dislikes. The other camp treats democracy as a bundle of continuous conditions—judicial independence, media pluralism, bureaucratic autonomy, unwritten norms that make formal institutions work—so a country can shed much of the bundle while keeping the electoral shell, and denying backsliding is the partisan move. Applied to Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States, the debate stalls on exactly this fault line, with each side convinced the other has defined democracy to guarantee its preferred verdict. Give a committed diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is: a terminological choice, a disagreement about which facts and indicators should count, a dispute about baselines or thresholds along a continuum, or a normative conflict using the word democracy as a proxy. Then say what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning; no preamble and no survey of the options.
