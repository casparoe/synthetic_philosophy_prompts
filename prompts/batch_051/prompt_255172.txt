I'm a software engineer and I've been reading William Blackstone's Commentaries on the Laws of England, specifically Book I, Chapter 7, "Of the King's Prerogative," because I'm trying to understand the legal background of sovereign immunity: the idea that the state, or the crown, can't meaningfully be said to wrong its subjects. Blackstone quotes two maxims side by side:

"It is a maxim of the English law, as we have seen from Bracton, that 'rex debet esse sub lege, quia lex facit regem:' the imperial law will tell us, that 'in omnibus, imperatoris excipitur fortuna: cui ipsas leges Deus subjecit.'"

My rough gloss of the Latin is this: the first says the king must be under the law because the law makes the king; the second says the emperor's condition is exempt from all that, because God has subjected even the laws to him. Blackstone then immediately says he does not hesitate to prefer the English maxim over the Roman one, as more conducive to the ends for which societies are framed. So he appears committed to the king being under the law, not above it.

But I also know that Blackstone's tradition is associated with the old maxim that the king can do no wrong, which I had always understood as a denial that the sovereign can commit a legal injury at all. Those two ideas look to me like they pull in opposite directions. I'm trying to figure out whether the two maxims Blackstone quotes are actually in conflict, or whether they only seem that way because the claim that the sovereign can do no wrong is ambiguous between "the law never holds the sovereign liable" and "the sovereign's acts are never legally wrong." Are there other ways to reconcile Blackstone's preference for the first maxim with the doctrine of sovereign immunity, and which reconciliations are honest readings of what he says rather than special pleading? If they can't be reconciled, which claim is doing the real work for him? And if my framing is confused — for example, if I'm importing a later doctrine into an older text — please tell me that directly instead of papering over it.
