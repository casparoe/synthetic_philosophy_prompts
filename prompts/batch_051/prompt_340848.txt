I sit on a regional flood-planning committee. An economist colleague has been circulating an argument that seems to show the precautionary approach we rely on under deep uncertainty is simply incoherent. I cannot find the flaw, and I want to know which step to reject and what rejecting it costs.

The argument runs as follows. (1) In Ellsberg's set-up, an urn holds 90 balls: 30 red and 60 that are black or yellow in an unknown proportion. Four gambles pay $100 on a single draw: R pays on red, B on black, RY on red or yellow, BY on black or yellow. (2) Most careful decision-makers prefer R to B, because R gives a known one-in-three chance while B gives an unknown chance between zero and two-thirds; and they prefer BY to RY, because BY gives a known two-thirds chance while RY gives an unknown chance between one-third and certainty. (3) Those two preferences are two expressions of one disposition: when one option has known odds and the other has unknown odds, prefer the known option. (4) But preferring R to B means treating red as more likely than black, while preferring BY to RY means treating black-or-yellow as more likely than red-or-yellow; since yellow is common to both of the latter options, that is treating black as more likely than red. (5) So the same pair of choices treats drawing black as both less and more likely than drawing red, which amounts to having no coherent probability assignment at all. (6) The disposition in (3) is what underlies precautionary practice under deep uncertainty: holding larger reserves against a flood risk whose probability cannot be estimated than against one whose probability is well measured, or paying a premium to avoid a poorly understood catastrophe. (7) Therefore the precautionary approach is a form of incoherence and should be abandoned.

Either identify the exact step at which the argument slips, explain why that step looked unavoidable, and say what a defender of precautionary practice must give up to reject it; or, if no step slips, explain what it would mean to accept that precautionary practice is irrational.
