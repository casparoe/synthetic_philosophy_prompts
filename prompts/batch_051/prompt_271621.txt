A religious organization that runs a large network of online discussion spaces for people exploring faith adopts a standing rule for its spiritual counselors, whom it calls companions. Under the rule, which the organization names the policy of reserve, companions may listen, ask questions, and reflect a seeker's own words back to them, but they may not offer arguments for God's existence, recount their own experiences, volunteer historical evidence, or otherwise supply anything that could function as evidence for God. The stated rationale is theological: God declines to overwhelm anyone with evidence, because faith elicited by overwhelming evidence would not be freely given, so the organization refuses to supply what God has deliberately withheld; the companion's role is to remain with the seeker inside the hiddenness rather than relieve it.

Treat the policy of reserve as a mechanism to be analyzed rather than a stance to be endorsed or condemned. How would it actually behave? Give an account of what it incentivizes among the companions themselves, among seekers at different stages of doubt and commitment, and among the organization's leadership and external competitors. Identify the most plausible forms of gaming: seekers probing for indirect evidence, companions signaling their own convictions through tone, question selection, or deliberate silences, rival organizations advertising their readiness to present exactly the evidence the first refuses to give, and any other strategies that seem likely. What second-order effects would the policy produce — on who stays and who leaves, on the spiritual temperaments the community selects for, on its public reputation, and on the way the companions come to understand divine hiddenness itself? Which of these failure modes could be fixed by tightening, auditing, or revising the rule without abandoning it, and which could not be fixed without turning the policy into a different thing entirely? Conclude by asking what the mechanism's success or failure would show about the theological claim it institutionalizes: would a community that successfully works inside divine hiddenness make the claim that hiddenness safeguards free faith more plausible, less plausible, or merely reveal that the analogy between an institution and God carries no weight either way?
