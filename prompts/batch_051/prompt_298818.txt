I'm in a third-year philosophy of religion course and we've spent the last few weeks on divine impassibility, i.e. whether God can suffer. The two people in my seminar who can't stop arguing are, let's call them Alex and Priya. Alex is a classical theist impassibilist: God can't suffer, can't be acted on, and doesn't have emotions the way we do, but still genuinely loves and acts in the world. Priya's a passibilist: God can and does suffer, above all in the incarnation and the cross, because a love that can't be wounded isn't really love. Our tutor has stopped trying to referee and has instead asked for the document they should have produced after a term of arguing past each other: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration. I want you to draft that document. Please write it in British English.

I'd like it anchored in this passage from Anselm of Canterbury, Proslogion, chapter VIII (Sidney Norton Deane's 1903 translation):

BUT how art thou compassionate, and, at the same time, passionless? For, if thou art passionless, thou dost not feel sympathy; and if thou dost not feel sympathy, thy heart is not wretched from sympathy for the wretched ; but this it is to be compassionate. But if thou art not compassionate, whence cometh so great consolation to the wretched? How, then, art thou compassionate and not compassionate, O Lord, unless because thou art compassionate in terms of our experience, and not compassionate in terms of thy being.

Truly, thou art so in terms of our experience, but thou art not so in terms of thine own. For, when thou beholdest us in our wretchedness, we experience the effect of compassion, but thou dost not experience the feeling. Therefore, thou art both compassionate, because thou dost save the wretched, and spare those who sin against thee; and not compassionate because thou art affected by no sympathy for wretchedness.

Both Alex and Priya claim Anselm and read this passage in completely opposite ways, so make it do some actual work: open the agreed-claims section by saying precisely what the passage does and does not assert, and make one of the cruxes explicitly about whether Anselm has given a real account of compassion here or just redefined it out of existence. Maybe also get at the distinction he's drawing between our "experience of the effect of compassion" and God's not "experiencing the feeling" — is that a distinction with a difference, or a retreat?

For the structure, I'd like a numbered agreed-claims section first, then a disagreements table. In the table, for each row give: the exact disagreement, phrased so each side would accept the wording of its own position; the crux underneath it; a test, case, or arguement that both sides would accept as bearing on it; and each side's prediction recorded in advance. I suspect the real cruxes include (a) whether suffering or being affected is a privation or a perfection, (b) whether affective states like compassion necessarily involve being acted on, and (c) how far the suffering of the incarnate Son can be attributed to the divine nature itself (the theopaschite question) without dissolving the two-natures doctrine. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal — e.g. they're using "suffering" for two different things — say which part and why. And don't force it where there's no clean crux; note where the dispute stays messy.

That's it. The deliverable shouldn't argue for one side, and it shouldn't be a dialogue — it's the constructive text both could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact.
