I am currently taking a metaphysics of science course, and for my term paper I am working through the Russellian position that causation should be eliminated from our fundamental picture of the world rather than reduced to something more respectable. The position, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows. Mature physical theories state exact functional relations — differential equations, conservation laws, symmetry principles — and these do not require any distinction between cause and effect. Since the fundamental laws are time-symmetric while the causal relation is asymmetric in time, causal claims cannot reflect anything in the fundamental equations; the apparent directedness of cause and effect is an artifact of initial conditions and of our parochial epistemic situation. Causal talk survives, on this view, only in the special sciences and in ordinary life, where it is a useful but dispensable heuristic, and its usefulness there is no more evidence that the world contains causes than the usefulness of talk about sunrises is evidence that the sun really rises.

I want to stress-test this position rather than simply classify it. Please tell me what would have to be true for the eliminativist to be wrong. Specifically: (1) What would count against the claim that physics can get by without causes? If, for example, a serious interpretation or extension of fundamental physics made causal structure primitive and not recoverable from anything else, would that be decisive, merely awkward, or something the eliminativist could absorb? (2) What would a reductivist rival have to show to establish that the eliminativist has discarded a notion that is still doing genuine work — for instance, by giving an account of causation in terms of counterfactual dependence, regularities, or invariance under intervention that earns its place in scientific practice? (3) Which of these possible developments would be decisive against eliminativism, and which would only force a retreat to a more qualified view, one that concedes causal concepts are indispensable for explanation or experimental design while denying that causes figure in fundamental ontology? And which of these escape routes are live in current physics and philosophy, rather than merely logically possible? (4) What would a sophisticated defender of the position concede as a real cost of the view, or as evidence that genuinely counts against it, even while retaining it?

I want an honest map of the position's exposure: where it risks being touched by new physics or better philosophy, where it has quietly insulated itself by qualification, and whether that insulation reflects depth or unfalsifiability.
