You are helping me prepare a handout for an advanced undergraduate philosophy of language reading group on the debate that gets summarized as “do words have meanings?” The deliverable is a “Which theory of word meaning do you actually hold?” sorting quiz, with a scoring key. The quiz should classify respondents among the following seven positions, which must appear as named leaves with one-sentence summaries: (1) classical definitionalism — a word has one determinate meaning, or a small set of them, analyzable in necessary and sufficient conditions, and apparent variation is contextual specification; (2) ambiguity-first/homonymic pluralism — each distinct sense is its own lexical item, and many apparent “related senses” are really independent words sharing a form; (3) prototype/family-resemblance semantics — meaning is a graded structure of central and peripheral cases, with no common core present in every use; (4) generative-rule polysemy — the lexicon contains rules that systematically generate related senses such as animal/meat, container/contents, author/work, and process/result alternations; (5) minimal invariant core plus pragmatic enrichment — each word contributes only a thin stable content, and the rest is supplied by context; (6) radical contextualism about ad hoc concepts — there is no stable lexical meaning to retrieve, only a schema or pointer from which each use constructs a concept on the fly; (7) meaning-as-use — “meaning” names no entity at all, only family patterns of use across a form’s life in a language.

Build the instrument from the logical space rather than from that list. First identify two or three diagnostic dimensions along which the positions come apart — for example, one word or many, invariant core or none, rule-governed extension or unstructured resemblance, and meaning as a stored entity or as a practice. Then write questions whose answer options realize combinations of those dimensions, and only afterwards locate the seven positions among the occupied combinations. Note the unoccupied combinations and say briefly whether each is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The quiz should have nine questions. Each question presents a concrete linguistic case with a short array of example sentences. Use at least six of the following cases, and add one or two of your own in the same style: chicken (animal vs. meat), book (physical volume vs. informational content), bottle (container vs. contents), “Shakespeare is on the top shelf” vs. “Shakespeare wrote Hamlet” (author vs. works), open (open a door, an umbrella, a shop, a possibility), cut (cut grass, a class, a deck, a film), healthy (healthy diet, urine, child), and hard (hard surface, problem, person). Every answer option should be a concrete judgment about that case — for example, same word with one broad meaning, same word with two related rule-generated senses, two words sharing a form, or no meaning assigned until context builds one — and must be answerable by someone who does not already know the labels. Do not use the names of the seven positions or technical terms like “coercion” or “polysemy” inside the questions themselves. No question should ask respondents to choose a label or to answer “do words have meanings?” in the abstract; sorting must run only through the cases. If a respondent is tempted to answer “it depends on context,” force the choice: every such option must specify what, if anything, the word itself contributes across contexts.

The scoring key should map patterns of answers onto the seven positions, with each position’s most awkward commitment stated in one sentence. Mark explicitly which pairs of leaves the quiz cannot reliably separate even after scoring — plausibly prototype/meaning-as-use, minimal-core/radical-contextualist, and definitionalist/minimal-core — and say why the instrument cannot separate them. The angle is that this group currently treats “context” as a conversation-stopper; the quiz should make contextualism pay for itself by forcing respondents to say whether any stable content survives across contexts. Avoid citations, bibliographies, and named authors; the instrument must stand on its own.
