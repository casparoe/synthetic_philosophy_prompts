At dinner last week I watched two friends get into a argument about robots, rats, and what counts as a representation. Marco does SLAM and state estimation in our robotics lab; Priya is more into ecological psychology and embodied cognition. I mostly just listened and I honestly still can't tell who I agree with. Can you adjudicate the whole exchange step by step? Here's roughly how it went.

Marco: "Our robot literally stores a metric map and a pose estimate. That's a representation of the world. It's not just a metaphor — the internal variables stand in for the walls and landmarks."

Priya: "For the robot, sure, because you wrote the code that way. But then you turn around and say the rat's place cells are a map, and you're just importing the robot's architecture into the animal. The rat is coupled to the maze through its body; place-cell activity might be a dynamical attunement to the walls, not a stored picture of them."

Marco: "But the place cells fire for a location regardless of the route the rat took, and they replay during sleep. Route-independence plus offline replay is exactly what you'd expect from a map and not from a local reflex. So the rat has something that stands in for where it is."

Priya: "'Stands in for where it is' is doing all the work. A thermostat's bimetallic strip stands in for the room's temperature in a loose sense too, but you probably wouldn't call that a representation. If you would, then the word covers everything from thermostats to cartography, and it stops explaining the interesting differences."

Marco: "Okay, maybe the thermostat does represent temperature in a minimal sense. But then you can't just draw the line at the rat; you need a principle for when representation becomes rich, off-line, and recombinable. My principle is route-independence plus replay, and the rat passes."

Priya: "And my principle is decoupling. A state is representational only when the system can deploy it away from the thing it's about, like you thinking about the maze at dinner tomorrow. During navigation the rat's activity is locked into the sensorimotor loop, and the loop does the work. I'd reserve 'representation' for off-line redeployment."

Marco: "But on that view the rat has no belief about where it is while it's navigating — it just behaves. Yet rats take shortcuts and detours when you block familiar paths, which looks like standing knowledge of the maze, not just coupling. You've made 'belief' so demanding that almost nothing real-time qualifies, just to keep the word clean."

Priya: "I'm not making belief more demanding; I'm relocating it. The know-how is in the animal-environment system — the affordances of the walls, the history that laid down the paths — not in a little model in the head. What's in the head matters because of what it lets the rat do, not because it pictures the maze."

Marco: "But when you formalize what it lets the rat do, you get a control policy with internal states that track the geometry. The representations sneak back in under another name."

Priya: "Only if you insist that the scientist's state-space description is a fact about the rat's cognitive economy. The rat doesn't read off its own state variables. That description is your tool, not its content."
