I was chatting to a historical linguist the other week and she hit me with an argument that's been bugging me since. Paraphrased, it goes:

1. The Neogrammarian hypothesis is that sound change is regular: when a sound changes, it changes in every word where that sound occurs in the relevant environment.
2. But every language has stray words that didn't undergo a change they 'should' have, given their sounds and history.
3. Historical linguists deal with these by saying they're analogical formations or borrowings.
4. Yet those two explanations are, in practise, flexible enough to catch any counterexample after the fact — the very fact a word didn't change gets taken as proof it must be an analogy or a loan.
5. A hypothesis that can be made to fit every observation isn't falsifiable.
6. So the claim that sound change is regular, supposedly linguistics' closest thing to a scientific law, isn't a scientific claim at all.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and what would a better version of that step say? I'm less interested in the yes/no than in the precise location of the trouble. Answer in British English.
