I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and most of my work is in procedural content generation: I build systems that produce levels, meshes, textures, and other assets by varying a set of numeric parameters within constraints. Lately I have been reading Hofstadter's account of creativity, and one idea keeps bothering me: his claim that the crux of creativity is variations on a theme. As I understand the model, a creator starts from a theme — a pattern, phrase, motif, or concept — and creation consists largely in twiddling knobs: adjusting one dimension of the theme at a time, sliding along some spectrum, and then evaluating what the variation preserves, loses, or transforms. On this view, the deep skill is not the variation itself but knowing which knobs exist, which ones are worth turning, how far to turn them, and when a variation has changed the theme's identity into something genuinely new instead of merely decorating it.

I find the model seductive because it looks like a description of what my systems already do — and that is precisely what worries me. My generators explore parameter spaces and produce thousands of variations, but only a small fraction feel like anything beyond the same object with noise; the few interesting ones feel almost like accidents of the seed.

My question: please describe the most important implications of Hofstadter's knob-twiddling model of creativity for how I should design procedural-generation systems, and for how I should think about the originality of their outputs and of my own role in curating or guiding them.

Do not give me a balanced survey of readings or a list of caveats. I want a committed answer: state plainly the two or three implications you think are most important, defend why those should change how I work, and take a definite stance even where the point is contestable.
