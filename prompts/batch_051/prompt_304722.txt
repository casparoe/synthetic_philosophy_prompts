During my career as a research scientist I saw complex projects repeatedly handled by a method that now strikes me as philosophically interesting: no single person held the entire design; the work was divided among specialist teams, each operating with deliberately local knowledge, each producing specifications and summary reports for the level above, with integration happening at the top. I have since been reading about factored cognition and the recursive scheme called HCH (humans consulting HCH), in which a question is answered by decomposing it into subquestions: at each node, a human faces only a small, well-scoped subproblem, may call on the same scheme for further decomposition, and passes a summary upward, until the leaves are questions simple enough to answer directly from a short context. A commentary I read exploited this resemblance, using the engineering firm as an analogy for HCH in order to argue that a network of bounded reasoners, each with a narrow context, can in principle reproduce the output of a single unbounded ideal reasoner — and that we therefore should not evaluate a deliberative system by asking whether any one participant understands the whole. I would like an audit of this analogy. Please give the correspondence explicitly: what is supposed to map onto the specialist teams, the summary reports, the integration at the top, and the absence of anyone with the whole design? Then identify where the mapping breaks down, separating the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that do not. Among the candidates I keep turning over: information is irretrievably lost in upward summaries; a firm normally has a shared blueprint, specification language, or architecture that HCH does not get for free; a firm's decomposition is revisable by someone with authority to reorganize the whole, whereas the recursive scheme lacks such a vantage point; engineering decomposition works because interfaces are stable and well-understood, while an open-ended conceptual question may not factor cleanly; and errors, hidden biases, or strategic behavior can compound through a recursive chain of summarizers. The author's conclusion was that the bounded-reasoner network can match the ideal reasoner. Does that conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it? If the analogy is doing illicit work, what would be a more honest comparison — and what exactly would that better comparison establish about the limits of factored cognition as an idealization of deliberation?
