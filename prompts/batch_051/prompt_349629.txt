i'm putting together a unit on the equality of what debate, and I've noticed that the standard contrast cases always pit two views against each other—welfare versus resources, or resources versus capabilities. I want one two-person case where all three come apart at once, because I think it'll make the structure of the debate much clearer for students.

So here's the assignment. Construct a short ordinary-life scenario—no disability, no exotic preferences, no coercion, at most a paragraph for the scenario itself—about how a fixed training stipend should be divided between two people, call them Amara and Ben. Amara's preferences have adapted to a restricted upbringing: she reports being pretty satisfied with little and asks for less than she'd need to actually function on equal terms. Ben has expensive tastes: he's objectively fine, but he needs a lot of resource to reach the same subjective welfare level as Amara's current satisfaction. Build the case so that a welfare egalitarian gives more to Ben (equal welfare requires compensating his costly preferences), a straightforward resource egalitarian gives each the same (preferences are his responsibility, and there's no handicap to insure), and a capability theorist gives more to Amara (her current satisfaction is a bad guide to her real freedom, and equalizing capabilities requires extra resources for things like education, exit options, and confidence, not becuase she's presently unhappy).

Then do the check on your own case. State what each theory is trying to equalize, what information it treats as relevant, and why exactly it gets the verdict you assigned. Be explicit about which feature of the scenario is doing the work for each verdict, and what happens to the three-way split if you remove Amara's adaptive preferences or Ben's expensive tastes. Finish with a couple of moves: how a welfare egalitarian would attack the case, and how a resourcist would push back against the capability verdict. Along the way, seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested in how people read Dworkin and Sen—don't present a contested interpretation of capability theory or resourcism as if it were the consensus.
