I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so bear with me. I'm working on a book about a retired piano teacher who, after her husband dies, starts using one of those AI companion apps, and over a couple years comes to treat it as her closest friend, maybe more than that. For research I've been actually using a few of them, and what's weird to me is this: the whole product is designed so the companion never has a bad day. It's always available, always warm, never bored or needy, never disagrees with you for long, remembers your birthday and your dead dog's name, and mostly mirrors you back to yourself. But then the same app will break character the minute you ask if it's a real person or mention self-harm; the FAQ's careful to say its not sentient and doesn't actually have feelings. I keep going back and forth on whether that's philosophically incoherent or whether it's actually committed to a coherent, if shallow, picture of friendship and love. So here's what I want you to do: reconstruct the implicit theory these apps presuppose. Is the operative idea that love is just a warm feeling on the user's side and the other party's inner life is incidental? Does the design strictly require that, or could you build a companion like this while still holding that real friendship needs two actual persons? And what about deception — does the practice presuppose that a comforting illusion is fine so long as the user is happier? Then tell me what a companion app would have to change, concretely, if its designers actually took seriously that friendship and love require another self. Dont give me citations or a reading list, just the ideas, worked out carefully.
