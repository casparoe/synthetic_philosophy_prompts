Six months ago my daughter was identified as deaf, and since then I have been struck by how two conversations about signed language run on parallel tracks without ever meeting. Among linguists and much of the Deaf community, it is treated as settled that a signed language such as ASL is a natural language in the full sense: it has a phonological level organized around handshape, movement, and location; it has productive morphology and syntax; children acquire it on roughly the same maturational timetable as spoken languages; and it can support abstract thought and literacy when a child is given timely, rich access to it. Among several clinicians and relatives, however, I keep encountering a different picture. Our ENT specialist and an early-intervention coordinator have both described spoken language as the real driver of cognitive and social development, and have presented signing as a helpful bridge or compensation rather than an equal end in itself.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is no longer fundamentally empirical. I suspect the two sides are answering different questions under the same word "language": one side means something like "a complete grammatical system that a mind can acquire and use," while the other means something like "speech-based symbolic communication that connects a child to the wider hearing world." If that is right, the public debate persists because the dispute is partly verbal but also partly substantive—a disagreement about what a child needs to flourish, and about which sorts of evidence count as evidence of flourishing.

I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this impasse rather than a verdict about who is right about signed languages. Is my hypothesis correct, or is there a genuine empirical or formal question still open—about the role of modality in first-language acquisition, about the consequences of delayed language access, or about how one should compare spoken and signed literacy—that neither side has formulated cleanly? And what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a sharper distinction, a different kind of study, a change of question, or an acknowledgment that the sides rest on incompatible starting intuitions about what a language is for? I am not looking for a survey of sign language linguistics; I am looking for an account of why a conclusion that seems settled in one community has so little purchase in another.
