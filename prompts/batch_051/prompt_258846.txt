I'm sitting in on a metaethics seminar, and I've got a whole semester of the professor's judgements but I still can't reverse-engineer her taste. The course works through twelve hard cases (trolley variants, a deathbed promise, punishing an innocent person to stop a riot, that kind of thing), and after each unit she says how many of the twelve a theory gets right by her own lights. I also counted the moving parts in each theory. Her ranking, best to worst:

- Act utilitarianism. One principle (maximize utility). Gets 5 of 12. She calls it the most beautiful thing in the course, "one principle and you get a whole moral world." The seven misses don't seem to count against it much.
- Two-level consequentialism. Two principles (an act principle plus a rule-selection principle). Gets 7 of 12. "A real trade; each part earns its keep."
- A three-part deontological package (a duty principle, a conflict-resolution clause, and a tie-breaker). Gets 9 of 12. "Starting to feel like a basket, but each part still does real work."
- A four-principle contractualist hybrid. Gets 11 of 12. She calls it curve-fitting.
- A five-principle commonsense pluralism. Gets 11 of 12 (the fifth principle buys zero new cases), and she says it "gets everything by stipulation, so it gets credit for nothing."

Notice the pattern: from one principle up through four, each extra principle buys exactly two new cases. So it could be that any extra principle has to buy more than two, or it could be that she just always prefers fewer principles and the pattern is a coincidence.

Next week's theory: three principles — a welfare principle, a respect principle, and an equality principle — that gets all 12 cases right. On pure efficiency it's the best thing on the table: fewer principles than two of the theories she panned, and more cases right than anything else we've read. But she has never loved anything with three principles as much as the one- and two-principle theories.

What do you predict she'll say about it? Is she running lexicographic parsimony (fewer principles always wins; fit only tie-breaks), a flat exchange rate (each extra principle has to buy more than two new cases), or is her real complaint about ad hocness — principles reverse-engineered to hit the cases — rather than about the number per se? Which earlier theory is the closest analog, and does that depend on which rule you're attributing to her? Finally, if her reaction next week wouldn't settle it, give me a follow-up case that would.
