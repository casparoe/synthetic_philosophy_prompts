Here are the criteria I use for deciding whether a casual social commitment amounts to a promise — the kind whose breach the other party may reasonably resent and which calls for an apology or some other repair, rather than merely a later correction:

1. The agent performed a conventional act of commitment (accepted, agreed, RSVP'd, confirmed) rather than merely predicting, describing an intention, or expressing a hope.
2. The other party reasonably took the act to be a commitment and reasonably shaped their plans around it.
3. The commitment's content is determinate enough that later nonperformance can be identified as such.
4. The agent had meaningful control over the relevant performance.
5. The agent intended to be taken as committing, or at least accepted that the other party's expectations would be formed by the act.

Now consider this case. A colleague sends a calendar invitation for a twenty-five-minute one-on-one, with the usual 'Yes / Maybe / No' response options. You respond 'Maybe' and add a note: 'Tentative — will do my best to be there for the first ten minutes.' The colleague reads this as an undertaking to appear for the opening segment, passes up another time, and organizes the start of the meeting around your being there. You join eight minutes late, leave after six minutes, and say nothing until asked. When the colleague says you failed to do what you had promised, you answer that 'tentative' and 'will do my best' promised only an effort, not attendance, and that your effort was genuine.

Does your response qualify as a promise under the five criteria? Take each criterion in turn and say where it yields a clear answer, where it underdetermines the answer, and what further fact or principle would settle it. Then say whether the case shows that the criteria themselves need revision — in particular, whether a promise to try is best understood as a promise whose content is an effort, as a conditional promise, or as no promise at all, and how that classification should bear on the lateness.
