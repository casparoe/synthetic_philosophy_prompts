I'm a novelist, and I've been brooding over how much I can trust my first-person narrators when they explain there own motives. The philosophy I keep circling is the reliability of introspection: on one side, the idea that you have privileged access to your own mental states, like reading off a private screen; on the other, the idea that introspection is constructive, that we often don't know our actual reasons and just confabulate plausible stories after the fact, using the same behavioral evidence about ourselves that an outside observer would have. Dostoevsky's Notes from Underground feels like the test case. The usual reading treats the Underground Man as brutally lucid about himself—a man who knows exactly what he's doing and does it anyway out of spite. I'm interested in the opposite pressure. He is constantly introspecting, explaining his spite before we've seen it, narrating his motives in real time, announcing why he's about to do the humiliating thing, and he's both alarmingly self-aware and obviously wrong about himself.

So here's the prompt: give me a reading of Notes from Underground as a study of introspective unreliability. Point to the scenes where the book dramatizes the constructive, confabulating picture of self-knowledge most sharply—the billiard-room officer, the Zverkov dinner, and the Liza episode seem to me to do slightly different work—and say what each one contributes. Then say where the book resists that picture: are there places where his introspection is plainly accurate, and how is a reader supposed to tell the difference between those places and the confabulations? Since I'm a novelist, I also want to know what the form achieves that a philosophy paper can't: how does the fact that he's writing for an imagined audience, performing his self-examination rather than simply having it, change the philosophical point? And be honest about the ways the novel doesn't fit the theory—I don't want Dostoevsky reduced to an illustration.
