Long-running argument in my reading group and I'm hoping a diagnosis will keep us from just repeating ourselves. For months we've been stuck on whether results from computational complexity actually matter for epistemology and philosophy of mind. One camp says: if the inferences your theory of rationality requires are NP-hard—say, computing the posterior a Bayesian would compute, or finding a shortest proof of a given formula—then no finite physical agent can comply, so the theory isn't merely idealized, it's false as a claim about what an agent can do even in principle. The other camp says: so what? Complexity is an implementation detail. An 'ought' that can't be reached still does normative work, the way physics proceeds with frictionless planes and point masses; idealization doesn't falsify the norm, it just tells you actual agents will approximate it badly in finite time. We've reached the point where both sides can predict the other's moves, and I cant tell whether this is (a) a verbal dispute about the word 'rational,' (b) a genuine normative disagreement, or (c) a hidden empirical or formal question about what idealizations are allowed to ignore. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, say what, if anything, would count as progress given that diagnosis, and be explicit about how confident you are in the diagnosis and what the single point is you're least sure of.
