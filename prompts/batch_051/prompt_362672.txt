I'm taking a course on the philosophy of combinatorial games this term, and we spent a whole lecture on strategy-stealing arguments. The proof for Hex — assume for contradiction that the second player has a winning strategy, have the first player make an arbitrary first move, then "steal" the second player's strategy afterward while treating the extra stone as harmless — is easy to follow mechanically. But my professor said the interesting part isn't the proof, it's the hidden assumption: the argument only works if having an extra move or extra piece can never be bad for you. He called this the strategy-stealing assumption, and said it's a substantive monotonicity condition, not a logical freebie.

I don't think I fully understand what that condition amounts to. In Hex, extra stones obviously can't hurt, but in chess an extra move can hurt, right? Zugzwang is the standard example, but I can't tell whether zugzwang is the deep reason the argument fails or just the usual symptom. Also, I remember Hex is finite and has no draws, and that seems to matter: without draws the proof gives you a win rather than just "not a loss"? What I'd really like is an explanation of the conceptual issue under the strategy-stealing assumption: what exactly it says, why the proof is worthless without it, why it holds in games like Hex and Chomp but not chess, and how the no-draws condition fits in.
