I'm a clerk on our state supreme court, and I'd like you to act as an expert in jurisprudence and the rule of law. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming the possible principles that could fit our past cases before you commit to any of them.

Our court's track record on old laws that have barely ever been enforced—desuetude stuff, though we've never actually used that word in an opinion—is a bit of a mess. Here are the past decisions:

1. Sunday-trading case. A 1902 statute banned selling "all goods, wares and merchandise" on Sundays, except fresh food and medicine. It had basically never been enforced for seventy years, until a big-box store started opening Sundays and a citizen sued to enforce it. We refused to apply it. The gist of the opinion was that everyone had long treated the law as dead, and reviving it now would unfairly surprise people who had ordered their lives around Sunday shopping.

2. Fortune-telling case. A 1911 ordinance made it a misdemeanor to take money for "telling fortunes, palmistry, astrology, or pretending to exercise any occult or magic power." There were almost no prosecutions after the 1950s. Then an online psychic network set up a call center in our state. The city charged them, and we upheld enforcement. We emphasized that they were a commercial operation, that the fraud-prevention interest was still live, and that they hadn't shown they actually knew about the law and relied on it being dead.

3. Indecent-performance case. A 1927 statute punished "indecent, lewd, or obscene" public shows. It had lain unenforced for decades until a burlesque troupe got charged. We refused to apply it, but the opinion was all about vagueness—the terms were too indefinite to give fair notice—and we explicitly said we were not deciding whether nonenforcement alone could kill an otherwise clear old statute.

4. Loitering case. A 1933 "disorderly persons and night-walkers" ordinance, unenforced for half a century, got revived to shut down a late-night art fair. We refused. The opinion said the enforcement was surprising and selective, and that there was no current public interest in dusting off the old language.

The new case: a 1904 city ordinance makes it illegal to "hawk, peddle, or vend any goods or victuals on the public streets" without a permit. The city hasn't required street-vending permits in living memory, and a whole food-truck economy has grown up without them. The city has been issuing health permits, doing safety inspections, and collecting taxes from these trucks, while never once demanding the old permit. Now restaurants and some residents want the city to enforce the ordinance to cap the number of trucks and limit their locations. City hall says the law is on the books and they have to enforce it until the council repeals it.

Our judge wants a memo: what should we decide if we want to be consistent with our own precendents? Which of the four cases is the closest analog, and under each plausible reading of our track record, what rule would we be adopting and how would it come out for the food trucks? Then give me your best guess, and tell me what further case would cleanly separate the readings. She also wants a paragraph on the rule-of-law tension here—not being a gotcha legal system, but also not having judges quietly repeal laws by ignoring them.

Don't worry about citations or real-world doctrine; just treat our past decisions as the law you have to reconcile.
