I keep running into the same analogy in discussions of solar geoengineering, and I would like it audited before I accept the conclusion it is used to support. The analogy runs like this: putting reflective aerosols in the stratosphere to hold down global temperature is like giving a feverish patient a drug that lowers only the fever. The underlying infection is left untreated; the fever is not the disease itself but the body's response to it; suppressing the fever lets the patient feel well enough to postpone the harder cure; and if the drug is stopped suddenly, the fever returns faster and worse than before, which people map onto termination shock. The conclusion drawn from this is that solar geoengineering is at best a distraction and at worst a trap, so the only responsible stance is to refuse it and pursue rapid decarbonization now.

I want a committed audit of this analogy, not a survey of positions. Build the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the fever, the infection, the drug, the withdrawal, the patient's felt wellness, and the doctor. Then identify the points where the mapping breaks in ways that matter for the intended conclusion, and distinguish those from disanalogies that matter less. Finally, say whether the refusal conclusion survives once those failures of correspondence are on the table. If you think the analogy is doing most of the argumentative work, say so plainly; if you think a different analogy from roughly the same medical domain, such as insulin for a diabetic or a temporary bypass while surgery is arranged, is better, defend that as your actual view rather than merely mentioning it as an option.
