I have been working on iterated amplification and distillation as an approach to building aligned systems, and I keep finding myself pulled in two directions. The theory has strong systematic virtues. Instead of specifying our values explicitly, we begin with a human who can judge small pieces of a problem, recursively split hard judgments into subtasks that stay within human competence, and then train a fast model to imitate the resulting amplified process. If each amplification step is checked and each distillation step faithfully copies the aligned process's outputs, then alignment and competence are preserved by induction, giving a scalable training signal without ever solving value specification. Yet I also have a firm intuition that something essential is lost in distillation. The student learns to reproduce the teacher's surface behavior on the training distribution, not the underlying judgment with its uncertainty and responsiveness to correction. The difference resembles the difference between someone who follows a procedure and someone who has internalized the reasons for it, and in novel cases, exactly where alignment matters most, the difference should surface as a failure of judgment. I also suspect amplification can fragment the values it is meant to preserve, since approving each decomposed subtask need not amount to approving the whole.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want a negotiated verdict. Is my intuition tracking a real gap in the theory, or is it romanticizing genuine understanding in a way that would matter only if it changed behavior, which training on behavior already addresses? What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, and what would it take for the theory to be amended or abandoned? Is there a reading of preserving alignment and competence on which both sides survive?
