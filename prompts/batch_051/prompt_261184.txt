I'm drafting a short essay on John Maynard Keynes's A Treatise on Probability, and the part I keep coming back to is Chapter Six, The Weight of Arguments. As I read it, Keynes claims that probability is a relation between a proposition and a body of evidence, and that this relation need not be numerically measurable. He then introduces weight of argument as a seperate thing, roughly the amount of relevant evidence that the probability is based on, and he suggests that new evidence can increase the weight of an argument even when it leaves the probability unchanged, or moves it neither toward certainty nor away from it. My essay's main claim is that this idea captures something important about scientific inference that gets lost when we insist on collapsing everything into a posterior probability or a p-value: a conclusion based on two clean, well-controlled experiments can be more robust than one based on a hundred messy observational studies, even if both point in the same direction, because the weight of the evidence is different. I also want to argue that Keynes's point is not just a psychological observation about confidence but a logical feature of the evidence.

But I'm not sure the Treatise supports that last move, especially since he seems to deny that weight itself is on the same scale as probability, and I don't know whether he thought weight could make one rational bettor act differently from another without collapsing weight into a probability. What I'd like is comments on the essay idea: is my reading of Chapter Six plausible or am I treating the weight of arguments as a more developed theory than it actually is there? Would extending it to talk about robustness in empirical science be a fair use of Keynes, or does it smuggle in a modern concern he doesn't have?
