For a type-design studio's internal design review, construct a decision tree that sorts a reviewer's verdicts about particular marks into one of five positions on what makes the lowercase 'a' an 'a'. The instrument will be used to settle, for any disputed glyph, whether it should be treated as an 'a', as a different letter, or as a non-letter mark — without the reviewer ever being asked to name or accept a theory.

The five positions, which the tree must be able to reach, are:

1. Essential-form: there is a single underlying form of 'a' that every genuine 'a' instantiates, however imperfectly.
2. Prototype: 'a' is a graded category organized around one or a few central exemplars, and marks are more or less 'a' according to their resemblance to those exemplars.
3. Script-role: a mark is an 'a' just in case it plays the a-role inside its writing system, so a visually identical mark in another script need not be an 'a'.
4. Conventionalist: 'a'-hood is fixed by the practices and standards of the relevant community of users, not by any shape or feature that floats free of those practices.
5. Style-relativized: whether a mark is an 'a' is relative to the conceptual grid of the style or font in which it occurs; there is no fully style-free answer, and recognition balances categorial sameness against stylistic consistency. (This is the kind of position the Letter Spirit project was designed to make vivid.)

Build the instrument from the logical space first and the names second. Identify the independent questions that actually separate these positions — for example, whether membership is all-or-nothing, whether identity is fixed by shape or by role, and whether any answer can be given apart from a particular style — and lay out the combinations of answers they allow, noting which combinations are occupied by the five positions and which are unoccupied. For each unoccupied combination, say briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

At least these six cases must be used as branch material at or near the first few levels:

- the double-storey 'a' of a serif text face;
- the single-storey 'a' of handwriting and many italics;
- the Cyrillic small a (U+0430), which can be visually identical to the Latin 'a' but is a distinct character;
- the Greek alpha;
- the at-sign @;
- a hand-painted shop-sign 'a' that readers can identify only from the word around it.

You may add up to two further cases if the logical space demands them. Phrase every node as a concrete yes/no question about one of the cases that a reviewer can answer without knowing the five positions — for example, "In a Cyrillic text, is a mark visually indistinguishable from a Latin double-storey a the letter a?" — and under no circumstances ask reviewers to self-identify with a named view.

The audience is a mixed group of type designers, art directors, and one skeptical engineer who works on font encodings; they have about ten minutes in a design review and mostly need to decide whether the disputed glyph should inherit the encoding, kerning, and sorting behavior of 'a'. Keep the tree to between six and eight decision nodes and no more than six leaves.

Present three things in order: a compact logical-space table showing which positions occupy which answer combinations; the tree itself as a numbered sequence of yes/no questions leading to leaves, with each leaf given a two-sentence statement of the position as a reviewer who lands there would hold it plus one further disputed glyph that would most strain it; and a separation report stating which leaves issue identical verdicts on all six cases and differ only in their underlying rationale, and exactly what additional sort of case or question would be needed to split them.

Write the instrument so it stands entirely on case judgments: do not include citations, a reading list, designers' names, or any appeal to typographic history.
