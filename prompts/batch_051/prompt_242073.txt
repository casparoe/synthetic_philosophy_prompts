I've been on a real Hofstadter kick again and I've mostly talked myself into his knob-twiddling model of creativity: creativity is variations on a theme, where the theme is some existing pattern or style with parameters you can twist, and the actual creative act is noticing which knobs exist, which ones are load-bearing, and which corners of the resulting space are worth exploring—not some ex nihilo flash of novelty. I want you to attack this properly, not just play devil's advocate. What would have to be true for it to be wrong, or at least importantly incomplete? Which actual cases of creative work put the most pressure on it, what would a committed knob-twiddling person have to count as a genuine defeat rather than just saying that's still turning a knob you haven't named yet, and are there results from cognitive science, AI, or the history of math/science/art that would seriously damage it? Also, what does this view predict that rival accounts of creativity don't, so that a case could actually count against it instead of being compatible with everything? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether the view as it's smartest defenders state it is basically insulated from counterexamples—and if it is, whether that's a bug rather than a feature. I find myself least sure about cases where the space of knobs itself had to be built, like the invention of zero or the jump from arithmetic to set theory, so I'd weight defeaters in that direction more heavily.
