I've noticed a pattern I don't have a word for, and I'm not sure whether it's one phenomenon or two. Picture a computer program that has to assign probabilities to mathematical claims it hasn't proven yet — things like "this program halts," "the millionth digit of pi is a 7," or "there is no proof of P=NP shorter than a googol symbols." It improves over time, proving things and running further computations. Two things seem to happen with every procedure of this kind I can actually write down.

First, at any given moment its current probabilities are almost certainly incoherent in the old betting sense: there's some combination of bets, each at a price the program would currently accept or offer, which loses money for the program now matter how the facts turn out. And the incoherence isn't only over weird self-referential sentences; sometimes it's over ordinary combinations like "A and B" versus "A" and "B."

Second, if the program keeps thinking, its probabilities on individual statements stop jumping around and settle in ways that look sensible for whatever it has spent a lot of time on. But the traps don't vanish so much as migrate: at any finite time there are still combinations of sentences, usually ones it hasn't gotten around to, where the old incoherence reappears. No finite amount of thinking removes all the traps; only the infinite limit would.

The odd part is that the exploitable moments and the convergence feel like two sides of one process, not a bug where the incoherence is just a temporary artifact.
