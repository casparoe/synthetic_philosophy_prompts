Two friends got into an argument at a party about whether humans are any less 'sphexish' than the digger wasp Dennett liked to talk about. Quick background: the wasp paralyzes a cricket, leaves it at the burrow mouth, goes inside to check things, comes back out and drags it in; if you move the cricket a few inches while she's inside, she redoes the whole pointless inspection over and over and never just carries it in. (1) The first said that's exactly why we find it funny — a person would notice the cricket moved and skip the check. (2) The other said no, we do the same with more layers: when our scripts fail we run a 'what went wrong' script, and when that fails we run a 'what went wrong with my what-went-wrong script' script. (3) She came back that he's missing the point: the wasp cannot take its own routine as an object and revise it, but a human can catch himself mid-ritual, feel silly, and stop. (4) He said catching yourself and stopping is itself just another disposition firing, so she's only moved the sphex up one level; if she wants a self that isn't a bundle of dispositions, she's smuggling in a ghost. (5) She said he'd turned a real bug into a metaphor and then demanded the metaphor produce a self; the real difference is one of degree — humans respond to more of the situation — and degree is exactly what matters for the kind of flexibility worth calling free will. Neither would budge, and I honestly couldnt decide who'd won. Please go through the exchange move by move, say who is right about what, and say who is more right overall.
