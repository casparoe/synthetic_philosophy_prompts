I just got home from a three-hour argument with a friend about whether a large language model "actually understands" anything it says, and I've been turning it over all evening. His whole case rested on the old Darwin/Turing inversion: Darwin showed you don't need an intelligent designer to get something that looks designed—natural selection, which has no understanding at all, builds organisms that look purpose-built—and Turing showed you don't need comprehension to get competence—a machine plodding through simple rules can do arithmetic or play chess as if it knew what it was doing. So, my friend says, a chatbot is the same move once more: it can be fluent and useful without understanding a single word, and whoever insists there has to be understanding "in there" is making the watchmaker mistake that Darwin already killed off. I pushed back that Turing's machines were hand-coded rule-followers, whereas a language model is not following rules anyone wrote: it gets trained on a huge pile of human text, and the network ends up doing something that looks a lot like having an internal model of the world. My friend replied that this just pushes the inversion down one level—the training process is mindless too, so now you have competence without comprehension twice over, and my feeling that "something must be in there" is no better than the feeling that a sunset needs a painter. I suspect he's overextending the idea, but I couldn't pin down exactly which premis is doing the work now. So: does the Darwin/Turing strange inversion survive a system whose competence is learned rather than designed? Please walk through which premise the language-model case actually puts pressure on, whether there is a repaired, weaker version that still says something true, and whether my friend is entitled to his conclusion.
