I am a novelist, and I sit on a committee that awards public grants to individual writers. Each year, the application asks us to state the expected public benefit of the proposed work, and each year I read some version of a sentence I have also written myself: that literary fiction exercises a reader's capacity to imagine what it is like to be someone else, especially someone whose circumstances and values are unlike the reader's own, and that this capacity is a civic good. I want to believe it. I am afraid I believe it mainly because it is flattering and because no one ever asks what it would look like if it were false.

I would like you to treat that claim as a hypothesis rather than a piety. Construct the strongest feasible test of the more ambitious version of it — not that reading a novel leaves a person momentarily more charitable on a questionnaire, but that the sustained practice of reading literary fiction develops something durable and transferable: a reader becomes better at noticing, representing, and acting on what matters to people whose evaluative perspective is foreign to them. Tell me precisely what result would count as evidence for the claim, what result would count against it, and what an apparently positive result could be explained by other than the novel itself — the leisure, the narrative content, the book's prestige, the reader's prior sympathies, or the mere act of practicing self-description.

Finally, I want you to be candid about the residue. Suppose the test comes out positive. Would it show that fiction has the kind of value a funding body invokes, or only that it produces a measurable side effect? And if the deepest part of the claim is that novels give a reader something like insight into another human life — not just a reflex but a kind of understanding — can any behavioral test reach that, or does the test only ever measure its byproducts?
