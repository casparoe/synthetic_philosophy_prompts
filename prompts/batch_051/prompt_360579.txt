I've been teaching a unit on collective wrongdoing and I keep running into a problem with the word "complicity." My students and I agree on the easy cases, but the moment someone asks what the word actually means we sound like we're just listing examples. So here's what I want: a working definition of complicity, one that does real classificatory work rather than just tracking a vibe. I'll give you the cases I use in class, and I want you to propose the definition, run it through every case, and tell me where it gives an answer that feels wrong or where it goes silent. Then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. Don't just say the cases are context-dependent and leave it there; push for the best single definition you can get.

The cases: a getaway driver who waits outside a robbery and genuinely doesn't know that one of the robbers is armed; an engineer who writes one subroutine of a car's emissions software, knowing the point of the whole system is to defeat the test but never seeing how it fits together; a person who buys a phone knowing that some of the cobalt in the battery was probably mined by children; a shareholder who buys stock in a mining company after reading credible reports of forced labor in its supply chain and wants only the dividend; a bystander who watches a stranger get beaten and walks on without calling the police when they easily could; an adult who accepts rent money from a sibling, knowing the sibling earns it selling opioids without a prescription; a scheduling clerk at a clinic who arranges appointments for procedures she considers wrong but performs none of them herself; someone who repeats a false rumor that another person started, not knowing it's false but knowing it will do serious damage; and a social media company that doesn't create misinformation but whose recommender system keeps amplifying it because it drives engagement.

For what it's worth, I'm confident the getaway driver and the engineer are complicit, and I'm confident the bystander is not. I once would have called the phone buyer and the shareholder not-complicit too, but I can't articulate what separates them from the clerk, and the clerk is the one that actually keeps me up at night. I also have a suspicion, which you should entertain even if you end up rejecting it, that the ordinary word does two seperate jobs: one descriptive, having to do with being in the causal network that produces a wrong, and one condemnatory, having to do with being morally on the hook for it. The hard cases are hard because those two jobs come apart. If you buy that, give me the two definitions and say which of my cases move between them; if you don't, tell me why a single definition can do both jobs.
