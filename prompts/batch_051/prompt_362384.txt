I teach a low-level undergrad course on language and mind, and next week we're doing writing systems. The thing I want the session to land is that an alphabet isn't a neutral recording device: it's a theory of which sound differences in a language count, and how phonemic an alphabet is turns out to be a question about what the script chooses to encode — phonemes, allophones, morphology, sometimes history. I've got about 18 students and 80 minutes, no TA. Design a hands-on activity, ideally a game with teams, invented scripts, and a scoring scheme, where they run into this themselves before I name it. I want the rules to force the key decision on them: do you give [pʰ] and [p] one symbol or two, and do you spell a plural suffix by its surface sound or by an underlying form, and what breaks downstream of each choice. Include a tiny artificial language — a dozen words or so, a few minimal pairs, one allophonic rule, one morphophonemic alternation, that's plenty. Then give me rules and materials, a timeline, a scoring scheme that doesn't need real prizes, a debrief that maps whatever went wrong in the game onto the theoretical point, and an honest note on what the game can't reproduce about how real scripts evolve. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to fix it on the fly. No lecture up front, and don't announce the takeaway — a script encodes a theory of which sound contrasts matter — until the debrief.
