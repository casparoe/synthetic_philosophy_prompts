I'm writing a term paper on whether reward signals are literal valence — whether the numerical reward in an RL agent is the same kind of thing as pleasure and pain in an animal — and I keep getting stuck on one passage in Sutton and Barto's Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction (section 1.3). They define the reward function like this:

"A reinforcement learning agent's sole objective is to maximize the total reward it receives in the long run. The reward function defines what are the good and bad events for the agent. In a biological system, it would not be inappropriate to identify rewards with pleasure and pain."

They clearly meant this as an formalization move — rewards are just numbers, and the pleasure/pain comment is flagged as applying to biological systems. But here's the case I can't shake. Suppose I build a quadruped robot whose reward function includes a large negative term whenever joint current spikes in the pattern that predicts physical damage to the actuators. Over training it learns to flinch, favor its non-damaged side, and avoid motions that produce those spikes; if you watch it, it looks for all the world like nociception and pain-motivated behavior. If I apply their own passage to this robot: is the negative reward literally pain for it, or is the "in a biological system" qualifier doing real work? And if it is doing work, I want to know what the underlying difference is supposed to be — what makes the same functional signal valence in a rat but mere bookkeeping in the robot. I'm less interested in guessing what Sutton and Barto would say today than in what their text actually commits them to, and whether there's any coherent reading that keeps the formalism while blocking the conclusion that my limping robot suffers.
