I am a novelist, and I want a working definition of "slur" precise enough to guide decisions about which words appear in a manuscript, in whose voice they occur, and with what kind of authorial framing. I am not asking for a catalogue of offensive words or for references to the scholarly literature; I want the underlying concept, so I can reason about cases I have not already settled.

Here are the cases the definition should classify. (a) A character, out of contempt, directs a well-known racial epithet at another character. (b) During an argument, one character calls another a liar and a coward. (c) A character who belongs to the targeted group uses the epithet from (a) to or about another member of that group, not out of contempt but as a gesture of shared identity or defiance. (d) The narrator quotes another character's insult without endorsing it, so the word appears on the page only as mentioned: He called me an ___ . (e) In a political novel, a character denounces an opponent as a "thug" and a "globalist," and the surrounding context makes clear that these words are being used to do the work of racial or ethnonational contempt. (f) A character in a historical novel uses a term that was, in the depicted period, the ordinary and respectful designation of a group and is now widely rejected as demeaning; I have in mind something like a once-standard clinical label for a disability, but the details do not matter.

My unreflective verdicts are that (a) is clearly a slur and (b) is clearly not. About (c) through (f) I am genuinely undecided, and I suspect they force a choice about whether a slur is located in the word itself, the speaker's intention, the target's group membership, the context of utterance, or the audience's uptake.

Please commit to one definition and hold it. Run it through every case, and where it gives a verdict that seems wrong or no verdict at all, either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict rather than laying out the alternatives. Format your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the proposed working definition; (2) your verdicts on (a) and (b); (3) your verdicts on (c) and (d); (4) your verdicts on (e) and (f); (5) the single verdict you hold least confidently, with a one-sentence statement of whether you would revise the definition or defend that verdict.
