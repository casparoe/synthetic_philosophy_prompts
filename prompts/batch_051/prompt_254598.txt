I work at a foundation that gives about forty grants a year in global health and development, and we've always told ourselves we're picking the projects that do the most good. I've been reading James Lenman's paper Consequentialism and Cluelessness, and his argument seems to be that for almost any action we can't actually know the long-run consequences well enough to compare alternatives—there are just too many unpredictable ripple effects. If we took that seriously, we'd be paralysed. I don't think my coworkers are going to read Lenman, but they will use a checklist if it feels practical.

Please write a decision procedure my team can actually run on a grant proposal, grounded in Lenman's worry without being paralyzed by it. The procedure should say what questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where we should stop and reject, and where we should hand the decision to a person because it's a values question rather than a prediction question. It should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then apply it to two proposals: a bednet distribution program with decent short-term evidence on child mortality, and a policy advocacy campaign to raise alcohol taxes in several countries, where the effects are likely to be big but very diffuse and hard to predict. Include at least one place where the procedure gives an answer we won't like.
