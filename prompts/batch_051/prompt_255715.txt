I am drafting a short statement of my philosophical commitments about parenting, children's rights, and the state's role, and I want to know how well the pieces fit together before I rely on the package. Here is what I would sign:

1. Parenting should be licensed, for adoptive and biological parents alike: the state may screen prospective parents, and a child should not be placed with, or left in the care of, adults who could not pass such screening.

2. The principal justification for licensing is not merely the prevention of abuse. It is the child's right to an open future—the interest children have in reaching adulthood with a broad enough range of life options that they, rather than their upbringing, can settle what kind of life to lead.

3. The right to an open future is not violated whenever a child's options are narrowed. It is violated only when options are narrowed in a way that damages the child's eventual capacity to reflect on, revise, and choose among ways of life. A child raised with particular values has fewer options but may still have the right fully honored.

4. Parents may raise children in demanding and value-laden forms of life—strict religious practice, intensive training in a single art or sport, maintenance of a small language—provided the child remains able, in time, to step back from that form of life and leave it.

5. But the state may and should intervene when a parental decision will predictably lock a child out of whole domains of adult life, such as refusing all schooling, socially isolating the child, or refusing clearly needed medical care.

6. Licensing should enforce only a good-enough floor: no abuse or serious neglect, adequate material and emotional provision, and a demonstrated disposition to treat the child as a separate person. Licensing should not scrutinize the parents' values, and it need not certify that a home will maximize the child's future range of options.

7. For an adult, the bare fact that a choice forecloses future options never counts against it: vows, lifelong marriages, irreversible medical choices, and similar self-binding commitments can be perfectly reasonable.

I want you to test the set for internal consistency rather than argue for or against any single commitment. Go through the list and identify every pair of commitments that is in genuine tension, as opposed to merely appearing so. Rank the tensions by severity, and for each say which commitments pull against each other, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision would cost. Then identify at least one pair that looks incompatible at first glance but on reflection is not, and explain why it is not.
