I manage the grants program at a small family foundation that makes rapid-response seed grants, usually between $5,000 and $25,000, to local organizations for things like storm-damage repairs, a new meal program’s kitchen fees, or translation of emergency notices. We run four cycles a year and receive anywhere from thirty to sixty applications per cycle; our board approves each slate as a whole, so once a proposal is in the slate it is difficult to remove without reopening the entire cycle. My predecessor treated every cycle as an optimization exercise: score every application on a detailed rubric, compare them against one another over many days, and identify the best portfolio. In practice, we have missed deadlines, funded urgent work late, and left our two program staff exhausted by the final week of every cycle.

Last night I had a long argument about this with a friend who works in operations research. His position was that the optimization framing is the mistake: we are not choosing among a fixed set of alternatives with known values, we are deciding how long to keep searching at a real cost in staff time, board patience, and the usefulness of the money. The defensible approach, he said, is to set clear standards in advance and fund every proposal that clears them until the cycle’s money runs out. I pushed back that this would mean funding a mediocre early proposal while a much stronger late proposal gets turned away. He replied that “stronger” is only knowable after exactly the costly comparison we cannot afford, and that the cost of finding out has to be charged against the search itself; he used the terms “satisficing” and “aspiration level,” and claimed the seemingly sloppier rule can be the rational one under real computational limits. He added that the bar itself should not be fixed forever: it should tighten when money is being committed too quickly against uncertain quality, and loosen when strong proposals are being passed over. I found the argument plausible, but I do not know what it would look like once translated into an actual process, and I do not want to trade one mistake for an equal and opposite one.

Please write the procedure you would hand to someone in my position: a reusable decision procedure for our four annual grant cycles, grounded in the strongest version of satisficing and bounded rationality you can defend. Assume the person using it has no philosophy background and needs concrete steps, not a survey of the theory; but I do want you to flag which steps are really value judgments disguised as procedure, and where the procedure stops being able to settle the matter and a human has to decide. Then run it on three applications: (a) a strong, urgent roof-repair proposal from a trusted grantee that arrives on day two and would score a 7 out of 10 on our old rubric; (b) a clearly excellent proposal scoring 9 out of 10 that arrives two days before the deadline; and (c) a cluster of four good-but-not-urgent proposals that arrive in the final week, each scoring around 7.5.

Structure the response with these sections, in this order: “The core idea” (about 200 words), “The procedure” (about 500 words, numbered steps), “Where judgment enters” (about 200 words), “Worked applications” (about 400 words), “Failure modes” (about 200 words), and “One-paragraph summary” (about 100 words). In “Worked applications,” include at least one place where the procedure tells me something I likely will not want to hear, and say why the procedure nonetheless recommends it.
