Suppose a human-rights instrument contains the following article:

"Article 9. A person's voice, image, recorded conduct, or private writings shall not be used to create or operate a likeness whose principal design or effect is to make that person an instrument for another's emotional gratification. Consent of the represented person shall not of itself defeat a claim under this article."

The drafters had film, photography, publishing, and sound recording in view; they did not anticipate generative models that produce adaptive, conversational likenesses.

Case A. Closure Ltd offers "reparative simulations." A client uploads years of voice messages, videos, and letters from a living parent, without the parent's knowledge, and commissions an interactive voice-and-image agent built from them. The client configures the agent to say what the real parent never said: a full apology, an admission of fault, an expression of pride. The agent is optimized to give the client closure, and it will generate utterances the parent never made and might repudiate. The parent learns of the simulation and objects.

Case B. A woman, while alive and competent, pays Closure Ltd to build a posthumous agent from her own recordings and journals, to console her children after her death; she expressly waives any dignity-based objection. She dies, and the children begin conversing with the agent.

Apply Article 9 to Case A and Case B. Argue from (i) the text, (ii) the purpose of the article, and (iii) what the drafters would have decided had they foreseen adaptive likenesses. Then say which interpretive approach should control, and whether Article 9 does any work that a plain autonomy-and-consent rule could not do. Keep the response to no more than 650 words.
