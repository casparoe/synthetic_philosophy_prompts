Write a dialogue between an imprecise probabilist and a strict Bayesian about whether a rational agent's doxastic state should be represented by a set of probability functions or by a single precise probability function. The strict Bayesian should press three objections. First, dilation: on an imprecise representation, learning relevant, unproblematic evidence can leave an agent less opinionated than before, which seems to reverse the expected effect of evidence. Second, sequential choice: imprecise decision rules appear to permit choosing one plan now and abandoning it later without any new evidence, because different choices may be licensed by different members of the representing set. Third, elicitation: under scoring rules or betting arrangements, imprecise credences seem to become behaviorally indistinguishable from some precise credence, raising the worry that they are either idle or secretly precise. The imprecise probabilist should answer each charge and then press the strict Bayesian to account for ambiguity-sensitive preferences of the kind elicited in Ellsberg-style urn cases, without simply redescribing the agent's information or preferences. Both characters should be equally sophisticated, respond to the other's actual arguments rather than shifting to adjacent issues, and end by locating the deepest point of disagreement between them.
