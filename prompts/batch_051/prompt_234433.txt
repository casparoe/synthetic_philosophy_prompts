I'm an econ PhD student and I've noticed I run a policy in everyday life: whenever someone offers to trade with me—a random swap, a used phone at their price, concert tickets, whatever, not a gift or a favor—I treat the offer itself as evidence that my own valuation is inflated or that they know something I don't, and my default is to say no unless I can point to a non-informational reason for the trade (they need cash, they're hedging, different risk tolerances, etc.). A friend says this is silly because plenty of trades are genuinely mutually beneficial and if everyone thought this way the whole economy would sieze up. But isn't that basically what the no-trade theorem says should happen? My rough understanding is: if we're both rational with common priors, and each of us knows the other is rational, we shouldn't be able to find a purely informational trade, so a proposed trade signals that one of us is the sucker, and you should update away from the deal. Is my heuristic defensible? What exactly can I infer from an offer, and how do I tell the cases where accepting makes me an epistemic sucker from the cases where I'm just being paranoid and leaving value on the table? I'd like a version of the rule that keeps whatever is right about it without making me impossible to trade with.
