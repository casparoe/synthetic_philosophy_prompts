I am a high school student reading excerpts from al-Ghazālī’s The Incoherence of the Philosophers, and I am confused about two parts of his discussion of natural causality. In one part, he seems to say that the connection between what we call a cause and what we call an effect is not necessary: fire does not burn cotton by its own power, and death does not follow from decapitation by its own power. God creates the effect at the moment of the cause, and God could create the effect without the cause or the cause without the effect. But later in the same discussion, he also seems to say that God has established a stable pattern or habit in the course of the world, and that we are right to expect fire to burn and food to satisfy hunger because this is how God has willed creation to run.

My question is whether these two strands can be reconciled without making one of them a mere concession to the philosophers. Is the distinction between a habit and a necessary connection doing real work for him, or is it just a verbal escape hatch? If the habit can be broken at any moment by God, how can it ground the kind of reliable expectation he seems to want to preserve? And if the habit is utterly reliable in practice, what is left of the claim that the connection is not necessary?

Please explain the strongest readings on both sides, then say which reconciliation you think is most faithful to what al-Ghazālī is trying to do, and which reconciliation amounts to special pleading.
