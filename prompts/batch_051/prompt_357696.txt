I am a PhD candidate in philosophy of science, and I co-teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Determinism, Prediction, and Chaos" — sixteen students, mostly a mix of philosophy and physics majors, in a seventy-five-minute session. We are about to open the unit on chaos theory, and I would like to begin with a structured in-class activity rather than the usual lecture-style introduction of the Lorenz equations or the logistic map. The goal is to have students feel, before they are handed the vocabulary, the force of two related claims: that a fully deterministic rule can be genuinely unpredictable in practice, in the sense that two trajectories can start from states that are indistinguishable at the precision anyone in the room can actually represent, track one another for a while, and then part company for good; and that an intricate, stable global structure can emerge from an utterly mechanical local procedure whose point-by-point course no one can foresee. I want these ideas to show up first in what students do and observe, not in what I say afterward.

Please design the session as a runnable activity, ideally with two integrated stages — one for sensitive dependence and the butterfly effect, one for strange attractors and the emergence of long-run structure — framed as a single forecasting-and-plotting game. Specify the exact recurrence or rules, the initial conditions to hand out, the rounding and recording conventions, the materials (worksheets, calculators, dice, transparencies, string, or whatever you judge best), and a point scheme that creates real competitive stakes without using money. Include a minute-by-minute timeline for the seventy-five minutes, from setup through cleanup, and a debrief that maps concrete moments in the activity onto the concepts of determinism, sensitive dependence, a predictability horizon, invariant sets, and strange attractors — naming the idea only after the students have already run into it. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, and be frank about which feature of genuine chaotic systems the activity cannot reproduce and where I will have to supply it through explanation afterward.

Make definite choices throughout — one recurrence, one set of initial conditions, one scoring rule, one debrief sequence — and give your reasons for each choice rather than laying out alternatives. I would much rather have a single coherent design I can run on Monday than a survey of things one might do.
