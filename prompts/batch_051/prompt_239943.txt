I spend my days deciding which of the people in my novels actually have an inner life and which are just bodies with dialogue. Some characters I write from inside the skull, so to speak; others never get a single unspoken thought, and readers pick up on the difference immediately. Nobody finds that philosophically dodgy. But if I used the same kind of evidence — speech, movement, what a person notices or misses — to seriously entertain that a real person is empty inside, I'd be told I was either mad or cruel. Is there a principle that makes it fine to deny inner life in fiction but not in real life? The obvious story is that in a novel there is a fact about what the author wrote in and what she left out, and in life there isn't. I'm not sure that survives the conceivability argument: a physical duplicate of me, behaviorally identical and lacking phenomenal consciousness, would likewise involve no authorial fact of the matter. So give me the strongest justification you can for the asymmetry, test it against that duplicate, and then say straight wether I should keep the asymmetry — and if not, which side I give up: the confidence about strangers, or the confidence about my own characters.
