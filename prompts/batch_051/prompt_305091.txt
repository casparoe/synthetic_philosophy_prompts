The following are notes for a short essay I am drafting on the time-average/ensemble-average distinction in ergodicity economics, and I would be grateful for comments on the argument as it stands.

Consider a gamble in which an agent repeatedly stakes her entire bankroll on a fair coin: if it comes up heads, her wealth is multiplied by 1.5, and if tails, by 0.6. The expected multiplier on any single flip is 1.05, so the ensemble average of her possible wealth grows without bound as the number of flips increases. Yet any actual trajectory is multiplied by 1.5 × 0.6 = 0.9 over every two flips, giving a per-flip time-average multiplier of roughly 0.949 and thus a declining path. My essay argues that the proper lesson to draw from this divergence is narrower than it is often taken to be. The case does not, I maintain, refute expected utility theory: a decision-maker with a concave utility function may already reject such sequences, since the arithmetic expected value of wealth is not the object she is maximizing. What the case exposes is rather a modeling error that arises when payoffs combine multiplicatively and a single agent’s exposure cannot be diversified away. In such settings, the quantity that is additive over time for a single path is logarithmic growth, not wealth, so expected-wealth maximization is the wrong criterion. Time-average growth maximization is best read as a corrective for this class of dynamic choice problems — a substantial corrective, but not a free-standing rival to expected utility theory, and better described as a norm of prudence than as a theorem about optimal choice.

I would especially value comments on three claims. First, is my reconciliation with expected utility theory too concessive? One might think ergodicity economics is presented precisely as a replacement for expected utility reasoning, and I want to know whether a defensible stronger thesis survives once it is granted that growth-rate maximization can be represented by expected logarithmic utility. Second, does my restriction to multiplicative, non-diversifiable wealth dynamics understate the scope of the distinction? Are there non-ergodic structures outside wealth accumulation where the time-average and ensemble-average perspectives come apart in ways that would compel a deeper revision to decision theory? Third, is the concept of prudence doing real work here, or would the essay be stronger if it stated the underlying norm directly in terms of avoiding long-run decline rather than in terms of maximizing geometric growth?
