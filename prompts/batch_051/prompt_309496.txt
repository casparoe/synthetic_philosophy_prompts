I have been trying to work out what is physically real in a gauge theory, and one example keeps troubling me. On one side, I keep reading that gauge freedom is just redundancy in the description: one can add the gradient of a scalar to the vector potential without changing the electric or magnetic fields, and so the potential is often treated as a mere bookkeeping device. On the other side, the Aharonov–Bohm effect seems to show that the potential itself has to be real. An electron passing through a region where the magnetic field is zero still undergoes a measurable phase shift, and that shift is obtained by adding up the vector potential around the loop enclosing the magnetic flux. If the potential were only a dispensable piece of bookkeeping, why would a measurable interference pattern track it? Should I conclude that the potential is real after all, and that the usual view that only gauge-invariant quantities are physical is too strict? Or is something more subtle going on, in which only a loop-shaped or global part of the potential is real while its local values remain surplus? I would like a clear account of what survives as real once gauge freedom is taken into account.
