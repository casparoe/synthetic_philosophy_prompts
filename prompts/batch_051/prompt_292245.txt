I write about science and ideas for a living, and a few weeks back I was at a conference where the hallway talk kept landing on the same question: can a tiny chance of an enormous future good justify enormous sacrifices now? That night I ended up at a bar with two people who'd heard the same talks, and they immediately went at it.

Dana said: think about it this way. If you honestly think there's a one-in-a-billion chance that some project locks in a future with, say, 10^30 lives, then that project has an expected payoff of around 10^21 lives. By your own lights you shouldn't just be willing to spend a lot on it — you should feel pulled to.

Reed said: that's the problem, though. The same move works with a one-in-a-trillion chance of 10^50 lives, and then a one-in-a-quadrillion chance of 10^80 lives. Whatever amount you're about to spend, I can name a bigger number and a smaller probability that swamps it, so expected value never actually tells you where to stop.

Dana said: that's the old mugging worry, but it backfires. If a stranger asks me for my wallet in exchange for an infinite reward, my mistake isn't the arithmetic. It's that I don't credit his story with the probability it would need. Expected value just makes you face up to what your own credences already imply.

Reed said: in the cases people actually fight about, your credences aren't "yours" in any strong sense. A one-in-a-billion chance of a 10^30-life future is a number someone typed into a spreadsheet. When the result flips depending on whether the cell says 10^-9 or 10^-12, and no evidence separates those, the output is just reporting the arbitrary part.

Dana said: vague probabilities are a real problem, but the fix is to sharpen them, not to throw out expected value. And be careful: if you rule that everything below 10^-9 gets treated as zero, you'll end up treating a thousand independent one-in-a-billion chances as zero too, even though jointly they're about one in a million. Strict cutoffs don't stay consistent for long.

Reed said: that's fair, a flat cutoff was shorthand. My point isn't that we should legislate a threshold. It's that when the inputs are unsupported point estimates, running expected value on them and then acting like the answer has authority is the less responsible move. Sometimes the honest output is "not enough to go on."

Dana said: but "not enough to go on" is itself a threshold, just with a friendlier name. Somewhere you're deciding that the rough expected value isn't strong enough to act on. If it's all you have, why is quietly setting it to zero better than acting on your best rough guess?

Reed said: because the entire fanatical result depends on a moral assumption that expected value doesn't question. It treats a tiny risk of harm to actual, existing people and a tiny chance of bringing about an enormous number of merely possible people as the same kind of entry in the same ledger. The math only looks compelling once you've already decided that possible lives count like actual ones.

A text from our editor broke it up before either one capitulated. I've been turning it over since. Could you referee the exchange move by move — who's right about which specific point, where each is overreaching or changing the subject, and who is more right overall? Also, I'd like you to separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested, and not present a contested view as the consensus. And please use American English.
