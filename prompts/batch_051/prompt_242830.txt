I'm taking a course on consequentialism and population ethics this semester, and I keep coming back to a problem about the aggregation of very small harms. The version we discussed asks us to choose between two options: one person is subjected to fifty years of continuous, severe torture, or an astronomically large number of people—a number so large that it stops being intuitively graspable—each receives a single dust speck in the eye, a brief, minor irritation with no lasting effects. A simple additive calculation seems to say the dust specks are worse, because the summed pain across all those people exceeds the one person's enormous pain, yet almost everyone's pre-theoretical judgment is that the torture would be the worse choice. I understand the standard philosophical responses: rejecting the additivity of harms, treating intense suffering as lexically prior to mere annoyance, or reading the example as a reductio of consequentialism. What I do not have a good feel for is what the choice actually looks like from inside a life, and how people would hold themselves to a decision made at that scale.

For a final project, I would like you to write a short story that dramatizes this problem rather than arguing about it in the abstract. The setting is yours to choose, but it should be concrete and lived-in—for instance, a public-health agency weighing a vaccine with a tiny probability of catastrophic side effects against one that guarantees a mild headache for billions of people, or a court trying to decide whether millions of almost imperceptible privacy violations can together outweigh a single brutal wrong. The characters should genuinely reason their way through the tradeoff, and the story should surface the philosophical pressure points without resolving them too neatly: whether harms are the kind of thing that can be summed across separate people at all, whether there is a threshold below which a harm stops counting or counts differently, how the separateness of persons bears on the arithmetic, and what changes if the many know they were selected to bear the small harm—or never learn. I am less interested in a story in which a character delivers a lecture on these points than in one where the dilemma emerges naturally from the plot and the choices the characters face, and where the ending does not pretend the problem has a clean solution.
