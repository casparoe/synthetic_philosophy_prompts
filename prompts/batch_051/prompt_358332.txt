In my ethics and moral psychology class we've been reading about dispositions and moral worth these past weeks, and theres this asymmetry in ordinary judgment I can't stop turning over. Take two officials. The first spent a decade in a graft-heavy procurement office and turned down bribes maybe a dozen times, some big enough to actually matter to her. The second spent the same decade in a job where everyone knew there was no point offering him anything, so he was never tested at all. Both have perfectly clean records. But everyone I've asked, me included, calls the first incorruptible and wouldnt say that about the second — we say he's "never been tested" or "just hasn't been tempted."

I can't tell if that's tracking a real difference between the two people, or just tracking how much evidence we happen to have about them. It's not like the second official is more corruptible, at least as far as anyone knows. Now add a third case: someone who would have taken a bribe if it had ever been offered, but through pure luck never was. If we somehow knew that about him, we wouldn't call him incorruptible despite the clean record — which makes it seem like the label is really about the disposition, not about what actually happened. But then why do we withhold the label from the second official instead of just saying we don't yet know whether he has it?

I want the strongest principle that generates the asymmetry between the first and second officials, tested against that third case. Then give me an honest verdict: should I keep the asymmetry, give it up, or keep something weaker — like the first official deserves more admiration but the word shouldn't track that? And if "incorruptible" does pick out a real property, is the thing we're actually praising always a virtue, or do we sometimes admire something more like rigidity?
