In my philosophy of science course we have been discussing verisimilitude — roughly, how close a false statement can come to the truth — and I have been trying to work out whether my own judgments fit any single principle. I would appreciate an analysis of what, if anything, my verdicts add up to.

Suppose the complete truth about a particular time and place is: the sky is overcast, it is raining, and the temperature is 72°F. Consider these four claims:

(a) The sky is overcast and it is raining.
(b) The sky is overcast, it is raining, and the temperature is 71°F.
(c) The sky is overcast, it is raining, and the temperature is 90°F.
(d) The sky is overcast, it is raining, and it is snowing.

Here are my verdicts. Claim (a) is true but incomplete. I judge (b) closer to the truth than (a): (b) is more complete, and its only error is a one-degree miss. I also judge (a) closer to the truth than (d): the extra claim in (d) is not just false but wrong about which kind of precipitation is occurring, and I would rather stop with two truths than add a conjunct like that. For the same sort of reason, I judge (a) closer than (c). I have no firm view about whether (c) or (d) is closer. I notice that (b) and (d) have the same abstract structure relative to (a) — each conjoins one false claim with the same true partial claim — yet I treat them differently. Is there a single account of closeness to truth that delivers all of these verdicts? What would it say about further cases, such as a claim that adds that the temperature is 72.5°F, or one that adds a false conjunct about an unrelated subject? If no single principle captures all of my verdicts, which of them should I revise, and why? Please respond in American English.
