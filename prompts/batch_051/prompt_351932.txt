Since I retired from a research career in physiology, I have been reading the philosophy of medicine, and the debate I keep returning to is the one between naturalist and normativist accounts of disease — roughly, whether disease categories track value-free departures from normal biological functioning or inevitably encode judgments about what states are bad for a person. I want you to work through a real case for me. In 2017 the American College of Cardiology and the American Heart Association lowered the diagnostic threshold for hypertension from 140/90 to 130/80 mmHg; with a single committee decision, tens of millions of adults previously classified as healthy were reclassified as patients. The change was defended as bringing the category into line with the biological facts about cardiovascular risk, and attacked as a value-laden expansion of the treated population that served interests beyond the patient's. Analyze this episode through the two positions. Be disciplined about the mapping: say what a naturalist should say the committee was doing and whether naturalism can yield any verdict about where the threshold ought to fall; say the same for a normativist; identify which features of the episode each view explains and which it must strain to accommodate (the trial evidence, the risk–benefit judgments, the conflict-of-interest charges, and the fact that no one's body changed when the number did); then say what the episode does to each view — confirmation, anomaly, or a case it never anticipated — and where each is simply silent. Keep the entire answer under 700 words.
