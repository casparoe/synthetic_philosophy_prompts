I have been arguing with a colleague about whether forgiveness can be granted unconditionally or whether it must await the wrongdoer's repentance, and she offered the following line of thought. It seems to show that the very forgiveness we most admire is something no self-respecting person could grant. I find that conclusion hard to accept, but I cannot locate the step that goes wrong.

(1) To forgive an unrepentant wrongdoer is to give up one's resentment toward them without any acknowledgment, apology, or assurance that they repudiate what they did. (2) But resentment is not a mere private feeling; it is the stance through which a victim insists that a wrong was done and that the wrong matters. (3) To give up that stance while the wrongdoer continues to stand by the wrong is to stop insisting that the wrong matters. (4) A self-respecting person cannot stop insisting that a serious wrong done to them matters, at least not while the wrongdoer still stands by it. (5) Therefore, a self-respecting person cannot forgive an unrepentant wrongdoer. (6) Yet the cases most often held up as paradigms of admirable forgiveness — forgiving an offender who will never repent, or an enemy who will never apologize — are precisely cases of forgiving the unrepentant. (7) So either self-respecting people are barred from the most admirable acts of forgiveness, or our admiration for such forgiveness is really admiration for a failure of self-respect.

I would most like to keep (6), and I am also fairly attached to (4), though I am prepared to learn that I have bought too restrictive a picture of what self-respect demands of victims. Where, if anywhere, does this argument go wrong? If it does not go wrong, which premise is the least costly to abandon, and what does abandoning it force one to say about the relationship among forgiveness, resentment, and self-respect?
