I am taking a course this term on the political economy of institutional failure, and I am writing a paper on whether the notion of a multipolar trap can do real explanatory work in historical cases. I would like an analysis of the Athenian decision to launch the Sicilian Expedition in 415 BCE through the lens of multipolar traps and races to the bottom. By a multipolar trap I mean a situation in which several actors each respond rationally to the incentives created by the others, and the resulting equilibrium is one that all of them would disprefer; a race to the bottom is the dynamic version, in which competitive pressure drives each actor toward a more extreme or more destructive position than it would have chosen in isolation.

Please treat the relevant external actors as Athens, Sparta, Syracuse, and Athens' subject allies, and separately treat the internal actors in the Athenian assembly: Nicias, Alcibiades, and the broader competitive field of speakers and voters. Start by reconstructing the incentive structure as it would plausibly have appeared in 415. What did each actor stand to lose by showing restraint, and how did each actor's locally rational move depend on what the others would do if Athens held back? Pay particular attention to the way Athens' empire functioned as a treadmill, in which any visible weakness was expected to invite revolt among the allies and opportunistic war from Sparta, and to the way domestic competition for standing in the assembly made caution expensive, since a speaker urging restraint could be read as timid or defeatist.

Then map the actual sequence of the decision onto a race-to-the-bottom structure. Where exactly is the trap? Is it best described as an interstate security dilemma, as a domestic audience-cost dynamic, as a competitive escalation among speakers bidding for public favor, or as several of these reinforcing one another? Be precise about who the players are, what their options and payoffs were, and what exactly counts as the race.

Next, say where the model fits poorly or stays silent. Nicias attempts to dissuade the assembly and instead makes the expedition larger; the assembly does not simply weigh his warnings about cost and distance in the way the model's rational actors would; the Egestaeans' misleading promises inflate the expected payoff; and once the expedition is under way the Athenians find it very hard to cut their losses. Are these failures of information and judgment rather than features of a trap, or do they show that the trap operates not only between states but also within the city's deliberative process itself?

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction and tell me what the episode shows about the theory. What would need to be true about the counterfactual of Athens showing restraint in 415 for this to count as a genuine multipolar trap rather than an ordinary strategic miscalculation? Does the concept earn its keep here, or does it collapse into the vague claim that many actors interacting produced a bad outcome? Be explicit about where the lens is doing real work and where you would have to force the case to make it fit.
