The principle in question is a person-affecting one: a wrong must be a wrong to someone, in the sense that some identifiable individual ends up worse off than she would otherwise have been. Parfit's nonidentity cases are meant to show that this principle is too restrictive. Consider the 14-year-old girl who decides to have a child now, even though she cannot give the child a good start in life. We feel she has done something wrong. But, as Parfit points out, the child cannot complain of being made worse off by the decision. If the girl had waited until she was older, a different child would have been conceived, from a different egg and a different sperm. So the actual child's only alternative was never to have existed at all. And since a life that is on balance worth living is better than never existing, the child is actually better off for having been born, not worse off; the decision benefits the child, and the only thing open to criticism is that the girl has made the world, considered impersonally, somewhat less good than it might otherwise have been.

The depletion example makes the same point at the level of policy. Suppose a government uses up a finite resource now, so that the people who live later are somewhat worse off than those who would have lived under a more conservative policy. No actual future person is made worse off, because the two policies would have produced entirely different people; yet we still think the choice is wrong. According to Parfit, these cases show that our moral thought is not fundamentally person-affecting: we should replace the person-affecting principle with an impersonal principle, of the sort utilitarianism provides, which ranks outcomes by the amount of well-being they contain rather than by how any particular individual fares. An objector might say that the child is wronged even if she is not harmed, since wronging and harming are distinct. But once we accept the impersonal picture, this distinction does no independent work: the only thing that could make the girl's decision wrong is that the alternative outcome would have contained more well-being, and that is precisely what the person-affecting principle could not capture.
