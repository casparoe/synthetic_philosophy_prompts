I am a PhD student in philosophy of biology working on the units of selection debate, and I want a dialogue that forces two of the main positions in that debate to engage each other directly rather than talk past one another. On one side is the gene's-eye view: the gene is the fundamental unit of selection, alleles are the only stable replicators, organisms and groups are temporary vehicles or environments, and what appears to be selection at higher levels is ultimately differential replication of genes. On the other side is multi-level selection theory: natural selection can act simultaneously at several levels of biological organization, and in some cases — most prominently the evolution of altruism and cooperation — between-group selection does real causal and explanatory work that a purely genic redescription would obscure rather than preserve.

The interesting version of this dispute is not about whether the mathematics can be made to come out the same. Most careful participants grant that standard covariance-based partitions allow the same change in gene frequencies to be decomposed in different ways. The live question is whether the two frameworks are therefore equivalent, or whether one locates the real causes, adaptations, and explanatory dependencies while the other is at best a notation and at worst a distortion.

Please write a dialogue between a committed gene's-eye theorist and a committed multi-level selection theorist, set just after a seminar on the evolution of worker sterility in the eusocial insects. The two characters are trying to determine whether their disagreement is substantive or merely presentational. Do not let either character be a strawman and do not decide a winner in advance: each should state the strongest version of their position, grant the other's points when they are good, and respond to what the other has actually said rather than moving to a new argument of their own. Over the course of the dialogue, have them work through these questions: whether a genic redescription of altruism preserves everything the multi-level account explains; whether formal equivalence, if granted, would show the dispute is verbal; and what kind of observation or theoretical result would rationally force one side to cede explanatory ground to the other.
