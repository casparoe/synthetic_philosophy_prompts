Ok ive fallen down a property-law rabbit hole and I can't figure out what picture of ownership is actually doing the work. Here's the setup. In most common-law states, if I own land but never visit it for years, and a stranger moves onto it, fences it, farms it, pays the property taxes, and treats it as his own openly for the whole statutory period (ten or twenty years depending on the state), a court will eventually hand him title. Its called adverse possession, and it means I lose my land with zero compensation. Western water law does something similar: water rights are use-it-or-lose-it. A farmer who keeps diverting water keeps his right, but someone holding a paper water right who stops using it for a stretch of years can have it declared abandoned and the water reassigned to someone who will actually put it to work. So far the pattern seems consistent: the law treats ownership as something you keep only by using, improving, or working. But then the same legal system will enforce my copyright in a novel that sits in a drawer for thirty years, unread and unpublished. So is the law quietly committed to a labour theory of property, or is that overreading? What does the practice strictly presuppose, and what is it merely compatible with? And where would the idea fit that acquisition is only legitimate if there's still plenty left, of similar quality, for other people—since adverse possession transfers land to the squatter without checking anything like that? I want the implicit theory of ownership reconstructed, and I want to know what would have to change in these doctrines if the underlying view of what ownership is were replaced.
