Last term my debate coach, Mr. Alvarez, introduced what he calls reasoning katas: short weekly drills in which the team reduces short passages to premise-conclusion form, works through base rates and conditional probabilities on made-up scenarios, and names the fallacy in a canned argument. My psychology teacher, Ms. Okafor, happened to overhear one of these sessions and told him afterward that she doubted the drills would do much good. They ended up in a long argument, which I stayed to listen to, and I have been turning it over ever since.

Mr. Alvarez said the drills are like the forms a martial artist practices. No one thinks a kata is a real fight; the point of repeating the movements slowly and in isolation is to make them automatic so they are available under pressure in an actual match. Ms. Okafor replied that the analogy should make us suspicious rather than optimistic. In martial arts, she said, people who only practice forms are not good fighters; the training that transfers involves sparring against live resistance. Real arguments are messier than drills: the premises are not labelled, you rarely notice that you are in a reasoning situation at all, and you are often defending a conclusion you already care about. Skills trained on tidy puzzles, she said, tend to stay on tidy puzzles; transfer of training is strong only near the trained task and weakens as the context changes.

Mr. Alvarez answered that he had never claimed katas alone were enough. The team also does live rounds in which they apply the same moves to a current controversy, so the program is kata plus sparring, which is exactly how a martial arts gym would run it. Ms. Okafor said this concession gave away the game: once the live rounds are doing the work, it is unclear the isolated drills add anything. The transfer may come from the realistic practice, or from a general habit of slowing down, while the katas are only a costly ritual. She added that the analogy cuts the other way, because a martial artist does not expect a balance form to improve his cycling; reasoning may be less like one muscle and more like a bundle of context-specific skills.

Mr. Alvarez said that even if reasoning is context-specific, the drills rehearse exactly the components that recur across contexts: recognizing conditional structure, separating factual claims from evaluations, updating a probability in light of new evidence. That, he said, is what makes them katas rather than random exercises. Ms. Okafor answered that the worst failures of reasoning are not missing skills but motivated ones. A person who can solve the base-rate problem about a taxi will still ignore base rates when the conclusion threatens his identity. Unless the drill is specifically about noticing the feeling of wanting a conclusion, she said, it leaves the real problem untouched.

Mr. Alvarez replied that noticing that feeling is itself something that can be drilled, and that his drills sometimes do exactly that. Ms. Okafor said that was an interesting retreat: if the effective ingredient is attention training rather than reasoning practice, then the martial-arts analogy has stopped earning its keep.

I could not tell who had the better of it. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each claim, say whether it is mostly right or mostly wrong and why, then say who is more right overall, and say what, if anything, both of them are missing about how far rationality can be trained in this way.
