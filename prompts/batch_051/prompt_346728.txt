Consider the legal and clinical practice of honoring advance directives. A competent adult signs a document directing that, if she later develops advanced dementia and can no longer remember her family or the values she once held, life-sustaining treatment should be withheld. Years later she has exactly that dementia: she cannot recall signing the document or holding those values, and she now seems calm and content in her daily routines. Many courts and hospitals nevertheless treat the signed directive as decisive for what may be done to her. Reconstruct the account of personal identity over time that this practice is best understood as presupposing. Explain, in particular, why a Lockean criterion of identity by memory or consciousness fits this practice poorly, how Reid's brave-officer objection deepens the difficulty for any purely memory-based criterion, and what the practice forces us to posit instead. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely coheres with, and identify where it remains philosophically underdetermined. Finally, spell out what bedside and courtroom practice would have to look like if the Lockean criterion were adopted wholeheartedly, and say whether honoring the directive then becomes incoherent.
