I run a small family foundation — two people, about fourty grants a year — and we try to be evidence-based givers. We keep hitting the same wall: a proposal claims its long-run effects, through norm shifts or people it influences or chain reactions, could be enormous, but there's no real evidence for any of that. The obvious comeback is that every action has unpredictable ripples, so by that logic we couldn't justify any grant at all. I want a reusable screening procedure, grounded in what philosophers call the problem of consequentialist cluelessness, that we can run in under an hour per proposal to decide when cluelessness should actually kill or shrink a grant, when it's just an all-purpose skeptical trick, and what to do in between. Give me exactly five steps, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. For each step, say whether it's a mechanical test or a judgement call no procedure can eliminate. Then, within each step, show it firing on two cases from our queue: (a) a think-tank grant pitched as maybe reshaping housing policy in thirty years but with no causal chain to back that up, and (b) a ballot-initiative contribution with predictable near-term effects but murky effects on political norms. If a step can't be stated and applied in two sentences, it's too complicated for us to use.
