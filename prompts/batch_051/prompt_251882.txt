I am a novelist writing a legal drama, and I need a scene in which two characters argue about whether purely statistical evidence can satisfy the civil burden of proof. Please write the scene as a dialogue.

Here is the situation. A cyclist is struck by a delivery van on an unlit road and cannot identify the company. It is conceded that the van belonged to one of two firms, Acme or Bolt, and that Acme operates roughly eighty percent of the vehicles on that road at that hour. Acme is bankrupt and uninsured; Bolt is solvent. The cyclist sues Bolt, and her entire case is the market-share statistic: there is no eyewitness, no paint transfer, no camera footage. Under the familiar blue bus reasoning, this is naked statistical evidence, and it is said to be insufficient even though the probability favors the plaintiff. Yet in the diethylstilbestrol litigation, courts let plaintiffs recover from pharmaceutical companies according to market share when the plaintiff could not identify which company made the drug that injured her. I want the scene to stage the strongest version of the disagreement about whether these cases can be reconciled, or whether the resistance to naked statistical evidence is itself a mistake.

One character, a plaintiff's attorney, should defend the position that the statistical evidence ought to suffice, or at least that the law is inconsistent in rejecting it. Her core claims should include these: a civil verdict answers to the preponderance-of-the-evidence standard, which is a probability threshold; an eighty percent chance is stronger than much individualized evidence the law accepts, such as an eyewitness identification with a known error rate; and the search for evidence that seems to fit this particular defendant is itself a statistical inference dressed in narrative, so the distinction between statistical and individualized evidence is a psychological preference rather than an epistemic difference.

The other character, a retired judge who has written on evidence, should defend the position that there is a principled difference between naked statistics and case-specific proof. His core claims should include these: a verdict is a public declaration that this defendant did this act, and it is wrong to make that declaration about a person solely on the strength of class membership; naked statistics are hostage to the choice of a reference class and cannot be tested by the ordinary adversarial tools; and proof requires a warranted belief about the particular occurrence, not merely a favorable frequency.

Write a genuine back-and-forth. Each character should raise the hardest objection to their own view and answer it: the attorney must say how the gatecrasher example, in which a person known only to be sitting in a stadium where a majority entered without paying is nonetheless not treated as a proven gatecrasher, can be squared with her position; the judge must say why the diethylstilbestrol market-share recoveries are not a counterexample to his. The conversation may also sharpen the point by contrast with the criminal standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, where both should say how far their arguments extend. They should answer one another's actual arguments rather than restating their positions, and neither should be a straw man. The scene should end with each character having been forced to refine their view rather than concede, and it should not rely on citations or named authorities, since the characters must carry the arguments themselves. Aim for a self-contained scene of roughly fifteen hundred words.
