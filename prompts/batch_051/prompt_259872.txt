I'm a novelist, and in the manuscript I'm drafting I've got a character who keeps pulling the exact move the Tortoise does in Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles": he'll accept every premise and every conditional you give him, but then demand one more conditional before he'll take the conclusion. Here's the bit of Carroll's dialogue I keep coming back to:

"Then Logic would take you by the throat, and force you to do it!" Achilles triumphantly replied. "Logic would tell you 'You can't help yourself. Now that you've accepted A and B and C and D, you must accept Z!' So you've no choice, you see."
"Whatever Logic is good enough to tell me is worth writing down," said the Tortoise. "So enter it in your book, please. We will call it
     (E) If A and B and C and D are true, Z must be true."

I want a essay that uses this passage to get at a general philosophical question: when is an infinite regress vicious, and when is it just a harmless way of describing something we do anyway? The Tortoise’s regress seems to generate an endless chain of conditional premises, but real reasoners don't get caught in that loop. So: is this regress vicious, benign, or merely apparent? Weigh the main interpretations, but don't end on "it depends." Defend a conclusion. Keep it accessible enough for a novelist who hasn't sat in a philosophy seminar in a while, but don't shy away from precise terminology. And don't give me a bibliography — I can't use it.
