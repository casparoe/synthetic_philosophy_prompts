A friend and I spent most of Sunday afternoon arguing about a book he had just finished — one of those histories that says the Black Death made the modern labor market, or that a few bad harvests made the French Revolution — and I kept falling back on the familiar objection: this writes about volcanoes and bacilli as if they had intentions, and turns human beings into weathervanes. But when he pushed me to say when an explanation that assigns a volcano, a drought, or an epidemic the leading role becomes environmental determinism rather than honest history, and where the line is between a cause and an agent, I found I could not state the distinction cleanly, and I am not sure my students could either.

I am teaching an upper-level historical methodology seminar this term, and I want to make this argument into a final exam question that requires students to actually work through the issue rather than perform an opinion about it. Please draft a question for a 90-minute, 20-mark exam, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The question should be answerable entirely on the basis of two short case descriptions you will include inside it — for example, a volcanic eruption that brings two successive harvest failures in an agrarian state, and a newly introduced disease that kills a third of a city's population within two years — so the exam tests reasoning rather than recall. The question should force candidates to distinguish necessary from sufficient conditions, to run a counterfactual test at a specific spatial and temporal scale, and to say precisely where a non-human event's causing a human response ends and its determining that response begins, rather than just defining environmental determinism or saying that germs lack intentions. The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth summing to 20, plus a one-line note on how candidates typically get each point wrong. The whole package — question, model answer, and marking scheme — must come to no more than 1,000 words.
