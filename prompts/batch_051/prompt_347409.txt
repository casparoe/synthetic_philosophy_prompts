I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, so I ask you to take my secondhand summary of the philosophical material as the honest report of an outsider, not as a careful reconstruction.

I have noticed a structural resemblance between two distinctions that come from very different places, and I want to know whether it is deep or superficial.

In the philosophical material I have picked up, there is a distinction between two ways a consideration can matter. Some philosophers describe at least some rights as side constraints or trumps: they do not go onto the same scale as welfare, so that a larger amount of welfare could outweigh them; instead they set boundary conditions on what may be done in pursuit of welfare at all. Other considerations appear to be treated as tradeable entries in a single optimization, and I have also seen the rival view that rights are really only instruments for protecting underlying interests, which would put them back onto the scale.

In my own work, I maintain a similarly sharp line between two kinds of requirements. Hard invariants — memory safety, not corrupting user data, not leaking secrets, honoring the privacy settings a user actually chose — are never traded against performance; no improvement in latency or uptime justifies shipping code that can violate them, and when two invariants conflict we redesign rather than optimize across the conflict. Soft objectives — latency, throughput, uptime, cost, recall on search results — are balanced against each other continuously.

The apparent mapping is this: side constraints correspond to hard invariants, and the optimizable good corresponds to soft objectives. Both distinctions mark a class of considerations as outside the tradeable scale.

What I want to know is whether that mapping carries weight or is mostly a verbal analogy. What would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer from one domain to the other? Where does the moral notion of a right do something that a software invariant does not: being owed to someone, grounding a complaint when violated, surviving changes in who designs the system? And where does the software notion do something the moral one does not: being automatically checked, carrying no intrinsic authority beyond the designer, being buggy in ways nobody intended? Does the analogy incorrectly predict that rights conflicts can be resolved the way invariant conflicts are, by redesigning the system until the conflict disappears? Finally, in plain terms: does the fact that an engineering practice gets by with a hard-constraint/soft-objective distinction suggest that the special concept of a right is unnecessary, or is that inference precisely where the analogy misleads?

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Keep the response to at most 900 words.
