I keep returning to a debate in the foundations of mathematics—classical versus constructive mathematics—that has persisted for roughly a century without either side moving. The classical camp treats every closed mathematical statement as bivalent and reasons freely with excluded middle and double-negation elimination, so a proof that the nonexistence of an object leads to contradiction licenses an existence claim without exhibiting the object. The constructivist camp identifies truth with the existence of a proof or construction: to assert ∃x P(x) is to possess, or give a method for producing, a witness, and ¬¬P does not license P. Both sides accept the same ordinary arithmetical facts, the fruitfulness of classical analysis, and the derivability facts once a logic is fixed; what they reject is each other's standard of assertion. My working hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about any mathematical fact or about derivability, but about meaning and norms: the sides legislate different senses of "true" and "exists" and different requirements on proof. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a hidden formal or factual question underneath that could decide it? If it is conceptual, classify it precisely—a difference about the meaning of "true," a difference about admissible inference rules, a difference about the subject matter of mathematics, or something else—and say what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress, or why none should be expected.
