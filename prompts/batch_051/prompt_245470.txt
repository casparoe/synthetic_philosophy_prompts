I am an undergraduate enrolled in a seminar on language endangerment and the value of linguistic diversity, and for my final project I have to design and run a sixty-minute session in a first-year linguistics course with about sixteen students. I want the session to be a structured simulation rather than a lecture, so that students run into the phenomenon before it is named. My rough idea is to divide the group into small speech communities, give each community a distinct set of tokens standing in for words or grammatical distinctions, and then run a trading and coordination game whose rules are adjusted round by round so that using the largest community's tokens becomes progressively cheaper, more efficient, or more prestigious. I want the mechanics to produce the standard trajectory of language shift on their own — bilingualism as an unstable intermediate state, a generation-like reset in which some varieties stop being passed on, and finally the death of one or more varieties — without the facilitator having to announce that this is the point. The debrief should then map what happened onto the philosophical arguments about what, if anything, is lost when a language dies. In particular, I want it to separate two claims undergraduates often conflate: the loss of a particular variety, and the loss of linguistic diversity as a property of the whole population. I also want the debrief to bring out the prospective, option-value point that we may not know in advance which distinctions will later prove useful, so that a student who leaves thinking each community merely lost a handful of tokens has to reckon with the possibility that the aggregate loss is different in kind from the loss of any individual word.

Could you work out the full design? Please include the rules and materials, the number and length of rounds, a payoff scheme that does not require real money, an explicit criterion for when a variety counts as dead, and a debrief that connects specific moments in the game to the philosophical issues. I would also like a frank note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for example, participants may identify too strongly with their assigned community and refuse to switch languages even when the material incentives favor switching, or they may try to coordinate across communities in ways that real language shift would not allow — and how I should handle those failures in the debrief rather than papering over them. Finally, I want an honest statement of what the simulation cannot reproduce about the value of linguistic diversity and what I will have to supply myself as facilitator, especially the non-instrumental attachment that real communities have to their languages, which a token economy may not capture.
