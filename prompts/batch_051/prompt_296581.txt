I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so cut me a little slack. My current book has an argument running through it about a woman who treats an agreed meeting time as a small promise: if she says "see you at three" and shows up at 3:06, she broke a promise to you, even if you were perfectly content scrolling on your phone. Some characters think she's principled; others think she's invented a moral category out of nothing and is just a pain to have coffee with.

I want to know whether the claim that lateness to an agreed time is a broken promise even when it causes no harm is the kind of thing that can be tested. Design the strongest empirical test or observation you can that would bear on it. What result would favor the promise view, and what result would favor the view that lateness only matters when it actually inconveniences someone or wastes their time? Then be honest about what the test would and would not settle — wether people's annoyance or blame judgments really track the norm at all, or whether we'd just be measuring something like etiquette, not morality.
