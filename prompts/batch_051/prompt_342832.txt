I work as a software engineer and have no formal philosophy background, but lately I have been reading moral philosophy in my spare time, and the thought experiment I keep coming back to is Nozick's utility monster: one being gets enormously more utility out of every unit of a shared resource than anyone else can, with the result that a straightforward utilitarian seems required to hand the entire stock of resources to that one being, no matter how badly everyone else fares. The standard worry is that a theory with that implication offends against distributional fairness, against treating persons as separate lives rather than as containers feeding one big sum, or against using people as a mere means. I find myself wanting to stress-test the case the way I would stress-test a piece of software, by changing one parameter at a time and seeing which part of the original behavior depends on that parameter.

Here is the variant I have in mind. Keep the total stock of resources fixed at 1. Keep ordinary people simple: each ordinary person's utility from a share x is just x, so their marginal utility is a constant 1. Now change the monster so that its conversion efficiency saturates. Let its marginal utility be m > 1 for every unit it receives up to a threshold share s, with 0 < s < 1, but for every unit beyond s its marginal utility drops to some small nonnegative value ε. So the monster is extraordinarily efficient on its first s of the stock, and then nearly or entirely sated. For concreteness, you could run the numbers with m = 100, s = 0.3, ε = 0.01, and ten ordinary people, but I am also interested in how the allocation changes as s approaches 1 or as ε crosses 1 from below.

Please work through how the standard analysis changes under this modification. In particular: what allocation does a straightforward total-utility maximizer choose as a function of m, s, and ε? Under what conditions does the original conclusion, namely handing essentially everything to the monster, survive, and under what conditions does it collapse into a moderate transfer? Then the harder question: if the saturating monster gets only part of the stock, has the real philosophical objection disappeared, or does the modified case still pressure utilitarianism simply because one being's claims dominate everyone else's until it is sated? Try to say which of the usual objections, if any, survive the modification and which depended on the all-or-nothing conclusion. Be precise about the calculation rather than using the standard labels as a substitute. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the overall analysis and single out the point you are least sure about.
