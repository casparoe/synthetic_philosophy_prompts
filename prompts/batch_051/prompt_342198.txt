In the Letter Spirit approach to letterforms, the question of what makes every 'a' an 'a' is often approached by distinguishing two levels of constraint. Letter-level constraints specify what any instance of the category must possess—for an 'a', something like a bowl and a stem in the right relation—while style-level constraints act globally on every letter in a font at once: its weight, angularity, x-height, and the treatment of terminals. No individual glyph is fixed by either level on its own; a particular 'a' is what results when both sets of pressures are satisfied together. It seems to me that this two-level structure—an abstract category core plus a global style that varies it systematically—has the same shape as a puzzle in virtue ethics: what makes charging an entrenched position under fire and refusing to falsify a document both instances of courage? There, too, one might say that the virtue supplies a schematic core (roughly, responding to fear as the situation requires, neither rashly nor timidly) while the agent's character supplies a global style that shapes all of their responses across situations. But the disanalogies worry me. A letterform is made to be recognized, whereas a virtue is answerable to reasons and can be exercised well or badly; and a typeface's style is present at once in every glyph, whereas a character unfolds over time and is only partially manifested in any single act. Is there a genuine identity of structure here, or is the word 'constraint' doing merely metaphorical work? If the parallel is to be taken seriously, what would need to be true of an agent's character for the letter-level/style-level distinction to be genuinely instantiated in it—and what is one prediction the parallel makes about virtuous action that turns out false?
