One thing I keep tripping over in the carbon pricing debates is what the harm principle actually licenses when the harm is slow, diffuse, and probabilistic. Mill says in On Liberty, in chapter four, that society may interfere when someone’s conduct causes “a definite damage, or a definite risk of damage” to others, and he contrasts that with “merely contingent” or “constructive” injuries. So here’s my puzzle, laid out as an argument a friend gave me and I can’t shake it.

(1) Emitting a marginal ton of CO2 very slightly raises the probability that many future people suffer climate-related harm — floods, crop failures, heat deaths, displacement.

(2) If an act raises the probability of serious harm to others, then it imposes a risk of damage on those others.

(3) A marginal ton of CO2 does not cause any identifiable victim’s harm; the causal chain runs through globally dispersed emissions, weather systems, and generations. But the same is true of many risk impositions we regulate, like a factory’s airborne carcinogen or a bank’s leverage decision.

(4) In all these cases, the harm is probability-raising rather than a finger-pointable causal chain, so if the marginal ton is not a “definite risk,” neither is the factory emission.

(5) But Mill’s own examples in the same chapter treat gross negligence and unsafe conduct as proper objects of social coercion, even when the damage hasn’t happened yet.

(6) So either Mill’s harm principle permits regulating carbon emissions, or it is too narrow to permit routine risk-based regulation that almost everyone accepts.

(7) If the harm principle is too narrow for ordinary risk-based regulation, then it’s not a stable halfway house at all: it either collapses into libertarianism or quietly admits that “definite risk” is doing more work than Mill explains.

My friend says step (6) is the whole problem: carbon pricing advocates want Mill’s principle to be elastic enough for climate but stiff enough to block paternalism, and the passage won’t do both. But I feel like the slippage is earlier, maybe between (2) and (3), where “raises the probability” becomes “imposes a risk,” and then “risk” silently becomes “definite risk.” Or maybe the real issue is that “definite” is doing epistemic work — a risk is definite when we can quantify it and identify the mechanism — and Mill couldn’t have imagined risks that are only visible through integrated assessment models rather than direct observation. I’d like to know which step to reject and what it costs. If (3) is the weak point, does that mean the harm principle can’t reach climate? If (2) is, then what does it take for a probability increase to count as a risk imposition at all, and does that let too much off the hook?
