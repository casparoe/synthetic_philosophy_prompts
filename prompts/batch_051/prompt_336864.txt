I'm a senior backend engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I run a monthly 90-minute "engineering judgment" workshop for a group of ten mid-level engineers on my team. I recently came across Heidegger's tool-analysis — roughly, the claim that a tool is most itself when it disappears into fluent, absorbed use (ready-to-hand, Zuhandenheit), and that it surfaces as a distinct object with inspectable properties (present-at-hand, Vorhandenheit) only when the work breaks down. I do not want to lecture my team about this distinction; I want them to run into it themselves.

Design a runnable 90-minute workshop activity that makes ten working software engineers feel the shift from absorbed use of a tool to the tool's surfacing as a problem, before anyone names the distinction. The activity should center on a real, scripted coding task with small stakes, and it must not require anyone to have done prior reading.

Requirements:

- State the setup and materials: a specific coding task (for example, mechanically backporting a small patch across a dozen files) and the tool or environment participants will use.
- Give explicit roles and rules, including how teams are composed, what each participant does, and how they record moments in real time — for instance, a shared "breakdown log" where someone notes exactly when the tool itself edged into the foreground of their attention.
- Break the 90 minutes into a timeline with minute ranges, including a "disruption" phase in which the same task is repeated under a single, deliberately small, documented intervention (such as a remapped key binding, a changed syntax-highlighting scheme, or a deliberately misleading build message). The intervention should be subtle enough that participants first experience it as friction rather than as an announced change.
- Arrange the incentives and rules so that the phenomenon emerges from what participants are doing — e.g., the tension between speed and correctness, or the moment someone stops working with the tool and starts inspecting the tool — rather than from a facilitator explanation.
- Include a 10–15 minute debrief with specific questions that map moments in the activity onto readiness-to-hand, presence-at-hand, and the breakdown of equipment. The debrief should also say what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run — for example, participants attributing their frustration to a badly designed tool rather than to a shift in their mode of engagement — and how to handle it.
- End with an honest note on which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I, as facilitator, must supply in my own words during the debrief.

The deliverable should be concrete enough that I could hand it to another engineer and run it tomorrow without further clarification.
