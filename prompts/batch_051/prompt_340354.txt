I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm drafting a scene where a retired physics teacher runs an adult-ed class on the Newton's bucket argument — the old fight over whether space itself is a substance (substantivalism) or whether all motion is just relations among bodies (relationism). I want the scene's lesson to be a runnable classroom activity rather than a lecture, because I need to know what it feels like when students run into the problem themselves before anyone names it.

Setup: a community-college continuing-ed class, 14 adults, one 80-minute session, no real physics background but willing to work. There's a janitor's sink and a mop, so actual buckets and water are fine. The instructor has cheap stuff only — office chairs, string, tennis balls, a lazy susan, whatever you think will work.

Give me the activity as a novelists could stage it: rules or roles, a timeline from the first demonstration to the debrief, and what physical payoff the participants should hit (the water climbing the sides, the pull in their arms, the dizziness, whatever makes the asymmetry visceral). Then give the debrief where the instructor maps what happened to the substantivalist reading — the concavity of the water is supposed to force Newton toward absolute space because it can't be explained by the water's motion relative to the bucket — and to the relationist reply, say Mach's line that its rotation relative to the distant matter of the universe.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time he runs it (which student moves and objections come up, which analogies fall flat) and which feature of the idea the activity can't reproduce, so the teacher in my scene has to just state it. I'd rather the students feel the asymmetry of rotation — the fact that effects show up on only one side of a relative motion — than memorize labels.
