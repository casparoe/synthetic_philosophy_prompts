After two months of trying to teach my mom to use a spreadsheet, I've concluded she isnt really learning functions—she's carrying over a mental model of the filing cabinet. When the model works she says she "gets it"; when it breaks (e.g., SUM treating a column as one thing) she says the program is "inconsistent." Anyway, I started describing this with a term I want checked before I use it in an essay. I call it analogic spin: every concept is held in place by one dominant analogy at a time, and learning is less about adding new concepts than about rotating the analogy underneath an old word—like a shape that stays the same while the shadow it casts changes. The stronger claim: you cannot hold a concept without some dominant analogy, and "understanding" just means the current analogy's failures haven't reached you yet. I know Hofstadter and Sander argue analogy is the core of cognition, so I think this is compatible with them, but it feels like a stronger, more brittle version. Is it? Give me the clearest case where "one dominant analogy at a time" is false—like a concept that seems to require two incompatible analogies held at once—and tell me whether my stronger claim collapses into theirs anyway.
