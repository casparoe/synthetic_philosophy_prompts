In thinking about climate policy, I keep running into an argument with two moving parts. It goes roughly like this. Future people will be richer than we are, so a given amount of harm matters less when it lands on them than the same harm landing on poorer people today. And resources spent now on reducing emissions could instead be invested and grow, so that the best thing we can do for future people is to transfer wealth to them, not to strip emissions out of their future. The conclusion is that we should discount future climate harms relative to present ones, and that the present generation is justified in refusing large sacrifices that mostly benefit the far future.

What I cannot settle is whether that argument survives a specific development: the prospect of solar geoengineering, in particular injecting sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere to hold down temperatures at a cost that is small compared with mitigation. This seems to change the shape of the intergenerational problem rather than just its scale. The present generation can pay for the cooling cheaply and enjoy it immediately. But the cooling lasts only while the injections continue. If they stop abruptly—after a war, an economic collapse, or a political decision to pause—the warming that was being masked would arrive in a matter of a decade or two, concentrated on whatever generation happened to be alive. So the present decision keeps current benefits where they are made and moves a low-probability but severe risk onto later generations who had no hand in the choice.

My question is whether the standard argument for discounting future climate harms still goes through when the policy under consideration is of this second kind. The argument seems built for a world in which harms and benefits arrive as smooth streams that can be compared against each other. A termination shock is not a smooth stream: it is a discrete, possibly catastrophic risk imposed by the present on the future. Does the argument survive that change, does it need repair, or is it dead for this class of harms? I would like the answer to specify the exact premise the development bears on, whether that premise still holds for this class of harms, and whether the argument can be restored in another form rather than simply abandoned. I am not asking which policy should be adopted; I am asking whether this particular argument does the work it is usually asked to do here.

Please answer in American English.
