I have been thinking through an idea that is sometimes discussed under the name moral trade, and I find myself pulled in two directions by it. The idea, as I understand it, is that people who disagree about first-order morality can still strike bargains that leave each of them better off by her own moral lights, without either party having to concede that the other is right. Two people with different moral weightings can exploit something like comparative advantage: one might care far more about global health than about animal welfare, while the other has the opposite weighting, and each can take on work the other would have done at low priority in exchange for work she herself values highly. What attracts me is the structure of the picture. Moral trade requires no common moral currency, no first-order verdict between the two value systems, and no watering down of anyone's convictions; it converts deadlocked moral argument into a bargaining problem with a decision procedure — find unconsummated trades and make them. The resulting bargains are Pareto improvements with respect to the two value systems taken separately, and the apparatus extends inward to a single agent uncertain between moral theories, letting the consequentialist and deontological strands of her own view exchange concessions rather than fight over every choice.

Yet I have a firm intuition that recoils from the full-strength version of this. It is the thought that some moral commitments are not the sort of thing one may offer in an exchange, and that a person who offers them has not made a good bargain but has done something corrupt. Suppose someone says she regards a certain practice as a serious rights violation, and then agrees to stand aside on that practice in return for support on a matter she cares about more. The theory, taken at full strength, seems to tell her she has traded up by her own lights; my intuition says she has sold her judgement on a matter of principle, and in doing so has shown that she did not really hold it — or was treating it as a preference rather than as a moral finding. On this view, a moral judgement presents itself as a claim about what all of us owe to a person, binding regardless of what I would receive for abandoning it. To put a price on some wrongs is to misdescribe them before any particular transaction has been assessed. Some wrongs, I want to say, have no price — not a very high one, but none at all.

I do not want to be told which side wins. Present the strongest case for the theory's structural appeal and the strongest case for the intuition's resistance, and then work through the possible resolutions. What would have to be true for the intuition to be debunked — perhaps as a costly signal of trustworthiness, or as a psychological rigidity that protects one's side of a bargain rather than a genuine constraint on what may be exchanged? What would have to be true for the theory to be amended or abandoned — for example, by restricting trade to concessions each side already regards as permissible, and does that restriction simply push the problem elsewhere? And is there a reading of trade on which the two are compatible, for instance one on which the theory governs only the allocation of effort among independently permissible actions and never itself generates a permission to do what either party takes to be a grave wrong? For each move, say what would justify it, not merely that it is available, and finish by saying which you would actually make.
