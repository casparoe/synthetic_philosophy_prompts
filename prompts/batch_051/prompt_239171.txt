I have been working through Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason and I want an honest assessment of an idea I keep coming back to before I commit to it any further. The dualism, as I understand it, is the worry that practical reason seems bound to honor two distinct and equally authoritative standpoints: the personal standpoint, from which I have reason to seek my own good, and the impartial standpoint, from which everyone's good counts equally. When these standpoints recommend different actions — say, whether to spend an evening helping a friend or resting after a long week — practical reason appears to contradict itself.

The idea I have been calling an "indexical ought" is that this contradiction is a scope illusion. On my view, "what I ought to do" does not name a single question but two: "what I ought to do from the personal standpoint" and "what I ought to do from the impartial standpoint" are different questions, and each standpoint answers its own question correctly. The appearance of conflict comes from demanding one all-things-considered answer to two different questions and then treating the two verdicts as rivals. That is like hearing "it is raining here" and "it is raining there" and concluding that the speakers contradict each other simply because their reports differ. If this is right, Sidgwick's dualism is not a conflict inside practical reason but a kind of pun on the word "ought."

I want to know whether this actually dissolves the problem or merely relocates it. The sharpest objection I can see is that deliberation cannot stay inside a single standpoint: the agent has to act once, and the very same action can be genuinely choiceworthy from the personal standpoint while being genuinely criticizable from the impartial one — which still sounds like a real normative conflict about one deed, not two separate questions. I would also like to know whether I have inadvertently reinvented some version of contextualism about normative language, or something older, and where this view stops being illuminating even if it is not strictly false.
