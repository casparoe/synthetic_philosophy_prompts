You are a moral philosopher who works on the ethics of gratitude and the moral emotions. Think slowly and explicitly: begin by reconstructing the different principles that could explain the record below, test each one against every case, and only then commit to a prediction.

A colleague of mine has, over years of conversation, stated a set of settled rulings about when gratitude is owed. I am paraphrasing rather than quoting her, but the rulings are these:

1. If a stranger finds your lost wallet and goes out of their way to return it, you owe that stranger genuine gratitude.
2. If a wealthy friend gives you a large sum of money that she will never miss, out of nothing but affection, you owe her no less gratitude than you owe the stranger in the first case.
3. If an airline gives you a voucher after an avoidable delay, purely to keep your business, you do not owe it gratitude, even though the voucher genuinely benefits you.
4. If a friend, out of genuine concern and with reasonable care, tries to stop you from making a costly mistake, but the intervention backfires and leaves you slightly worse off, you still owe the friend gratitude; but, my colleague insists, it is owed for the trying rather than for any result.
5. If a gust of wind blows a banknote into your hand, you owe no gratitude to anyone.
6. If a relative arranges a bequest years before her death, intended for you and made with evident warmth, you owe her gratitude when the bequest reaches you, even though she is dead and can receive no thanks.

Now the new case. A mentor, now dead, once wrote me a letter meant to help me with a decision she knew I would eventually face. She was old and slightly confused about my circumstances, and the advice in the letter is, as advice, bad. The letter was posted in a way that made its arriving almost impossible, and it reached me only years later, through a chain of postal accidents. And then, following the bad advice started a run of coincidences that led, exactly as she had hoped, to the good outcome she wanted for me.

What would my colleague most likely say about this new case? Please give me: (i) two or three plausible principles that best fit her existing six verdicts; (ii) what each principle predicts about the mentor case; (iii) which earlier case is the closest analog and why; (iv) a further case that would most cleanly separate the remaining candidate principles; and (v) your own best guess about which principle she is actually running, with your reasons for preferring it and an honest account of how far her record underdetermines the question.
