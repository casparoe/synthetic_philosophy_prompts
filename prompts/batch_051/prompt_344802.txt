I'm drafting a paper on the analogy between rationality training and martial arts: the thought that rationality might be trained through codified exercises — katas — that are rehearsed until fluent, on the assumption that something carries over to live judgement in novel circumstances. I've collected what I take to be all of the standard mechanisms proposed for how such transfer could work, but I suspect there is one more worth naming, and I want you to find it.

Here are the standard candidates as I've formulated them.

(1) Identical elements. Transfer is proportional to shared features between the drill and the target situation. What generalises from spreadsheet calibration or odds drills is mostly facility with more spreadsheets and odds, not with real decisions.

(2) Fluency of components. Drills make small cognitive moves — reading a base rate, applying Bayes' rule, taking the outside view, asking for the reference class — cheap and fast. Under real-world load, only the cheap, fast moves actually get used, so the ones drilled are the ones that survive contact with a live problem.

(3) Metacognitive monitoring. What generalises is not the algorithms but the habit of asking diagnostic questions: 'Is this the kind of case where that failure is live?' 'What would I predict if I weren't the protagonist?' The drill's content drops away; the monitoring frame remains and gets applied to new materials.

(4) Identity and commitment. The effect runs through self-conception rather than cognition. Months of practice make one the sort of person who cannot comfortably ignore a base rate, so the transfer is really the transfer of a role or an identity across settings, not the transfer of a skill.

(5) Disposition formation. Drilling does not transmit knowledge or an algorithm; it installs stable dispositions — to pause, to seek certain classes of information, to distrust certain kinds of local confidence. Dispositions are triggered by features of the situation rather than applied by rule, so they can engage in contexts that resemble no drill the person ever did.

My question is this: give me a sixth mechanism that is genuinely different from all five. I am not looking for a rephrasing of any of them, a special case of one, or a conjunction such as 'fluency plus monitoring'. It should be a distinct channel through which it has been or could sensibly be proposed that drill-type practice improves reasoning in untrained situations. Once you state it, briefly mark how it differs from each of my five, and give one concrete example — a specific drill and a real-world context in which it would do work that the others do not do. If, after genuinely trying, you think there is no plausible sixth mechanism, say which of my five is carrying the most weight and defend that judgement instead; but do not settle for that without giving it a real attempt.

Format: put the sixth mechanism (or your judgement that there isn't one) in your first sentence, then explain. No preamble, and do not restate the question.
