I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been trying to make explicit the unspoken philosophy behind the way elite institutions select people. Selective college essay prompts and the standard battery of professional job-interview questions ask applicants to present their lives as a series of self-chosen commitments: moves away from home, curated projects, credentials, risks taken for growth. Obligations to a hometown, the need to take the first steady job available, or years arranged around caring for family tend to read, unless carefully reframed, as a deficit of ambition. What theory of a worthwhile life, and of which human capacities deserve opportunity and status, does this selection practice presuppose? Reconstruct that theory, separating the commitments the practice strictly needs from those it merely happens to reward, and say whether it is coherent. Then give the strongest statement of the rural and working-class outlook as a rival conception of a good life rather than a failure to strive, and explain what the selection practices would have to change to treat that outlook as a legitimate alternative instead of a problem to be explained away. Answer in American English.
