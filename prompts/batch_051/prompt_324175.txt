I am a philosophy professor. At a department lunch last week, two of my colleagues fell into an extended disagreement about a graduate student who has come to depend heavily on a large language model for paraphrasing and explaining difficult philosophical texts. I listened as a bystander and did not take sides; their exchange was more careful than the familiar "AI is making students lazy" conversation, and I would like it adjudicated rather than merely continued. Reconstructed as faithfully as I can, it went as follows:

(1) The first colleague said: Your worry is really just the old worry about epistemic dependence dressed up in new clothes. Almost everything any of us accepts rests on testimony, instruments, translations, and editorial choices we have not checked. A student who trusts a well-regarded commentary or a professor's gloss is not accused of losing her understanding, even though she could not reproduce the commentator's chain of reasoning either. The model is another instrument in that long line. What matters is whether the student ends up with accurate beliefs that she can put to work in her own reading, writing, and questioning; the fact that the tool's internal processing is opaque to her is irrelevant, because she was never required to become the tool.

(2) The second colleague replied: The analogy to testimony and commentary misses what those things are for. A commentary, for all its selectivity, is an attempt by someone to make reasons available to the reader; its author can be asked why a given gloss was offered, and the text is usually structured so that the student can recover the movement from text to interpretation. The model's output is different: it is generated without any agent who stands behind the explanation, and the explanation's coherence is a property of the prose, not of the student's grasp. Understanding is not true opinion plus good prose; it is possession of a structure of reasons. A student who absorbs such output has acquired conclusions in explanatory costume, and that is precisely the erosion we should worry about.

(3) The first colleague came back: That sets the bar for understanding absurdly high. Mature epistemic agents constantly use results they cannot derive. A physicist may integrate a theorem she cannot prove; a historian may rely on archival work she will never visit. In each case the borrowed item is warranted not by being internally reconstructed but by being embedded in the agent's further inferences. The student can embed an AI-generated paraphrase the same way: she can test it against the primary text, draw consequences from it, notice when it fails to square with other passages, and revise. If she can do that, she understands the material in exactly the sense that matters, even though the paraphrase did not arrive with a receipt of reasons.

(4) The second colleague answered: The physicist and the historian are not a good analogy, and noticing why shows what is at stake. The physicist who cannot prove a borrowed theorem normally can still say what the theorem is for in her argument, what it rules out, and what would happen if it failed; she knows its inferential place even though she does not know its proof. The student who leans on a model's summary typically cannot locate the borrowed claim in any such space, because the model has already fused exegesis, evaluation, and restatement into a seamless overview. The student gets a finished product in which the joints between reasons are no longer visible. That is not division of epistemic labor; it is dependency without any contract specifying which part of the structure the student is responsible for.

(5) The first colleague closed: That contrast is empirical, not principled, and the tool does not decide it. Whether the summary arrives as a seamless product depends on how the student uses it. Nothing stops her from asking the model for alternative readings, for the strongest objection to its own gloss, or for a list of the textual evidence on which the gloss depends, and then checking those elements against the source. Conversely, a student can memorize a commentary without being able to locate a single claim in a space of reasons—a failure we have always had—and yet we rightly do not blame the commentary. The variable that does the real work is the student's disposition to interrogate a received formulation. Blame that disposition, not the instrument. If anything, a model that can vary its explanation at will may make active interrogation easier than a fixed printed commentary does.

I would like you to adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each numbered move, state whether it is clearly mistaken, questionable, defensible, or clearly right, and give a brief justification. Then provide a verdict: which specific claims in the exchange hold up under scrutiny and which do not, what both participants are ignoring or under-describing, and how the dispute about whether epistemic dependence on an AI erodes understanding should be reframed once those omissions are supplied. Keep the evaluation tied to the arguments as actually made, not to your own preferred position on AI.
