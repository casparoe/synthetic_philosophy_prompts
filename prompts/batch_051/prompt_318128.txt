I run the verification desk at a mid-sized news organization, and I am trying to stop making publication decisions about unverified video and audio by feel. We receive roughly two dozen clips a day — leaked recordings, eyewitness footage from conflict zones, activist videos, official bodycam, anonymous tips — and for each one a producer has about twenty minutes to recommend to the duty editor whether we treat it as genuine enough to publish, hold it, or kill it. We have no forensic lab, and the person doing the first pass is a reporter, not a philosopher or a signal-processing expert. The stakes are high in both directions: publishing a synthetic clip as real can ignite a news cycle and defame someone, while sitting on real footage of an atrocity or an abuse of power is its own kind of failure. I used to believe there was a defensible default here. The idea was that producing a convincing fake was costly, rare, and left detectable traces, and that this fact operated as a kind of epistemic backstop: ordinary documentary footage could be accepted in the absence of specific reason to doubt it, because the world was arranged in a way that made large-scale undetectable fabrication impractical. I no longer believe that arrangement holds; cheap generative tools have removed much of the backstop, so the absence of visible manipulation now means far less than it did. But I also do not want to slide into the nihilistic opposite, treating every recording as worthless until a forensic lab blesses it, because we would then fail to report things that did happen and that matter. What I think I need is a procedure grounded in the epistemology of testimony, epistemic dependence, and evidence — something that captures the difference between positive evidence that a recording is authentic and the mere absence of evidence that it is fake, and that keeps those two from being conflated once the backstop is gone. Please write me a reusable decision procedure my producers can actually run. It should consist of questions asked in a fixed order, with, for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure says publish, where it says hold or kill, and where it stops being a procedure and hands the decision to a person because the remaining judgment is evaluative rather than factual. Flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as mechanical checks. Then run the procedure on three cases: a leaked video of a public official apparently accepting an envelope of cash days before an election; battlefield footage supplied by an anonymous source that appears to show an atrocity; and an audio recording in the voice of a candidate making a damaging remark. Make at least one of the three a case where the procedure tells us to do something we will not like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot settle. Format matters to me: write the entire response as continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. If the procedure has steps, express them in the run of sentences rather than as a formatted list.
