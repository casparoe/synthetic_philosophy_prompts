For a chapter of my dissertation on self-stultification objections to epiphenomenalism, I need a worked example: please construct a thought experiment to the following specification.

The case must feature a subject who forms a true, justified phenomenal judgment — a judgment whose content is that the subject's current visual experience has a particular phenomenal character — in a world where that phenomenal character is an epiphenomenal property. By epiphenomenal I mean: the property is caused (or in some other suitable sense produced) by an underlying physical-functional state, and it itself causes nothing, including the subject's judgment. The scenario should be ordinary and contemporary — for example, a technician matching a precisely specified color sample — and the narrative itself should take no more than one paragraph.

The standard version of the paradox of phenomenal judgment would say that such a judgment is only accidentally true: since the phenomenal character is causally inert, the judgment is driven entirely by the underlying physical-functional state and would occur whether or not the phenomenal character was present, so the subject's correctness cannot be credited to any epistemic competence. Build the case so that this objection fails on its own terms while the epiphenomenalism remains intact. You may not secure the result by stipulating that the judgment is reliable, by giving the phenomenal character a second causal route to the judgment, or by positing an unexplained perfect correlation.

After the scenario, provide the following:

1. State the judgment, the relevant phenomenal character, the underlying physical-functional state, and the complete causal chain from that state to the judgment.
2. State the psychophysical relation between the underlying state and the phenomenal character in the case, and explain why the judgment is true.
3. Explain why the judgment is justified, or amounts to knowledge, under a reliabilist or other externalist account, even though the phenomenal character is causally inert.
4. Identify the structural feature of the case that does the work: what exactly prevents the truth of the judgment from being a coincidence.
5. Say what happens if that feature is removed, and whether the case collapses back into the standard paradox.
6. Give the strongest reply available to a defender of the paradox, and say whether the case survives it.
