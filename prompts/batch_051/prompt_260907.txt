I am a novelist, finishing a book that turns on a man who acquires a way of acting with no chance of being found out — a flat, contemporary descendant of the ring of Gyges. Writing him has exposed an asymmetry in my own reactions and in those of my earliest readers, and I cannot tell whether it rests on a defensible principle or on moral self-deception.

Take two cases.

A character finds a wallet dense with banknotes, walks it to the police, and declines the finder's fee. My readers find the act plainly admirable. Then I add one sentence revealing that he saw a security camera trained on the spot where the wallet lay. The admiration collapses, and one reader called the man a fraud. A good act done from fear of being found out, it seems, loses nearly all its credit.

A different character lives indefinitely with the impunity device. He harms no one badly, but he helps himself constantly in small ways: he evades fares, keeps cash he sees strangers drop, takes things from shops, and commits roughly one quiet kindness for every two quiet thefts. My readers do not admire him, but they do not condemn him either. They call him realistic, and say that anyone in his position would do the same. I find I agree with them.

So I treat the fear of being found out as the least respectable reason for doing right, and at the same time as the load-bearing reason most of us do right. When it produces a good deed I discount the deed; when I imagine it removed I expect the ordinary person, including myself, to help himself, and I do not think much less of him for it. Those two reactions cannot both be correct unless they are tracking some distinction I have not yet articulated. I do not need the familiar moral restated; I need the strongest principle you can find that generates both reactions, tested against the neighboring cases: the person who returns the wallet because she is revolted by theft; the person who returns it hoping for a reward; the person with the device who uses it only to read other people's mail; the person with the device who never dares to use it at all. Then tell me honestly whether the asymmetry survives, and if not, which of my two reactions I should give up. Keep the answer under 1,000 words.
