I'm a science jounalist working on a column about whether insect farming startups should treat their animals as potentially sentient. The move that keeps nagging me is the scale argument: suppose the chance that any individual black soldier fly larva can suffer is tiny, say one in five hundred, but a single facility raises hundreds of millions of them at a time. Then the expected amount of suffering comes out huge even at that low probability, and in practice the number of larvae can be pushed high enough to make any non-zero probability feel morally overwhelming. An editor told me to rank the following four challenges to that argument, from the one that should most weaken it for a policymaker who cares about animal welfare to the one that should least weaken it, and then say what the ranking implies for whether insect farming should get the same regulatory precaution as lab mice. The challenges: (a) the low probability isn't based on solid evidence, its a placeholder for our ignorance, and expected-value reasoning with made-up numbers is garbage in, garbage out; (b) most credible models of insect behavior don't require anything like subjective awareness, so the relevant probability isn't one in five hundred, it's more like background noise from model uncertainty; (c) scale cuts both ways: replacing vertebrate livestock with insects might reduce total expected suffering once you include the animals the insects replace; (d) the scale argument is too abstract to guide action because you can always crank up the number of individuals enough to swamp any probability, so the conclusion depends on an arbitrary population boundary. I want a committed ranking, not a survey. Lead with your ranking in the first sentence, then justify each placement, and end by saying which challenge you think is most neglected in the public conversation right now.
