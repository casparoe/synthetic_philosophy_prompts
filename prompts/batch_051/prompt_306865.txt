I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, preparing a two-hour session for a reading group of academic staff and graduate students, mostly from the natural sciences and theology. The topic is Galileo's view of the relation between Scripture and natural knowledge, and I want the session to turn on the passage from St Augustine's *De Genesi ad litteram* that Galileo places near the start of his *Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina*:

“Now keeping always our respect for moderation in grave piety, we ought not to believe anything inadvisedly on a dubious point, lest in favor to our error we conceive a prejudice against something that truth hereafter may reveal to be not contrary in any way to the sacred books of either the Old or the New Testament.”

My group is not interested in yet another rehearsal of the “science versus religion” conflict story. I want them to understand the structural principle Galileo is defending: that in physical questions, what can be established by sense experience or necessary demonstration should not be overridden by a literal reading of Scripture, and that such readings may need reinterpretation when demonstrable results conflict with them. I need a plan for how to teach this, not the explanation itself.

Please act as a senior teacher of early modern philosophy of science. Start by brainstorming a few possible ways to open the session, then select and justify the best. Then give me: the order in which the necessary ideas must come, with what depends on what; the one example or case that should carry the lesson, and what it must have and lack; the misconceptions this audience is likely to bring or form, and at which point in the sequence each should be headed off; a closing check by which I could tell that they have understood the principle rather than merely accepted Galileo's conclusion; and what to leave out at this level and say so.
