In a lot of conservation writing, bulldozing a forest or draining a wetland gets described as destroying an animal's home, like demolishing an apartment building while the tennants are still inside. From that analogy people usually slide to the conclusion that habitat destruction is obviously bad for the animals themselves, not just for us or for biodiversity. But here's what I keep getting stuck on. There's something clearly right about the analogy at the level of the event: the bulldozer really does kill and injure actual animals, and they really do lose the place they lived. But if many wild animals' lives are mostly short and brutal — most of them die as juveniles, starve, get eaten, or die of disease — then the home being demolished might not be a place worth staying in, and some people in the wild animal welfare debate argue that the fewer wild animals a piece of land supports, the less net suffering there is. So take that habitat-loss-is-like-home-demolition analogy seriously and tell me how far it can actually carry. What maps onto what: is the habitat the house, the resources the food and utilities, the individual animal the tenant? Where does the mapping start to leak — animals can't pack up and move to a new place the way tenants can, they don't seem to have preferences about the structure itself, ecosystems rebuild themselves while apartment buildings don't? What does the analogy quietly import, especially the assumption that the baseline life in the home is net-positive and worth continuing? And once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that habitat destruction harms the animals themselves in something like the way home demolition harms residents actually survive? If you had to replace the home-demolition analogy with one that doesn't already assume an answer either way, what would it be? I want the argument laid out, not just a verdict. And please write in American English.
