Im a philosophy professor doing some outside consulting for a foundation that administers a pre-AGI bargain, and I've been asked to reason through a clause everyone thought was boring until this year. The clause in the founding trust deed says:

"Upon the Distribution Day, the Trustees shall divide the entire Fund into equal shares among all natural persons alive at the time of the Demonstration; provided, however, that if any such person has died before the Distribution Day, the share that would have gone to that person shall lapse and be divided among the remaining such persons."

The Definitions section says 'Distribution Day' is the twentieth anniversary of the 'Demonstration,' and 'Demonstration' means the first public demonstration of an artificial general intelligence. That has already happened, so we're now in the interval.

The case: a beneficiary was alive and healthy at the Demonstration. Seven years later she drowned, was declared legally dead, and was cryopreserved under a protocol she had signed. Four years after that, an AGI-designed intervention revived her. She's now alive, competent, and claiming her full share. The trustees say her share lapsed when she died, and they reallocated it among the other beneficiaries; she says "died" has to mean permanently dead, since the point was not to punish people for dying. Nobody thinks the people who wrote this had reversible death in mind.

I dont much care who wins. What I want is a careful walk through the four readings: the plain words, the original purpose of the pre-AGI bargain, what the drafters would have said if someone had asked them about reversible death, and what a reasonable reader would take the clause to mean. Tell me which reading you actually think is right, and then say what would change your mind about that—which argument or consideration would flip you, and where your own reading is most likely to fail.
