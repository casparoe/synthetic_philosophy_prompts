I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. My team builds a system that surfaces recommendations about ethically sensitive choices, and I have been asked to write an internal note on whether such a system can transmit or cultivate anything like moral judgment. In the course of reading around, I came across the following passage in an essay by a technology commentator. It presents itself as a summary of what it calls the standard philosophical position on moral testimony. I would like to know how much of it I can rely on before I borrow its framing.

The passage reads:

"Philosophers distinguish between testimony about how things stand and testimony about what matters. When a seismologist tells me that a fault line is slipping at a certain rate, I can come to know that fact simply on her authority. But when a trusted colleague tells me that a particular data-collection practice is wrong, what I acquire from her is, at most, borrowed opinion. It cannot amount to moral knowledge, because moral knowledge requires understanding the considerations that support the judgment, and that understanding cannot be transferred from one person to another the way information can. A moral conclusion accepted on someone else's say-so is therefore closer to being told that a wine is excellent than to being told a fact: absent access to the grounds yourself, you can repeat the verdict but cannot hold it as a moral judgment. On this picture, moral competence is more like a skill than a stock of propositions. This is why philosophers regard moral deference as always a mistake. It follows that any person or machine that merely tells you what is right or wrong is, for that very reason, unable to transmit moral knowledge—and so no AI system can improve your moral thinking by telling you what it thinks."

I am inclined to accept the early distinction between factual testimony and moral testimony, and the claim that moral knowledge involves something like understanding rather than mere true belief, but the move from there to the claim that moral deference is always a mistake seems to me to run ahead of the argument, and the conclusion about AI seems stronger still. I would like an accuracy audit of the passage grounded in the underlying philosophical debate: which parts faithfully capture a recognized position, which parts are simplifications or distortions, where the author conflates claims that a careful version of the view keeps apart, what a defender of the richer position would reject in the passage, and whether the conclusions about moral deference and AI advice actually survive the needed corrections. Please give me that analysis directly, without bibliographic references or citations; I am after the shape of the argument and its weak points, not the literature.
