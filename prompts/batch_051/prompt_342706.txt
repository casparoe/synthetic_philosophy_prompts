Last week I was following a decision-theory thread where two regulars went back and forth about a Luring-Lottery-style game, and I genuinely couldn't figure out who was right. The setup: 100 players, all equally reasonable, each knowing there are exactly 100 and unable to coordinate, each privately choose a nonnegative number of lottery entries. The $1,000,000 prize is divided by the total number of entries, and a uniformly random entry wins.

One regular—call her Nova—said the usual superrational advice for this is unstable: everyone rolls a hundred-sided die and submits one entry only on rolling a 1. If the other 99 follow that rule, submitting one entry for sure has an expected payoff around half a million dollars, while following the rule pays around five thousand. A procedure with a 100x unilateral temptation isn't a rational solution; it's a fragile norm.

The other—call him Leo—said that objection sneaks in the assumption that you can swap your algorithm while everyone else's stays fixed. The premise is identical reasoners. If your procedure says "submit for sure," then by symmetry all 100 of them run it, each entry has a 1-in-100 shot at $10,000, and your expected take is a hundred dollars. The die-roll rule is asking what procedure a person should run given everyone else runs the same one, and it beats always-submit by a lot.

Nova came back that "everyone else runs the same one" is an assumption, not a fact about the world. In a one-shot no-communication game the other players are seperate physical systems; your postcard doesn't causally affect theirs. If superrationality says to treat my action as evidence about strangers' actions, that's exactly the magical thinking the idea gets accused of. Best-response reasoning is just what optimizing against an independent world means.

Leo answered that best-response reasoning is what manufactures the trap: if you expect the others to submit some total, your best response is to submit that many, so a population of best-responders escalates to astronomical entries and a prize worth microcents. A universalized policy that self-destructs isn't "the default rational standard"—it's a bad algorithm. Superrationality is trying to pick the best policy that can recommend itself to everyone symmetric with you, and there's no stable point where that policy defects on itself.
