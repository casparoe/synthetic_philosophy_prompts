I have been trying to settle my mind about the epistemology of disagreement, and I have landed, tentatively, on a moderate conciliationist position. As I understand it, the view holds that when I discover that an epistemic peer—someone roughly equal to me in intelligence, information, and freedom from obvious bias—disagrees with me about a proposition on which we share the relevant evidence, my confidence should move substantially in the peer's direction, even if it need not always fall to suspension of judgment. I also accept the independence constraint: I cannot simply dismiss the peer by relying on the very reasoning whose soundness is in question. I treat this as a fully general claim about epistemic rationality. It applies to contentious questions in philosophy, politics, and religion as much as to mundane matters of fact, and I accept that it applies to conciliationism itself.

What I want is not another defense of the view, but a serious account of what its falsehood would look like: what would have to be true for this position to be wrong, or for me to be rationally required to abandon it? I am especially interested in pressure points that arise from within the view's own commitments—for instance, whether the independence constraint, applied to itself, forces a damaging loss of confidence in conciliationism; how the view fares if the uniqueness thesis is false and a single body of evidence can rationally license more than one doxastic attitude; and whether peer disagreement is best treated as higher-order evidence about my own reliability or as something to be weighed against my first-order assessment of the evidence. Which possible cases, theoretical results, or considerations from adjacent debates would be decisive, and which would be merely awkward? What would a committed conciliationist have to concede as a genuine cost, and what could they reasonably set aside as a misapplication of the view?

Finally, I would like an honest judgment about whether the moderate version is so heavily qualified—by restrictions on who counts as a genuine peer, or on which disagreements trigger revision—that it risks ceasing to make any risky claim at all, and, if so, whether that kind of insulation is a serious problem for an epistemological view rather than an acceptable refinement of its scope.
