After a long argument with a friend about Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling, I find myself caught between a theory I respect and an intuition I cannot shake. The theory is roughly this: the ethical is the universal. A genuine moral requirement is one that any rational agent in the relevant situation could recognize, and no one may claim a private exemption from moral law on the strength of a special relation to God, a cause, or a community. I am drawn to this because it is public, principled, and gives morality a tidy decision procedure, whereas every claimed exception looks like special pleading. The intuition is that Abraham, in resolving to sacrifice Isaac, is not simply a would-be murderer. The story seems to be tracking something real — something like a single individual being placed in an absolute relation to God that sets them above the universal, in what Johannes de Silentio calls the teleological suspension of the ethical — even though I would recoil from anyone who applied that idea to an actual child.

You are a professional philosopher specializing in Kierkegaard and in Kantian and Hegelian ethical theory. Think carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming the strongest formulations of each side. Then walk me through the possible resolutions: debunk the intuition as an artifact of narrative or religious authority, amend or abandon the universalist ethic, or show how the two are reconcilable. For each move, say what would justify it, and end with a negotiated verdict rather than simply choosing a side.
