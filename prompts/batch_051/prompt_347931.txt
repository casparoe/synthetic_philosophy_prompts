For a history essay, I reconstructed an argument that seems to show something I cannot accept: that once we admit overdetermination, no historical explanation is objectively better than any other. I cannot find the mistake, and I would rather not be forced into saying that all such explanations are equally arbitrary. Here is the argument as clearly as I can state it.

(1) Every event of historical interest has an enormous causal background: many conditions were necessary in the circumstances, and often several distinct factors were each sufficient on its own (overdetermination).
(2) A historical explanation selects a few factors from this background and presents them as the causes.
(3) Because the background contains far more factors than any explanation selects, many different selections can all be factually correct.
(4) One selection is objectively better than another only if some principled criterion separates the genuinely explanatory factors from mere background. The natural criterion is difference-making: a factor explains an event only if, had that factor been different, the event would have come out differently.
(5) Overdetermination breaks difference-making: when two factors are each sufficient, changing either one alone leaves the outcome in place, so the natural criterion says that neither factor explains the event.
(6) Therefore, for an overdetermined event, either no factor is genuinely explanatory, or the natural criterion has failed and any selection of conditions is as good as any other.
(7) Since major historical events are routinely overdetermined in something like this way, historical explanation of them must be either false or arbitrary.
(8) But that is absurd: in an overdetermined riot, a sudden price spike and a violent police response both explain the riot in a way that, say, the existence of the kingdom does not.

Which step should I reject? Explain why that step looked inescapable from the inside, and then price the option: what would the main accounts of causal explanation in history have to give up or add if they reject it?

End with a single line of the form \answer{...} giving the number of the step (or steps) you reject.
