I'm a novelist, so in theory I spend my days in other people's heads. But I've started noticing a split in myself that I don't have a word for. I honestly believe — would argue with anyone — that every stranger walking past me has an inner life as thick as mine, full of memories and private jokes and plans I'll never see. But my attention hasn't gotten the memo. Left to itself, it hands me low-resolution versions of people. The man who fixed my boiler last week exists in my mind as a mood, a pair of hands, and maybe one stock worry; I have a whole weather system. It's not that I think his interior is thin. I just catch myself filing him as a supporting part. If I make an effort, I can open the lens and picture him as the protagonist of his own day — with rooms I can't see and a backstory I couldn't guess. But it is exactly that: an effort, like the work of writing a minor character well. And the moment I stop paying the bill, he collapses back into a bit-part.

Writing fiction shows me the same mechanism from the other side. First drafts always have the point-of-view character brimming with layered thought, while the secondary characters get, if I'm honest, one want, one wound, and a speech tic. I know how to fix it — I have to spend actual "interiority budget" on them, and once I do they thicken up. But what interests me is that my default as a novelist and my default as a person are the same default. So here's what I want to know. Is there a name for that default: the automatic, mostly unconscious habit of treating other minds as lower-resolution than my own even though I disbelieve every part of that picture? I know it isn't solipsism, because I genuinely reject the solipsist's claim; and its not the pleasant little shock you get when you remember that a stranger has a whole life — that shock feels like the exception that proves the thing exists. Is it just selfishness, or absentmindedness, or is it its own thing? I'm less interested in the label than in the carving: whether this is a belief, a bias, or a habit of attention; where its edges are; whether it's the same thing on the page and on the street; and how you would keep it from collapsing into plain egocentrism on one side and what people online call "main character syndrome" on the other.
