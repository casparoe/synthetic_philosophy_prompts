Read the Wachowskis' film The Matrix (1999) through the lens of the Boltzmann brain hypothesis. By that hypothesis I mean the claim that, in a sufficiently large or eternally fluctuating universe, a momentary disembodied observer with a full set of false memories may be vastly more probable than an ordinary embodied observer with a real past, so that most apparent observers like us would be chance fluctuations rather than evolved beings with a genuine history. The standard philosophical reading of the film is Cartesian: Neo's world is a machine-produced illusion, and waking from it reveals a deeper, more real world. Consider pressing the film with the Boltzmann worry instead. Once Neo has accepted that one entire apparent world, with its history and other minds, was unreal, what grounds does he have for treating the ruined world he wakes into — its machine war, the underground city of Zion, his own body and memories — as anything other than the content of a chance fluctuation arranged to look exactly like this sequence of awakenings? Write a critical reading that takes both the film and the Boltzmann-brain idea seriously. Which scenes or motifs does the Boltzmann reading illuminate, and where does it distort or strain? Be explicit about the ways the film resists this reading: for instance, its real world is stable, law-governed, shared, and causally continuous, whereas a Boltzmann brain is fleeting and solipsistic. The reading should be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration of the hypothesis, and do not distort the hypothesis to fit the film. End by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate that the bare thought experiment alone does not.
