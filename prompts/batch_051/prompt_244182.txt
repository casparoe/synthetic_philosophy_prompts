I am a novelist drafting a book whose central character, a retired diplomat, has spent decades defending a broadly Kantian thesis about perpetual peace: that peace is not a natural condition but something politics must build, and that three interlocking developments make it buildable — republican constitutions that force those who decide for war to bear its costs, commercial interdependence that raises the price of conflict, and a voluntary federation of such states that extends peace by treaty rather than by conquest. I am drawn to the thesis, and I want the novel to subject it to genuine stress rather than use it as either a straw man or a mouthpiece.

I would like a map of the position's exposure. What would have to be true — or what would have to happen — for this Kantian optimism to be wrong? I am not looking only for the familiar objections; I want the full terrain a careful defender would acknowledge. Which historical cases, empirical findings, or changes in the character of warfare count against the view, and which of them are decisive rather than merely awkward? Which of the three pillars is most vulnerable, and what would the world look like if that pillar failed while the other two held? What does the Kantian view predict that its rivals — realism, skepticism about the democratic peace, the claim that war is driven by forces no constitution can tame — do not, so that observing the rival's prediction would count specifically against Kant? Are there developments since Kant's time that fall outside the theory's categories — drone warfare, private military contractors, cyber operations, climate-driven instability, proxy war — such that a defender must now qualify the view, and which qualifications are principled rather than ad hoc? I also want an honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether that would be a defect in a thesis of this kind.

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed defender would concede as counting against the position rather than dismiss as out of scope. Personally, I would be most moved by evidence that one of the mechanisms runs in reverse — that interdependence can give states coercive leverage, or that popular politics can be more belligerent than court politics — so I would especially like to see whether serious versions of that reversal exist and how much damage they do.
