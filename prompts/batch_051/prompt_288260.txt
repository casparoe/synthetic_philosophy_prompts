Ok so I'm in a constitutional law/political theory course this semester and our term paper is on judicial review, and I keep getting tangled up in the standard countermajoritarian objection. The version we've been taught: in a democracy laws should trace back to the people, so when unelected judges strike down a statute passed by elected legislators, something illegitimate has happened — a handful of people who answer to no one just overruled the branch that's supposed to track majority opinion. The arguement seems to run on a factual comparison: the legislature is the majoritarian institution and the court isn't, so when they conflict, the court is the one that needs a special justification.

Here's the problem. The country I'm writing about has a legislature that's gerrymandered so badly the majority party can lose the popular vote by a few points and still keep a supermajority, an upper house that's massively malapportioned, near-total party discipline (members vote with leadership no matter what their district wants), and committees that actually draft the bills and are basically captured by donors. So the premise that the legislature tracks majority opinion is false, or at least no more true than it is for the courts.

What I actually want to know: does the countermajoritarian objection survive in that setting? Does it die, does it need a repaired version, or does it quietly turn into a different objection — something about accountability rather than majoritarianism — once the factual premise goes? I'm asking about the fate of the argument itself, not whether judicial review is good policy in that country.
