Hey! I'm a software engineer, don't know much philosophy, but my housemate is doing a philosophy degree and keeps going on about Hobbes. Apparently Hobbes had this whole thing about geometry being the model case of real knowledge because we literally construct the objects ourselves: a circle is something we draw with a compass, so we know its causes the way a craftsman knows a table, whereas nature is God's handiwork and we only observe it from outside. He apparently thought he'd squared the circle too and got in a big public fight with a mathematician named Wallis about it. My housemate's gloss was that for Hobbes geometry wasn't certain because it was about eternal Platonic forms, it was certain because it was made by us.

What I keep wondering is how Hobbes would have handled non-Euclidean geometry. In the 1800s people figured out you can drop Euclid's parallel postulate and get other consistent geometries, but they're not something you can sit down and draw with a compass on a flat page in the obvious way. Would he have rejected them, or would he have said they're fine because we constructed those too, our construction just got more abstract? And then on a related note: what would he make of proof assistants like Lean, where a proof is a piece of checked code built up from axioms and inference rules? On one reading that's the most 'made' a piece of mathematics has ever been; on another reading its not the sort of hands-on, diagrammatic construction he seemed to have in mind. I'm trying to use this to understand what his view actually commits him to about abstraction in geometry. Can you walk me through how Hobbes himself, or at least a loyal Hobbesian, would most plausibly react to each development, and say what assumption of his is doing the heavy lifting in each case?
