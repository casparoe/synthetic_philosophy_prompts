A while ago my friend Ben and his partner did one of those preference trades: he sat through a film he had no interest in so that she would come to a restaurant she did not fancy, and afterwards they both joked that they had each paid in boredom and come out ahead. Nobody sees that as problematic, me included. But when I suggested Ben do the same kind of thing with his political commitments — support a colleague's campaign-finance proposal, which Ben thinks is mildly bad, in exchange for the colleague supporting Ben's housing proposal, which the colleague thinks is mildly bad — Ben reacted as though I had told him to sell his vote. He said you cannot go around spending your principles like loose change, and that swapping support on things you think are wrong is different from swapping Saturday plans. Structurally, though, it looks awfully similar: each side gives up something they disvalue to get something they value more, and both can come out ahead by their own lights. There is a real asymmetry in how people judge these two kinds of trade, and I am not sure I can defend it.

I want to know whether anything actually justifies the asymmetry. Give me the strongest principle you can find that makes the political-moral trade wrong while leaving the ordinary preference trade fine — is it about the goods being non-fungible, about integrity or identity, about third parties being affected, about democratic norms, something else? Then pressure-test that principle against close cases: two donors trading which charities they support, a vegetarian voting for a slightly bad animal-welfare bill as part of a package deal, a journalist backing one bad clause to get a good law through, asking a friend to drop a deeply held conviction in return for a favour. I want an honest verdict at the end: is the asymmetry justified, or is it mainly a psychological hang-up we would be better off giving up?

Please write the answer in British English.
