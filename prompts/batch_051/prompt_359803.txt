In _The Right to Privacy_ (1890), Warren and Brandeis write that "numerous mechanical devices threaten to make good the prediction that 'what is whispered in the closet shall be proclaimed from the house-tops.'" I work on a project involving a non-invasive brain-computer interface that continuously reconstructs a person's silently articulated inner speech from scalp-level electrical signals and stores the reconstructed text locally on the device, even when the person never intends to communicate it to anyone. The underlying thought seems to be exactly the one Warren and Brandeis were tracking: what is private must not be seized and given legible form without the person's choice. But the case differs in one respect they did not foresee: the "whisper" never left the person's own head until the device made it readable. Does their principle that the individual is to be let alone reach a device that never publishes the reconstructed thought and only ever displays it back to the same individual? Lay out what the words of the passage require, what the purpose of the passage requires, and what Warren and Brandeis would most likely have said if asked about such a device, then say which of these three should control and why.
