I have been trying to explain to a junior colleague why reasoning about mutable state is so much harder than reasoning about pure functions, and I have concluded that much of the difficulty is concentrated in the phrase "the same object." But the more I try to define it, the less confident I am about what it means. Consider these seven situations. (1) Two variables bound to the same mutable list; an append through one variable is visible through the other. I take this to be a clear case of one object. (2) Two lists built independently with the same elements; mutating one leaves the other untouched. I take this to be a clear case of two objects. (3) Two immutable values of the same type with the same value, in a language with no well-defined way to distinguish value-equal instances; some implementations may intern them so that they even share memory, but that sharing appears to be an implementation accident. (4) A single mutable object that is updated, over time, until none of its original fields still hold their original values; I want to say it is still the same object, but I cannot say what carries its identity across the changes. (5) A copy made just before original and copy are mutated in different directions; the two are clearly distinct after the mutations, but what were they at the moment of copying? (6) Two entity instances loaded from a database row with the same primary key and currently identical data; I want to treat them as two views of one underlying thing, yet they are plainly two objects in memory. (7) An object that is serialized and then deserialized into another process; the replica has the same values but no shared memory with the original.

I would like a working definition of "same object," intended for reasoning about programs, that gets the clear cases right, gives a defensible answer to the borderline ones, and says why aliasing and mutation make programs hard to reason about. Lead with the definition in one sentence and no preamble, then run it through all seven cases, and finish by saying which of your verdicts you regard as least secure and why.
