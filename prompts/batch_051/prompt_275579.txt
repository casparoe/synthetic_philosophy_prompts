Bit of an odd one but I'm a nurse on a post-op ward and I keep noticing something that bothers me. We ask every patient to rate their pain out of ten, and we're trained to treat that number as basically authoritative — if someone says nine and is sat there chatting, we still record nine and give the meds. But the same ward uses a completely different system for patients who can't self-report (dementia, delirium, intubated): we score them on things like grimacing, restlessness, vital signs. So the practice seems to say that if you can speak, you're the authority on your own pain, but if you can't, your pain is something we can read off your body. I've been trying to work out what picture of self-knowledge the hospital is actually committed to. Does the self-report part presuppose that being in pain is luminous — that if you're in pain, you're in a position to know you are — and maybe something like the KK principle applied to pain (if you know you're in pain, you're in a position to know that you know)? Wether the whole thing is coherent, or are the two assessment systems quietly presupposing different theories of mental states? What would the pain-assessment policy have to look like if the hospital took seriously the idea that no mental state is luminous? I'm after the implicit epistemology, not a nursing-practice answer about which tool is more validated.
