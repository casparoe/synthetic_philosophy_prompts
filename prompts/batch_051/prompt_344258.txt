I've been going in circles on whether love admits of reasons the way belief or admiration does, and I'm trying to extract the principle from my gut verdicts on a stack of cases. The taxonomy I've internalized from the usual debates is roughly: appraisal views say love is justified when it correctly responds to something valuable in the beloved — their qualities, or their personhood itself (Velleman-ish); bestowal views say love isn't a response to antecedent value at all, but a volitional stance that makes the beloved valuable to you (Frankfurt-ish); relationship views say love is justified by the relationship or shared history rather than by the person's properties (Kolodny-ish). None of these gets all my verdicts, so I want to know whether there's a principle that does.

Here are the cases. (1) I love my sister, and if you asked why I'd cite that she's funny, loyal, understanding. But if she lost all of those tomorrow I'd grieve, but I'm confident the love would survive and I wouldn't call that irrational; the reasons I'd cite don't feel load-bearing. (2) But I had a friend I loved for a decade; then I found out he'd been quietly sabotaging a project I cared about and lying to me the whole time. I didn't decide to stop loving him — the love just collapsed once I saw him accurately. That also felt right, as if love is answerable to who the person actually is. (3) Swapping intuition: if I love my partner for her wit, warmth and patience, and then I meet someone wittier, warmer and more patient, it would be crazy — a category mistake, not merely disloyal — to think I now have more reason to love the second person, or any reason to transfer the love. So love can't just be response to valued properties the way admiration is. (4) Two people in an arranged-ish relation who never would have chosen each other, who grate in small ways, stay together twenty years, raise kids, nurse each other through illness — and at the end they genuinely love each other. I'd say that love is fully justified, which again makes the relationship itself look like it's doing the work. (5) A friend of mine loves someone almost everyone else finds unlovable: selfish, boring, flaky. I've watched this love make the person better, and I've also noticed that to my friend this person really is valuable in a way I can't see from outside. I can't bring myself to say my friend is making a mistake, but I also can't point to any property of the beloved that justifies it — as if the love is doing the valuing rather than responding to value. (6) My own child vs. an equally charming, equally needy child I've never met: of course I have more reason to care about my own kid, and that reason seems entirely relational, not about the child's properties. (7) The fanatical case: someone loves a near-stranger who, as far as anyone can tell, has no redeeming qualities and no real history with them — a years-long fixation from a distance. This one I definitely want to call unjustified, pathological even. But if bestowal is true, I'm not sure what grounds the criticism: if the lover bestows value, then the beloved is valuable to them, and what's left to object to? Appraisal at least lets me say the appraisal was inaccurate.

So my question is: what principle, or small set of principles, best captures these verdicts? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop or reinterpret, and which of the three camps survives, with which clauses doing the work in each case? Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence — the principle or principles you end up endorsing and which of my verdicts you're overriding — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
