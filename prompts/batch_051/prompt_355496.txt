I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, and the part of my dissertation that keeps giving me trouble concerns the formal structure of well-being — specifically, whether pleasures and pains can always be traded off against one another on a single scale. In our reading group we keep circling back to a family of positions that I will call lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism. As I mean it here, the position has two components. The first is an asymmetry: the prevention or relief of suffering has a kind of urgency that the production of positive well-being does not. The second is a threshold thesis: beyond some level of suffering — the sort of state one would unhesitatingly describe as agony — disvalue becomes lexically prior for that subject, so that no quantity of positive experience in the same life, however large, is sufficient to compensate for even a small amount of such suffering; compensation here means rendering the life that includes the suffering at least as good overall as the corresponding life without it. I want to set aside the stronger view that all suffering is lexically prior to all pleasure, and for the most part I want to hold aggregation across different persons fixed, though you should mention it if you think the intrapersonal and interpersonal questions cannot be cleanly separated. Whether the threshold could be vague rather than sharp is part of what I am asking, not something I am stipulating away.

I would like your actual credence — a number between 0 and 1 — that this threshold-lexicality thesis about an individual life is true, against the more standard Archimedean picture on which any finite amount of suffering can be compensated by some finite amount of positive experience for the same person. But the number matters less to me than the derivation. Please decompose the question into its principal sub-questions, assign a rough probability to each, and show how you combine them. Your decomposition should address at least the following: (i) whether pleasure and pain are commensurable along one additive dimension of value at all; (ii) conditional on full commensurability failing, whether the failure is localized to extreme intensities rather than present across all intensities; (iii) whether a threshold, if there is one, is an objective feature of the experience itself or is partly constituted by the subject's evaluative and cognitive attitudes toward it; and (iv) how much of the appeal of the threshold view is genuinely a claim about the value of experienced states, as opposed to a claim about rational choice under uncertainty when one possible outcome is catastrophically bad. Finally, tell me which of these terms your final credence is most sensitive to, and what sort of evidence or argument — introspective, neuroscientific, structural, or otherwise — would move that term most. If it helps to anchor things, my own credence sits at roughly 0.5, while a colleague in the group is at roughly 0.15; I would be interested in where you think that disagreement is most likely located.
