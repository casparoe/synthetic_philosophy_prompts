I've been chewing on whether an ant colony or a Portuguese man o' war is "really" one organism for like a year now, and I landed on a rule that feels clean to me. I call it the single-funeral test. Something is one organism to the extent that it can die as one event: you can point to a moment when the whole thing is gone, and at that moment the parts dont just wander off and keep living on their own—they go down with the ship. A dog passes it (nobody points at the liver and says the liver moved out and got a new life). A man o' war passes it too I think, because the zooids can't survive if the float dies, so the whole package dies at once. An ant colony only half-passes: the queen dies and the workers keep puttering around for weeks, so it ends up looking more like a village than an animal. But here's the wrinkle thats bugging me. A lot of my gut bacteria can't live outside me, and when I die the microbiome crashes as part of the same event. So by my own rule, am I one organism with my gut bacteria? Does the single-funeral test accidentally glue every obligate symbiote to its host and make a "holobiont" one individual, or does it just show that "organism" is a sliding scale and I shouldn't expect a clean line?

I keep seeing the word holobiont when I google this, but I havent actually read the papers. What I want to know: (1) is this test already a named idea, and if so what do people call it? (2) What is the most damning counterexample—somewhere the single-funeral test says "one organism" and that's obviously wrong, or vice versa? (3) Give me the three strongest objections and tell me which one actually kills the test rather than just bruising it.
