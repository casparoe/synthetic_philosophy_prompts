I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on the ethics of mood-altering biotechnologies, and I've talked myself into a view that I also find, at the level of my gut, unacceptable. The view is a version of the hedonic imperative: suffering is just intrinsically bad, not a necessary spice or a tool we happen to value; if we could use gene editing, designer drugs, or neural interventions to phase out the worst suffering and gradually raise the floor of human well-being — without cognitive impairment and without turning anyone into a wirehead — then we would be obligated to do it, on pain of allowing preventable misery. The argument is clean: the asymmetry between pain and pleasure, the fact that no one wants their suffering for its own sake, the way we already treat anesthesia and antidepressants as unambiguous progress. It gives one principle that covers a huge range of cases, and I keep failing to find a non-arbitrary place to stop once I accept its first step.

But when I try to actually picture the endpoint — a world where no one can grieve deeply, fail crushingly, or sink bellow, say, a six out of ten, and where everyone walks around with a default of gentle contentment — I recoil. I have a firm intuition that this world would be shallower than ours, not better: that some forms of suffering are bound up with love, accomplishment, honesty, or just with being keyed into how things really are. I also suspect, when I'm being uncharitable to myself, that this intuition is exactly the kind of thing the theory should debunk: a status quo bias, or a confusion of the instrumental value of suffering with intrinsic value. And I can see the move — reframe the recoil as a heuristic, keep the theory. But I can also see the opposite move: the recoil is tracking something that a single-axis account of value leaves out, and the theory needs a second principle it cannot give without becoming ad hoc.

So I don't want a winner. I want to know what it would actually take for each thing to give way: what evidence or argument would show the intuition is just a bias to be dropped, what would show the theory is missing something, and whether there's a version of the abolitionist project — a high floor but no ceiling, a right to suffer, limits on paternalism — that lets both survive. Basically: when a tidy theory and a firm intuition collide here, which one should I trust, and how would I tell?
