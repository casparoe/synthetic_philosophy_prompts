I teach high-school English and I keep going back and forth on whether to keep teaching Adventures of Huckleberry Finn, mostly because of the n-word, which is all over the book. I'd like to read it through the semantics and pragmatics of slurs. Philosophers of language disagree about whether a slur's offensiveness is part of its literal meaning, or something about what using it does, or an attitude it expresses; some even asks whether the harm lives in the speaker or in the word itself. Use that toolkit on the novel. Huck narrates the book, so the word usually appears in his voice: does the novel lean on a gap between Huck's naive use of the word and what that use does to a reader, and is that gap different from the gap between a narrator and an author? Is the book a case study in something like reclamation, or does it mostly reproduce the slur? Point me at specific scenes or choices, and be explicit about where the philosophical picture fits the novel and where it strains. End by saying what the novel shows about slurs that a plain philosophy article on the topic can't. Also, tell me how confident you are in the overall reading and which point you're least sure of.
