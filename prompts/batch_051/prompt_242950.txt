I'm a biology PhD student and I've been trying to get clear on what I actually mean when I say a biological part has a function. I wrote down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, and each one feels completely right on its own, but I can't state a principle that gets all of them. Maybe you can tell me whether there's a coherent account hiding in here, or which of my verdicts I should give up.

1. A normal human heart. Its function is to pump blood. No hesitation.

2. A fish in a polluted lake is born with a mutation that makes an enzyme that happens to break down a synthetic chemical that humans only started dumping in the water thirty years ago. The fish survives because of it, but no ancestor had the mutation and there hasn't been any selection for it yet. I don't think the enzyme has the function of breaking down the chemical. That's just a lucky accident with a usefull side effect.

3. A beetle's wing-cases are covered in a secretion that was selected because it hardens them. The same secretion is sticky and also keeps the beetle anchored to leaves in heavy rain, which helps it survive. The stickiness came along as a side effect of the hardening, and as far as anyone can tell there has never been heritable variation in it, so selection has never had anything to work with. Still, I want to say that one of the secretion's functions is anchoring the beetle to leaves.

4. A bird breaks its wing in a collision and can't fly. The wing's function is still to fly—it's malfunctioning, not functionless.

5. A population of cave fish has been in total darkness long enough that its eyes are tiny, degenerate, and can't form images. I wouldn't say the eyes still have the function of seeing. They had that function, and lost it.

6. A flower part started out as a side effect of how the stem of the plant grows, with no selection for it. Over the last several million years it has been favored by natural selection because it guides a bee to the flower. I'd say its function now is to guide the bee to the flower. Origins as a side effect don't matter here.

The problem is obvious when I line them up: in 2 present usefulness without any selection doesn't make a function, but in 3 it does? And in 4 being broken doesn't take away the function, but in 5 it does? Is there a principled account of biological function that gets all six, and if so what does it say about a seventh case—say, a bacterium that has been given a gene in the lab which pumps out an antibiotic, but the gene will never leave this one lab strain? If there's no principle that gets all six, tell me which verdict is the one to drop and what the cleanest replacement is.
