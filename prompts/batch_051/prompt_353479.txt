You are a professional philosopher who specializes in Kantian ethics and in turning ethical theory into procedures that non-philosophers can actually run. Please think carefully, and before you write the procedure, begin by brainstorming the main ways a committee could apply a universalizability test badly; use that to shape the procedure rather than simply listing my worries back to me.

I am a product manager at a mid-sized software company, and I sit on our product ethics review board. We meet weekly for ninety minutes and review three to five proposals for consumer-facing features: pricing and subscription choices, default settings, nudges of the sort people call dark patterns, changes to recommendation algorithms, data-sharing arrangements, and similar decisions. The board is an engineer, a designer, a lawyer, and two business leads; I am the only member with any philosophy background, which is one undergraduate Kant course from years ago. The company has agreed, partly at my urging, to screen features through a Kantian universalizability test before they go to the vice president for final approval. My job is to turn that test into a reusable procedure the board can run without my re-teaching it every week. We need something that fits in a meeting agenda, forces the right questions in the right order, and yields a recommendation of approve, revise, or escalate.

As I understand the underlying idea, the formula of universal law says, roughly, act only on maxims you could at the same time will as universal law. Applying it seems to involve three steps. First, state the maxim behind the feature; but it is often unclear whether the maxim should be the specific act (showing this misleading retention prompt to this group), the role-based policy (as a growth product manager, I will cut churn by whatever means are legal), or the fully general one (I will increase shareholder value). Second, universalize that maxim and ask whether a world governed by it is even conceivable without contradiction; if not, the action violates a strict duty. Third, even if it is conceivable, ask whether I can rationally will the resulting world; if not, the action violates an imperfect duty. I need you to turn this into a board procedure because I suspect many proposals fall through the gaps: acts and omissions blur when a feature quietly defaults users into something, the choice of description can rig the answer, mixed motives are the norm in a company, and non-philosophers will have trouble distinguishing genuinely failing the test from merely disliking the imagined world.

Please give me the procedure in concrete form: the ordered questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what to do when the answer is unclear, where the procedure stops with a no or sends a feature back for revision, and where it hands the decision to a person because the step is a judgment call rather than a mechanical test. At every point, show how the procedure handles the choice of maxim description, the act/omission distinction, institutional roles, and mixed motives. Then run the procedure on three worked examples: (1) a feature that auto-enrolls users in a paid subscription after a free trial unless they actively cancel; (2) an algorithm change that deprioritizes content from creators who are less likely to subscribe; and (3) a data-sharing proposal that would quietly sell aggregate user location data to a third party. Make sure at least one worked example reaches a conclusion a product manager would not like, and show how the procedure earns that conclusion rather than importing your own or my own preferences. End by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do: where it will still let bad features through, where it will block harmless ones, and what residual judgment the board will have to own.
