You are a historian of science who also works on the philosophy of historiography. Before drafting anything, think carefully and, in your working notes, break the Whig template into its component claims before applying them; do not use “Whiggish” as a term of abuse without saying what it means. The episode is the Galileo affair, roughly from the first theological challenges to Copernicanism through the 1633 trial and its aftermath. But the object of study is the telling of the episode, not the episode alone. Treat Whig history as a theory of narration: it makes the present the standard by which the past is judged, arranges the past as a series of steps toward that present, makes the actual outcome look inevitable, and divides historical actors into forerunners and impediments. Your task is to run this lens against the Galileo affair in both directions: what does the Whig lens light up in the standard story, what does it smudge or omit, and what does the case show about Whig history as a way of writing history?

Take these facts as the agreed record, and do not rest the argument on anything more fine-grained: Copernicus’s De revolutionibus was published in 1543. Catholic theology took geocentrism to be the received sense of several scriptural passages, and the Council of Trent had restricted private interpretation of Scripture against the consensus of the Fathers. In 1616 the Roman authorities condemned the Copernican doctrine as false and put De revolutionibus on the Index until corrected. Galileo, who had argued in private letters that Scripture is written in a language accommodated to ordinary human understanding and must be reinterpreted when it seems to conflict with demonstrated natural knowledge, was admonished not to hold or defend Copernicanism. In the same period the Church’s most influential theological voice, Cardinal Bellarmine, allowed that if a true demonstration of the earth’s motion were produced, one would have to reinterpret the scriptural passages rather than declare the demonstration false; he doubted only that such a demonstration existed. Galileo published his Dialogue on the two chief world systems in 1632; in 1633 he was tried, made to abjure, and lived the rest of his life under house arrest. Do not cite further scholarship or produce a bibliography; the question is conceptual.

Organize the response exactly as follows:

2. “The story the template selects” (about 250 words). Produce the Whig reading of the affair: which facts are foregrounded, which are backgrounded, who are cast as the agents and who as the obstacles, and what destination the narrative treats as inevitable.

Good candidates include: the Church’s hermeneutical position, including Bellarmine’s conditional willingness to reinterpret Scripture, was not straightforwardly obscurantist; Copernicanism in 1616 was not a demonstrated theory, and some of Galileo’s own arguments, such as the tides, were wrong; the conflict was entangled with patronage, Counter-Reformation politics, and authority, not only with nature versus Scripture; and the affair did not end in a clean victory for science. 5. “Bottom line” (about 100 words). In a single paragraph, state what this episode, read through Whig history and against it, teaches about how the history of science should be written.
