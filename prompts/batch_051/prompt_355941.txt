I'm a novelist, so this isnt abstract for me: I'm drafting a novel narrated from very deep inside a character's head, and the whole style depends on the idea that if she feels cold, or has a headache, or tastes coffee, she's in a position to know she does. A philosopher friend says there's a tight-looking argument that no mental state is like that — he called it anti-luminosity. As I understood it: take a condition that fades in tiny degrees, like feeling cold. Start where you definitely feel cold, end where you definitely don't, with each step so small you couldn't tell adjacent cases apart from the inside. If feeling cold is luminous — if being in it guarantees you're in a position to know you're in it — then at the last cold moment you'd be in a position to know you're cold. But knowledge can't run that close to the edge: if you know you're cold in one case, you're cold in every neighbouring case indistinguishable from the inside — otherwise your belief could easily have been false, and a lucky true belief isn't knowledge. Since the neighbouring cases are indistinguishable to you, knowing you're cold at the last cold moment would force you to be cold at the first not-cold moment, which is impossible. Run that on any gradual condition and nothing is luminous. He says it also undercuts the KK principle, the idea that if you know p you're in a position to know that you know p.

But here's the thing. I'm not trying to draw the exact boundary where 'cold' stops. Right now I'm attending to a particular ache behind my eye, and the idea that I could fail to know I have it feels worse than false — it feels like it would make first-person narration a kind of controlled confabulation. I don't mean introspection is generally reliable; I mean this one, token, present, fully attended-to state.

So there's the clash: an argument that seems to show self-knowledge can never carry that guarantee, and an intuition I find it hard to even imagine giving up. Which should yield? Is there a reading where both are true — I know the token ache but not under a maximally precise description, or the argument rules out luminous categories but not the fact that I'm feeling something? Or is my intuition exactly the thing the argument is built to debunk, and if so what's the debunking move I should accept? Walk me through what it costs to take each fork. And since I'd rather not just be sold one side, tell me what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would flip your verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
