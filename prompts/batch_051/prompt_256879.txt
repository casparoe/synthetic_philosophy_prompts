I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive me if this is underbaked. In the book I'm writing there's a character — a retired moral psychologist — who defends something she calls the "reciept test" for telling moral preferences apart from moral biases. I made it up for her, but I've now half-convinced myself its right, and I need someone to kick the tires before I let her be too confident on the page.

The idea, roughly: a moral conviction is a bias for you if learning the full causal story of how you acquired it — upbringing, self-interest, disgust, tribal loyalty, whatever — would make you want to disown it, or at least discount it in your own deliberation. It's a preference if it survives the same disclosure: you hear exactly where it came from and you still say, no, I'm keeping that one. The central claim is that this is all the preference/bias distinction amounts to. There's no deeper fact about whether the conviction really is a preference or really is a bias. The same attitude with the same content can be a preference in one person and a bias in another, depending on what they'd do after seeing the receipt. My character also thinks this means you can be wrong about your own preferences: saying "that's a preference of mine" is a prediction about how you'd react to a disclosure you haven't actually undergone, not a report on something you can just introspect.

An example I've been using: someone who opposes an inheritance tax because they stand to inherit might drop the conviction once they see that that's the whole story — bias. But someone else, same conviction, same self-interest, might shrug and say, sure, that's where it started, and I'd keep it anyway — preference. The content doesn't settle it; the reaction to the origin story does. My character is weirdly fine with the implication that a stubborn bigot's convictions count as preferences, since the test isn't a compliment, it's just a classification.

What I want from you: (1) is this coherent, or does it collapse somewhere when you push on it; (2) does it already exist under some name I should know (no reading list needed — just the nearest idea and how mine differs from it); (3) what's the strongest version of it, stated better than I just did; (4) where does it actually break — give me the three hardest objections and say whether any of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable. And if you've got a case it simply can't handle, something I can hand to my character to make her squirm, I'd love that too.
