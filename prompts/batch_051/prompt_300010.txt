For a while I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about competition, and I've landed on a set of views that I can't tell whether they all hang together. I'd like an audit of the set rather than a defense of any one position: go through the commitments below and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each real tension is.

My commitments, as plainly as I can state them:

1. Competition in activities a person chooses — sports, games, music auditions, and the like — builds character. It teaches honest self-assessment, persistence under pressure, and respect for excellence.

2. Competitive grading and class rankings in schools are mostly harmful. They train children to treat another child's success as a threat to their own standing.

3. Competition among firms for customers is, on balance, one of the best arrangements we have. It keeps prices down, raises quality, and punishes stagnation; I cannot see market rivalry as a moral problem in itself.

4. Being a "competitive person" in the everyday sense — someone who turns everything into a contest and cannot enjoy something unless they are winning — is a vice, or at least a badly formed trait. It breeds envy and blocks the enjoyment of goods that are not positional.

5. Excellence is unavoidably comparative. One cannot know that one has done something well without comparing one's performance to a standard, and standards take their authority from comparison with how well others have actually done. The pursuit of excellence and the acceptance of some broad form of competitiveness therefore go together.

6. A good society should try to minimize envy, because envy corrodes trust and leaves people worse off even by their own ordinary measure of satisfaction with life.

I genuinely do not know which of these is the weak point, so please do not just flag every pair as "in tension" for the sake of it. What I want is: (a) the real tensions, ranked from most to least severe, each with a statement of which commitment or distinction has to give and what the cheapest revision is, including what that revision costs as a view; (b) the pairs that are only apparently in tension, with the reason why the appearance is misleading; and (c) a candid note on where your own assessment is most likely to fail — which argument or consideration, if it turned out to be sound, would flip your overall verdict or reorder the ranking. Please write the answer in American English.
