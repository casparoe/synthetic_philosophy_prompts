I have recently been diagnosed with early-stage Alzheimer's disease and, while I still have full capacity, I have signed an advance directive refusing artificial nutrition and hydration should I later develop severe dementia and no longer recognise my family. My family is now divided over whether that refusal should still bind if, years from now, I seem content and sometimes accept food when it is offered. The disagreement has forced me to state the argument behind my directive as precisely as I can. It runs as follows. (1) A competent adult has the right to decide what is done to her body, and that right extends to decisions about what is done to it in the future. (2) The person who signed the directive and the later patient are the same person, because there is one continuous human life here. (3) Autonomy is not a matter of isolated moments; if it means anything, it means the right to run one's own life over time, and that includes making now the decisions that will govern one's later care. (4) The later patient, having lost the relevant capacity, cannot competently revoke the directive, and mere contentment, changed preferences, or passive acceptance of food are not the kind of revocation autonomy recognises. (5) Therefore the earlier refusal should be honoured even if the later person seems content or resistant.

As a professional philosopher working on medical ethics and personal identity, I would be grateful if you would think through this argument carefully and tell me whether it is valid. If it is not, say exactly which step fails, what reading of the key term or terms would make it succeed, and whether the argument can be repaired. Please write your answer in British English, as continuous prose in the style of a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
