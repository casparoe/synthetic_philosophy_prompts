I cover artificial intelligence and the philosophy of mind for a general-audience science publication, and I have been trying to get clear about whether Dennett's two-bitser response to Searle's Chinese Room still works against the kind of AI we have now. As I understand it, Searle's argument tries to show that whatever is going on in the room is at best derived intentionality, borrowed from the programmers, and that this falls short of the original intentionality human beings have. Dennett's two-bitser, if I have the dialectic right, was meant to show that this contrast is doing no real work: the human's intentionality is itself the product of blind evolutionary and neural processes, so treating it as a metaphysically distinct original kind looks like a leftover intuition we should give up.

What nags me is that the two-bitser was aimed at a picture of AI as an explicit symbol-processing program whose rules and goals were handed to it by human designers. That picture is no longer a good fit for the large language models I report on. These systems are not given a hand-written rulebook for semantics; they are trained on enormous amounts of human linguistic behavior and acquire something that looks like human conceptual competence before anyone gives them a specific task. That seems to alter the structure of the original/derived distinction in an important way: the system's competences are not directly delegated from the programmer's intentions, but they are also not produced by the kind of evolutionary history Dennett pointed to in the human case. Does Dennett's two-bitser survive this changed assumption, or did Searle's challenge gain a new foothold? Which premise is actually under pressure, and is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still important?
