I'm in a reading group on scales of civilization, and last week we got stuck on whether any single number can capture "advancement." One person floated a composite index and we spent the evening trying to predict how it would actually behave in the world rather then in the abstract. Here's the proposal. A country's Advancement Quotient is: the base-10 log of its per-capita useful energy throughput in watts, plus the negative base-10 log (in meters) of the smallest feature size its best fabrication lab can reliably pattern, multiplied by the fraction of its energy that does not come from burning biomass. So it's a mashup of a Kardashev-style energy scale and a Barrow-style inward scale that gives credit for mastery of the very small. Development banks would use the score to allocate cheap loans, and the league tables would be published annually.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than a position to praise or trash. I want to know what the index actually rewards in the short run and in the long run, and how a clever small country could game the formula without becoming more advanced in any intuitive sense. What would it do to research priorities and energy policy once aid and cheap credit are tied to the ranking? Where does the index diverge most sharply from what a reasonable person would call advancement, and which of those divergences are baked into the idea of a single scalar rather than fixable by tweaking coefficients? Walk through the incentives for governments, for the aid agencies, and for the researchers who produce the measurements.
