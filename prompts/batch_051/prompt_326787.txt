I'm a few months into a philosophy of biology PhD and I keep running into the tautology objection to natural selection, so I finally asked a friend who does evolutionary biology how philosophers actually deal with it. Her summary of the propensity interpretation went roughly like this. The historical worry is that the slogan survival of the fittest is circular, because fitness was being defined as actual reproductive success, so the claim that the fittest leave more offspring is just true by definition. The propensity interpretation, she says, fixes this by treating fitness as a probabilistic disposition: an organism's fitness is the probability that it will survive and leave offspring in its environment. And since fitness is now a probability, natural selection becomes a substantive empirical claim — if type A is fitter than type B, A will in the long run leave more offspring than B. So the tautology objection is basically dissolved.

I've read enough in the meantime to think the broad shape of this is right, but I suspect she's overselling it a little, especially around what probability means in the definition and what the theory is actually supposed to predict. Could you go through her summary piece by piece and tell me which bits a careful defender of the propensity view would happily accept, which bits they'd want to rephrase or qualify, and whether her conclusion — that adopting the propensity interpretation makes the tautology objection go away — actually follows from the accurate parts?
