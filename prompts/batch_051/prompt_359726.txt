I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of cognitive science course this term, and next week I have to give a short presentation to my tutorial group — first-year students from mixed majors — on an idea the instructor calls the strange inversion of reasoning. As I currently understand it, the claim is that nature can produce things that look exquisitely designed, and that engineers can produce systems that behave intelligently, without any of the underlying processes understanding what they are doing. The instructor has paired Darwin's version (roughly, competence without comprehension through natural selection) with Turing's version (roughly, building a thinking machine out of parts that do not think) and wants them presented as two faces of one idea rather than as two separate slogans. I do not need the idea explained to me; I need a plan for explaining it in about fifteen minutes.

My question is about order, example, and pitfalls. What has to come first, and why — what does the audience need to have in place before the inversion can even land, and which of the two inversions should set the frame for the other? I want one carrying example to run through the whole presentation: what features does it need so that Darwin's version and Turing's version stay distinct without becoming unrelated? I also want a map of predictable misconceptions. I can already see two: students may hear "no comprehension" as "the process is merely random," and others may slide from "competence can exist without comprehension" to "comprehension is an illusion that never does any causal work." Where exactly in the sequence should each be headed off, and with what distinction? At the end, what single question should I ask the group to check that they have actually got the inversion rather than just repeated a slogan? And finally, what should I openly refuse to explain at this level — what would be true but too confusing for first-years — and what should I say it is being saved for?
