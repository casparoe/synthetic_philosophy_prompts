I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, running a one-off session for an upper-level undergraduate seminar of 14 students, with 80 minutes available. The topic is moral luck. I want an activity that makes students feel the tension between the control condition on moral judgment—that people can be praised or blamed only for what was under their control—and the way our judgments of actual agents track outcomes, opportunities, and traits that those agents never chose. By the end, students should see that the problem is not merely that we are careless in letting luck matter, but that some of the pull of outcome-based judgment survives even when we explicitly set out to eliminate it.

The activity should be designed around resultant luck and circumstantial luck, with the distinctions themselves emerging from what happens rather than being named up front. Please do not have me lecture first, and the term "moral luck" should not be used until the debrief.

Specifically, I would like a design with the following features:

1. A forced-choice phase in which each student, under role conditions they did not choose, must select between a lower-risk and a higher-risk course of action. The roles should give different students different choice sets—for example, some have a genuinely safe alternative available and others do not—so that circumstantial luck surfaces, not only resultant luck. Materials should be simple (a deck of cards, tokens, printed role sheets) and require no real money.

2. An outcome phase with real randomization, in which identically risky choices can produce different results. At least one unlucky outcome should be salient enough that the group feels the pull to judge the unlucky agent more harshly than an identically situated lucky agent, even though the choice was the same.

3. An agent-regret moment before any public judgment: students who draw an unlucky outcome must each decide privately, or in writing, whether to transfer some of their own tokens to the harmed party and how much, with no instruction about what would be appropriate. This should later let the debrief separate the agent's first-personal need to make amends from third-personal blame.

4. A structured final task in which the group must draft a short rule stating how much an outcome should count in official assessment, and then apply that rule to their own earlier informal reactions—the aim being to let them catch themselves endorsing a rule that condemns judgments they themselves just made.

Please give full rules, materials, a timeline for 80 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity to the distinction between resultant and circumstantial luck, to the control principle, and to agent-regret. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it—for instance, students policing each other into pretending outcomes do not matter, or treating the exercise as a strategy game rather than an occasion for noticing their own judgments. Be frank about which features of moral luck the activity cannot stage: by itself it can gesture only indirectly at constitutive luck, since the risk-tolerance students bring to the exercise is itself unchosen, and it cannot stage causal luck at all. The debrief should supply those limitations explicitly rather than pretending the exercise covers the whole phenomenon.
