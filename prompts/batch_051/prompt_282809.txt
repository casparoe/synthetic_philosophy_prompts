I’m in an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of mathematics course, and we’ve just spent two sessions on supertasks and Thomson’s lamp. I’ve been asked to help design a classroom activity for our 75-minute seminar, which has 16 students. I want a runnable activity whose participants come away feeling the force of the claim that a completely specified infinite sequence of operations need not determine a final state. Please design the activity around Thomson’s lamp as a live game that forces students to make a prediction about the lamp’s state at the end, to stake something small on that prediction, and then to confront the fact that any prediction they make is undercut by the very rule they were given. Include exact roles—who is the lamp, who is the switch operator, who are the bookmakers, who are the record-keepers—rules for compressing the switching intervals, a 50-minute game timeline, and a 20-minute debrief script. In the debrief, map particular moments, especially the moment when the group realizes the bets cannot be settled, onto the structure of Thomson’s argument: the alternating sequence, the absence of a limit, and the difference between a rule that tells you what to do at each finite step and a rule that tells you what holds after all the steps. Be committed: make definite choices about the game mechanics and defend them, rather than laying out alternatives for me to pick from. End with an honest note on which feature of the original thought experiment the activity cannot physically reproduce—presumably the fact that the real lamp requires an actually completed infinite series—and how a facilitator should compensate for that gap.
