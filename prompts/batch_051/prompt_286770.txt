I run a small independent press that publishes dual-language poetry, and we are about to begin a monthly feature of translations of verse written under heavy formal constraints: sestinas, villanelles, ghazals, sonnets, acrostics, lipograms, palindromes, Oulipian procedures, and poems in which rhyme or meter carries a real structural or tonal load. No translation can preserve every one of these features, and in the past my translators have fallen into predictable camps: some preserve the meter at the cost of the sense, some strip the form entirely and give a prose-like paraphrase they call the content, and some reproduce the generating rule so mechanically that the English result is no longer recognizable as a poem. I need a single reusable decision procedure for my two line editors and the roughly dozen translators they oversee. The practical constraints are these: most translators are trained in one source language but are not trained prosodists or theorists; each translation must move from first draft to final text within about a week; I have to review and sign off on what is published; and our readership includes many bilingual readers who will notice what has been dropped.

Please ground the procedure in a defensible account of what formal features actually do, whether semantic, aesthetic, structural, conventional, or merely constraining, rather than in an unexamined slogan of fidelity. The deliverable should include: a checklist or ordered set of tests that an editor can apply in under an hour to a source poem and a draft; the distinctions that must survive even when the procedure is simplified for non-specialists; two or three worked examples, such as an acrostic whose hidden message belongs to the poem's meaning, a fixed form like a sestina where the constraint is largely arbitrary, and a translation in which rhyme is doing ironic or tonal work; at least one case where the procedure gives an answer my editors are likely to resent; and an explicit statement of the points where the procedure cannot decide for them and a translator's judgment must take over.
