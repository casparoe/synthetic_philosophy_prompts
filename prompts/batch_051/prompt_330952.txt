I am a second-year undergraduate reading Philosophy and Law, and I am currently taking a course on free expression which has spent several weeks on the contrast between the First Amendment tradition in the United States and the European approach to hate speech. I have been asked to run a session for my college philosophy society: roughly eighteen students, mostly second- and third-years from mixed disciplines, about half with some political philosophy, in a single seventy-five-minute meeting. I want the session to make participants feel the force of both approaches before either is named, and to come away seeing that the two frameworks are not simply pro-speech and anti-speech positions, but rival ways of answering the question of what speech is and what it does to a person.

Let me set out the contrast as the course has drawn it, so that your design can be precise. On the American view as we have studied it, restrictions on expression are suspect when they target the message itself; the regime is broadly viewpoint-neutral, protects deeply offensive political speech, and confines regulation to a narrow periphery concerned with conduct and direct consequences rather than with the beliefs or hatred the words carry. On the European view, expression is understood as capable of injuring the dignity, security, and equal standing of members of vulnerable groups, and the state may lawfully criminalise, for example, incitement to racial or religious hatred or, in several jurisdictions, Holocaust denial. The hard thing is that each framework treats what the other regards as its central achievement as either a serious deficiency or a serious wrong.

I would like you to design a runnable classroom activity, not a lecture and not a prepared debate. It should be a structured game, role-play, or simulation with concrete rules, roles, and materials, for example a short case packet of boundary disputes, a drafting brief, or a constrained negotiation. Participants should run into the problem themselves: the rules, incentives, or roles should push them towards choices that expose what each framework is prepared to sacrifice, and the pull of the other framework should be felt before the debrief names it. There should be no lecture up front, and the two frameworks should not be named until afterwards. The activity should include at least one moment in which a rule or decision that seemed obviously correct from one perspective condemns a case that the other perspective would protect, or protects a case that the other would condemn.

The activity must fit seventy-five minutes, work for sixteen to twenty participants, require no equipment beyond paper and perhaps a board, and involve everyone throughout. Please include: (1) a minute-by-minute plan; (2) the full text or specification of any short cases or rules I would need to print; (3) a debrief that maps particular moments onto the theoretical distinctions, including at least viewpoint-neutral regulation versus content-based restriction, the gap between regulating the message and regulating its harms, the contrast between an autonomy-centred and a dignity-centred picture of what speech does, and the paradox of tolerance; and (4) a frank note on what the exercise cannot reproduce, for instance the lived position of a member of a targeted group or the actual machinery of enforcement and criminal sanction, and how I should supply that in the final discussion.

Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how I should intervene while preserving the discovery element. I would be grateful for a design specific enough that I could run the session from your instructions alone, and I would ask you to write the answer in British English.
