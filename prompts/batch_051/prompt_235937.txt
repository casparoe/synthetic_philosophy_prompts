Question: "'Sherlock Holmes' doesn't refer to anyone in the actual world. Yet we talk about Holmes all the time, and it seems right to say that, in Conan Doyle's stories, Holmes is a detective who lives on Baker Street. Drawing on at least two theories of names, explain (a) what, if anything, a sentence like 'Sherlock Holmes is a detective' means, and (b) how it can be true inside the fiction but not true in the actual world."

Student answer:

"The two main options are Millianism and descriptivism. On a Millian view, the whole semantic contribution of a name is its referent, so an empty name contributes nothing to the proposition expressed. That makes negative existentials like 'Holmes does not exist' look as if they can't be true, or can't even fully say anything. Descriptivism handles this better: the name is shorthand for a cluster of descriptions, such as the detective who lives at 221B Baker Street and is friends with Watson, and those descriptions are meaningful even if nothing satisfies them. Russell strengthens this into the claim that 'Holmes is a detective' has the logical form of an existential sentence, so it comes out false rather than meaningless when nothing uniquely fits the description.

Frege is a kind of middle option, but he bites the bullet differently. He agrees the name can have sense without a referent, but he thinks the whole sentence ends up false, because one of its parts fails to refer. He also says fictional discourse is a case where we care about sense rather than reference, so the lack of a referent doesn't stop us from following the story.

Turning to fiction, we have to separate what's true inside the story from what's true in the actual world: 'Holmes is a detective' is true in the fiction but false, or at least not true, simpliciter. The standard way to capture this is with an operator: 'in the fiction, P' is true when P holds in the relevant possible worlds, roughly the worlds where the story is told as known fact. Walton and Lewis basically agree on this even though their vocabulary is different. Once the operator shifts us to those worlds, 'Holmes' can refer after all: it picks out the individual in those worlds who plays the Holmes role. So the name is only 'empty' from outside the operator, and refers from inside it, which is why the sentence is true in the story but false out here."
