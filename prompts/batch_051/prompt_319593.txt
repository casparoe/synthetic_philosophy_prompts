In a reading group on the philosophy of biology, one participant has been defending the propensity interpretation of fitness against the familiar charge that natural selection is empirically vacuous. Her standard move is to reach for an analogy. Fitness, she argues, should be understood on the model of a coin's bias, or of a crystal's solubility. A coin can be biased toward heads even if, in some particular run of tosses, heads comes up less often than tails; a lump of salt is water-soluble even if this very lump is never actually dropped into water. Likewise, she maintains, an organism type can be fit even if, through bad luck, it leaves fewer descendants than a less fit type in some actual sequence of events. The analogy is being made to do real argumentative work: it is supposed to show that fitness is a genuine dispositional property of organism types, causally relevant to reproductive outcomes but not identical with them, and therefore that natural selection does not reduce to the empty claim that whatever leaves more offspring leaves more offspring.

I want to know how much weight this analogy can actually carry. First, give me the correspondence table in full: what plays the role of the coin, the bias, the toss, the sequence of outcomes, and what plays the role of the solute, the solvent, and the dissolving event once the image shifts to solubility; say where the mapping lines up well and where it begins to strain. Then address the places where the analogy may be quietly importing assumptions from its source domain: the stability of the propensities across trials, the independence of tosses, the idea that there is a single disposition rather than a loosely connected family of them (viability, fecundity, mating success, and so on), the fact that the environment partly specifies which dispositions are in play and changes as the population evolves, and the problem of identifying the bearer of the propensity in the first place. For each disanalogy, tell me whether it matters for the specific work this analogy is being asked to do — answering the tautology objection — or whether it is a harmless idealization. End with a verdict: once the disanalogies are on the table, has the tautology objection been dissolved, or has it merely been relocated into the problem of measuring fitness independently of actual reproductive success? If the coin or solubility picture fails, say whether some better analogy is available, and whether the propensity interpretation stands or falls with it.
