I’m a junior in high school and preparing a short talk for our philosophy club on whether instrumental music can be sad. My friend Chloe has taken a firm position: she says it can’t, because sadness is a mental state, and sound waves don’t have mental states. She also argues that when a piece has no lyrics, title, or composer’s program, calling it sad is just a learned association—something we project onto the music rather than something the music itself possesses. I find this too quick. When I listen to a slow piece in a minor key, the sadness seems somehow to be in the music, not merely caused in me by it. But I can’t say what “in the music” could mean without treating the music as if it had feelings.

My music teacher once mentioned that the nineteenth-century critic Eduard Hanslick argued that music does not represent definite or nameable emotions, yet also insisted that this doesn’t reduce music to empty sound or mere subjective response. I don’t fully understand what he meant, and my teacher warned me not to treat music as a kind of emotional telegram. How should I answer Chloe? Is she right, and if not, where exactly does her argument go wrong—and is there a defensible way to say that music is sad without either treating it as a person or reducing the sadness to nothing more than what listeners happen to feel?
