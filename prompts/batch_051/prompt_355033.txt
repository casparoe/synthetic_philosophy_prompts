At a dinner party recently, a friend quoted Epicurus's slogan "death is nothing to us" to settle an argument about whether it is rational to fear one's own death. He took the line to show that such fear rests on a simple mistake and that equanimity toward death is the only rationally defensible attitude. I am not yet convinced that the slogan does that much work, because it seems to shift as one presses on it: "us" points sometimes at the living person, sometimes at the person who no longer exists, and "nothing" seems to mean different things in "nothing to the living" and "nothing to the dead." In my former scientific work I was used to statements that had a single, testable content, so I want to know exactly what is being asserted before granting the inference. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan might be used to express, from the nearly trivial to the substantive and possibly false, say which of them are defensible, and indicate which, if any, actually support my friend's conclusion that fear of death is irrational?
