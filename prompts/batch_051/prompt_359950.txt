You're an intellectual historian who works on the French Enlightenment and on formal social choice theory, so you know Condorcet backwards and forwards. Think carefully — start by quietly working through the moving parts of the jury theorem in your own words before you commit to any predictions.

Here's the task. Imagine Condorcet wakes up today, gets a week of briefings, and then has to give his honest take on some modern mechanisms for collective decision-making: prediction markets, futarchy (people vote on what outcomes they value, but they bet on which policies will actually produce them), liquid democracy (you can vote directly or delegate your vote to someone else), and AI-assisted judging or policy analysis. For each one, predict where he would come down and give the reasoning he would probly offer — not the lazy version where he likes anything because it involves math, but the specific arguments someone with his jury-theorem commitments would actually make.

The interesting pressure points: the theorem says that, with independent voters who are each more than 50% likely to be right, large majorities converge on the truth. But so much of modern life — social media, viral news, correlated punditry — breaks that independence. Would he take that as a reason to redesign institutions in order to restore independence, or as a deeper problem for the whole program? Remember that he also knew majority rule can cycle, so his enthusiasm for aggregating judgments was never unqualified — make that tension do some work. Also, Condorcet was a practicing reformer, not just a mathematician: he wanted probability to guide real constitutional design. So try to get at where he would see these new mechanisms as a natural extension of his project, where he would see them as a dangerous perversion of it, and which one would give him the most trouble deciding. End with the one hard question he would ask the people pitching these tools, and don't let him dodge it.
