A position I keep encountering, associated with Daniel Dennett, runs roughly as follows. The real danger of AI systems that can pass as human is not that they will become conscious or seize power, but that they will function as counterfeit people. The guiding analogy is counterfeit currency. A single counterfeit bill does its damage less through the loss suffered by the person who ends up holding it than through what it does to everyone's willingness to accept bills at all: once counterfeits are circulating, each acceptance of a bill becomes a small act of trust that the bill may not repay. In the same way, the argument continues, once AI can produce text, voices, and faces that pass as human, the harm is not just the occasional successful deception; it is a general erosion of the default trust on which testimony, journalism, legal evidence, and ordinary conversation depend. The proposed remedy follows the currency analogy: require AI systems to bear visible markers of their non-human status, and prohibit passing them off as human, just as currency is made difficult to counterfeit and knowingly passing counterfeits is a crime.
