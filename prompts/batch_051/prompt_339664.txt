I'm finishing a reading group on envy, resentment, and justice, and for the last session I want to close with a poem rather than another recap of the readings. The thread that kept resurfacing for us was the Rawlsian split between envy and resentment. The way we read it, resentment is a moral emotion: it says someone has gotten more than the rules themselves entitle them to, so the right response is to object to the arrangement, fix the rules. Envy is not really moral in that way; it's distress at another person's advantage even when that advantage is perfectly fair, a kind of leveling-down urge to see the other person lose the good simply because you don't have it, and Rawls treats it as a vice. But then he also says envy can be excusable under certain conditions, roughly when inequalities are so large that they eat at people's self-respect, so the envy is evidence that the basic structure has failed even though the envious impulse aims at the wrong thing. That last move is the one none of us could quite let go of.

So I'd like you to write a villanelle — the actual 19-line form with two refrains recurring in the proper pattern — in the voice of a single speaker who can't tell whether what they're feeling is envy or resentment. Maybe they've watched a colleague get a promotion under rules that are technically legal but obviously rigged, or a neighbor come into money through no wrongdoing, and they keep sliding between wanting the person's good destroyed and wanting the arrangement itself challenged. One refrain should be the envious voice and the other the resentful voice, even if neither announces itself as such; the way the refrains keep trading places and then collide in the closing two lines should do the structural work of showing why the two feelings are almost indistinguishable from the inside while being morally different from the outside. And in that final collision, try to leave the reader unsure whether the envious refrain has become excusable because of the conditions the resentful refrain describes — don't resolve it with a tidy moral, since thats precisely where our discussion stalled.

Write as a poet who actually knows the philosophy, not someone who name-drops it. Before you draft, take a few sentences to brainstorm what the two refrains will be and how the middle turn of the poem will shift their meaning. Then give the poem.
