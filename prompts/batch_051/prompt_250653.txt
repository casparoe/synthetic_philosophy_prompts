I've been trying to design an epistocratic arrangement that avoids the standard objection that any special power given to experts will be captured and turned into permanent rule by a credentialed class. I call the proposal a "negative epistemic veto," and I'd like an honest assessment of whether it's coherent, whether it already exists under another name, and where it actually breaks down.

The idea is this. For a narrow class of legislative decisions that are both high-stakes and hard to reverse—say, long-term environmental commitments, changes to the fiscal or monetary constitution, or authorizing a genuinely new class of weapons—a panel of domain experts would be selected by sortition from a pre-registered pool of credentialed practitioners in the relevant field. The panel could not propose legislation, amend it, or enact anything. Its only power would be to impose friction: by a two-thirds vote, it could require that the legislature pass the measure by a three-fifths supermajority after a six-month cooling-off period. The panel would then dissolve. The claim is that this preserves democratic self-rule because the demos retains the final say, while reducing the risk of irreversible error in technical domains. It is meant to differ from John Stuart Mill's plural voting proposal in *Considerations on Representative Government* by being negative rather than positive, domain-specific rather than general, and rotating by sortition rather than grounded in a standing educational class.

I think this is genuinely distinct from the usual epistocracy proposals, but I'm not sure. Would a committed democrat have to reject it? Does the sortition-of-experts element actually solve the capture problem, or does it just move the contest to the composition of the pre-registered pool and the credentialing process? And is the "negative-only power" difference real, or would a veto of this kind inevitably function as a positive gatekeeping power in practice?
