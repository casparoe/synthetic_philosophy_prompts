I am an undergraduate enrolled in a university seminar on moral responsibility, and I have been asked to take part in a grading-calibration exercise for our midterm essays. Below are the question that was set and a student's answer to it. Please grade the answer and provide the specific, point-by-point comments a conscientious grader would attach to that grade. I would like the feedback to flag subtle errors, not just obvious ones, and to explain how the grade follows from the strengths and weaknesses you identify. Please give a numeric mark out of 20.

Question:

Explain P. F. Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes in his essay Freedom and Resentment. Your answer should cover (i) what the reactive attitudes are and what they respond to; (ii) the distinction between the participant stance and the objective stance, including how the two kinds of consideration that Strawson distinguishes—excuses and exemptions—relate to that stance distinction; and (iii) why Strawson thinks the truth of determinism would not constitute a general reason to abandon the reactive attitudes.

Student answer:

Strawson's overall aim is to move the debate about free will and determinism away from abstract questions about whether determinism is compatible with something called free will. He focuses instead on the actual practices through which we hold one another responsible. These practices are structured by the reactive attitudes: emotions such as resentment, gratitude, indignation, and guilt, through which we respond to the quality of will that others seem to manifest toward us (and, in the self-directed case, to our own conduct). Resentment, for example, responds to perceived ill will or a lack of due regard, while gratitude responds to good will toward us.

In having these attitudes, Strawson says, we occupy the participant stance: an engaged, non-detached relation to other people as fellow members of a moral community. By contrast, the objective stance consists in setting the reactive attitudes aside and treating a person as an object to be managed, handled, cured, or trained. On Strawson's view, the objective stance is appropriate only toward people who are not apt targets of the reactive attitudes, such as the severely mentally ill or very young children.

Strawson also distinguishes two different kinds of consideration that alter our responsibility responses. Excuses, such as the claim that the agent did not intend the outcome or did not know what she was doing, operate within the participant stance: they show that the act did not really manifest the ill will it seemed to, so they modify a particular reactive attitude while leaving intact the agent's standing as a member of the moral community. Exemptions, by contrast, such as the observation that the agent is severely mentally ill or only a child, do not deny that the act had whatever quality of will it had. Instead, they tell us that the agent is not a suitable object of the reactive attitudes at all, and they accordingly move us toward the objective stance.

Determinism, Strawson argues, is neither an excuse nor an exemption. It is a perfectly general thesis about the causal order of events; it does not bear on whether any particular act expressed ill will, and it does not single out some psychologically abnormal class of agents as unfit for the participant stance. So the usual route into the objective stance is not available to it. More fundamentally, the participant stance is not something we can simply decide to leave behind: the reactive attitudes are inescapable for human beings, and a life in which we viewed everyone, including those closest to us, under the objective stance at all times would not be recognizably human. Since these attitudes are unavoidable, they are rationally justified, and that is why the truth of determinism gives us no general reason to abandon them.
