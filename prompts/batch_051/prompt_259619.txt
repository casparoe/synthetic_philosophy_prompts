I am a philosophy professor serving on a university committee that reviews proposals for small, faculty-supervised evening discussions at which wine may be served. The committee meets twice each month and typically inspects six to twelve proposals; all decisions are final unless a proposal is sent to the dean for a values-based exception. The other committee members are a dean of students, a college head, and a campus safety officer, none of whom know ancient philosophy. I have been asked to draft a reusable decision procedure, not an essay, grounded in a passage I keep returning to.

In R.G. Bury's translation of Plato's Laws, Book I, the Athenian says:

"For consider: in the case of a man whose disposition is morose and savage (whence spring numberless iniquities), is it not more dangerous to test him by entering into money transactions with him, at one's own personal risk, than by associating with him with the help of Dionysus and his festive insight?"

I take Plato to be recommending something more than a safety rule: wine, carefully administered, works as a low-cost test of dispositions and as practice in self-mastery. The committee's current default is to approve only events with a predictable, quantifiable educational agenda and to reject anything that looks like social drinking. I want the procedure to take Plato's claim seriously without turning every wine-accompanied dinner into a philosophy seminar.

Please write a decision procedure the committee can actually use. It should include:

1. The questions to ask in order, phrased in ordinary language, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each.
2. The point at which a proposal is rejected because it fails the Platonic test condition, and the point at which a non-Platonic reason—safety, consent, institutional purpose—can also stop the process.
3. The distinctions from Laws I that must survive simplification, and which parts of Plato's argument you would not import into a modern university context.
4. A one-page summary checklist the campus safety officer could tick through in ten minutes.

Then run the procedure on these three proposals:

- A classics professor proposes a term-long graduate seminar on Greek lyric poetry that ends each week with wine and informal conversation; the explicit goal is participant well-being and cohort bonding.
- A residential college head, who is also a philosophy instructor, proposes a monthly evening called a "Platonic test," in which residents over age 21 drink a set maximum amount while a sober moderator introduces deliberately provocative discussion; participants write private self-assessments before and after.
- A faculty development office proposes a weekend retreat where wine is served at a concluding dinner, with no structured discussion, on the rationale that colleagues will later be more candid in workshops.

Please include one case where the procedure gives an answer I will not like, and explain why I should accept it. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do—where it must hand the decision to a person rather than a checklist.
