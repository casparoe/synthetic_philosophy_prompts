I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. My working vocabulary is the technical one of language-model engineering: next-token prediction, conditional distributions, autoregressive objectives, pretraining and fine-tuning corpora, sampling and decoding, calibration, RLHF, reward models, evaluation metrics, and ground truth. I want to understand Harry Frankfurt's distinction between lying and bullshit—and its relation to ordinary error—by restating it in that vocabulary, and I want a rigorous account of what does not survive the restatement.

As I understand the distinction: lying requires that the speaker believe the claim is false and nevertheless assert it with the aim of producing a false belief in the hearer. Lying therefore depends on a certain relation to the truth, because the liar has to keep track of how things are in order to hide them. Bullshit is different: the bullshitter is not trying to get the facts wrong, but lacks a concern with whether the claims are true or false, and speaks toward some other goal, such as suiting the conversational circumstances or producing an effect in the audience. Ordinary error is distinct from both: the speaker means to track the truth and misses. There is also a stronger claim often read out of Frankfurt: bullshit is more corrosive to the norm of truth than lying, because the liar still acknowledges that norm while the bullshitter has stopped caring about it.
