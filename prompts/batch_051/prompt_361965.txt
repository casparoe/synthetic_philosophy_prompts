Suppose that, starting next year, a cheap and reliable welfare meter for farmed aquatic animals becomes available. The device is handheld, costs a few dollars per reading, and gives a validated real-time score of negative affective state—pain, fear, distress—in bony fishes and decapod crustaceans. It works on farmed salmon, trout, and shrimp, and it can be operated by any trained technician or inspector. Its readings are widely accepted by scientists, inspectors, and certification auditors as accurate and difficult to game; assume the underlying science is settled in-world, so there is no serious doubt that the device tracks something genuinely analogous to pain. Nothing else changes directly: no new welfare legislation is passed in anticipation of the device, and any change in practices, standards, or markets has to be produced endogenously.

Predict how welfare in finfish and crustacean aquaculture actually changes over the next twenty years. Cover at least: slaughter and pre-slaughter stunning for salmon, trout, and shrimp; stocking density; eyestalk ablation (the common hatchery practice of removing one eyestalk of a female shrimp to induce spawning); live transport of crustaceans; certification schemes and retailer sourcing; and the divergence between countries that adopt the device as a regulatory standard and those that do not. Which changes come quickly through market pressure, which come slowly through regulation, and which are resisted almost regardless of what the device shows?

Give a definite projection rather than a menu of scenarios, and defend it by reasoning about mechanisms, incentives, and constraints. For each major claim, say whether it is robust or a guess, how confident you are, and which one or two assumptions, if they failed, would most change the overall picture. Do not provide a bibliography or citations; this is a request for an argued forecast, not a literature review.
