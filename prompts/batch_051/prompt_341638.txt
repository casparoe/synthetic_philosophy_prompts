I am currently taking a metaphysics course, and we have spent the last few weeks on the special composition question—the question of when some things compose a further thing. The standard answers we covered (nihilism, universalism, and the proposal that only living organisms compose) all seem to me to buy their clarity at too high a price, so I have been trying to work out a middle criterion of my own and would like an honest assessment of it before I commit to it in my term paper.

I am calling it the causal-coupling criterion. The central claim is that some things compose an object just in case they form a mutually load-bearing network. One thing is load-bearing for another, as I am using the term, when removing the first would, over the span of time the arrangement normally persists, make a non-negligible difference to the future course of the second; and a collection composes an object when its members are linked into a single connected network by that relation. A table passes the test: remove the top and the legs fall over; remove a leg and the top will tip or collapse. A heap of sand fails it: remove a grain and almost no other grain's future trajectory is altered, so there is no heap, only grains arranged heapwise. A hydrogen atom passes it, since the proton and the electron each would behave very differently in the absence of the other—which also shows the criterion is less restrictive than the living-organisms view, while still excluding arbitrary or causally inert fusions.

I can already see two worries. First, the criterion may overgenerate, since two stars in a wide but gravitationally bound orbit would presumably count as a single object, as might any collection whose members interact in very slight ways. Second, I have not been able to spell out "non-negligible" without reintroducing exactly the kind of vagueness I was trying to avoid. Could you spell out the strongest objections to the criterion, say whether any of them is decisive rather than merely a technical nuisance, and tell me whether I have simply rediscovered an existing position in the mereology literature under another name?
