So I was just arguing with a friend about Ulysses contracts, and she kept falling back on the standard Elster-style point: binding yourself in advance is only justified when you're protecting your considered preferences from a predictable, temporary sliver of yourself—Ulysses still wants to hear the sirens and survive, he just can't be trusted during the singing. But here's what I couldn't get past: suppose the binding mechanism isn't a mast and ropes. Suppose its an irrevocable smart contract or an AI agent that enforces the earlier instruction forever, no matter how much the later self has changed, and it can't be talked out of it. Does the Ulysses contract argument survive that change? Or does it break and end up justifying something else—like letting a dead self govern the living? I'm asking about what happens to the argument, not whether people should use the gadget.
