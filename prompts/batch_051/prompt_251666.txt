I am producing a long-form philosophy podcast, and in two weeks I will record an episode with a moral philosopher whose recent book defends a rehabilitated Schopenhauerian ethics of compassion. Her position, as she presents it, is that Schopenhauer was right that compassion—Mitleid, understood as immediate participation in another's suffering rather than a condescending or self-congratulatory sentiment—is the only moral motive that is both psychologically real and finally defensible, and that the later turn to duty, principle, and impartial rule was a mistake. She grants that pity has familiar pathologies: it is partial, easily manipulated, prone to burnout, and can demean the person it fixes upon as a sufferer. Her standard answer is to distinguish raw pity from what she calls disciplined or reflective pity, and to claim that the latter is untouched by Nietzsche's criticisms—that Nietzsche was diagnosing only the raw form, and that a mature ethics consists in cultivating the reflective form rather than replacing pity with principle. I have interviewed her once before and know her pattern: she does not deny objections so much as absorb them, patiently showing that they apply only to raw pity while her own account already contains the correction. She is difficult to catch out because the distinction keeps expanding to cover whatever has just been said.

Please write me a sequence of six to eight questions, with the follow-up you would press after her likely reply, that makes this absorption strategy progressively less tenable. Begin by forcing a precise statement of what distinguishes disciplined from raw pity, and in particular what it is that does the disciplining. Then move through the hardest pressure points: if the disciplining standard is something other than pity itself—say impartiality or respect for persons—then her view begins to look like a disguised principle-ethics; if it is more pity, then the bias and partiality may simply reappear at the level of the discipline. Press the concern that even disciplined pity makes another's suffering its intentional object and in that way still condescends or fixes the other in the role of victim. Press the limits of a motive so tied to the concrete and near-at-hand when it comes to distant strangers, future generations, or the morally unappealing. And press the empirical problems of compassion fatigue and the familiar finding that a single named victim moves people more than a statistic. The arc should close with a question about what evidence, experience, or clear counterexample would count against her view—not something she could absorb into the distinction, but something whose acknowledgment would force genuine revision. The questions should engage the substance of her claims; please do not make them requests for citations or literature surveys.
