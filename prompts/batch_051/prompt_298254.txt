I have been developing a principle I call the Single-Trajectory Criterion, and I would like an honest assessment of whether it is a contribution, a restatement, or a mistake.

The background is the distinction from ergodicity economics between ensemble averages and time averages. An ensemble average averages over many parallel copies of a process at a single moment; a time average averages a single copy as it unfolds. In non-ergodic processes these diverge: a gamble that multiplies wealth by either 1.5 or 0.6 with equal probability has positive expected value per round, but its typical trajectory shrinks over time, because the relevant growth rate for a single path is the geometric mean rather than the arithmetic mean.

My principle takes this distinction and makes it normative. The Single-Trajectory Criterion says that a policy, gamble, or institutional arrangement is justified for a person only if its justification could be lived by one person as a single continuous trajectory through time. Ensemble-average benefits count as reasons only insofar as they coincide with time-average benefits for the affected person; where the two diverge, only the time average has justificatory force. In short, you may not cite an average across possible lives if no possible single life could actually traverse the path that average describes.

This seems to me to explain several things. It explains why it can be rational for a particular person to decline some positive-expected-value gambles: the expectation is carried by parallel versions of her, none of whom is her. It explains why a public-health intervention that is good in aggregate can still fail to justify itself to the person exposed to the risk, if repeated exposure over a lifetime would be ruinous. It even seems to me to rederive the force of deontological side-constraints: you may not kill one person to save five, because the five saved lives are parallel branches in a non-ergodic aggregation, while the one person killed is a single trajectory that is simply ended.

I am aware this could be confused with ordinary risk aversion, expected utility theory, or Rawlsian ideas about the separateness of persons. Tell me: is this a genuinely distinct principle, or just one of those views in new terminology? What is the closest existing view? What does it get right, and where does it break — for example, are there cases where ensemble averages are legitimately decisive even when they cannot be lived as a single trajectory?
