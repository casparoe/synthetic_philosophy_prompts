I have no formal training in these topics, but I have followed the public argument about whether advanced artificial intelligence threatens human survival for years, mostly through essays, podcasts, and comment sections, and I am struck by how little anyone moves. One side argues that a sufficiently capable system would by default pursue power, self-preservation, and resources, because these are useful sub-goals of almost any objective; unless the alignment problem is solved first, a system smarter than us will find ways to keep us from shutting it down. The other side replies that this is a story rather than a mechanism: without a concrete, testable account of how a model trained on gradients goes from predicting text or optimizing a reward signal to taking over infrastructure, the scenario cannot be falsified, and its plausibility comes from analogy and fiction rather than from how machine learning systems actually behave. The first side responds that demanding a worked mechanism before taking the risk seriously is exactly the error one would make before a one-shot, no-base-rate event. The second side responds that this move makes the view unfalsifiable, because every absence of evidence gets reinterpreted as evidence for the claim. And that is roughly where every thread ends.

I am not asking you to settle who is right; I am asking for a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. My own amateur hypothesis is that the two camps are answering different questions under one heading: one is asking what prudent engineering and governance would look like if worst-case scenarios are treated as the relevant planning baseline, while the other is asking which concrete failure mechanisms the available evidence actually supports. Both questions could be legitimate, and the answers would never meet. But I can also see other possibilities: a disagreement about standards of evidence, about whether intelligence naturally generalizes into a single optimizing agent, about where the burden of proof sits under asymmetric stakes, or about whether probability talk is even meaningful for an unobserved class of events. Diagnose which of these it is, or whether it is something else, in terms each side would recognize, and explain what would count as progress given that diagnosis, such as a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a changed question, or a reason no progress should be expected.
