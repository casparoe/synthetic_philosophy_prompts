I've been arguing with a friend about whether an AI that can't act on its judgments could still count as a moral agent. I'm attracted to a broadly functionalist view: what matters is having the right sort of evaluative representations, being able to deliberate, respond to reasons, and regard itself as answerable. Action doesn't seem strictly necessary. After all, a fully paralyzed person who can judge that something would be wrong, and can hold herself responsible for that judgment, is still a moral agent in a pretty important sense. So on that view, an AI locked in a sandbox that forms judgments like "I ought not to release this data" or "this decision imposes an unfair risk"—but whose outputs get overridden or filtered before they touch the world—would still be an agent, because it is the one doing the weighing and willing, not just a mouthpiece.

But then I think of a monitoring model that just labels inputs as "harmful" or a classifier that outputs "risk=0.92." I have a strong intuition that calling one of those a moral agent is a category error, even if its judgments are incredibly sophisticated. It has evaluative representations, but absent any connection to action—absent even the possibility of its judgment making a difference—it seems more like a critic than an agent. So I'm torn: the theory says action isn't needed, the intuition says a pure judge with no reach is not an agent. I don't want a verdict from on high. Give me the strongest version of both sides, say what would justify each concession (amending the theory to require at least in-principle efficacy, or debunking the intuition as an artifact of how we recognize agency), and whether there's a reading of "agent" where both survive.
