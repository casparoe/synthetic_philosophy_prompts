A blog post I read lays out futarchy like this: "The idea is to separate what we want from how to get it. Under futarchy people still vote, but not on policies: they democratically agree on a metric for national welfare, like GDP per capita or some wider measure of well-being, and then prediction markets decide which policies will move that metric the most. When a policy is proposed, people bet on whether it will raise or lower the metric; if the market says it will raise it more then the alternatives, the policy gets enacted. Traders have skin in the game, so they only bet on what they actually think is true, not on what looks good on tv, which makes the market's forecast about as unbiased and accurate a picture of the world as you could hope for. So futarchy keeps democracy where it matters, at the level of values, and replaces it with markets only where it doesn't, namely the means-end question of which policy works." The post ends by saying futarchy would finally remove interest-group politics, because nobody can buy a whole prediction market but you can absolutely buy a senator. I'm inclined to buy the "democracy sets the goal, markets pick the means" split, but the stuff about markets being unbiased and interest groups disappearing feels too strong. Is this a fair summary of Hanson's futarchy? Go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a defender would accept, what's being conflated or overstated, and whether the final claim about interest-group politics survives.
