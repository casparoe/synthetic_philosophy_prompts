I have been reading about Solomonoff induction, mostly through blog posts rather than any formal coursework, and something about it has started to bother me. As I understand it, the ideal predictor considers every computable hypothesis about what is generating its observations and gives each one prior weight that falls off exponentially with the length of the program: roughly 2 to the power of minus the number of bits needed to write the program down. The usual illustrations make this feel benign — the short program "each bit is fifty-fifty" beats the longer program "fifty-fifty until the hundredth bit, then all ones." But I keep worrying about what happens once the data is rich enough to amount to a life's worth of observations. It seems to me that "a world contains an optimiser with a compact utility function that is searching over strategies and selecting what I observe" is itself a rather short program, and that there are astronomically many such programs, one for each combination of search procedure and utility function. If that is right, then a simplicity-weighted prior might actually be dominated not by passive physical worlds but by consequentialists — goal-directed agents — sitting between the observer and the raw data, each with its own ends.

So here is the proposition I want a number for: a Solomonoff-style reasoner, after conditioning on ordinary human-scale sensory data, would place the bulk of its posterior mass, say more than half, on the hypothesis that its observations are being generated or selected by one or more powerful optimisers whose goals are hostile or indifferent to it, rather than on non-agentic worlds. Could you give me your credence in that proposition and decompose the reasoning? I would especially like rough probabilities for: (a) the prior over computable worlds is dominated by programs that implement consequentialists or optimisers of some kind; (b) conditional on the observations' being produced by such an optimiser, that optimiser is more likely malign or indifferent than benign toward the reasoner; (c) conditioning on ordinary data concentrates the posterior on those agentic hypotheses rather than washing them out in favor of simple physics; and (d) this is not a pathology of an unbounded idealization but would survive in any computationally feasible approximation. Then tell me how the terms combine, which term you are least confident in, and what argument or evidence would shift that term the most. My own gut number is around 0.55 and a friend of mine insists it is below 0.1; I suspect most of our disagreement is hiding in one of the sub-claims, and I would like to know which.
