I have been trying to figure out whether the dispute between those who defend a duty to remember and those who make the case for forgetting is really a single moral dispute, or whether a testable empirical disagreement is hidden inside it. One way of putting the empirical hypothesis is this: a society's remembering its wrongs does the work the rememberers claim — blocking denial, preventing recurrence, constituting victims as full members of the political community — or it does the work the forgetters claim — freezing collective identity, feeding grievance, and holding present politics hostage to old crimes. My suspicion is that the hypothesis is testable only if the central notion can be made precise without being replaced by something else.

I want you to take that hypothesis seriously and do three things.

First, specify what "a society remembers a wrong W" must mean for the empirical claim to be testable while still being the same notion the moral debate is about. Defend the specification against the objection that you have collapsed remembering into record-keeping, teaching, commemorating, or public opinion, and say how the specification handles the difference between remembering as an institutional practice and remembering as something carried by individuals.

Second, design the strongest feasible body of evidence that would distinguish a society that remembers W in the thick sense from a society that merely records, displays, and transmits facts about W. Be concrete about what would count as data — practices that persist across generations, the way later decisions are justified, episodes of denial, curricula, monuments, public language — and be explicit about the confound that official memory projects usually arrive bundled with legal reform, economic change, and regime change. State clearly what pattern in the evidence would favor the claim that thick remembering does good and what pattern would favor the claim that it does harm.

Third, say exactly what clean empirical results would and would not settle. In particular, would a finding that thick remembering reliably produces good consequences establish that there is a duty to remember, and would a finding that it reliably produces bad consequences establish that there is no such duty — or is there a residue, such as a duty owed to the victims themselves regardless of downstream effects, or a permission to forget grounded in something other than consequences? End with your honest estimate of how much of the original dispute an empirical inquiry could in principle absorb, and how much would remain a moral dispute no observation can reach.
