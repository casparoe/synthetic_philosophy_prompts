In *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined*, John Austin gives a distinctively institutional reason for the maxim that ignorance of the law is no excuse. On his view, the maxim does not rest on a fiction that everyone knows the law, nor primarily on a claim that each citizen negligently failed to learn it. It rests instead on something closer to this consideration: if courts had to decide, case by case, whether a particular defendant's ignorance of a valid legal rule was something the defendant could have avoided, the administration of law would become unreliable, and litigants would have a standing incentive to manufacture doubt about what they knew. The cost would fall not only on the culpable or the knowledgeable, but on the general practice of making and enforcing rules at all.

I want to test whether Austin's institutional argument can be separated from the substantive fairness question. Construct a thought experiment in which a realistic legal system—say a municipality enforcing a little-known licensing or environmental rule—introduces an ignorance-of-law defense specifically aimed at meeting Austin's worry. The defense must satisfy all of these specifications:

1. It applies to a rule that no ordinary citizen can be assumed to know from everyday social life, not to mala in se offenses.
2. It does not require the judge to read minds: it uses only evidence a real court can actually handle, such as publication records, translation, accessibility of the official text, or whether the defendant consulted knowledgeable officials.
3. The procedure must be administrable at scale, not just workable for a single elaborate trial.
4. The scenario must show one defendant who clearly meets the defense and one who clearly does not, where the difference between them is purely in their epistemic access conditions and does no independent moral work.

After the scenario, walk through Austin's argument and say precisely where the institutional objection would still bite despite the design. Then say which feature of the design, if removed, makes the case collapse back into the standard maxim, and how a defender of Austin would attack the scenario.
