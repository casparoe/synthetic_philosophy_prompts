I keep finding that my views on forgiveness are more consistent in the particular than in the abstract, and I would like you to help me extract the principle from the cases.

1. My sister borrowed four thousand dollars from me, swore she would repay it, then gambled it away and lied about it for two years. She has now been sober for six years and has apologized in detail. I forgave her, and I believe I should have done so — not because her apology automatically earned forgiveness, but because continuing to withhold it would eventually have been a kind of cruelty.

2. A close friend repeated a secret I had asked her to keep. She apologized once, plainly, without excuses. I forgave her immediately. That seems simply right.

3. My uncle molested me when I was a child. He is now dying and still denies that it happened, but he has sent word through my cousin that he asks my forgiveness. I have refused even to respond, and I do not think I owe him anything.

4. My father belittled me throughout my childhood and died without ever apologizing. A year ago I decided, on my own, that I forgave him. That felt appropriate. But I doubt I could have done it — or should have — while he was alive and still belittling me.

5. My ex-wife had an affair, told me herself, apologized, and asked whether we could stay together. I said "I forgive you" quickly, mostly to keep the marriage. I later came to mean it, but at the time the words were closer to an aspiration than an act. I still do not know whether that first utterance was a lie, a promise, or a kind of forgiveness.

6. A colleague used my material in a paper without asking and has never acknowledged it. We are professionally cordial now and I no longer stew about it, but I would not call what I have done forgiveness: I would call it writing off the account.

7. A friend's teenage son killed a man while driving drunk. At sentencing, the widow said she forgave the boy. I was moved and inclined to call it admirable. Another friend insisted the widow had no standing to forgive him, because the forgiveness belonged to her husband, who is dead. I cannot settle this.

8. My mother knew, or at least suspected, what my uncle was doing and looked away. She has apologized, but only in general terms — she is sorry for "whatever she failed to do." I keep going back and forth on whether I can forgive her, and whether it would even be possible to forgive her before she names the specific thing she is asking to be forgiven for.

9. A stranger stepped hard on my foot and then apologized. I said it was fine and meant it. That seems too trivial to count as forgiveness at all.

10. A friend presses me to forgive my uncle anyway, on the grounds that forgiveness is something I do for myself, not for the wrongdoer, and that it would free me. I resist this. It seems to make the wrongdoer a prop in my own therapy, and I do not want to perform something called forgiveness toward a man who would receive it as an absolution I do not intend to give.

Notice, in particular, the pair of cases that most surprises me. I forgave my father although he never apologized, but only once he was dead; I refuse to forgive my uncle although he too is unrepentant, and the only obvious difference is that he is still alive and still asking. I cannot tell whether that difference is doing real work or is a rationalization.

The working principle I keep reaching for is roughly this: forgiveness is owed once the wrongdoer has acknowledged the specific wrong and the forgiver no longer needs the resentment as self-protection; and forgiveness may be given without repentance only when the relationship can no longer be renegotiated, which explains the father but not the uncle. But I can see the principle straining against the widow in case 7 and the mother in case 8, and it gives me no natural place for the self-directed act in case 10.

Please give me the best principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures these verdicts, or comes as close as is honest. State it precisely, and identify what each clause is doing: which of my cases, or which feature of a case, it is there to handle. Apply the principle to a few further cases I have not considered, and tell me what it predicts about the two cases I cannot settle: my mother, and the widow. Say plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and whether those verdicts should be revised rather than saved by complicating the principle. Above all, I want the principle to tell me whether forgiveness can ever be obligatory and whether it can ever be unconditional — or whether those are the wrong questions to ask.
