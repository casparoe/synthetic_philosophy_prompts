I'm helping run a week-long workshop for policy analysts, engineers, and grantmakers who evaluate forecasts about advanced AI systems, and I keep seeing people slide between claims about what a system can do and claims about what it will want. I want a decision-tree instrument for the first afternoon that sorts each participant into a position on the following question: how much can you infer about an AI's goals from the fact that its capabilities are very high? Start everyone from the same shared commitment, that they are being asked to predict what a system vastly more capable than any human would be trying to do given only that it is vastly more capable, and let the first question branch from there. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of named views: work out the branch questions first, then the combinations of answers those questions allow, then locate positions in those combinations. Every node should be a concrete question someone can answer without already knowing the labels, and each leaf should be labelled by its commitments rather than by an author's name. The distinctions I most need the tree to respect are these: whether constraints on terminal goals and claims about convergent instrumental subgoals are kept separate; whether convergence is intended as a rational necessity or an empirical regularity; and whether the convergent subgoals are only self-regarding things like self-preservation and resource acquisition or also other-regarding things like cooperation and reputation. Make sure the tree can land a participant in at least these leaves: the view that very high capability itself narrows the class of possible terminal goals; the orthodox package on which terminal goals are orthogonal to capability but certain instrumental subgoals converge across nearly any final goal; a view that accepts orthogonality but treats convergence as a contingent feature of how real systems happen to be trained rather than a deep fact about goal-directedness; and a view that regards the convergence claims as overfit to toy agents whose goals are specified in advance. Aim for a compact instrument of roughly seven leaves, but do not force the space beyond what it supports, and explicitly flag any two positions the tree cannot separate.

As you present the tree, tell me which separation or leaf assignment you are least confident about, and say what consideration, conceptual, mathematical, or empirical, would lead you to restructure it; in other words, identify where the tree is most likely to go wrong and what would change your mind about it.
