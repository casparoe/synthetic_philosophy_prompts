I am a novelist preparing to write the second half of a book whose central character has been alive, unaged, for centuries. The structural decision I must make is whether her condition should be rendered as an in-principle tragedy or as a very long ordinary problem, and Bernard Williams's Makropulos argument bears on that decision directly. Let me set out my understanding of the argument so that we are anchored to the same claim. Williams took the woman in Karel Čapek's play, kept alive by an elixir for more than three centuries, to have reached a state of profound tedium and to be ready to stop living; he generalized from this what I will call the Makropulos conclusion. A human being whose psychology is like ours has, among her desires, some whose objects give her reasons to go on living. In an endless life under favorable conditions, either her character remains recognizably the same, in which case those life-giving desires will eventually be exhausted and boredom will come to dominate, or her character changes enough to keep generating such desires, in which case the later person is so psychologically removed from the original that the original's death has not really been avoided. Immortality would therefore be either unlivably tedious or not the same person's survival.

Here is the proposition on which I want your credence:

P: The Makropulos conclusion is broadly correct for beings whose psychology is continuous with ours. For an actual human being living endlessly with health and material security assured, there comes a point after which she either no longer has desires whose objects give her a reason to go on living, so that continued life is dominated by boredom or indifference, or the only way to sustain such desires is a degree of psychological change that severs the relevant continuity between the earlier person and the later one. The phenomenon is therefore not merely an insufficiency of imagination or a defect of a particular character.

I am not asking whether the outcome could vary with constitution or circumstance; uncertainty about that should be folded into your number, not used to avoid giving one.

4. Narrative bias: how likely is it that the intuition behind the Makropulos case is largely an artifact of the thought that a life, like a story, can have shape and meaning only if it ends, a prejudice a novelist might be especially liable to?
