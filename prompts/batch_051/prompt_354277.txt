Consider two versions of the same project team. In the first, every member has independently read an internal report showing that the project will miss its deadline; each privately believes this on identical evidence. But no one knows that the others have read the report. In the second version, the first-order facts about the project and each person's evidence for them are exactly the same, but the circulation of the report was itself public: each member knows that everyone has read it, knows that each knows this, and so on without bound. The two versions thus differ only in the higher-order structure of everyone's knowledge, not in what any individual knows about the project.

Ordinary practice treats these two states as radically different. In the first, the team continues to plan against the official deadline, and a member who unilaterally acted on the report would be seen as having gone rogue. In the second, ignoring the report is a collective failure, and a member who announces in the meeting that the deadline will be missed has changed something in the room, even though the announcement is, for every listener, first-order news to no one. The same line separates an open secret from an acknowledged one, an unpopular regime before and after its unpopularity can be spoken, and a joke that is unmakeable from one that becomes tellable once the shared thought has been said aloud.

The asymmetry I want examined is this: we treat the transition from everyone knowing a fact to the fact's being common knowledge as a real change in the social world, even though no individual has learned anything of the first order. Is this asymmetry justified, or is it a piece of useful social stagecraft? Give me the strongest principle you can find for why common knowledge should license action, speech, planning, blame, or coordination that universal individual knowledge does not. Then test that principle against hard nearby cases: situations where only the first few levels of mutual knowledge do any work and the infinite hierarchy is idle; public announcements made by a speaker who is known to be unreliable; and cases where the further levels of everyone-knows are known to be unattainable. End with an honest verdict on whether ordinary practice is tracking a real feature of the situation or a convention we nonetheless have reason to keep.
