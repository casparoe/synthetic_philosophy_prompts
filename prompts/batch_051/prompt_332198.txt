I'm a software engineer, not a political theorist, but I've been reading a bit about the British constitution after a long forum argument about whether the monarchy matters. I ended up convinced by a view I found in Walter Bagehot's The English Constitution, at least as it was explained to me. His idea is that the constitution has two parts: the dignified part, which includes the monarchy and the House of Lords, and the efficient part, which is the cabinet and the Commons. The dignified parts exist to win the loyalty and obedience of the public through theatrical, emotional appeal, while the efficient parts actually govern. For Bagehot, the genius of the English system is that it keeps these two functions separate: the public gets a king to revere, while the real business of power is carried out by elected politicians who are answerable to Parliament. Without the dignified part, the efficient part would lack the authority to rule; without the efficient part, the dignified part would be empty show. He even says something roughly like a republic can't do this because it has only efficient institutions and no way to capture the imagination of the masses, which is why the English system is uniquely stable.

I've sort of adopted this framework as a general way to think about constitutional monarchy, not just Britain. What would count against it? I don't mean just "monarchies have had revolutions too" — I mean, what specific observations, cases, or arguments should actually move me? I find the emotional-identification point pretty persuasive, since lots of people in the UK do seem to care way more about the royal family than about parliamentary procedure. But does Bagehot's distinction itself hold up when you look at Sweden or Japan, where the monarchy is much more stripped down and ceremonial? Is the split between dignified and efficient power too neat because real power also relies on public sentiment? And if the view has been qualified so much that it can't really be tested, is that a problem for a thesis like this, or just the normal cost of doing this kind of political analysis?
