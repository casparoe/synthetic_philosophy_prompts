I cover philosophy and the sciences of mind for a general-interest magazine, and I am trying to work through a variation on a classic anti-utilitarian thought experiment. In the familiar utility-monster case, one being derives vastly more pleasure from any unit of resources than ordinary people do, so a utilitarian calculus appears to require feeding the monster first while others are deprived. The standard objection is that utilitarianism ignores the separateness of persons: it nets one person’s loss against another person’s gain as though they were fluctuations within a single life.

Now vary the case as follows. The utility monster is not a separate being. Instead, a known neurological condition strikes exactly one person each month, temporarily raising that person’s capacity for pleasure and pain by several orders of magnitude. The condition moves through the population in a fixed, public rotation: everyone gets a turn, everyone then returns to ordinary sensitivity, and the cycle repeats. While a person is amplified, the utilitarian policy directs nearly all resources to that person; during the rest of the cycle, each person is an ordinary beneficiary. Assume the deprivations involved are severe but survivable and fully recoverable.

A critic says this is only the utility monster wearing a rotating mask. A defender replies that the rotation changes the moral arithmetic, because each person is now compensated within a single life for the sacrifices they make at other times. Work through what each side should say, and then consider two versions of the case. In the first, an episode leaves the person’s memory and values intact, so they genuinely experience the episode as their own. In the second, the amplification is so extreme that it severs psychological continuity: afterward, each person regards the episode as having happened to someone else. Does the separateness objection retain its force in both versions, in only one, or in neither? If it survives, state precisely what residual complaint remains; if it collapses, explain whether that is because the rotation has introduced a within-life compensation mechanism that the original case lacked.
