I have been reading Lucretius, De Rerum Natura Book 3, in a course on ancient ethics, and I think there is a testable psychological hypothesis buried inside an argument that is usually treated as purely conceptual. Lucretius claims that the man who imagines his corpse being torn by wild beasts or consumed by flames is making a kind of perspectival error: he is not really imagining his own death but rather remaining as a spectator of his own absence. The more famous symmetry move follows: since we are not troubled by the thought of the infinite time before we were born, when we did not exist, we ought not to be troubled by the infinite time after our death, when we shall not exist. In class this is debated as an argument about whether death is bad for the person who dies; but I want to separate out the empirical claim that the asymmetry between our attitudes to prenatal and postmortem nonexistence is driven by an imaginative failure, and that correcting that failure would shift the fear of death. How would you test that? I want a concrete experimental design: who should the subjects be, what manipulation should force them to confront the symmetry rather than merely reading it, what control condition would isolate the mechanism from general mortality salience effects, and what outcome measures would distinguish reduced fear of death from changed abstract opinion about whether death is bad? Then say what a positive result would and would not settle. Even if the corrected-imagination intervention reduces fear, does that show Lucretius was right that death is not a harm, or only that we can train ourselves into one attitude among others?
