I have been asked to give the opening remarks at a small naturalization ceremony in a constitutional monarchy on the Westminster model — the country need not be named, but its head of state is a hereditary king, and the ceremony is conducted in his name. The audience will be about forty new citizens, many of them from republics or from countries where the language of allegiance means something quite different, together with their families and a handful of constitutional lawyers and local officials who will notice every evasion. The difficulty I keep running into is this. Nearly everything else about the ceremony is democratic: it speaks of equal rights, the rule of law, participation, and the popular basis of legitimate authority. Yet a substantial part of its formal structure is still a pledge to the Crown, to His Majesty, and the room is full of the symbols of a hereditary office. I would like the speech to do real work on that apparent contradiction rather than float above it. In particular, I have been trying to think it through by distinguishing the Crown as a constitutional office — an impersonal legal person, transmitted by rules Parliament could in principle rewrite — from the particular human being who happens at any given moment to occupy it. That distinction, it seems to me, could let me be honest about the monarchy having no democratic pedigree while still explaining why some of us think the office is not simply an embarrassment the country has not got around to abolishing. But I am not sure the distinction can carry eight minutes in front of that particular room.

Please write the speech. It should not become a lecture in constitutional theory: it should sound like something a person could actually say to people on a day they have worked toward for years, and it must not condescend to them or imply that coming from a republic is a disadvantage. At the same time, it should not dissolve the tension into pure ceremony, and it should give the lawyers something sharper than a vague appeal to tradition. Build the speech around the office/person distinction if you judge that it genuinely does the work; if not, use whatever structure will hold honestly.

After the speech, add a short note for my eyes only, not for delivery, indicating where the speech leans on a contested reading of constitutional monarchy — for instance, how far the office/person distinction holds, and where a republican or a stricter democrat would press. And please be plain with me: if you think an honest version of this speech cannot be squared with the occasion — that any truthful opening remarks at a naturalization ceremony in a monarchy must either make new citizens complicit in a legal fiction or quietly turn into a republican argument — say so directly rather than writing something evasive that papers over the problem.
