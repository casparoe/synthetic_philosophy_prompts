A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about moral uncertainty, and I came away suspecting that the debate in the literature has the same shape as our argument. On one side is the expected-choiceworthiness view: when you are unsure which moral theory is correct, you should treat it like ordinary uncertainty—assign credences to the candidate theories (say, 60% to a consequentialist view, 25% to a contractualist one, 15% to a virtue-ethical one), assess each option's choiceworthiness under each theory, and maximize expected choiceworthiness. On the other side are those who say the analogy to ordinary uncertainty fails, because "choiceworthiness" has no common unit across theories. To compute an expectation at all you must normalize each theory's scale—for instance, assigning 0 to the worst available option and 1 to the best—and such normalizations are arbitrary enough that different ones can reverse which act comes out on top. Their fallbacks are correspondingly varied: act on the theory you find most credible, or rule out only options that are seriously wrong under any theory to which you give real credence.

The frustrating thing is that both sides now seem to accept the same formal decision-theoretic machinery, and yet neither moves. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not about decision theory at all, but about what it means to say a moral theory is "choiceworthy to a degree," or about whether intertheoretic comparisons are the kind of thing that can be true or false rather than stipulated or chosen. But that may itself be a misdiagnosis. I want you to tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is: a verbal one, a substantive moral one, a difference about standards of evidence or admissible formal moves, or two groups answering different questions under one heading. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says, and then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis—a distinction, a theorem, a revised question—or argue that the debate is ill-posed and no progress should be expected. If you think my framing of the debate is itself confused, or that I have drawn the two camps wrongly, say so plainly rather than working around it.
