I'm writing something on Dennett's intentional stance and the current crop of AI systems, and I've realised I don't actually have a crisp account of what it is to believe something that I'm happy to put in print. So here's what I'd like from you: a working definition of belief — not a survey, not a potted history, just a definition I could defend — tested against the following cases. I want the definition to be of the real thing, not just 'behaves as if', unless you want to bite that bullet and say that behaving as if is all there is.

The cases:

1. My colleague, who is asleep right now, and has for years believed that Paris is the capital of France. (Your definition shouldn't make belief flicker out whenever someone loses consciousness, unless you want to defend that.)
2. A thermostat that bends a bimetallic strip and switches the boiler on when the room hits 19 degrees. I'm confident this isn't belief, and I want the definition to tell me what it's missing.
3. A chess engine that searches a line, registers that its queen will be lost in three moves if it castles, and then castles anyway for other reasons, having dropped the queen from its evaluation. Does it believe the queen is in danger?
4. A self-driving car that brakes for a pedestrian half-seen in fog, on a road it has never driven before.
5. A large language model that, when asked in a normal context, says 'Paris is the capital of France' and then, when asked in a different context with enough leading prompts, says France's capital is Lyon. What does it believe between the two questions — or is that the wrong question?
6. A person with a phobia who will tell you, sincerely and without any hedging, that the spider in the bathtub is harmless, while their pulse is racing and their behaviour says otherwise.
7. A company whose official policy states that safety is its highest priority, while its budget, hiring, and design decisions point the other way.

For each case I'd like an explicit verdict from the definition, and I'd like you to flag which of your verdicts you're least confident about and say whether that instability is a flaw in the definition or a real feature of the concept. I'm especially interested in whether the definition makes belief turn on what's inside the system, on what the system does, or on whether treating it as a believer pays off. Keep it British English, and dont worry about citing anyone — this is for my own notes.
