I run a monthly discussion group for adults who enjoy arguing about rational belief but have no formal training in philosophy — the regulars are an actuary, a nurse, a retired schoolteacher, and a couple of high-school debaters. We have spent the last two meetings circling one question: what does it take to say that a person rationally believes something? It is now my turn to lead a ninety-minute session, and I want to push the group into two familiar-looking puzzles that I keep failing to keep separate in my own head. In the lottery puzzle, there is a fair lottery with a million tickets and one guaranteed winner. For any particular ticket, it seems rational to believe that it will lose, and yet it also seems rational to believe that one of the tickets will win. So a person ends up with a set of beliefs that cannot all be true at once. In the preface puzzle, an author writes a book and checks each sentence so carefully that she rationally believes every sentence in it; she then writes in the preface that, all the same, at least one of those sentences is false. Again, she ends up holding beliefs that cannot all be true at once. The group can handle something thorny, but I do not want to lecture for ninety minutes.

I do not need the material explained to me; I need the session designed. Plan the order in which the pieces have to be introduced and what depends on what. Specifically, which puzzle should come first, and what has to be in place before the second one can do its work rather than look like a rerun of the first? I have been assuming the lottery should be the carrying example because it is numerical and clean, but I have begun to suspect that the numbers push people toward a boring dispute about probability thresholds and away from the deeper trouble. Is that suspicion right, and if so, what example should carry the whole session instead, and what does that example need to have — and need not to have — if it is to keep the real difficulty in view? I also want a map of where the group will predictably go wrong. My guesses are that someone will say rational belief just means believing what is more likely than not; someone will say the author has simply made a mistake by believing every sentence once she admits error; and someone will say the contradiction dissolves because there is no rule forcing a person to believe what obviously follows from things she already believes. For each of these — or for better ones, if I have misdiagnosed the group — where in the sequence should I head it off, and how should I use it rather than merely refute it on the spot? Finally, what one question or tiny task at the end would show me that they have seen the shared problem and not just concluded that rationality is messy? And what should I tell them I am deliberately leaving aside tonight, so that a neighboring question does not take over the room?
