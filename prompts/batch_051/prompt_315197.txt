I recently moved from a country where tipping is neither expected nor common to one where leaving a clearly substandard tip at a restaurant is treated not as a failure of generosity but as something close to a moral debt, where queue-jumping elicits genuine anger, and where, at the same time, nearly everyone crosses against an empty road and treats the jaywalking law as a fee rather than a prohibition. This combination of attitudes is puzzling to me, and I would like it made explicit. What view of practical norms does it presuppose? Is there a coherent underlying theory that explains why tipping and queueing are treated as binding in something like the way moral requirements bind while jaywalking is not, or is the operative distinction not between genuine and merely conventional rules but between obligations owed to identifiable individuals and rules owed to the state or the law? I would like the answer to (1) reconstruct the implicit theory of convention and obligation that best licenses this pattern; (2) separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, including where rival theories would produce the same outward behavior without endorsing the same picture of obligation; and (3) say whether a visitor who never consented to these local conventions is nonetheless obligated to follow them, and what would have to change in the practice if one held instead that only harm-based moral rules, and not conventions as such, bind everyone.
