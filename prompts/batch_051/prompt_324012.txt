I coordinate a research ethics committee at a teaching hospital. We see around fifteen proposals a month, mostly psychology and public health studies. Our problem is drift. We have a written list of principles — informed consent, keep risk minimal, don't exploit vulnerable groups, fair compensation, that kind of thing — and a folder of past decisions, but the two have come unmoored. Last month we approved two studies that differed mainly in how much we paid participants, and nobody could explain after the fact why the line fell where it did. On hard cases the members vote from the gut and then we reverse-engineer a principle to match, so the rulebook now has so many carve-outs it contradicts itself.

Ive heard there's a method in philosophy that's exactly this: you go back and forth between your general rules and your judgments about particular cases, revising one or the other until they fit together. But I don't know how to turn that into something a room of physicians and community reps can actually run in forty minutes per proposal.

Please write me the step-by-step procedure. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each before moving on, the point at which the procedure stops with a no or escalates to the full committee rather than me deciding, and the rule for when we should revise a principle versus revise our gut reaction to the case. Also say how we know the rules and the cases hang together enough to stop for the day, so we don't chase perfect consistency forever. One thing I want built in: not every member's first reaction should count equally — I want the procedure to screen out the 4pm, hungry, annoyed reaction and only feed the considered ones into the method, if that's even possible to do.

Then run the procedure on these three from our actual docket and show the committee what it recommends:

1. A study that pays participants $200 for a two-hour session where one hour is a deliberately tedious and slightly embarrassing public-speaking task, but the risk is otherwise minimal. Our written rule says pay must not be so high it coerces people into risks they wouldn't otherwise take. We've approved similar amounts before for well-off participants and rejected $150 for a study recruiting homeless participants.

2. A study that deceives participants about the point of a memory task. Our rule says deception is allowed only with debriefing. The researchers say debriefing now would ruin a follow-up study they've already scheduled with the same people, and that the nondisclosure is the only way the second study runs.

3. A proposal to interview formerly incarcerated people, recruited through a reentry program and paid by us. Our rule says no research on current prisoners, but these people are out — though they're on parole and the program staff who referred them hold real power over them.

For each one, tell us the answer the procedure gives, which step did the real work, and which step was the point where judgment couldn't be eliminated. Then be straight with me: what kinds of cases will this procedure stall or misfire on, whether it can force an answer the committee doesn't like — rejecting a study we'd normally rubber-stamp, or admitting our past decisions were inconsistent — and whether a principle with enough exceptions to match every past decision is still doing any work.
