I am a PhD student in economics working on decision theory, and after a seminar on Bayesian rationality two members of my cohort got into an argument that has now been running for days, with neither willing to concede. Ravi opened with a standard formal result: an agent with a fixed utility function over consequences, a fixed set of available actions, and a fixed prior over states can never assign negative expected value to a costless signal, because whatever the signal says she may simply ignore it and take the action she would have taken without it; the best expected payoff available after seeing the signal, averaged over possible realizations of the signal, is therefore at least the best expected payoff available before. Lena replied that this is an artifact of a deliberately narrow setup. People reasonably decline genetic tests even when no treatment decision would change, because the result itself would bring dread; if one then builds that dread into the utility function, the refusal becomes rational, but only because the word costless has been quietly reinterpreted to exclude the very costs that make avoidance sensible. Ravi answered that this is not a counterexample to the result but a correct application of it once the payoff description includes emotional states, and that the theorem's real content is diagnostic: whenever someone sensibly avoids free information, one should be able to say exactly which assumption has been relaxed, and if no assumption has been relaxed the avoidance cannot be defended as maximizing expected utility.

Lena then raised two harder cases. First, a fact can change what an agent wants rather than what she believes; for example, learning that a dish contains an ingredient she will find revolting once she knows it is in there changes her preferences in a way that is not a mere update over a fixed state space. Second, a present-biased agent can rationally prefer ex ante not to learn the result of a medical test even if the test costs nothing, because she knows her future self would overreact and take a destructive action; the current and future selves disagree about how heavily to discount the near term, and refusing the information is a way for the current self to bind the future one. Ravi conceded that both cases fall outside the formal setup but insisted this is a feature rather than a defect: the result stays true within its assumptions, and a criticism that changes the assumptions is not a refutation. Lena ended by saying that if every apparent case of rational avoidance can be absorbed as a newly specified utility cost, a preference shift, or a split between selves, then the principle has no empirical teeth and is protected from disconfirmation by construction.

They have asked me to referee, and I cannot tell how much of their exchange is substantive. Please go through it move by move and adjudicate: for each step, what is right, what is overclaimed, and where the disagreement is genuinely about rationality rather than about how a formal model is framed; state which of Lena's cases truly fall outside the standard result and which are better seen as applications of it to a richer payoff description; and identify anything both parties seem to be missing.
