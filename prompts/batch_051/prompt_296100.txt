I've been defending the view that when two people's moral views come apart, they can permissibly strike a 'moral trade': a deal where each side, judged by its own standards, ends up with a world it prefers. My running example: I pay you forty dollars to skip steak for a month. By my lights, you were going to do something seriously wrong and I just prevented it for forty dollars — a bargain. By your lights, steak isn't wrong at all, so you took forty dollars to skip something you barely cared about. Both of us prefer the post-trade world to the no-trade world using are own criteria. Tell me what would count against this. Don't just give me the 'morality isn't for sale' slogan. I want the strongest objections a smart consequentialist and a smart deontologist would each raise, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to say which ones a committed defender of moral trade should themselves grant are real costs rather than rhetoric. Then be honest about whether the view can be patched to dodge all of them, and whether a version that survives everything has stopped risking anything — and if so, whether that's a problem.
