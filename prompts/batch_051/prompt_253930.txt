I'm on the academic integrity board at my university, and last month we got a case that's turned into a whole thing. A student is accused of selling exam answers: there's an email from her account with an answer sheet attached, and a short phone video, supposedly shot in the library, of her joking about what she charges. The email she doesn't really dispute (says a friend sent it from her laptop as a prank), but she flat-out denies the video—claims it's a deepfake, that someone took photos of her and a clip of her voice from a class presentation and stitched them together. Half the board thinks that's absurd and says we should just take the video at face value like we always have; the other half keeps saying video used to be the epistemic backstop—the thing you could fall back on when witness testimony conflicted—and now it isn't, so we need to actually decide what replaces it. The chair has asked me to draft a short decision memo for the committee: how should we handle contested audiovisual evidence in integrity cases going forward, and what specifically should we do in this case? It should lay out two or three candidate policies (keep treating video as presumptively authentic, require independent corroboration for any contested clip, require some sort of provenance or chain-of-custody trail, or a mix), recommend one, and take seriously the strongest argument on the losing side—especially the worry that 'it's a deepfake' becomes a universal excuse that lets actually guilty students walk. Our standard is just 'more likely than not,' and the lawyers won't lets us simply ban video evidence. The committee is a mix of faculty, students, and one IT person, so write it for people who get the technology only at a general level.
