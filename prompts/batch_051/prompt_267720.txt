I teach a philosophy of history seminar on the epistemology of archives, and a recurring move in discussion is to dismiss any inference from a documentary silence with the proverb 'absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.' It came up recently when a student argued that the near-total absence of women from the surviving membership rolls of a particular medieval guild is evidence that the guild excluded them, and another student countered with that proverb. I would like you to lay out the distinct precise principles the slogan might be expressing—about search effort, the different reasons a record can be missing (never created, destroyed, not yet found), base rates, and the conditions under which a silence is itself evidence—and for each say whether it is a trivial platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false. Then tell me which of those readings, if any, would actually license the dismissal of the student's inference, and indicate what consideration would change your verdict or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
