Take the ship of Theseus and change one thing about what's known, rather than what happens. The planks are replaced one at a time, and every discarded plank is kept and built into a second ship, as in the usual version. But suppose all the timber—original and replacement alike—was cut at the same time from the same grove and worked up by the same shipwrights in the same way, so no plank carries a tell-tale age or style. The harbour office's log, the only written record of when each plank was swapped and which of the two ships is the reassembly, is destroyed in a fire before anyone consults it. The workers were day-labourers who never kept notes, and the two ships have since sat unmarked, been weathered by the same wind and salt, and had their serial numbers sanded of, so nobody is left who can reliably say which is which. The replacements and the reassembly definitely happened; the only thing lost is the information about which of the finished ships is which.

Does losing that information change the ontology—what there really is, and which thing is Theseus's ship—or only the epistemology, what anyone can know about a fact that stays determinate? Is the original now determinately one of the two ships but forever unknowable, determinately neither of them, or does the loss of the log make its identity indeterminate, or the question empty? Work through what at least two standard accounts of persistence would say about the missing log—say, the view that identity goes with an unbroken history of the object, and the view that identity goes with the original planks—and whether they come apart here. Then the practical bit: should a harbourmaster who has to register one of the ships as the original treat the identity as merely unknown, or as something the fire genuinely destroyed?
