I keep going in circles with a friend about the problem of the fall, and I've begun to suspect our real disagreement is buried one level below the headline question. The setup: God creates finite rational agents—the angels—already good, with no inherited corruption, no bad environment, no prior temptation. One of them sins anyway. How can a first evil act happen at all? My credence that such an act is metaphysically possible—possible in the broad logical/metaphysical sense, not just conceivable to us—is about .65. She's around .15, because she thinks the 'guise of the good' principle is close to analytic: a rational will can only pursue what it takes to be good, so the first sin would have to be a cognitive slip, mistaking something bad for something good, and she doesn't count that as genuinely willing evil. I want your own credence in the proposition that a finite rational creature, created good and placed in perfect epistemic and moral conditions, could nonetheless choose what it fully recognizes as evil as its first morally significant act. Don't just hand me a number; decompose it. At a minimum: (a) how likely is it that the guise-of-the-good thesis really holds for all rational wills; (b) how much weight should go to the thought that 'choosing evil' doesn't require taking evil as a reason, but can be a refusal, an omission, or a deliberate failure to keep the will fixed on the good; (c) could God have made the creature free but impeccable from the start, and if so how much does that undercut the possibility claim; (d) is an unmotivated, primitive turn toward evil a genuine region of modal space, or just a name for our failure to understand. Give each sub-question a rough probability, show how they combine into your number, say which one your final credence is most sensitive to and what evidence or argument would move it most. Then tell me which of those terms she and I are probably actually fighting about, given .15 and .65, and which of us is on the shakyer ground there.
