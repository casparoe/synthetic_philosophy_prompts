I recently got into a long argument with a colleague who studies public attitudes toward biomedical technology, and I am still turning it over. His claim is that most people who say they would decline a hypothetical treatment that could add twenty or thirty healthy years to their lives are rationalizing. Because the option has never actually been available, he argues, people manufacture dignified reasons for accepting a death they cannot avoid: they say a natural lifespan has value in itself, that a life should have a coherent narrative arc, or that the old ought to make room for the young. He predicts that those reasons will quietly disappear once such treatments really arrive. What unsettles me is that his position looks unfalsifiable as stated: any expressed preference against life extension can be written off as rationalization, while his own enthusiasm for it is never subjected to the same suspicion.

The philosophical question underneath is what it even means for a reason to be a rationalization rather than a genuine reason, and how evidence could tell those apart.
