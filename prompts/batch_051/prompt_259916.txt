I'm taking metaethics this term and I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about moral language by going case by case, because each theory we read seems right about some sentences and awful about others. Here are my verdicts:

- When someone says "stealing is wrong," I don't think they're reporting a fact the way they are when they say "stealing is common." It feels more like they're taking a stance, like saying "boo stealing." So far so expressivist.
- But when I say "if stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong," I'm obviously not booing stealing in the first half. I can say the whole thing while I'm genuinely torn about whether stealing is wrong, and the sentence still feels meaningful and even possibly true.
- Same for "either stealing is wrong, or I've badly misunderstood my parents." The words "stealing is wrong" seem to mean what they always mean, but I'm not taking a stance against stealing when I say it in there.
- "Stealing is not wrong" feels like it commits the speaker to something more than just failing to disapprove. But I can't say what, if "stealing is wrong" isn't the kind of thing that can be true or false.
- "Is stealing wrong?" asks the same thing whether I ask it or you do, which is weird if the meaning is tied to the speaker's attitude.
- "John believes stealing is wrong" reports whats in John's head, not mine, so "stealing is wrong" there can't just be my disapproval doing the meaning.

Is there a single principle about what moral sentences do—stating facts, expressing attitudes, something else—that gets all of these, or close? If not, which of my verdicts would you overturn, and what's the least bad account? Also, what does that account say about "If stealing is wrong, then stealing is wrong"? Is that trivially true, or does the expressivist have to say something weird about it?
