I have been trying to decide when the fact that I value something more once it is mine than I would value it if it were not mine reflects a real preference, and when it reflects only a bias. I have recorded my gut verdicts on six cases, along with a working principle I have been testing. I would like you to find the best version of the principle that captures the cases, repair it where it fails, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.

For each case, hold my overall wealth and other options fixed, so the comparison is simply between how I regard the thing when it is mine and how I would regard it if it were not.

1. The conference mug. I was handed a plain logo mug at a conference. If I did not have one, I would pay at most $3 for it. Half an hour later, when a colleague offered to buy it, I found I would not sell for less than $9. My verdict: the $9 does not represent a real preference; it is a quirk of the moment, and I should in fact sell at any price above about $4.

2. My grandfather's watch. I own my grandfather's mechanical watch. An identical model in comparable condition sells online for about $400, but I do not care about watches and would not pay more than $50 to replace it if it were lost. I have, however, refused an offer of $1,000 for it. My verdict: the refusal is legitimate; what I value is having his watch, not having a watch.

3. The stock I picked badly. I bought shares in a company for $12,000; they are now worth $7,500. I no longer think this company is a better investment than a broad index fund, and if I had $7,500 in cash today I would not buy these shares. Yet I have not sold them for $7,500, and when I imagine selling I find I would need something close to $10,000 to do it, because I do not want to turn a paper loss into a realized one. My verdict: this is bias, and the price I paid should not matter now.

4. The shares my mother left me. My mother left me shares in the company where she worked for thirty years, now worth about $15,000. I would not choose that company with $15,000 in cash today, and the position is far too large a part of my savings. But selling feels like severing something, and I have delayed it for two years without being able to articulate why. If pressed, I cannot name the price at which I would actually sell. My verdict: my best guess is that this is mostly the same bias as in Case 3, but there is also something like the watch's legitimacy in it, and I cannot say which wins.

5. The condo. I have lived in my condo for twelve years. A comparable unit in my building would sell for about $420,000. If I were moving to this city now and knew only what a buyer normally knows, I would not pay more than $350,000 for this unit, because leaving aside the life I have built in it I could find comparable space elsewhere for less. But I have turned down a $420,000 offer, because I would lose neighbors I have known for years, the garden I have built, and the particular light in my bedroom in winter. My verdict: this is legitimate; those losses are real, and a new unit with identical square footage would not come with them.

6. The unused gym membership. I have kept a $50-a-month gym membership for six months without going once. If I did not have it now, I would not sign up. Cancelling feels like losing a standing option, even though I could rejoin at any time for a small sign-up fee. My verdict: this is bias; the option is one I do not in practice exercise, and it is not worth $50 a month to me.

My working principle is this. The gap between how I value something when I own it and how I would value it if I did not is a legitimate preference when owning the particular thing changes the actual good I get from it — memories, daily use, relationships, security, or some other benefit that attaches to this particular object or situation and not to a fungible replacement. It is a bias when owning it changes only my reference point — the number I compare things to, the sensation of losing, or the default I happen to have — without changing the actual good I get. But I can already see trouble: the principle cleanly separates the watch from the stock I picked, but the inherited shares seem to sit exactly between them; the condo fits the principle only if I am willing to say the neighbors and garden are benefits I would not have paid for in advance; and I am not sure whether that is a distinction the principle earns or just a rationalization.

Please do the following. State the best principle, or a small set of candidate principles, in as precise and operational a form as you can, saying for each clause which of my cases motivates it. Apply it to the six cases and to two or three new cases of your own construction, and say where it departs from my verdicts. Then tell me, candidly, which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without becoming gerrymandered, and whether it would be better to overturn one of my verdicts instead. Finally, say how confident you are in the principle you settle on, and identify the specific point or inference you are least sure about.
