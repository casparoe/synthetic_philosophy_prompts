I’m putting together a lesson for an adult reading group on Douglas Hofstadter’s “A Person Paper on Purity in Language.” The group is bright but nonspecialist: they’ve read a few philosophy-adjacent essays, but they won’t have read the piece in advance. The essay works by building a satirical world in which “white” is a slur and “black” is the neutral term, then uses the reader’s discomfort to expose how ordinary language encodes assumptions about gender — most famously through the generic “man” and the way “he” supposedly covers everyone. My problem is that the satire is doing at least three things at once: it is making a point about how marked and unmarked terms work, it is making a separate point about the asymmetry between the categories “white” and “black” in American English, and it is using the first to leverage the second toward a conclusion about sexist language. Some readers may agree with the essay’s target conclusion while being unsure whether the argument actually supports it; others may resist the conclusion and dismiss the satirical device as a cheap trick. I want them to leave able to state the core mechanism — why replacing one marked/unmarked pair with another can reveal a default we didn’t know we had — without treating the essay as a proof that changing words changes thought, or as just a piece of clever outrage.

Plan how to teach the piece in about an hour, given that the group hasn’t read it and I won’t be distributing the full text in advance. I need: the order in which to introduce the essay’s moves, with the minimal summary of the fictional frame that must come first so the idea lands rather than being spoiled; the one linguistic example outside race and gender that would make the marked/unmarked distinction click before we get to the loaded material, and what that example must not be; the misconceptions I should expect — e.g., that the essay is claiming “white” is as damaging as the n-word, or that Hofstadter thinks grammar reform is a substitute for social change, or that the satire invalidates itself by being exaggerated — and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; and a final check for understanding that would distinguish having grasped the mechanism from merely having found the essay persuasive. Also say what I should explicitly leave out at this level and why.
