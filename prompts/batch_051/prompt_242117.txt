I am taking a course on practical rationality, and we recently discussed Quinn's self-torturer. I have reconstructed an argument from the case that seems to force the conclusion that rational preference is intransitive, and I cannot find the mistake.

In the case, a person controls a dial with settings 0 through 1000. Moving from setting n to setting n+1 adds a small fixed payment and an increase in pain too slight to notice. A single direct move from 0 to 1000 would add the accumulated payments but would bring pain the person finds unbearable, and he would refuse such an offer.

(1) For any n, the only differences between setting n and setting n+1 are a genuine monetary gain and an increase in pain below the threshold of notice. Since a rational agent cannot base a preference on a difference he cannot detect, he should prefer n+1 to n.

(2) Rational preference is transitive: if an agent prefers A to B and B to C, then he prefers A to C.

(3) From (1) and (2), by repeated application, the agent should prefer setting 1000 to setting 0.

(4) But the agent would refuse the single direct move from 0 to 1000 for the accumulated payments, and this refusal seems rational. So he should not prefer 1000 to 0.

(5) Since (1), (2), and (4) cannot all be true, and I see no independent reason to doubt (1) or (4), the argument pushes me to reject (2): rational preference need not be transitive.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep transitivity, but the only ways I can see to keep it are to say that an imperceptible pain difference can justify rejecting a certain gain, or that the agent should accept the move to the unbearable setting after all. Which premise should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
