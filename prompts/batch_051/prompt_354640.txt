I am a novelist developing a book about work, craft, and the possibility that we systematically overvalue difficulty. Before I commit the manuscript to this theme, I want to stress-test it in a short piece of fiction.

Please write a short story, roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words, centered on a violin maker. It should stand alone as fiction, but its conceptual task is to dramatize a live dispute about whether effort is valuable in itself. One side should hold that struggle is only a cost, valuable—if at all—for what it produces or reveals; the other should hold that some efforts are constitutive of the value of the thing made or of the life that includes them, so that eliminating the effort would not merely cheapen the product but change what it is. The situation should press on this from both directions. For example: the maker's machined instruments are now indistinguishable from her hand-carved ones in blind listening tests, yet she keeps hand-carving some commissions, and she cannot tell whether this is conviction, sunk cost, vanity, or a story her patrons are paying to believe. I am not asking for a resolution; a strong story will make both positions feel earned and leave it genuinely unclear which has the stronger claim.

After the story, include a note of no more than 300 words separating what is reasonably settled from what is genuinely contested among people who have thought carefully about the value of effort, and flag any place where the story leans on a contested reading. Do not present a contested view as the consensus.
