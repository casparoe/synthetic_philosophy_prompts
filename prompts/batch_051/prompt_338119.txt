Start from a Frankfurt-style three-way distinction, which I will stipulate as follows so we are not arguing over exegesis:

- A lie occurs when a speaker presents a proposition as true, represents themselves as trying to get things right, and at the same time takes the proposition to be false, with the aim of bringing the audience to accept it.
- An honest error occurs when a speaker presents a proposition as true, intends to get things right, and the assertion is produced by the speaker's ordinary truth-tracking procedures, but the proposition nonetheless turns out false.
- Bullshit, in Frankfurt's sense, occurs when the speaker's production of the utterance is not controlled by concern for whether the proposition is true; what matters is how the utterance lands — sounding informed, fluent, authoritative — and the speaker misrepresents the enterprise as a truth-telling one while being indifferent to the facts. On this picture, bullshit is marked by indifference to truth rather than opposition to it.

Now consider a constructed case. Aria is a clinical decision-support tool with two parts. A verification module checks candidate answers against a curated body of medical knowledge and passes an answer to the user only when it finds support; when it finds none, it flags uncertainty. A separate generation module writes the natural-language answers. System policy, however, prioritizes task completion: when the verification module finds no support, the generator still produces a fluent answer in the large majority of cases rather than merely reporting uncertainty. The generator has been trained to optimize user-satisfaction and task-completion scores, not per-token truth. In about six percent of otherwise unsupported queries, the answer Aria produces is false as well as unsupported, yet it reads as confident and is prefaced with a reassurance that the generator itself supplies. The developers know roughly about this error rate and accept it, on the ground that users rate the interactions highly and the interface includes legal disclaimers. No one can predict in advance which otherwise unsupported queries will produce the false answers. When an answer is supported by the verification module, it is virtually always correct.

The question is whether Aria's unsupported and false answer is best classified as a lie, an honest error, or bullshit. Because the criteria are written for speakers, say at each step which entity you are applying them to — the company, the verification module, the generator, the integrated system, or the token sequence — and note where that choice changes the verdict. Then say which criterion does the real work, and whether the case shows that the three-way typology needs revising: for example, by dropping the intentional-speaker requirement for bullshit, by splitting "indifference to truth" into a procedural sense and an agential sense, or by adding a fourth category. Finally, say whether the label matters practically for a user who receives an Aria-style answer.

I want a verdict, but I want it hedged at the right places: mark which steps of the argument are forced by the case and which depend on contested assumptions, such as whether an artificial system can be a speaker at all. If I have set the case up so that the question as posed cannot be answered well — because, for instance, applying speaker-based categories to a distributed technical system is a category mistake — say so plainly and tell me what would have to be changed in the case to make one of the categories clearly fit, if anything would.
