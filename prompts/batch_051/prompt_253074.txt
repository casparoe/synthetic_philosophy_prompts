I write about behavioral genetics for a general-audience publication. Once or twice a month I cover a study that reports a heritability estimate, and I usually have a few hours to file 800 words. My editor tends to reach for a headline of the form "X is 40 percent genetic," and I need a defensible way to know when that headline is licensed and when it is not. Please give me a reusable checklist, grounded in what heritability does and does not mean, that I can actually run in ten minutes on a new study. It should be a sequence of questions I ask in order; for each question, say what would count as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with "do not write that," and where the procedure cannot settle the matter and I have to make an explicit editorial judgment rather than pretending the number decided it. Then apply the checklist to three hypothetical studies: (1) a twin study reporting that "grit" is 37 percent heritable; (2) a genome-wide association study reporting that educational attainment is 20 percent heritable, with the authors claiming that genes explain a fifth of why some people finish college and others do not; and (3) a study reporting that BMI is 70 percent heritable in a sample of adults in one country, which I am told to cite in a story about an obesity-prevention program. Flag at least one case where the checklist tells me to kill a headline I would otherwise have run, and end with a short statement of what the checklist cannot settle on its own.
