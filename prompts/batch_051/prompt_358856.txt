I cover AI for a science and ideas desk, and I keep getting the same pitch: "our model isn't just accurate, it's legible" or "it explains its own reasoning" or "we trained it to be understandable rather than merely correct." I'm not an ML person, and I don't have the background to seperate a real legibility claim from a good story that happens to be attached to a correct answer. But I also don't want to be the reporter who either repeats the company line or dismisses the whole interpretability field as marketing. This comes up every few weeks, and the stakes are real: if I write that a model explains itself and it doesn't, readers end up with false confidence in a medical, hiring, or customer-service decision.

Give me a decision procedure I can run in about 40 minutes with the materials I usually have: a press release, a demo or paper, and one short call with whoever built the thing. It should be a checklist with the questions in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is a value judgment rather than a fact. My editor wants to see which step a claim failed at, not just a verdict, so make the steps distinct.

The thing I keep fumbling is what legibility is doing over and above correctness. I can verify accuracy on a benchmark; I can't tell whether an explanation is the actual reason for the output or a plausible after-the-fact rationalization. Some people tell me an explanation has to be portable — if the model's answer changed, the explanation would have to change with it — and others say a system only counts as legible if the person who bears the risk can act on the explanation without the vendor in the room. I don't know whether those are fair tests or too demanding, so the procedure should build in a line between "legible in principle" and "legible to the person it gets used on." It should also handle both kinds of claim I hear: models trained with legibility as an objective from the start, and models that just get an explanation layer bolted on afterward.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a radiology model that flags nodules and writes a short paragraph explaining each finding; (2) a customer-service language model whose vendor says it's legible because users can see its chain-of-thought; (3) a hiring-screen model where the vendor publishes feature importances and calls that transparency. Make sure at least one of these is a case where the procedure tells me no even though I'd love to write the responsible-AI angle, and end by saying what the procedure cannot settle.
