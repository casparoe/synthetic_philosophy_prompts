The literature on joint action is full of theories, and short on an agreed statement of the target. Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of collective intentionality and joint action has to deliver — the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must classify correctly, the distinctions it must keep apart, and the broadly accepted findings about coordination, commitment, and early joint engagement it must not contradict. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it binding rather than optional. Your list should at least cover: (1) two people walking together versus two people walking in parallel toward the same destination; (2) complementary-role cooperation — one steers while another pushes, or one holds the ladder while the other climbs — where the participants may share no single representation of the plan; (3) improvised joint performances, such as a duet or a pickup game, without explicit agreement; (4) large, anonymous, distributed joint action, such as a mass online campaign, where common knowledge among all participants is impossible; (5) joint action under orders, threat, or manipulation, where each participant acts intentionally but not wholeheartedly; and (6) at least one case of your own construction that the standard catalogues miss — for example, joint action under systematically asymmetric information, or a shared intention that each participant falsely believes the others hold. Be explicit about the distinctions no theory may collapse: doing something jointly versus merely coordinating; having a shared goal versus each person having the same goal; being committed to the joint activity versus being committed only to one's part in it; and the joint action's being intentional at the group level versus each contributing act's being intentional individually. Then mark each desideratum as non-negotiable or negotiable, and say what separates the two classes. In particular, take a position on whether common knowledge of the shared intention is a genuine requirement or a modelling convenience of one tradition, and whether irreducibly group-level intentional states can be required without sacrificing the demand that group facts supervene on the mental states of individuals; if some subset of your desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, identify it and explain why. State which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular theories. End with a brief scorecard, if it helps, of how a we-intention view, a shared-planning view, and a joint-commitment view fare against your list, but keep the weight on the list and its motivation.
