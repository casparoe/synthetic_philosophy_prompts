I am teaching John Patrick Shanley's play Doubt (alongside the film adaptation) in a philosophy and theater course, and I want to pair it with Wittgenstein's later remarks on religious belief. The strand of Wittgenstein I have in mind is his insistence that religious belief is not an empirical hypothesis that evidence could confirm or refute; it is more like a framework or picture by which a believer lives, so that assessing it by asking whether the evidence is sufficient already mistakes the kind of commitment it is. Read the play through that lens. Sister Aloysius's certainty about Father Flynn is explicitly not grounded in evidence, while the younger Sister James keeps reaching for testimony and proof. Is Aloysius an instance of the non-evidential certainty Wittgenstein wants to describe, or of its corruption? Where does the play dramatize the difference between faith and a demand for proof, and where does it resist the Wittgensteinian picture—by making certainty look like arrogance and self-deception, and by ending with the woman of certainty undone by doubt? I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the play to an illustration of the philosophy, and it should not tame the philosophy to fit the play. Also tell me what the play adds to the philosophical debate that the philosophical material alone does not. Finally, whatever reading you defend, say which consideration would most likely push you to the opposite reading, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
