I am editing a volume in which a committed Popperian and a committed Kuhnian have agreed to conduct an adversarial collaboration on a single question: is science rational during a scientific revolution? The two share a great deal at the level of description. They agree that mature sciences sometimes undergo wholesale reorganisations — what the Kuhnian describes as a paradigm shift, and what the Popperian prefers to call the replacement of one bold conjecture by another after severe testing — and they agree that such episodes differ from the routine refutation of a single hypothesis because the standards of assessment themselves seem to change in the course of them. What they cannot yet state precisely is the content of their disagreement: whether rationality requires paradigm-independent standards of assessment, whether a scientist who abandons a previously successful framework for one that initially fits the evidence worse is being rational, and whether the normative question can be separated from the descriptive question about how scientists in fact behave.

Please draft the document this collaboration should issue. It should open with a numbered section of agreed claims — about the character of theory change, the minimum that rationality requires, the role of anomalies and auxiliary hypotheses, and the constraints an adequate account of scientific change must satisfy — followed by a disagreements table. In the table, separate descriptive claims about how scientists in fact behave during revolutions from normative claims about how they ought to behave; state each side's position in wording that side would accept as a fair formulation; and distinguish any disagreement that is merely verbal, for example if one side uses the word rational to mean conforming to the received rules of a period while the other uses it to mean conducive to truth by any available means, from the residual disagreement that survives that clarification. Then identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer to one of them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify the kind of historical evidence or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side predicts that evidence will show. Finally, add a short section on what each side accepts would count against its own view.

Where you need a concrete case, anchor the document in at least one widely known historical episode — the replacement of phlogiston chemistry by Lavoisier's oxygen theory, or the reception of Copernican astronomy — but keep the historical detail coarse so that no part of the statement turns on specialist facts. The document should be something both could sign without feeling misrepresented, so avoid trading the disagreement down to vague platitudes about how both rigorous testing and historical sensitivity matter.
