I am a philosophy professor, and I would like an independent adjudication of an argument I heard between two colleagues of mine. After a departmental colloquium on institutional corruption, the conversation turned to a senior official in a city public-health department who oversees procurement and is known for refusing every gift, including small holiday tokens from long-standing contractors, and for recusing herself from any decision involving someone with whom she has had even a single meal — a practice that has sometimes delayed important contracts. The two colleagues disagreed about whether her conduct is admirable incorruptibility or a moral defect. Neither asked me to take a side.

The exchange went roughly as follows:

1. The first defended the official. Incorruptibility, he said, is a disposition to be unmoved by the incentives that corrupt judgment — gifts, favors, hospitality — and since the boundary between a harmless courtesy and a corrupting incentive cannot be drawn reliably in advance, a blanket rule of refusal is the only way to make the disposition robust. The occasional cost is the price of a clean decision process.

2. The second replied that this mistakes the virtue for a rule. A genuinely incorruptible person does not need a blanket prohibition; she can tell a bribe from a courtesy in the case at hand. Automatic refusal is therefore not incorruptibility but a failure to exercise judgment — corruption by procedure, in which the rule rather than the merits decides.

3. The first answered that this is too sanguine about case-by-case judgment. The point of the disposition is precisely that it cannot depend on the self-assessment of the person exposed to the incentive, because that person is the least reliable judge of her own motives; allowing exceptions reintroduces the vulnerability the virtue was meant to close.

4. The second said that this proves too much. If case-by-case judgment is unreliable here, why trust it anywhere? The official still must judge what counts as a meal or a social tie, whether recusal is proportionate, and whether delay harms the public. A no-exceptions rule does not remove judgment; it relocates it to less visible places and presents the result as principle.

5. The first replied that relocation is not nothing. The judgment that remains concerns public, checkable facts — Was there a gift? Was there a meal? — rather than private motives that the exposed official cannot assess. And the errors are asymmetric: overcaution is public and correctable, while a wrongly accepted gift is a hidden, cumulative corruption risk.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. You are an expert moral philosopher working on virtue and institutional roles; think through each move carefully and charitably before you write, and do not include that preliminary reasoning in your response. Then give exactly five numbered items, one for each move, with no introduction or conclusion. Each item should be one or two sentences: rate the move on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. In the fifth item, rate the fifth move and justify the rating in the first sentence, and use the second sentence to identify the most important consideration that both parties are missing.
