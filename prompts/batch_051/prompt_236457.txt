For next week's property seminar I've been turning over a mechanism for software whose owner has basically vanished. The background weirdness: with land or a table, ownership can lapse or be lost by abandonment—leave the field untended long enough and a squatter eventually owns it. But a program just keeps an owner, even if that owner is a bankrupt shell company or an estate that doesn't know it holds the rights. So here's the machine. (The numbers are negotiable; don't fixate on them.) After 15 conescutive years with no recorded sale, license, or update, anyone can petition the copyright office to declare a program derelict. The petitioner has to do a good-faith search for the current rights-holder—registries, SEC filings, the usual due diligence—plus a one-year public notice. If no owner surfaces, the petitioner gets a five-year non-exclusive maintenance license. During those five years, if they ship at least two documented patches or fixes per year and the owner stays silent, the copyright vests in them outright. If the owner *does* turn up before the five years are out, they get the rights back but owe the petitioner compensation for the maintenance work at a court-set rate.

Treat this as a piece of clockwork, not as a policy I'm defending. What does it incentivize among legacy vendors, IP trolls, the people who already run 'abandonware' sites as a hobby, and ordinary users? Whats the cheapest reliable way for a company that wants to keep its old code off the list to game the 15-year no-update trigger—say, by shipping a null update that changes a version string? What happens to a fork that got built during the five-year window if the true owner reappears on day 1,700—does the fork become infringing retroactively, and who eats the liability for the people who downloaded it? And finally: where does this whole scheme fail in a way that can't be fixed by adjusting the thresholds or the compensation formula? Be concrete.
