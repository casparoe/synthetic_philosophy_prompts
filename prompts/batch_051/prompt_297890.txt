I am a PhD student in moral psychology working on the value of emotional equanimity, and I keep returning to a passage in Epictetus’s *Enchiridion* that seems to state, in an unusually stark form, a commitment I want to test. The passage is from Chapter 12 in George Long’s translation:

> For it is better to die of hunger and so to be released from grief and fear than to live in abundance with perturbation; and it is better for your slave to be bad than for you to be unhappy.

I take this passage to assert a strong axiological ordering: freedom from grief and fear is so paramount that it is better to die than to lose it, and better that a person close to you be bad than that you yourself be unhappy. I want you to treat that ordering as a live position, not simply as a rhetorical flourish, and tell me what would count against it. I am not asking for the standard anti-Stoic objections about passivity or coldness in general; I want something closer to a map of the position’s exposure. What observations, arguments, intuitions, formal results, or empirical findings from affective science or moral psychology would have to be true for this ordering to be false, or at least rationally abandoned? Which of those are decisive, and which merely awkward? Does the view risk anything at all, or has it been insulated by qualifications? And if it is insulated, is that a problem for a normative ideal of this kind?

Please write your answer in British English. Please structure it under the following headings, in this order:

1. **What the position actually says** (about 250 words). Restate the principle you think Epictetus is asserting here, distinguishing the general claim about external goods from the particular claim about another person’s badness. Set aside the obvious historical offence of the slave example and treat it as a test of priorities.

2. **What would count decisively against it** (about 300 words). Identify the strongest possible defeaters, ranked from fatal to highly disruptive. These may be empirical findings, formal results, thought experiments, or tensions with other commitments a defender of Epictetus is likely to hold.

3. **What would count merely awkwardly** (about 250 words). Distinguish objections that a careful defender can absorb by clarifying the scope of the claim, such as cases where equanimity seems to be purchased at someone else’s expense without violating the position’s internal logic.

4. **What the position commits its defender to** (about 200 words). Draw out the least comfortable implications that a defender cannot easily disown, including implications about close relationships, moral development in others, and the value of one’s own life.

5. **Insulation and unfalsifiability** (about 200 words). Assess whether the position, as stated and as typically defended, has been qualified into unfalsifiability. State clearly whether you think such insulation is a defect in a view of this type or a legitimate feature of a normative ideal.

6. **Your verdict** (about 150 words). Say which defeaters should move a rational defender, which should not, and whether the core axiological ordering survives scrutiny.
