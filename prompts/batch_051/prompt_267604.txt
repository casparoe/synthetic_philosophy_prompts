I have a cluster of intuitions about vague predicates, and I would like to know what theory of vagueness, if any, they add up to. The cases below all concern the predicate 'bald.' Consider a sequence of people P_0, P_1, ..., P_N, where P_n has exactly n hairs; P_0 is plainly bald, and P_N, for very large N, is plainly not bald.

Here are my verdicts.

1. Borderline gaps. Somewhere in the middle there is a region of borderline cases. I cannot bound that region precisely, but the people in it strike me as cases where 'P_n is bald' is neither true nor false: for a borderline man m, the sentence 'm is bald' is not true and not false.

2. Excluded middle survives. For that same borderline man m, I accept the disjunction 'm is bald or m is not bald.' I regard the disjunction as true, not merely as a useful or acceptable thing to say.

3. Penumbral connections. Let m and m' be two borderline men, with m having exactly one more hair than m'. I accept 'if m is bald, then m' is bald,' even though both embedded sentences are, by my verdict in (1), neither true nor false. I also accept 'not (m is bald and m' is not bald)': those two men cannot come apart in that direction.

4. No sharp boundary. I deny that there is a sharp cutoff in the sequence: no particular n is such that P_n is bald and P_{n+1} is not bald. I do not merely mean that I cannot identify the cutoff; I mean that the sentence 'some n is such that P_n is bald and P_{n+1} is not bald' is one I am inclined to call false.

5. But tolerance fails somewhere. I accept that the tolerance step 'if P_n is bald, then P_{n+1} is bald' is not true in general, since otherwise this would force the absurd conclusion that everyone in the sequence is bald. Still, for any particular n you offer me, I am unwilling to assert that P_n is bald and P_{n+1} is not bald; each candidate counterexample to the tolerance step looks false, or at least not true.

6. Higher-order vagueness. The boundary between the clear cases and the borderline cases is itself not sharp. If I introduce 'definitely bald,' I do not think there is a crisp point at which the sequence stops being definitely bald and starts being merely borderline. Any account that saves my first-order verdicts by drawing a clean second-order line here will have missed something.

My question is this. Is there one theory of vagueness — supervaluationism, epistemicism, a degree theory, or some modification of one of them — that captures all six verdicts? State your preferred account, or a small set of candidates, precisely enough that it yields verdicts on the cases above. For each part of the account, say which of my verdicts it is answering to. Then apply the account to a fresh case I have not listed: a sorites series for 'is a heap,' together with a penumbral comparison ('if this small collection is a heap, then this slightly larger collection is a heap'), and the iterated claim that some borderline collection is 'definitely definitely a heap.' Finally, be explicit about which of my verdicts your account cannot accommodate without strain. If some of my verdicts have to go, tell me which, and say why the resulting account is still the best reflective equilibrium.
