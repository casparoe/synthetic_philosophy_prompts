My school's philosophy club has put me in charge of its next meeting: a single forty-five-minute session on the skeptical rule-following problem associated with Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein (often called Kripkenstein), for about fifteen members who have read a little philosophy but have not seen this material. I understand the puzzle well enough to run the session; what I need from you is a teaching plan, not an explanation of the problem.

Here is the target as I understand it. A person who has used the '+' sign all their life is challenged to say what fact about them determines that by '+' they meant plus rather than 'quus,' where quus is stipulated to agree with plus on every sum the person has ever actually computed and to return a different value, say 5, for at least one sum they have never computed. The skeptical point is not that they might get a future sum wrong; it is that no fact about their past behavior, their dispositions, their mental images, or their feeling of understanding seems to single out plus rather than quus.

Take the role of an experienced philosophy teacher who has taught this material many times and knows where intelligent beginners go wrong. Think through the structure of the problem before you commit to a sequence; you may find it useful to brainstorm candidate misconceptions and then discard any that turn out to be versions of one another.

Then give me the plan. I need at least the following. First, the order of ideas and what depends on what: which moves must come earlier because later ones presuppose them, and which should be postponed. Second, the single example or case that should carry the whole session and what it must have and lack — in particular, whether the plus/quus case itself should be the carrying example or should wait until the end while a simpler case carries the load. Third, the misconceptions the students will predictably bring or form — for example, that the puzzle is only about ambiguous or vague words, or that a disposition to answer a certain way settles what '+' means — and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. Fourth, one final question or exercise that would show whether a student has genuinely understood the point rather than merely nodded along. Fifth, what should be left out at this level, stated explicitly as something being set aside. Do not assign readings; the session must be self-contained.

End with a final line beginning "Summary:" followed by a one-sentence statement of the point the students must leave with.
