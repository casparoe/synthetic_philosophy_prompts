I'm about to teach a unit on rational belief, and I've been going back and forth on how to present the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. Everyone uses them as gotchas—"oh, so your view can't handle this?"—and it's left me not actually knowing what a theory in this area is supposed to do in the first place. A friend of mine thinks the preface paradox is just the author being hyperbolic when they say they're sure the book contains an error, and that we shouldn't build requirements around a figure of speech. I'm not convinced, but it got me thinking about what the non-negotiable data points really are.

So, before I pick a side, I want the desiderata. What does a theory of rational full belief (or rational acceptance, if you think acceptance is the right notion rather than belief) have to get right? Anchor the list in the two paradoxes, but don't stop there: which cases must it handle, which distinctions it can't collapse (full belief vs high credence, statistical evidence vs evidence about a particular ticket or a particular sentence), which closure-like or aggregation principles it has to respect or explain the failure of, and which results from probability theory it can't just wave off. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Then flag which are negotiable and which aren't, and say whether you suspect any subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable—because if the paradoxes force us to give up something like closure or agglomeration, I'd rather know that up front than have it blow up when I teach the unit. A table is fine, but the reasoning behind each requirement is what I actually want.
