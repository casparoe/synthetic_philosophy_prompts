I'm the programme director at a mid-size grant-making foundation. We give away roughly £8 million a year, mostly in global health and climate, and our board are committed consequentialists of a rough-and-ready sort — they want grants chosen by expected value, all things considered. The thing I keep coming up against is cluelessness. Beyond the first link or two of the causal chain, the downstream effects of a grant — economic, political, behavioural, on people who don't exist yet — ramify so wildly that we honestly can't tell whether the grant is net positive even roughly. I don't mean ordinary risk, where we can at least list the outcomes and attach probabilities. I mean the stronger sense where the space of possible outcomes isn't really surveyable, and two well-informed, equally committed consequentialists will arrive at opposite long-run verdicts on the same proposal.

I'd like you to turn this into something my team can actualy run. Write us a decision procedure for evaluating any proposal in under an hour: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because what's left is a value judgement rather than a factual question, and which theoretical distinctions you're keeping versus collapsing for the sake of usability. The board have to sign off on the eventual decisions, so the procedure should be explainable to people who won't read the underlying theory. Be explicit about where it will misfire. Then run it on two or three proposals: (1) a local after-school tutoring programme with modest but well-documented measurable effects; (2) a campaign for a specific institutional reform whose second-order effects are enormous and genuinely unknown; and (3) one where the procedure gives an answer I'm not going to like — tell me what it is and why the procedure says it anyway.
