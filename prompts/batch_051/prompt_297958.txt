I'm a software engineer and I don't have any formal philosophy background, so apologies if I'm mangling the terms. At work we're building an auditing tool that flags when a company's hiring or promotion pipeline leans on a protected characteristic, and I keep tripping over something that looks, from a systems perspective, like a wierdly specified function. The inputs are traits—race, sex, religion, national origin, age, disability, and so on—and the output is how much suspicion any use of that trait should trigger. But the tiers aren't steady. In US law as I (maybe sloppily) understand it, race, religion, and national origin get near-automatic suspicion and need a very strong, narrowly tailored justification. Sex sits in a middle tier. Age and disability sit lower, where almost any rational-sounding reason will do. I'm not asking you to fix my understanding of the doctrine, and I don't need case names or citations. What I can't figure out is what underlying model of equality would make this sorting coherent. If discrimination is wrong because it uses traits people didn't choose and can't change, religion shouldn't be near the top and age should be higher than it is. If it's wrong because it tracks groups that have been systematically subordinated, that fits race and maybe religion at some moments, but it makes sex and disability part of the same story, and it suggests the tiers should simply move as history moves. If it's wrong because the trait is irrelevant to the actual decision, then the tiering is beside the point—you'd just test relevance trait by trait, and a gratuitous use of a low-tier trait should be as suspect as anything. So: what theory of equality, or of what makes a ground of discrimination suspect, does the tiered practice actually presuppose? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory that best makes sense of the practice as it actually is, rather than the theory you happen to think is morally nicest.

And I'd like you to commit. Not a menu of "could be immutability, could be anti-subordination, could be relevance, the law is complicated." Pick the reading that does the most explanatory work—the one or two underlying features that actually predict how traits get bucketed—say why it beats the closest rival, and flag the one place where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, so that even a committed reading has to admit two theories fit. Then tell me what in the practice would have to change if the rival you rejected turned out to be right. I care about this because if I can't articulate the implicit model, I can't explain to a client why the tool treats one trait as more suspect than another, and right now my honest answer is basically "the law says so," which feels like it's hiding a real theory that I haven't found yet.
