Write an essay on the following variation of the handicap principle. The principle is standardly introduced through cases like the peacock's tail, where the signal is honest because producing it requires a cost that is paid even when the signal is accurate, and the cost is easier for higher-quality signalers to bear, so lower-quality signalers do not find it worth paying; the usual moral is that reliability requires a built-in wasteful cost. I recently joined a committee that screens applicants for a selective teaching fellowship, and we are weighing two policies: a costly entry requirement that all must complete, versus admitting applicants on their stated qualifications while auditing a random sample of their early work. The second option amounts to changing one feature of the standard model. Producing the signal is free, but there is a known probability that any claim about one's ability will later be tested in practice, and a claim found to have inflated the applicant's true ability triggers dismissal plus a penalty that outweighs the value of the position; accurate claims cost nothing. Work through, using that case or a similar one, whether this arrangement can separate high-ability from low-ability applicants the way the classic handicap can. In particular, specify under what conditions on the probability of testing, the value of the position, and the excess penalty a low-ability applicant would still prefer to inflate rather than reveal their type, and then judge whether a signal kept honest only by the risk of future detection should be classified as an instance of the handicap principle at all, or whether it is a different mechanism — one whose reliability comes from the probability of exposure rather than from a cost paid in the very act of signaling. Reach and defend a definite conclusion about whether the standard claims about costly signaling extend to this case or need qualification.
