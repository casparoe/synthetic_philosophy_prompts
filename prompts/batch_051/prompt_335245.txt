Write me a dialogue between two people who disagree about whether its fair to bind someone to a legal rule they genuinely could not have known about. Both characters should be sharp and know the territory, not one smart and one strawman. One defends the standard no-excuse line: they should push, among other things, on what would happen if ignorance excused (everyone cultivates it), on the coordinating function of law as a public standard, on how courts can't tell honest ignorance from faked, and on the idea that citizens have some duty to go find out what the law requires. The other defends a knowability constraint on legal liability: they should push on secret or unpublished rules, retroactive criminalization, a rule published only in a language the person can't read, administrative guidance that is technically public but practically inaccessible, and regulatory schemes so tangled that even a diligent person can't reconstruct what they require before acting. Make them actually respond to each other's specific points rather than exchange set pieces. Include the moment where each has to give ground: the first should concede that truly secret law is a problem for their view, and the second should concede that a blanket I-didn't-know excuse is unworkable. The real interest is where they still disagree after those concessions, so spend roughly the last third of the dialogue there instead of letting them converge too fast. Dont name-drop or use citations—make the arguments in your own words. Format: just the dialogue, short speaker labels, no stage directions or commentary. Hard cap: 850 words.
