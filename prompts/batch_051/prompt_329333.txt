So at work we keep having this arguement about whether a message that's technically true but clearly designed to make people believe something false is any better than a straight-up lie. I never know how to test it. A friend sent me this line from Augustine's On Lying, which I'd never read before:

"Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind."

What jumped out at me is that the test is entirely about a mismatch between what's in the speaker's head and what comes out in words or signs — not about whether anybody actually ends up fooled. So I want two small cases built to that exact sentence, because I want to see whether this old defenition carves the "lying vs misleading" line anywhere close to where my coworkers actually draw it.

The specs, in order:

1. A case where someone counts as lying by that sentence — ideally where they are trying to deceive and just failing — but nobody ends up with a false belief. Ordinary life, no exotic setups, one paragraph max.

2. A case where someone does not count as lying by that sentence, but they still deliberately leave another person with a false belief. Same constraints.

3. For each case, walk the diagnostic: the thing the speaker has in mind, the exact words or signs they put out, what the hearer comes to believe, and why the sentence gives the verdict it does. I don't want the verdicts stipulated ahead of time; they should drop out of the sentence.

4. Then tell me which detail in each case is actually doing the work — if I remove it, does the case flip? — and how somebody defending Augustine's sentence would push back on the second case. Would they say the misleading bit still counts as a "sign" under "signs of whatever kind," or that the speaker had the false thing in mind after all? And is that a fair reading of the sentence as it's written, or a stretch?

End with a one-sentence take on whether this definition actually captures the line my coworker cares about between lying and misleading, or whether it lets misleading-but-true cases slip through.
