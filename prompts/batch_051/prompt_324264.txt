A research foundation keeps a single register of claims that a whole field is allowed to treat as settled. Each registered claim gets a centrality score based on how many other claims in the register cite it or rest on it. There's a written rule: whenever a new result contradicts the register, the editors must restore consistency by revising whichever combination of registered claims has the lowest total centrality, except that a protected list of claims labelled 'logic and basic mathematics' may never be revised, no matter what evidence comes in. The founders say this just makes the analytic–synthetic distinction into an editorial policy: the protected claims are true in virtue of meaning, so no possible observation could overturn them, and revising them would amount to changing the topic.

I'm less interested in whether this is a good idea in the abstract than in how it would actually behave. What would it lead editors, researchers, and referees to do? Where does it break or get gamed? What happens to the field over a decade or two? Does the protected list end up doing any real work, or is it just a flag for what the managers would rather not touch, and are the failure modes fixable?
