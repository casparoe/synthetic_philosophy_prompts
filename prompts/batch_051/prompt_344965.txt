I have been trying to systematize my own responses to cases in the ethics of pest control, and I would like help finding the principle, if any, that captures them. I teach applied ethics, and I dislike the thought that my verdicts are just a patchwork of local reactions. The cases are these:

1. A mosquito lands on my arm at a backyard dinner, and a wasp lands on the rim of my glass. In each case I kill the insect immediately, and I regard this as trivially permissible, even though neither posed a serious risk of disease or sting.

2. A spider sits in the bathtub. I carry it outside in a cup. I would think worse of someone who crushed it simply because it was there.

3. A few aphids appear on a potted rose on my balcony, a purely decorative plant. I spray them with insecticidal soap; they are dead within an hour. I find this unremarkable and permissible.

4. A wasp nest forms above the back door, and my son is severely allergic. I have the nest sprayed and the colony killed. This seems to me not merely permissible but required.

5. Each spring ants trail into the kitchen, attracted by crumbs. There is no real health threat, only the unpleasantness of insects in the house. I put out borax-sugar bait; workers carry it back and the colony dies over several days. I do this without much unease, which puzzles me because the death is slow.

6. Mice get into the pantry. I set snap traps, which kill them quickly. I judge this permissible, but mildly distasteful, because mice plainly feel pain.

7. The same mice problem, but a neighbor recommends glue boards. I refuse. Death by starvation and struggling on an adhesive board strikes me as impermissible, even though the damage the mice do is identical to the previous case and even though snap traps are not perfectly instantaneous.

8. Termites are eating the main beam of the house. I have the house tented and fumigated. I judge this permissible, since the house would otherwise be structurally compromised, but I am uneasy that the fumigation kills every insect in the house, including ones doing no harm.

9. Slugs are eating seedlings in my vegetable garden. I will not use slug pellets, because the death seems slow and because I worry about poisoned hedgehogs and birds eating the slugs. I hand-pick the slugs at dusk instead, even though this is less effective.

10. My daughter comes home with head lice. I use a standard lice-killing shampoo. I regard this as permissible, perhaps required, even though it is a wholesale killing of small animals for a modest comfort interest.

My rough hypothesis is that two features do the relevant work: the weight of the legitimate human interest at stake, and the quality of the death inflicted, including its duration and whether anyone other than the target is harmed. But the hypothesis strains to explain case 5, in which a slow death leaves me untroubled, and it says nothing about whether and how the kind of animal should matter.

I would like you to find the principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that best captures the pattern. State it precisely, and say which clause each case motivates. Then test it on cases I have not yet settled and give its verdicts: [a] a city sprays insecticide from trucks on summer evenings to suppress mosquitoes that may transmit disease; [b] a greenhouse manager introduces small predatory insects that lay their eggs in aphids, which then die slowly from within, rather than applying a fast-acting chemical; [c] a homeowner runs an ultraviolet bug zapper on the porch all summer, killing thousands of flying insects indiscriminately; [d] a wildlife manager traps and kills an invasive beetle that is destroying a native tree species. If no single principle captures all ten, tell me which of my verdicts I should abandon and what the simplest principle capturing the rest looks like, and be explicit about the point at which a principle would have to become ad hoc to accommodate all of them.
