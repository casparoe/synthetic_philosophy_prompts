I'm a pain-management nurse, and the thing that finally got me reading philosophy of mind is how two patients with the same charted intensity can be living in completely different worlds. Last month one guy rated his dressing change an 8 and chatted through it, while another rated a 7 and was shaking and apologizing for crying. I'm taking a night class on philosophy of mind, and the instructor let me write my paper on pain since I deal with it every shift. Which is how I came across pain asymbolia — the rare condition where someone can locate and rate a painful sensation and still not mind it. The paper is about whether the badness of pain is part of the painful sensation itself or a seperate reaction to it.

For that paper, I need you to build a thought experiment that forces two theories of pain's unpleasantness to deliver opposite verdicts about a single, concrete drug. Theory one is a strong representationalism: the badness is internal to the representational content of the pain — the pain doesn't just represent tissue damage, it represents it as bad — so you can't strip away the badness and keep the same pain. Theory two is the attitudinal view: the sensory side handles intensity, location, and quality, while the badness is a further evaluative or motivational response to that sensation — exactly the thing pain asymbolics seem to be missing. I want the scenario to work like this: the attitudinal view says a drug that removes only the aversion/caring, leaving intensity and discrimination fully intact, counts as a genuine painkiller because it removed the badness itself; the representationalist says no — if the drug really removed the badness, then it has changed or destroyed the pain rather than made it easier to bear, so it is not a painkiller in the relevant sense. Constraints: ordinary clinical plausibility, no perfect introspection or mind-reading, the patient can't just be confused or bad at reporting, and the drug can't dull consciousness or the sensory/discriminative side — it can only change the patient's evaluative or motivational response.

Then do the check. Lay out the scenario in under a paragraph, state exactly which state of the patient the two theories classify differently, and walk through the reasoning each theory uses to get to its verdict. Identify the structural feature of the case that does the work, say what happens to the disagreement if you remove that feature, and finish with the strongest attack a representationalist could make on the case. The scenario has to generate the verdicts from the theories; don't just stipulate them.
