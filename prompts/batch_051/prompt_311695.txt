I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the ethics of consumption, and I live in a small housing co-op that has a written kitchen policy I keep coming back to. The relevant line says: "Using an animal product is permitted only when the product would otherwise go to waste and our using it creates no further demand for the production of animal goods." The clause was drafted after a long argument about dumpster-dived cheese, and everyone seems to have had supermarket waste in mind when they wrote it.

Now we've inherited three ex-battery hens from a rescue, and their eggs have become a sore point. For some housemates, eating the eggs is obviously fine: they would otherwise rot, we already have the hens and aren't buying more, and no one is breeding new layers because of our breakfast. For others, the situation stopped being contained when visitors started asking whether they could buy eggs from "rescue hens," and a neighbour began selling eggs from her own rescued flock because a little market for them had appeared; beyond that, hens will eat their own eggs to recover calcium, so taking the eggs means we've started supplementing their feed in a way tied to keeping them productive. So does using these eggs violate the rule? I'm less interested in a plain yes/no than in how the different readings — the literal words, the purpose behind the no-demand clause, what the people who wrote it would have said if asked about sanctuary hens and social imitation, and what a reasonable reader would take it to mean — come apart here. Also: if "demand" includes the messages our consumption sends to visitors and neighbours, does the waste exception ever do any work? Start with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question.
