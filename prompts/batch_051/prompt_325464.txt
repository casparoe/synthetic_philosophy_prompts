I teach a first-year undergraduate seminar with a unit on metaphilosophy, and one recurring problem is that my students arrive already knowing which side they take in the debate about whether philosophy makes progress: some are cheerful skeptics, others feel obliged to defend the discipline, and they mostly talk past one another because nobody has forced them to say what progress would have to look like before counting or dismissing it. I would like to fix this with a sorting instrument—a decision tree—that locates people in the debate on the basis of what they affirm or deny about concrete markers of progress, not by which label they prefer. The audience is first-year undergraduates who have read only a little philosophy, so every branching question should be answerable without knowing the names of positions or figures; I will supply those names at the leaves in class. Please aim for seven to ten leaves, and build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of isms: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the positions located in that space, with unoccupied combinations noted and briefly diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The distinctions I most want the tree to organize around are (i) whether progress requires convergence on correct answers to stable questions, or can instead consist in better questions, finer distinctions, or a wider menu of live options; (ii) whether the unit of progress is the individual inquirer, the discipline as a collective enterprise, or the surrounding culture; (iii) whether philosophy's value is primarily epistemic, or also therapeutic, practical, or civic; and (iv) whether persistent disagreement is best explained by the field's immaturity, the hardness of the subject matter, or the ill-posedness of some of its questions. Leaves I would like to see appear, under whatever names you think are fair and recognizable, include: a strong no-progress pessimist; a view on which philosophy dissolves pseudo-problems rather than solves them; a view on which there is progress but it does not look like the sciences; and a view on which progress is real, but it happens in the other disciplines that inherit philosophy's successes. The tree should also have a path for someone whose starting thought is that philosophy's most reliable product is clarification of alternatives rather than selection among them, and a path for someone who thinks the original question is too coarse to answer.

For the branching questions, write each as a choice about a concrete and fairly ordinary observation rather than a request for self-classification. Two cases must be used in the tree: the observation that fields such as physics, psychology, linguistics, and formal logic were once part of philosophy and later became independent disciplines while the residual questions kept being argued; and the observation that debates like the one over free will still lack consensus after more than two millennia even though the available positions are now described with distinctions earlier eras did not have. You may add other cases of that kind, but avoid questions that presuppose knowledge of particular authors, texts, or technical doctrines. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate without adding controversial questions, and in a short closing note tell me where the tree would need one more branching question if I wanted finer sorting. If one of my four organizing distinctions ends up doing no real work, or if an unlisted one is doing the work implicitly, say so.
