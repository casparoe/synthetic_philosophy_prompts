I am a novelist at the early stage of designing a speculative novel. The premise I want to take seriously comes from the problem of logical omniscience: real agents fall short of the idealised rational agents of epistemology, who are standardly assumed to know every logical consequence of what they already believe. My novel imagines that gap closing, but only for deductive logic.

Assume the following change occurs tomorrow, across the world, to every person over the age of fourteen with ordinary cognitive capacities. Whenever a sentence is a classical logical consequence of sentences they presently believe—that is, whenever it would be impossible for the believed sentences to be true and the new sentence false in virtue of their logical form—they immediately and effortlessly come to believe that sentence as well. Their verdict is introspectively certain, not a mere hunch. The faculty supplies no new empirical information and no access to the contents of other minds. It applies only to classical deductive consequence: it does not apply to inductive support, probabilistic reasoning, causal inference, natural-language interpretation, irony, or the detection of false premises. If a person's present beliefs are jointly inconsistent, the faculty makes them immediately aware of the inconsistency, but rather than deriving every sentence from it, it suspends inference from that inconsistent set until the person revises the beliefs.

I intend this as social prediction for the purpose of building a plausible setting, and I want a chain of reasoning rather than narrative. Everyone knows that everyone else has acquired the faculty.

Over the next fifteen years, what happens to at least three of the following areas? Choose the three where you think the most interesting mechanisms are visible: personal self-deception and compartmentalisation; political argument and ideology; legal reasoning, testimony, and contracts; mathematics, science, and philosophy; advertising, propaganda, and journalism; and everyday conversation and humour.

For each chosen area, trace the mechanisms. Identify which existing practices collapse quickly because they depend on people not noticing the consequences of their own beliefs. Identify which practices survive or adapt because they depend on false premises, suppressed information, ambiguity, probability, or emotional pressure rather than on invalid inference. Consider what new norms or institutions might emerge to compensate for the loss of bad deductive reasoning. Then close by separating your predictions into those you regard as robust and those you regard as fragile, and state which assumption would have to be revised for each fragile prediction to fail.

Please write your answer in British English.
