So I sit on the external trust-and-safety advisory council of a mid-size social platform, and we've been asked to rule on a disputed account before Thursday's meeting. The account is run by a small historical-education nonprofit; it posts high-resolution scans of 1930s–40s fascist and antisemitic propaganda posters and leaflets, each with a long thread underneath identifying the party or artist, explaining how the image was used at the time, and pointing out the visual tropes it relies on. The problem: a bunch of these images trip the platform's automated classifier for "hateful imagery," and some get recieved as user reports under the platform's ban on hate speech and glorification of violence. The account holders say they're documenting the material rather than repeating its message, and that the framing is what makes it educational. A bloc on the council thinks critical framing is basically unenforceable, since the image gets stripped of the thread's context whenever it's screenshotted or reshared, and that we'd just be building a loophole people can drive real propaganda through. We need a formal written opinion with a definite bottom line: either carve out an exception for this account, deny the exception, or approve it with specific conditions (interstitial warnings, no-save, a visible provenance badge, posting only on the account's own page — whatever you think is defensible). Draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting. It has to recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, and it has to engage the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly rather than just restating the policy.
