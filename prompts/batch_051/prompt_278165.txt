im a high school junior and for my philosophy club I got way too into this part of Plato's Laws, Book I (around 649d-e in the Greek numbering), where he basically says wine is a cheap, comparatively harmless test for seeing who someone really is before you trust them with money or power or whatever. The passage goes: "And are not these the conditions in which we are of the character described,—anger, lust, insolence, ignorance, covetousness, and extravagance; and these also,—wealth, beauty, strength, and everything which intoxicates a man with pleasure and turns his head? And for the purpose, first, of providing a cheap and comparatively harmless test of these conditions, and, secondly, of affording practice in them, what more suitable pleasure can we mention than wine, [649e] with its playful testing—provided that it is employed at all carefully? For consider: in the case of a man whose disposition is morose and savage (whence spring numberless iniquities), is it not more dangerous to test him by entering into money transactions with him, at one's own personal risk, than by associating with him with the help of Dionysus and his festive insight?"

I basically came away with the position that Plato's wine-test idea is more plausible than modern people give it credit for, even though we'd obviously never have our school actually do it. I want to know what would count against that. Not just "drinking is bad" but the actual philosophical and psychological holes. Which experiments or observations would show Plato is wrong that wine is comparatively harmless as a test, or that what gets revealed at a drinking party doesn't transfer to real temptations, or that the test creates the very vices it's supposed to expose? Also whether defenders of the idea have already insulated it by slipping in the phrase "provided that it is employed at all carefully"—and if so, is that a problem? Rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed defender of the wine-test position would themselves accept as counting against it versus dismiss as missing the point.
