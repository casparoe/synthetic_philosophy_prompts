I am marking a midterm for an undergraduate philosophy of language course that includes a unit on vagueness, and there is one answer I keep going back and forth over. A couple of evenings ago a friend and I had a long argument about whether borderline cases of vague predicates force us to say that some indicative sentences are neither true nor false; I suspect that argument is now making me alternately too generous and too exacting toward this particular submission. I would rather not let that private wrangling distort the grade, so I would like a dispassionate second opinion.

The question on the exam read as follows:

In an essay of a few paragraphs, consider the claim that the sorites paradox shows that the principle of bivalence is false for vague language. Your essay should (a) state the principle of bivalence; (b) reconstruct one version of the sorites paradox for a vague predicate such as “heap”; (c) explain how positing truth-value gaps for borderline predications is supposed to block the paradox; and (d) identify at least one significant cost or difficulty for the truth-value-gap response.

One student answered as follows:

Bivalence is the principle that every declarative sentence is either true or false, with no third status. The sorites paradox exploits the vagueness of predicates such as “heap.” One grain is clearly not a heap, and a million grains clearly is one. It also seems true that if n grains make a heap, then n+1 grains make a heap, since a single added grain is too small a change to matter. Repeated application of modus ponens then forces us from the clear case to the conclusion that one grain is a heap, contradicting the starting premise. A natural diagnosis is that bivalence is the culprit. If we allow a borderline number of grains to be neither true nor false, but instead to take a third truth-value, then each conditional premise in the troublesome region is false rather than true, the sorites argument comes out unsound, and most of classical reasoning is preserved elsewhere. The standard version of this idea is supervaluationism. On that view a sentence receives the value 1 if true on every admissible precisification, 0 if false on every admissible precisification, and 0.5 if true on some and false on others, giving a tidy three-valued semantics. The main cost is epistemological: there is no principled way to say exactly where the gap begins, and the notion of a borderline case is itself vague. A further cost is that the law of excluded middle may fail for borderline sentences, so we lose parts of classical logic. Still, this is preferable to accepting a sharp and unknowable cut-off, which seems metaphysically arbitrary.

If any error is partly compensated for by a correct surrounding idea, say so explicitly and explain the credit you would award.
