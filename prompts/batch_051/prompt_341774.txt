I've been roped into a dispute by a colleague in the law faculty and it's become a small puzzle I keep failing to solve to my own satisfaction. the clause at issue is from an environmental permit for a chemical works: "Condition 6(c): The Licensee shall take all possible precautions to ensure that no contaminants enter the groundwater beneath the Site or any adjacent land by reason of the activities authorised by this permit." The operator has done pretty much everything current engineering allows, but its new probabilistic risk assessment says the chance of liner failure releasing solvent into the aquifer during an extreme flood isn't nil — about one in sixty thousand per year, and a bit over the forty-year life of the plant. The regulator now argues that on the plain wording the condition is breached, or at least that the permit cannot be renewed, because if a release is possible at all then the operator hasn't ensured it won't happen. The operator says that reading demands the physically impossible, so the phrase must mean something like reduce the risk until a release is no longer a realistic possibility.

I want you to work through the interpretation the proper way: the bare text, the purpose of the condition, what the drafters in, say, 1970 would have said if you had shown them a one-in-sixty-thousand number, and what a reasonable reader of a permit takes it to require. Which approach should win, and why? Then push into the philosophy a bit, because that's the part I actually care about: is the word possible in a clause like this the same possible as in modal logic, or is it doing something closer to fixing a probability threshold — and can an all-or-nothing modal clause be applied sensibly at all in a world where almost nothing is literally impossible? Answer in British English, please.
