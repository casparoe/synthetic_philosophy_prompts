You are an expert psycholinguist whose research covers lexical ambiguity, disambiguation, and how languages tolerate homonymy; you also advise technical-writing teams on controlled language design.

I manage the controlled-English terminology for the maintenance and flight-line procedures of a commercial helicopter operator. Our documentation standard currently enforces a hard rule: every approved term has exactly one approved meaning, and any second meaning must be expressed with a different term. The rule is expensive and brittle. Writers keep needing familiar words for second senses — seal for a gasket and seal as an action, pitch for a rotor-blade angle and pitch as the highness of an audible warning, charge for powering a battery and charge for pressurizing a hydraulic accumulator. Forcing clumsy paraphrases makes the prose feel unnatural to our technicians, and unnatural prose produces its own misreadings. But the one-word-one-meaning rule at least gives the safety review board a simple story: no shared forms, no ambiguity. I need a defensible replacement for that story.

Write me a reusable decision procedure — an ordered set of tests or a checklist in words — for deciding when a proposed second meaning for an already-approved term should be admitted as controlled homonymy, and when it must be rejected and the writers required to use a distinct term. Ground the procedure in what is actually known about how natural languages live with widespread homonymy: skewed sense frequencies, part-of-speech differences, syntactic frames and selectional restrictions, domain and register separation, redundancy and predictability, prosody in the spoken checklists crews use on the flight line (pitch, stress, tone), and the cost and reversibility of an error. The users will be technical writers and engineers without linguistics training; they should be able to run the procedure in thirty minutes, and each decision should be explainable to a safety review board that is suspicious of ambiguity. Mark clearly which steps are mechanical tests and which are judgment calls dressed up as tests.

Before you write the procedure, think carefully and start by brainstorming the mechanisms a language has for keeping distinct meanings of one form from interfering, and ask of each whether it survives in the stripped-down prose of a maintenance manual. Then include worked applications of the procedure to at least the three cases above. Ensure at least one case yields an answer our current policy would reject (admitting an ambiguity the board dislikes), and at least one yields a rejection the writers will resist; say which is which. Finally, say what the procedure cannot settle — the remaining cases where a human must decide without the checklist, and what should govern that decision. Throughout, separate what is well established in the study of lexical ambiguity from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
