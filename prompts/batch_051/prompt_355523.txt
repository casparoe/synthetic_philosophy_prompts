I've been kicking around an idea I call 'punishment without a punisher,' or sometimes 'ambient sanction.' The claim: most of our thinking about punishment assumes a punisher — someone (or some body) who decides, has authority, aims at the harm, and can stop it. But much of what gets called cancel culture or deplatforming isn't like that. The person quote-tweeting, the moderator who removes a post, the advertiser who pulls out, the HR person who quietly lets someone go — each is doing something small and locally defensible, but the person on the recieving end ends up punished in a way none of them individually chose. I want to say it's a kind of distributed punishment: the punishment is real, but it doesn't break down into any individual act of punishing. That would explain both why defenders say 'this isn't punishment, it's just consequences' and why targets experience it as punishment. It would also mess up the standard justifications: retribution needs a punisher who can fit the penalty to the offense; deterrence needs someone aiming the harm at a message; rehabilitation needs someone managing re-entry. If the punisher is no one in particular, those all fall apart, but the punishment still happens. Am I onto something, or just re-describing mob behavior in fancier words? Is there an existing framework that captures this better (informal social control feels adjacent but not the same), and where does the concept break? Lead with a one-sentence verdict, then reconstruct the strongest version of the idea, name the nearest existing concepts, and say precisely where it stops being distinctive. Keep it under 500 words.
