I've been reporting on a policy a big state university's biology program is about to roll out, and I can't decide whether it's a clever intervention or a bureaucratic monster, so I want to hand it to someone who thinks about this stuff for a living. You're a philosopher of biology who also has a good feel for how rules actually change behavior inside institutions; put that hat on and think carefully, maybe brainstorm a couple of failure modes before settling on your read.

Here's the scheme. Starting next fall, every intro biology course has to submit to a "code audit." Any time an instructor, slide, or required reading describes DNA as carrying "information"—or calls it a "code," "instructions," "blueprint," "program"—the material has to answer four questions right there: what is the sender, what is the receiver, what is the alphabet, and what counts as an error or misreading? If the instructor can't fill in those four blanks literally, they have to say out loud (or in the text) that the word is a metaphor, and then give at least one concrete way the metaphor breaks down. The department swears it isn't banning the language, just forceing people to notice when they're using it, and the audit is purely a teaching requirement, not a penalty system—no enforcement beyond the syllabus committee checking once a year.

Walk me through how this actually plays out. Who games it, and what are the standard workarounds by year three? Do lecturers just load all the code talk into one pre-approved paragraph and then talk about genes however they like? Do textbook publishers start adding the four-answer box in the margin so instructors never have to think about it? Does it change what students end up believing a gene is, or does it mostly train them to hear "code" as "metaphor" and then keep using it literally anyway? And where does the scheme fail first—at the sender/receiver question, at the alphabet question, at the error question—and can any of those failures be patched without gutting the one thing the rule is supposed to teach?
