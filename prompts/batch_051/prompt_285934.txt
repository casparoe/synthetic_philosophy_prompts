I find myself torn between a thesis I accept on reflection and an intuition I cannot dislodge.

The thesis is that determinism and predictability are different kinds of notion, and that their long association is a mistake. A system can be deterministic and yet unpredictable in several ways: through sensitive dependence on initial conditions, so that any finitely precise specification of the present state eventually loses its grip on the trajectory; through computational irreducibility, so that there is no shorter route to the later state than tracing the evolution step by step; and through algorithmic incompressibility, so that the trajectory admits no description shorter than itself. In none of these cases is a chancy event inserted into the physics; the evolution map still carries each state to exactly one successor. Equally, predictability does not require determinism: the decay of a single radioactive atom is unpredictable, while the half-life of a large sample is among the most reliable regularities in physics. The separation therefore seems sound, and it keeps questions about how the world is kept cleanly apart from questions about what any creature could know or compute.

The intuition is Laplace's demon. If the state of the world at one time genuinely fixes the whole of the future, then the future is in some intelligible sense already written into the present, and a perfect intellect in possession of the complete state and the laws ought to be able to read it off. And when I grant the demon exact, complete knowledge of the initial state and unlimited computational power, I cannot find any remaining gap: every obstacle I raise—finite precision, limited time, being embedded in the system one is trying to predict—turns out to describe a finite, fallible, internal reasoner rather than the demon. At that point the phrase deterministic but in principle unpredictable begins to sound like a retreat, in which the word deterministic is retained while the substantial vision of a world whose future is wholly settled by its present has been abandoned.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the Laplacean intuition to be the thing to give way, what it would take for the clean separation between determinism and predictability to be the thing to give way, and whether there is a reading of predictability on which both can survive: a world that is determined, predictable in one sense, and not predictable in another.
