A colleague and I have gone in circles over an argument that seems to prove far too much, and I cannot locate the step that fails. The argument concerns the legal difference between constitutional continuity and a coup, and it runs as follows.

(1) A change in a legal order is genuinely legal only if it is authorized by a rule that was already valid law at the moment of the change.
(2) The most basic rule of any legal order — the master rule that settles which other rules count as law — cannot itself be authorized by a further legal rule, since that would generate an infinite regress of authorizing rules.
(3) Therefore the master rule is not valid law because it was authorized; it functions as law only insofar as officials, courts, and much of the public in fact treat it as binding.
(4) A successful coup, when it stabilizes, replaces one master rule with another precisely by bringing about a situation in which officials, courts, and much of the public come to treat the new arrangements as binding.
(5) Hence what separates an ordinary constitutional amendment from a successful coup cannot be that the first was authorized and the second was not: both ultimately rest on the same brute fact, namely the acceptance of a master rule.
(6) If authorization cannot draw the line between continuity and usurpation, then no distinctively legal feature draws that line; legality either collapses into sociology or becomes indistinguishable from the self-serving pronouncements of whatever regime currently holds power.
(7) Therefore either constitutional continuity is a fiction and every established legal order is a retrospective act of self-validation, or legality must contain some self-authorizing foundation — which looks like claiming that a rule can pull itself into existence.

Both horns strike me as absurd, yet I cannot say which numbered step goes wrong. I would most like to keep two things: that there is a principled legal difference between a duly ratified amendment and a putsch, and that legality is not simply obedience to whoever in fact wields power. Where exactly does the argument go wrong, if it does? If nothing is wrong, which premise is the least costly to abandon, and what does abandoning it force me to say about cases where the rules genuinely run out — a disputed election decided by force, or a constitutional court that suspends the constitution in an emergency precisely in order to save it?
