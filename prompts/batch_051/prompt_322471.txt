I hold a fairly strict version of bivalence. For any meaningful declarative sentence, relative to a fixed context, what it says is either true or false: never neither, never both. I do not mean merely that every such sentence happens to have a truth value for all I know; I mean there is no third status. I also do not mean it only as a convenience for doing classical logic. I mean it as a claim about natural language and about the content of assertions: any alleged example of a truth-value gap is, on reflection, a case where the sentence is not actually meaningful in context, fails to express a proposition, is ambiguous between two propositions that each have a truth value, or where our inability to know or settle the truth value is being mistaken for the absence of one.

I want to know what would count against this view. I am not looking for a list of familiar counterexamples; I want a map of its exposure. What would the world, or our best theory of language, have to be like for my position to be false? In particular, please work through at least these hard cases: vague predicates and borderline instances; future contingent statements; empty names and failed presuppositions, such as a sentence attributing baldness to a present king of France when there is none; liar-type self-reference; category mistakes; and statements about borderline or unsettled cases in ethics. For each, distinguish between a treatment that genuinely preserves bivalence and a treatment that merely relabels the problem. What would have to be true for the natural treatment of these cases to be that some meaningful, contextually fixed declarative sentence is neither true nor false?

Then rank the defeaters from fatal or nearly fatal to merely awkward. I would like you to be explicit about what exactly is doing the defeating at each level: an observation about ordinary speaker judgment, a consequence for assertion or denial or reasoning, a semantic theory that predicts gaps and accounts for other data better, a logical result, or something else. Which of these should move me most if I care about natural language as actually used, not about formal convenience? Are there defeaters a committed bivalentist would themselves concede count against the view, not merely objections critics make?

Finally, please assess honestly whether the view as I have stated it risks anything at all. If every apparent gap can be reclassified in one of the ways I listed, has it been insulated so completely that no possible evidence could count against it? If it has, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is bivalence the sort of principle that is allowed to be unempirical in that way? And if I am wrong, which rival is best supported: a third truth status, a many-valued or partial logic, a view on which bivalence holds only for some restricted class of discourse, or something else?
