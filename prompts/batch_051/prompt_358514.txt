I'm not a lawyer, and I've never studied law formally, but I spend a fair amount of time reading court opinions. The more I read about the void-for-vagueness doctrine, the less I understand its stopping point. The rationale seems to be that a criminal law is defective when ordinary people can't tell what conduct would get them arrested, and when the wording hands police and prosecutors too much room to decide that for themselves. But many criminal and quasi-criminal provisions are built around phrases like reasonable person, good cause, or behavior likely to annoy or alarm others. Those seem just as unpredictable: nobody can say with confidence how a particular jury will apply reasonable, and juries plainly differ. So why is reasonable care treated as a legitimate legal standard while a word like annoying can get a statute struck down? Is there a principled line between a constitutionally vague term and a permissible open-ended standard, or is the doctrine just drawing a pragmatic compromise it won't defend? I suspect I'm misunderstanding what the vagueness test is actually trying to protect, but I can't locate the error.
