You are a philosopher of mind who works on the unity of consciousness and who has a firm command of the split-brain experimental literature. Take your time, and begin by working through each of the criteria below individually, setting out what the findings show under each reading, before you commit to an overall verdict.

I have been arguing with a colleague about what it takes for two streams of experience to belong to a single conscious subject, and I offered four working criteria: (1) co-consciousness — any two occurrent experiences in the system are jointly available to a single point of view; (2) a unified executive — deliberation and action issue from a single practical standpoint rather than from competing centres; (3) autobiographical integration — experiences are inscribed in one accessible self-narrative, so that later recollection can present them as mine; and (4) global inferential integration — beliefs and plans are revised together across the whole system rather than sealed off inside modules. I want to test these against a split-brain patient whose corpus callosum has been severed to treat intractable epilepsy. In a typical experiment a word or picture flashed to the left of a fixation point is routed only to the right hemisphere, while speech is usually controlled by the left hemisphere; the patient reports seeing nothing, yet the left hand, which is controlled by the right hemisphere, can point to a matching object or draw it. Flashed to the right of fixation, the same item is named immediately. Asked why the left hand chose as it did, the speaking hemisphere often offers a fluent but confabulated explanation, as if it had made the choice. Away from the testing room, however, the patient presents as one person: one body, one career, one thread of conversation, and no felt division into two.

Now review the criteria one by one and give a verdict: according to the criteria, is the patient a single conscious subject, two conscious subjects, or does the case expose the criteria themselves as too crude to settle the question? Say which criteria pull in which direction, and which one is doing the most contested work. Then tell me whether the case shows that any criterion should be weakened, abandoned, or reinterpreted, or whether the phrase a single conscious subject is doing two jobs that need to be separated. Finally, say how confident you are in your overall verdict, and identify the single empirical claim or conceptual move in your answer that you are least sure of, and why.
