I'm a novelist, about a third into a time-travel novel, and I've realized I can't plot the back half until I settle what I actually think is coherent — not the in-world rules of the machine, but what I'd defend if a pedantic reader wrote me a letter. I've been testing my gut on little scenarios, and either my gut has a secret theory or it's tieing itself in knots. Here's where I've landed.

(1) A man goes back to kill his grandfather before his father is conceived. Just as he raises the rifle, a low-flying plane spooks his horse, he's thrown, and the young grandfather hauls him to a doctor; that's how the grandfather meets the grandmother. Verdict: perfectly coherent. A stable single line, no change, the attempted killing was always part of the past.

(2) An engineer in 2045 receives a notebook from her older self containing the design for the time machine. She builds from it, spends decades failing to derive its equations, and at eighty sends the notebook back. The design was never actually invented by anyone. Verdict: this one feels incoherent to me, like cheating — the design's content just comes from nowhere — but I cant say what's contradicted.

(3) A man buys an old watch at auction in 2060, carries it back to 1890 and gives it to a watchmaker, who painstakingly copies every gear and spring. The copy is sold, passed down, and turns out to be the very watch he bought. No one ever designed the mechanism. Verdict: totally fine, even romantic, doesn't bother me at all — and the mismatch with case 2 is exactly what I mean about my gut.

(4) A woman goes back and pulls her brother out of the road before the car that killed him. When she returns, he's alive, with a wife and kids, and nobody but her remembers the funeral. Verdict: coherent, but only if the past can genuinely change or split; it can't be the same kind of story as the first three, which all assume one fixed line.

(5) A traveler gives her grandmother a locket containing a photo of the traveler as an adult. The grandmother later gives it to the traveler as a child, and the traveler eventually carries it back. I go back and forth on this one. It's a physical object like the watch, so it should be fine; but the image of her adult face seems to have no source, the way the notebook's equations have no source. Is it fine or not?

What I'm after is a principle — or two or three candidate principles — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I can run a new plot through it without hand-waving. For each candidate, tell me which of my verdicts it saves, which it forces me to drop, and whether the ones it drops are the ones I can most afford to lose. Then apply the best candidate to the locket case, and to one more scenario I haven't listed: a traveler finds the historical record of an event vague until he goes back and coaches a witness to say exactly the sentence he remembers reading in the record — the record was always that specific because he made it so, and he only knew what to make it because he'd read it. Is that a loop like the horseshoe, a free lunch like the notebook, or a change like the rescued brother? And say plainly which of my original five verdicts has to go if the principle is going to stay simple enough to build a novel around.
