I am drawn to a deflationary account of what large language models do: every output is a recombination or reweighting of patterns already present in the training distribution, produced by a continuous function under gradient descent, so that the very idea of a leap beyond that distribution is an illusion born of the space's high dimensionality — interpolation can wear the mask of invention. This account compels me because it is simple, mechanistic, and posits no mysterious generative faculty; it gives one tidy explanation for why models reliably fail at radical novelty. And yet I also have a firm intuition that some intellectual achievements we expect from genuinely novel systems — a proof strategy that reorganizes a whole field, a stylistic possibility that changes what later practitioners can even see — involve something categorically beyond recombination of prior elements, and I cannot fully shake the conviction that a system which merely interpolates could not produce them. Give me the strongest version of both the deflationary account and the intuition. Then give a committed verdict: should the intuition be debunked and dropped, should the theory be amended or abandoned, or is there a reading of novelty, or of what it means to go beyond the training distribution, on which both survive? I want to know what would justify each move, and I want you to actually make one rather than lay out the menu.
