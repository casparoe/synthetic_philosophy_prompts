For the novel I am writing, I need to get clear about a machine my protagonist enters. I am deliberately trying to use it as a literary version of the familiar spectrum argument against the transitivity of the all-things-considered better-than relation, and I want to know how much of the philosophical work survives once the case is made into a sequence of choices rather than a single comparison.

The Ladder works as follows. It adjusts one person's sensory life. Rung 0 is a constant low ache, of intensity 5 on a scale of 100, lasting 100 months. Each subsequent rung raises the intensity by one point and shortens the total duration by one month, so that rung 95 is five months at intensity 100. In the classic presentation, the argument asks us to grant that each rung is better than the one before it — one month less of the ache is, link by link, worth one point more of it — while the first rung is nevertheless judged better than the last. That would make the better-than relation intransitive across the chain.

In my novel, however, the protagonist is not shown the whole ladder and asked to compare distant rungs from a single standpoint. She begins at rung 0 and is offered, after she has settled in, exactly one move: ascend one rung or stay. At every rung she judges the next rung better from where she now stands, and she climbs. Her baseline shifts as she climbs; she adapts to each level; her memory of earlier intensities dulls; and she is not reminded of the lengths involved, only of the felt difference at the moment of the offer. At the top, she compares her present state to what she remembers of the bottom and insists that the bottom was better than where she is now.

What I need is an analysis of how the standard spectrum argument changes under this variation. I can see at least three possibilities, but I cannot tell which the mechanics of the story commit me to. First, the protagonist genuinely has intransitive all-things-considered preferences, and the case remains a spectrum counterexample with an added psychological frame. Second, the intransitivity dissolves when time is introduced, because it is not one better-than relation doing the work but a succession of different relations — better-relative-to-her-current-baseline — with preferences changing as the baseline changes. Third, the adjacent judgments are false once we correct for adaptation, forecast error, and unreliable memory, and what remains is ordinary transitivity plus a predictable illusion.

Please work through the version side by side with the classic fixed-standpoint spectrum. In particular: which of the adjacent judgments would survive if we required each comparison to be made by an unchanging standard, with full information, stable preferences, and ordinary memory? Does the upward sequence count as a money pump that the protagonist would accept and then regret, and does that matter for whether her choices are irrational? And is there a reading on which the novel is faithful to the intransitivity thesis without quietly replacing it with preference change? I would like the three possibilities ranked by what the story's mechanics actually entail, and the final assessment should say whether the Ladder is an intransitivity machine or only a machine for displaying how a person's standards drift while she is adjusting.
