I'm on my university's teaching and learning policy committee, and we have to decide Thursday whether to pilot a student-proposed "self-binding deadlines" system. The idea is that in any course whose only graded work is a final paper, students can choose during the first two weeks to set their own intermediate due dates, and if they keep the plan (they can cancel it until the end of week two), the registrar enforces those self-chosen deadlines with the usual late penalty, same as if the professor had set them. The student pitch is that students know they'll procrastinate, and this lets them borrow the university's enforcement machinery as a commitment device without imposing deadlines on anyone. Half the committee thinks this is a genuinely respectfull way to help students with self-control problems; the other half thinks it's either a trap (students will pick optimistic deadlines and then eat grade penalties for a predictable akrasia) or a way for us to pretend we've solved a problem while avoiding actual course design. Draft the short memo I'll circulate before Thursday: it should say approve, reject, or approve with changes; give a concrete time frame for the pilot if one is approved; and seriously engage the strongest objection that would come from whichever side loses, not just refute it.
