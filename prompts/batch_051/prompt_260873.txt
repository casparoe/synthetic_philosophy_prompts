The most striking feature of the current U.S.–China competition over advanced AI is that it has taken on the texture of a war neither government believes it can afford to lose and neither is willing to fight directly. Over the past several years the contest has been conducted through export controls on high-end GPUs and semiconductor-manufacturing equipment, industrial subsidies, restrictions on critical minerals, and official rhetoric in both capitals treating AI leadership — especially the possibility that the other side could monopolize frontier systems or artificial general intelligence — as a core national-security concern. Treat this as a real episode of costly conflict and analyze it through James Fearon's rationalist account of war. Fearon's puzzle is why states ever fight when fighting is costly ex post and a negotiated bargain would plausibly leave both sides better off; his main explanations are private information combined with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and issue indivisibility. The framework was built for interstate war, but your task is to test how far it can be stretched to an industrial-technological race conducted through policy rather than force.

Be precise about the mapping. Specify who the players are: unified states, or governments entangled with a handful of frontier firms whose incentives only partly align with state goals? Specify the good over which they are bargaining — control of frontier AI, the capacity to set global standards, the economic returns to automation, or the ability to prevent the other from converting a technical lead into coercion — and say what a peaceful bargain would have had to include, such as a verifiable agreement to cap compute or to share the technology's benefits. Work through Fearon's mechanisms one at a time. Does the race persist because each side holds private information about capability or resolve and has strong incentives to bluff or exaggerate? Is the binding constraint instead a commitment problem — either the fear that the other would secretly defect from any slowdown, or the preventive logic under which one side acts now because it expects the other to acquire a decisive and irreversible lead later? Is AI leadership genuinely indivisible, or is it divisible in principle and only rendered indivisible by how leaders have framed the stakes? Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: does this episode confirm Fearon's framework, force amendments for race dynamics rather than declared wars, or expose the rationalist vocabulary of states and bargains as narrower than it appears?
