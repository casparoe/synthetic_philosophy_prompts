As the deputy mayor for public safety in a city of roughly 300,000 people, I receive a steady stream of proposals to move responsibilities out of the police department and into unarmed civilian agencies: mobile crisis teams for mental-health calls, a civilian unit for low-level traffic enforcement, code-enforcement officers handling noise and nuisance complaints, mediation services for minor disputes. The mayor and city council want a written decision procedure my policy staff can use. The staff have no training in political theory and must complete the analysis within one working day, producing a clear recommendation for the mayor and council, who keep final sign-off. We see several such proposals each quarter, and mistakes cut both ways: keeping a function with armed police when it should not be there means needless coercion, while civilianizing a function that actually depends on the state's coercive authority creates dangerous gaps in force.

Ground the procedure in the classical idea that what distinguishes the state is its claim to an exclusive right to use physical force legitimately within its territory, and that citizens give up private self-help in exchange for this arrangement. But translate that idea into plain-language tests, not theory. The deliverable should include: (1) an ordered list of questions, with a specification of what counts as a satisfactory answer to each, where the procedure stops with a recommendation to keep the function with police, where it stops with a recommendation that civilianization is safe, and where it must hand the decision upward as a value judgment rather than a factual one; (2) a statement of which distinctions from the theory must survive translation — for example, legitimate versus actual force, coercion versus the mere possibility that a situation will escalate, the state's monopoly versus tolerated private security and citizen self-defense, and functions that are inherently coercive versus functions that are merely adjacent to coercion — and which distinctions can be dropped for staff use; (3) worked applications of the procedure to three proposals: diverting most mental-health crisis calls to an unarmed mobile team consisting of a clinician and a peer specialist, authorized to call police as backup; moving low-level traffic enforcement to unarmed civilian officers supplemented by automated cameras and mailed citations; and disbanding a plainclothes anti-gun unit and reallocating its budget to unarmed violence interrupters who have no arrest powers; and (4) an honest account of where the procedure will misfire, which questions it cannot settle, and what it would need from elected officials to be improved. At least one of the worked applications should yield a recommendation the mayor's office will dislike. Do not soften it; explain the reasoning plainly.
