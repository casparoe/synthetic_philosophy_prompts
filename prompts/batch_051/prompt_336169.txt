I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas desk, and I keep bumping into a contradiction I haven't been able to resolve, so I'd like you to pull it apart with me.

I recently saw a friend's employment contract for a mid-sized software company. The invention-assignment clause says that anything she creates, develops, or reduces to practice while employed — including work done in her spare time, on her own laptop, with her own software licenses — belongs to the company, unless it is entirely and demonstrably unrelated to the company's business. Then, on her first day, she got the onboarding talk about how the company wants people to bring their whole selves to work, and about its 20-percent-time policy for personal projects. The same institution seems to treat her ideas both as an asset to be captured by the firm and as an expression of who she is.

This Marx passage has been stuck in my head while I've been trying to seperate the rhetoric from the contract. It's from the section usually titled Estranged Labour in his Economic and Philosophical Manuscripts of 1844:

So much does the labor’s realization appear as loss of realization that the worker loses realization to the point of starving to death. So much does objectification appear as loss of the object that the worker is robbed of the objects most necessary not only for his life but for his work. Indeed, labor itself becomes an object which he can obtain only with the greatest effort and with the most irregular interruptions. So much does the appropriation of the object appear as estrangement that the more objects the worker produces the less he can possess and the more he falls under the sway of his product, capital.

I'm not looking for "Marx would hate this." The clause is a concrete practice, and I want to know what it presupposes. Does it presuppose that the products of a person's thinking are detachable commodities — that an idea can be cleanly transferred from maker to employer without loss to the maker? Does the bring-your-whole-self part presuppose the opposite, that work is self-expression? Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories of work, ownership, and the self that the practice rests on, and separate what's strictly presupposed from what merely fits. Also say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several rival theories would license it — and what a workplace policy built on something closer to the Marx passage would have to change concretely.

1. The practice and the puzzle (100–150 words): state the practice and why it looks inconsistent.
2. What Marx is saying (150–200 words): interpret the passage in your own words, before applying it to the contract.
3. What the practice presupposes (250–300 words): the implicit theory or theories of work, ownership, and self.
4. What it doesn't strictly presuppose (150–200 words): where the fit is loose or the practice is open to several theories.
