I hold the view that van Fraassen's Reflection principle is a genuine requirement of rationality for agents whose beliefs change over time, not merely a useful rule of thumb. Write P_t for my credence at time t and P_{t+1} for my credence at a later time t+1. The principle says that for any proposition A and any value r for which the relevant conditional is defined,

P_t(A | P_{t+1}(A) = r) = r.

In words: conditional on finding that my later self assigns probability r to A, I should now assign probability r to A.

I want to know what would count against this principle, treated as a live claim rather than settled orthodoxy. Tell me what would have to be true — which cases, arguments, or formal results would have to succeed — for Reflection to fail as a requirement of rationality, and sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. In doing so, please address at least the following:

- The standard apparent counterexamples: cases where I now know my future self will be misled, forgetful, less well informed, or reasoning badly. Are these counterexamples to the principle itself, or only to an overbroad reading of it?
- The defender's natural qualifications, such as restricting Reflection to future selves who are rational and at least as well informed as I now am. Do these qualifications save the principle, or do they hollow it out until it says almost nothing?
- The direction of deference. Why should a rational agent defer to a later self but not to an earlier self, a peer, or a known expert? If the asymmetry cannot be justified, what does that show?
- What the principle commits me to in harder cases: a future self reached through a deeply self-changing experience, a future self with different priors rather than more evidence, and a future self whose credences are causally influenced by my present credences.
- What a committed defender of Reflection would themselves concede counts against the principle, as opposed to what they would dismiss as a misapplication, and where the line between these responses lies.
- Whether Reflection, once qualified, risks being insulated against every possible refutation, and whether that is a special problem for a principle of rationality.

I would personally be most moved by a case in which violating Reflection is intuitively rational but the standard diachronic Dutch-book or deference arguments still seem to go through; if you think such a case exists, give it pride of place. I would also like to know what, if anything, Reflection rules out that a weaker norm — such as expecting one's future credences to be close to one's present credences — would permit.

End your answer with a single sentence beginning "Summary:" that states, in one sentence, how exposed you judge this position to be.
