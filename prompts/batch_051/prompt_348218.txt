After thirty years in physics, I'm new to philosophy, and I keep catching myself treating laws the way I used to treat data: as something that, if you stare at them long enough, tells you what theory generated them. Lately I've been puzzled by the contrast between how Europe and the United States handle surrogacy and egg donation. In most of Europe, paid surrogacy is banned or legally unenforceable, and egg donors are supposed to be altruistic, with any payment framed as expense reimbursement or a small inconvenience fee; in much of the United States, there's a market: agencies advertise egg donors, surrogacy contracts include negotiated payments, and intended parents shop across state lines. Same reproductive medicine, same basic wish for a child, but the practice looks like a gift in one system and a service in the other.

So here's my question, put as naively as I mean it: what philosophical commitments does each practice presuppose? I'm not asking which system is morally better, or what the statutes say. I want to know what the pattern commits a society to. Does the European approach presuppose that some things — a pregnancy, an egg — are the kind of thing that gets corrupted or wronged by being paid for? Or does it presuppose a different theory of parenthood, one where the person who gestates has a tie or a claim that can't be signed away in advance? Does the American market presuppose that reproductive labor is just another service, that payment can coexist with genuine consent, or just that the state shouldn't block two adults from contracting? And here's the part that bothers me: both systems are philosophically uneven. A lot of Europe lets clinics, doctors, and sometimes brokers make real money while keeping the woman's compensation near zero; the US let's you buy eggs and rent a womb, but draws the line at selling a kidney or buying a baby outright. Is one of these patterns consistent and the other not, or are both sitting on a mix of theories they haven't sorted out?

What I'd like from you: reconstruct the implicit theory or theories on each side; be clear about what the practice strictly presupposes as opposed to what it merely fits with; and tell me where the law is philosophically underdetermined, where more than one theory could license the same arrangement. Then say what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced — if you accepted that reproductive labor is ordinary work, do the European bans collapse? And if you accepted that some goods are demeaned or corrupted by markets, is the American practice incoherent, or just in need of different side-constraints? I'm steering clear of the vocabulary I've half-learned, like commodification and inalienability, because I want to see whether the practice forces those ideas on you too, or whether I'm imposing them on it.
