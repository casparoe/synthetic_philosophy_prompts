I don't have any legal training, but I've spent a long time thinking about when it should be possible to win a lawsuit over a rating, review, or prediction that turns out to be wrong or harmful. I've kept a list of my gut reactions to made-up cases, and I'd like help finding the principle that ties them together — or a clean statement of why one of my reactions has to go.

My gut reactions, in no particular order:

A restaurant critic writes a two-star review and calls the meal "a joyless, overpriced slog." The restaurant loses bookings and sues for defamation. My gut says this should fail. No one should have to prove in court whether a meal was or wasn't joyless.

A diner posts a one-star review saying "the soup was cold and the waiter ignored us for an hour." The owner says both claims are false and business dropped sharply. My gut says: if those factual claims are fabricated, the owner should have a real chance in court; if they're accurate, there's no case either way. The star rating itself bothers me less than the truth of the facts underneath it.

A stock analyst at a brokerage says a company is a "strong buy" and predicts the share price will double in a year. The stock falls 60 percent, and clients who relied on the call lose real savings. My gut says: if the analyst genuinely believed the call and was just wrong, no suit; but if the brokerage was quietly trying to unload the stock while pushing it on clients, that changes everything. The same sentence feels protected or damning depending on what was in the analyst's head, which I find strange but unshakeable.

A credit-rating agency gives a bundle of home loans its highest rating. The bundle collapses within a year, and internal messages later show the agency's own analysts had flagged the underlying loans as shaky. My gut says this one should be actionable — and not only because of the hidden doubts. The rating was issued as a measurement of creditworthiness, not as a matter of taste, and people were expected to lean their whole retirement on it.

A travel site calculates a hotel's "value score" using an algorithm no one can inspect. The hotel sues over a low score. My gut says the suit should fail. A score produced by a formula still feels like an opinion with extra steps, and if anyone should be allowed to challenge it, the claim should be about fraud, not about the low number itself.

A weather forecast says a storm will miss the coast; it doesn't, and a marina sues for the damage it could have prevented. My gut says no suit. A forecast is a guess about the future, and everyone understands that guesses fail.

A movie critic gives a film one star and says "the director has no idea what they're doing." My gut says no suit. That's clearly the critic's judgment.

The pair I can't reconcile is the stock analyst and the credit-rating agency. Both sold an expectation about the future. I'm comfortable saying one is mostly protected and the other should be open to suit, but I can't articulate the distinction without falling back on something flimsy like "the rating agency was more wrong" or "more money was at stake," neither of which feels like the real reason.

So here's what I'd like from you. Give me a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures my verdicts as well as possible, stated precisely. Then apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on: a car-safety outlet gives a new model five stars after testing, and a whistleblower later claims the manufacturer paid the outlet and the testers knew about brake failures they didn't report. May a buyer who relied on the rating sue? Apply the principle to one or two further cases of your own choosing that would test it, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, I should change. Finally, be self-critical: tell me what consideration or further example would lead you to a different principle, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
