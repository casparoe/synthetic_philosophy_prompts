I got into reading about the Buddhist idea of anatta — no-self, the claim that there isn't some solid unchanging 'me' behind the stream of thoughts and memories, just a bunch of parts passing the torch along. And then I look at how the legal system treats people and it seems to do the exact opposite. If a guy robs a store at 20 and gets caught at 40, the court has no problem saying it's the same person and locking him up. But that same system will also say, if he's genuinely changed, that he's been 'rehabilitated' and treat him as different enough to release early or seal his record. And then there's statutes of limitations: wait long enough and suddenly we stop treating you as the same guy who did it. So is the law assuming a solid self or not? I want to know what picture of a person this practice is actually committed to, whether its coherent, and whether you could run a criminal justice system on a no-self view at all — like, who exactly is being punished when there's no same me from 20 to 40, and what would actually have to change in how we punish if we took that seriously. I'm not after book recommendations, just the reasoning.
