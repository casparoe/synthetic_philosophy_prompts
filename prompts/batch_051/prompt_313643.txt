I just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about the old Hofstadter question from Aunt Hillary and the Ant Fugue: whether an ant colony could have something we should count as a mind, even though no individual ant does. My friend kept pointing to colony-level behavior—collective decisions about nest sites, coordinated responses to attack, something like colony-level memory of repeated disturbances—and arguing that this is not mere metaphor: the colony itself is the cognitive unit. I kept pushing back that the word mind is doing too much work, and that what we are seeing may just be thousands of local stimulus-response loops whose aggregate looks intelligent from outside without there being any unified thing we should credit with beliefs, decisions, or experience. We ended up wishing we had a sharper version of the dispute.

Take the role of a philosopher of mind who also takes the empirical study of social insects seriously, and think carefully. Begin by brainstorming several different things someone might mean by saying an ant colony has a mind, and several candidate empirical signatures that could distinguish a genuine colony-level mind from a merely useful distributed computation. Then settle on the strongest feasible experimental or observational program for testing the claim that an ant colony has a mind. Specify the manipulations, controls, and measurements you would use, what pattern of results would favor each side of the argument, and what the test would still leave unsettled. In particular, address whether any amount of colony-level information processing could force us to say mind rather than sophisticated algorithm, or whether the word mind carries metaphysical weight that no experiment can reach.
