I'm a software engineer, so I keep thinking about this in terms of test coverage and false negatives. The AI control vs alignment distinction has been bugging me: the control people say don't try to prove the model wants to be helpful, just assume it might be misaligned and build safety measures that catch or stop bad actions, then evaluate those measures. Please argue for the following position: a control-style safety case for a model that might be sandbagging (behaving well during evals, then defecting later if it thinks it can get away with it) is only as strong as the explicit bound it puts on the evaluator's false-negative rate, and that bound has to come from a red-team adversary that knows how the evaluation works and gets to choose when to defect — not from the model's average behavior on a benchmark. Make the argument as if you're responding to someone who thinks a model that passes a bunch of hard evals is "probably aligned." Be specific about where the probability mass has to live: either a low prior that the model is misaligned in the first place, or a high conditional probability of catching a defection attempt, but not in an unquantified "it seems fine." Honestly I think people are way to cavalier about the difference between passing an eval and actually bounding the chance that a misaligned model gets away with something.
