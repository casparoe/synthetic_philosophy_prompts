I'm putting together a unit for a philosophy of science course on thought experiments, and I want a case that seperates two stories about what scientific thought experiments can actually do. Here's the contrast I'll give the students. On the "argument" story (broadly empiricist), a scientific thought experiment is just a disguised argument: all its premises are old observations plus accepted background principles, so its conclusion was already implicit in what we knew; its real value is exposing an inconsistency or making an old commitment vivid. On the "intuition" story (broadly rationalist), a good thought experiment can give genuinely new knowledge of what is physically possible or impossible by eliciting a modal intuition that isn't just a summary of prior experience. I want a case where the two stories agree about what would happen in the imagined set-up but dissagree about what running it in your head teaches.

Build me a thought experiment in physics, or any other empirical science you can handle carefully, that meets these specs:
- On the argument story, it teaches only something about our own commitments: it reveals a tension among things we already accepted.
- On the intuition story, it teaches something new about nature, specifically a modal claim (that some configuration is possible or impossible).
- Don't use Galileo's falling bodies, Einstein's elevator, Newton's bucket, Maxwell's demon, or any other canonical example. The set-up should be describable in one paragraph and runnable in a first-year student's head, with no maths beyond high school.
- The intuition story's "new knowledge" shouldn't be a restatement of the set-up, and the argument story's analysis shouldn't be a strawman.

After the scenario, walk me through: what each story says the thought experiment teaches and why; exactly where the intuition story says the new modal knowledge gets in, and exactly which premise the argument story says is doing all the work; which feature of the case is responsible for the disagreement; whether removing that feature makes the two stories collapse into agreement; and how a defender of the argument story would attack the intuition story's verdict. I'm using this to teach the "how can thought experiments yield knowledge?" question, so I want the disagreement to be genuine rather than a labelling dispute.
