In the novel I am writing, employers can require workers to wear a cheap neural interface that decodes inner speech and visual imagery with reasonable accuracy. I keep returning to an old argument for cognitive liberty, which as I understand it runs in three moves. First, a state or any third party can regulate only what it can observe. Second, thought, unlike speech or conduct, cannot be observed. Therefore, thought lies outside the reach of regulation, and freedom of thought needs no separate defense. Now suppose the second premise is no longer true. Does the argument survive? If the conclusion can be reached by another route, state the new premise that does the work. If it cannot, say whether cognitive liberty must now be defended on different grounds, and whether those grounds were always the real ones. I am interested in the fate of the argument, not in a policy proposal.
