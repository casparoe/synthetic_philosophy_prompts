I recently sat in on a reading group session on Wang Yangming, and two members of the group became stuck in a dispute that none of us could resolve. The session had covered the essentials of his doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action: Wang denies that we first acquire knowledge and then, in a separate step, decide whether to act on it. On his account, knowledge and action are phases of a single process — knowledge is the beginning of action, and action is the completion of knowledge — so that knowledge that never issues in action is not yet genuine knowledge. His standard illustrations are experiential and moral: one does not know cold unless one has endured it, and one does not know filial piety unless one actually cares for one's parents; merely reciting the rules of filial conduct is not yet to know it.

The dispute went roughly as follows.

One member, whom I will call Elio, defended Wang. His opening point was that Wang saves moral knowledge from being cheap. If a person insists that she knows lying is wrong and then lies when doing so is convenient, Wang's reply is that she possessed only a verbal formula, not knowledge in the full sense. Genuine knowledge of wrongness is partly constituted by being the kind of person whose conduct registers that wrongness. Elio added that weakness of will, as it is usually described — knowing the better and doing the worse — is therefore not a brute fact about human agency but a symptom of incomplete knowledge.

Naomi objected that this is definitional sleight of hand. Weakness of will is a real phenomenon: a person can know perfectly well that she owes someone an apology and still not make the call. When Wang relabels every failure to act as ignorance, he has not explained the phenomenon; he has stipulated it away. If the doctrine is protected against all counterexamples by redefining each counterexample out of existence, it becomes unfalsifiable and trivial.

Elio replied that the relabeling has content: it tells us where to look. The shortfall is not in a detached intellectual faculty whose deliverances happen not to move the will. Selfish desire does not leave knowledge intact and then overpower it; it distorts the agent's perception, so that she no longer sees the full significance of what she is doing. That, he said, is why Wang's moral education aims at cultivating the whole person rather than at adding propositions to the mind.

Naomi answered that this smuggles in the very point at issue. Desires can bias perception, but sometimes they do not. There are clear-eyed occasions on which a person says, in effect, that she knows exactly what the action will cost and does it anyway. If Wang's doctrine cannot admit such cases except by insisting that the agent must somehow have been blind, the view is dogmatic. And if the claim is only that knowledge should, in the ideal case, issue in action, then the doctrine is merely the unremarkable norm to act on what one knows, and its talk of unity does no work. On either horn, she argued, the doctrine fails: it is false as a description of human psychology, or empty as an ideal.

Elio closed by denying that this dilemma tracks Wang's point. For Wang, the agent's perception, assessment, and motivation form a single orientation rather than a cognitive part that knows and a conative part that fails. The clear-eyed akratic Naomi described is an agent whose account of what she is doing is still incomplete, because she has not occupied the standpoint of the person who actually does it. The split between psychology and ethics, Elio said, belongs to the critics, not to Wang.

Naomi's last word was that if the only criterion for whether someone knows is whether she acts, then the unity of knowledge and action has been bought at the price of circularity. Unless Wang can say, independently of the action, what the missing piece of knowledge consists in, the doctrine is a tautology rather than a discovery.

They would not budge, and the session adjourned with the question open.
