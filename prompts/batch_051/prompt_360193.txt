I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about reasons for attitudes -- belief, admiration, fear, hope, amusement -- and my gut gives me different answers in cases that are structurally pretty similar. I want to know what principle, if any, captures my verdicts, and which ones I'd have to give up if no single principle gets them all.

Here are the cases and how I react.

1. A demon credibly threatens to hurt my kid unless I admire him for his cruelty. I think I have a reason to try to make myself admire him, or to take a pill that would cause admiration. But I do not think he thereby becomes admirable, and I'd deny that I have a reason to admire him in the sense relevant to whether admiration is correct or fitting.

2. Someone offers me $20,000 to believe the moon landing was faked, and I can verify the offer is real. I think I have a reason to do whatever would cause the belief (hypnosis, whatever), but I don't think I have a reason to believe it, full stop. There's a truth-related kind of reason, and this isn't one; but maybe more strongly, I want to say it isn't a reason to believe at all, just a reason to bring the belief about.

3. I have a mild elevator phobia. My therapist points out, correctly, that the fear is wrecking my life: I avoid buildings, it feeds anxiety everywhere else. This feels different from case 1. It sounds like a genuine reason not to be afraid of elevators -- not just a reason to cause the fear to go away -- even though the consideration (my life is getting wrecked) isn't about whether elevators are dangerous.

4. A patient with a grim prognosis is told by their doctor, correctly, that patients who genuinely hope for recovery live somewhat longer and better. I think the patient has a reason to hope for recovery, full stop, not just a reason to fake it or induce it. And I don't think it's merely instrumental in a way I'd be willing to demote.

5. A friend pays me to find his terrible stand-up set hilarious. I think I have a reason to laugh and to try to get myself to be amused, but not a reason to be amused; the money doesn't make the jokes any funnier. This feels like case 2, not case 4.

6. My anger at my ex is destroying my health and my other relationships, by my own lights and everyone else's. I think that fact gives me a genuine reason not to be angry -- not merely a reason to suppress or redirect the anger. This feels like case 3.

My working hunch has been something like this: a consideration is a reason of the right kind for an attitude only if it bears on whether the attitude fits its object (the demon's cruelty doesn't fit admiration, the bribe doesn't bear on whether the moon-landing-hoax belief is true). That principle handles cases 1, 2, and 5 fine, and says the therapist's point in 3 is really a reason to cause the fear to go away, not to be unafraid. But I don't believe that, and I can't see what makes 3 and 6 different from 1 and 2 without just hand-waving about the attitude types being different. And case 4 makes me worry that hope is doing something weird -- is it a belief-like attitude here, or a desire-like attitude, or is that thw wrong question?

So: what principle best captures my verdicts? Please state the principle precisely enough to apply to new cases, say which clauses do the work for which of my cases, and be honest about which of my verdicts it overturns or might have to.

Also apply it to two further cases I keep going back and forth on: (a) a parent's bias toward their own children, where the bias is useful but maybe also apt; and (b) a romantic partner asking me to trust them more because the relationship will go better if I do -- assume the evidence about the relationship going better is solid.

At the very end, after the discussion, put a last line: \answer{one-sentence statement of your principle}.
