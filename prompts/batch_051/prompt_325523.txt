A sculptor kneads a lump of clay into a statue; call the lump Lump and the statue David. Tomorrow David is squashed. Lump survives the squashing; David does not. Since identical things share every property, this seems to force the conclusion that Lump and David are distinct—yet they exactly coincide while David exists, sharing the same parts and the same place. I am collecting responses to this argument, and I already know the following six well enough that I do not need them restated:

(1) The coincidence view: Lump and David are two distinct material objects that exactly coincide while David exists; they differ in their persistence and modal profiles, and the relation between them—material constitution—is not identity.

(2) Statue eliminativism: strictly speaking there is no statue; only the lump (or only particles arranged statuewise) exists, and talk of David is a useful fiction.

(3) Relative identity: the question whether David is identical to Lump is incomplete, because identity is always relative to a sortal; the Leibniz-law argument for distinctness is rejected because it presupposes an absolute notion of identity.

(4) Four-dimensionalism: David and Lump are distinct four-dimensional objects, but they do not coincide in the problematic sense; David is a proper temporal part of Lump, so the two overlap only at statue-shaped temporal stages and diverge at other times.

(5) Dominant-sortal monism: there is only one object in the region, because each thing has a dominant sortal that fixes its persistence conditions; when the sortals "lump" and "statue" conflict, the subordinate sortal's persistence conditions are not authoritative, so no second object is present to coincide.

(6) Contingent identity: David and Lump are actually identical, but identity need not be necessary; from the fact that they could have differed—for example, the clay might never have been shaped into a statue—it does not follow that they are actually two things.

I want one more response that is genuinely different from these six—not a restatement, special case, or combination of any of them. State it precisely, explain how it blocks or dissolves the original argument, and then tell me honestly whether it is any good.
