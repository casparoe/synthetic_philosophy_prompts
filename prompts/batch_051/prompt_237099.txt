I retired from physics a few years ago and have been reading my way through the philosophy of time, mostly because it's the one corner of philosophy where I actually know the math. The Rietdijk–Putnam argument has been nagging at me. Here's how I understand it. In special relativity, two spacelike-separated events have no invariant time order: some inertial frames put A before B, some put B before A, and some have them simultaneous. So take an event that's in my future but outside my light cone — not something I could ever causally touch. There's a frame in which that event is simultaneous with my here-and-now. Putnam's move (and, independently, Rietdijk's) is: if what's real is what's simultaneous with the here-and-now, and no inertial frame is privileged, then that future event is already real. The standard extension chains this through enough intermediate events and you get the whole slab: the future is as real as the past, nothing ever comes into being, presentism is dead. To me this looks about as decisive as philosophy ever gets, and I admit I get a little irritated that philosophers keep treating it as one more move in a live game, usually acting like i've missed something obvious and not explaining what.

What I can't get straight is why the debate has been stalled since the sixties. Nobody on the presentist/A-theory side denies the physics, as far as I can tell. They say the physics doesn't settle what 'real' or 'now' mean, and that Putnam is taking a coordinate-dependent notion of simultaneity and quietly re-badging it as a metaphysical one. Then the eternalists say the alternatives amount to believing in a cosmic 'now' that no experiment could ever find while still claiming to accept relativity. Round and round, with everyone using the same equations and nobody moving.

My working hypothesis — and I offer it as a hypothesis, not a settled opinion — is that the real dispute isn't about physics and isn't even about whether relativity is correct, but about what a physical theory's invariant structure is allowed to do to words like 'real' and 'exists.' One side treats those words as whatever the best physics says they have to mean; the other treats them as carrying tensed content that relativity simply doesn't touch. But maybe I'm wrong, and there's a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work, or the two sides are answering different questions under one heading.
