I'm in a seminar on practical rationality and a friend gave me an argument that's driving me up the wall, because the conclusion is obviously too strong but each step feels load-bearing. Here it is, more or less as he laid it out:

(1) A rational agent can't be money-pumped: they won't accept a series of trades, each of which they prefer at the time, that lands them back with the exact bundle they started with minus some cash. (2) Now take someone whose preferences swing with hunger. At 1pm, before lunch, they'd rather have cake than grapes; at 3pm, after lunch, they'd rather have grapes than cake. (3) If they start at 1pm holding grapes, you offer cake for the grapes plus a dollar (small enough that they still prefer the cake), and they accept; at 3pm you offer grapes for the cake plus a dollar (small enough that they still prefer the grapes), and they accept. They end up back with grapes, down two dollars, and every step was made freely from their then-current preference. (4) By (1), that person is irrational. (5) But nothing in the setup turns on hunger specifically: any reversal over time, whether from age, taste, recovery from depression, or just changing your mind after learning something true about a career you nearly chose, has the same before/after structure. So rationality would forbid changing your mind about what's better for you, even on the basis of good evidence. That can't be right.

I want to say the lunch case is fine and so is the career case, but I can't see how to block the move from one to the other without special pleading. Wich step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd hate to give up the thought that someone who revises their preferences on evidence can be perfectly rational, but maybe I'm stuck with the cost somehwere.
