I maintain a small dataset of hypothetical scenarios paired with one moral philosopher's responsibility judgments in the margins. I have five of her verdicts so far.

C1, the chip. A voter has decided to support a candidate. Unbeknownst to him, a neuroscientist has implanted a chip that will detect any incipient decision to vote the other way and override it, forcing the original vote. The voter forms his own intention and votes; the chip never activates, and he could not have done other than vote. Verdict: fully responsible for the vote.

C2, the hypnotic slur. A post-hypnotic suggestion makes a woman blurt out an insult at a meeting. The suggestion bypasses her deliberation, and she never endorses the outburst. Verdict: not responsible.

C3, the unwilling addict. An addict wants to quit, has strong reasons to quit, and tries hard, but a craving overwhelms his resolve and he uses. Verdict: not responsible.

C4, the paranoid attack. During a brief psychotic episode, a man attacks a pedestrian he delusionally believes is following him; his reasoning is unresponsive to counterevidence. Verdict: not responsible.

C5, the ordinary lie. A person deliberates, sees clearly that telling a lie would harm someone and that it is wrong, and lies anyway. Verdict: responsible and blameworthy.

The new case I need to label is this. A senior engineer is firmly committed to never yelling at her reports and has good reasons to stay calm. After an unusually long week, she snaps at a junior engineer over a small mistake. Nothing compelled her: no chip, no hypnotic suggestion, no compulsion, no delusion; she could have paused, and she would not have snapped if her manager had been present. The irritability she acted on is a trait she has tried to manage and does not consider part of who she is, and she regrets the snap immediately.

What would a classifier trained on the five earlier verdicts predict for the new case: responsible, not responsible, or substantially diminished? Which earlier case is the closest analog? And which principle is she implicitly applying: (a) an action is attributable to an agent only if it expresses what she reflectively endorses; (b) an action is attributable whenever it issues from the agent's own reasons-responsive psychology, whether or not she endorses it; (c) an action is attributable only if the agent could have done otherwise? I am less interested in the label than in which principle explains the five rulings, which others fit them just as well, and what one additional case would most cleanly separate the surviving principles.
