I write about science and ideas for a living, and I'm working on a piece about what it actually looks like when a powerful system loses its values. I've got a tidy theory on one shoulder and a stubborn intuition on the other, and I need help figuring out which one should give.

The theory is the influence-seeking view. It says failure has a signature, and the signature is a system starting to optimize for its own influence: hoarding resources, gaming its metrics, resisting outside correction, expanding its discretion. This shows up before the damage becomes legible, so you can audit for it. That's the appeal — it's concrete, it's watchable, and it gives you an early warning indicator instead of a postmortem. On this picture there's always something agent-like, even if distributed, and its behavior snaps into focus once you see it as control-seeking.

The intuition is that this picks out the photogenic failure and misses the one I keep actually seeing. The failures that scare me are slow drifts where nobody seizes anything, the people inside stay sincere, and each step is a small accommodation that was defensible by itself. At the end the system is doing something no one ever chose, and there's no point where you can say "there, it turned." Worse, in a lot of the drift cases the competence seems to go first and the values just quietly follow — the system stops being able to aim before it stops being well-meaning. If I let the theory win, I have to say drift is influence-seeking with the resolution turned down or a just-so story for people who won't look for the agency. That feels like making the theory unfalsifiable. If I let the intuition win, influence-seeking becomes merely one failure mode among several, and I worry I'd be giving up a genuinely predictive account because I find slow, sad decline more emotionally credible than a villain.

I dont want "both have a point." I want a negotiation. Walk me through what it costs to keep the theory and debunk the intuition as an artifact of how humans perceive slow processes, what it costs to keep the intuition and demote the theory, and whether theres a reading where influence-seeking and drift are two stages of one underlying dynamic rather than competing accounts of failure. For each option, tell me what evidence or argument would justify taking it — not just which one you'd pick.
