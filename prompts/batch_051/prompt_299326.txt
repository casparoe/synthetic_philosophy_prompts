This term I've been teaching a seminar on disagreement, and I keep coming back to a thought I'd like an honest read on. I've been tentatively calling it "integrity arbitrage." The idea is that when two people or groups with very different value systems compromise, they usually do it badly: each side gives ground on something it actually cares about alot in exchange for a concession the other side cares about only a little, so everyone walks away feeling sold out. But there's no necessity about that. Value systems differ not just in content but in intensity; each side has commitments it holds loosely and commitments it treats as central. So in principle two parties could find a trade where each gives on a low-weight committment and gains movement on a high-weight one. Neither changes their mind, yet the total realization of both value systems goes up -- the moral analogue of gains from trade between countries that value goods differently. For example: on a university committee, a devout religious colleague votes with me on a curriculum proposal he's lukewarm about, while I, an atheist, support his push for a chaplaincy I'm lukewarm about; neither of us has abandoned anything central, and both of us get something we care about more. My hunch is that most "compromise is selling out" complaints are really complaints about a bad exchange rate, not about compromise as such.

I know this might just be economics talk stretched over ethics, and maybe it's been worked out elsewhere under a name I don't remember. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher working on ethics and social choice, and think carefully before answering. Start by steelmanning the idea: what's the strongest version of it, and what existing concepts does it come closest to? Then be candid. Where does "integrity arbitrage" break? I can already see trouble spots -- sacred values, incommensurability across value systems, the expressive meaning of a concession, self-deception about what one "loosely" holds -- but I want to know which of those are fatal and whether the concept still does any work after they're accounted for.
