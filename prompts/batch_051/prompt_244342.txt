You are a moral philosopher specializing in transitional justice and collective responsibility. I want you to reason carefully, so begin by working through one short paragraph of preliminary brainstorming: what would it actually mean for a later generation to be responsible for an atrocity committed before its members were born? Distinguish clearly between bearing responsibility, inheriting obligations, and inheriting shame or liability.

Now, consider the four kinds of guilt Karl Jaspers distinguishes in his post-war discussion of German guilt: criminal guilt, which concerns violations of positive law and is dealt with in courts; political guilt, which concerns the deeds of a state and the political community whose members bear consequences for the regime they tolerated or sustained; moral guilt, which attaches to each individual for what they themselves did or failed to do, and is judged by conscience; and metaphysical guilt, which arises from a failure to risk oneself to prevent evil against other human beings, grounded in a solidarity among humans as such.

Rank these four from strongest to weakest as a framework for explaining whether and how a nation can be guilty across generations. For each one, give a score out of 10 and a one-sentence justification for that score. Then defend your top-ranked category, or if you believe no single category works alone, explain which combination you would defend for an account of intergenerational responsibility, and state precisely why the others cannot do the work on their own.
