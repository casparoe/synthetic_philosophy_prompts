I help run a fact-checking desk at a small regional paper. Right now, whenever a questionable claim comes in, me and two other senior fact-checkers each look at it and we go with the majority — except that in practice we talk so much during the process that we almost always end up voting the same way, so the majority rule is a bit of a fiction. At a board meeting last month, one of our trustees, a former statistics professor, argued we'd get better accuracy for a fraction of the cost if we replaced ourselves with a pool of around thirty undergraduate interns. His reasoning goes like this. Suppose each intern is right about a claim only 60 percent of the time, barely better than a coin flip. If they vote independently, the probability that a majority of them is right gets higher as the group gets bigger, and with thirty of them it comes out to something like 85 or 90 percent. So a large group of mediocre judges beats three correlated experts, since the three of us essentially collapse into one expert. He even ran a little pilot where interns rated a few hundred old claims and got about 60 percent right, so the number isn't pulled out of thin air.

My gut says something is off. These interns all read the same three websites and have the same blind spots, and telling them not to talk to each other before voting doesn't change that. But I can't articulate where his argument actually breaks, and I don't want to just be defending my own salary. Ignore the HR question of whether we'd survive the restructure. I want to know: is his argument sound, and would thirty 60-percent-accurate interns voting without communicating actually give us better decisions than the senior desk? What would have to be true for his reasoning to apply to our case, and is it true here?
