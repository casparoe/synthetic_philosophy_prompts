I teach an undergraduate jurisprudence seminar, and we have just spent two weeks on the Hart–Fuller debate. In the last session a student proposed, partly as a provocation, that the natural-law side of the argument should be turned into an actual institution rather than left as a philosophical thesis, and I want to give the class a serious account of how such an institution would really behave. The proposal is this. Before any statute may be enforced by a court or an official, it must obtain a certificate of enforceability from a new, independent Legal Quality Commission. The Commission must refuse certification to any statute that, in its judgement, fails one or more of the following eight requirements: the statute must apply generally to a class of persons rather than named individuals; it must be published before taking effect; it must not operate retroactively; it must be intelligible to an ordinarily educated citizen; it must be consistent with the other statutes already in force; it must not require conduct that is physically or practically impossible; it must be reasonably stable, so that the law does not change faster than citizens can arrange their affairs to it; and there must be congruence between the rule as announced and the rule as officials actually administer it. A refusal of certification is recorded in a public Register of Defective Enactments. Once a statute appears in the Register it cannot be enforced by any court or official, but acts done while the statute was still uncertified are not reopened, and the legislature may pass a corrected version and submit it afresh. Treat this as a mechanism, not as an argument about whether natural law is correct. I want you to walk through how it would actually play out: what it does to how statutes are drafted, which bills legislators deliberately allow to fail certification and why, how the Commission becomes a political actor, how litigants and judges use the Register, and how ordinary citizens' planning is affected by the lag between enactment and certification. Give a committed analysis: say what you think the main effects and the first serious failure point would be, and whether that failure can be repaired without gutting the scheme.
