Modern legal systems and ordinary moral judgment treat inherited guilt as one of the few things that is categorically inadmissible. If someone is convicted of fraud, his children are not thereby criminals; we would regard it as a further injustice if a court sentenced the child for the father's offence merely because the child was the father's heir. The same practice, however, is far less scrupulous about the benefits and burdens of the offence. The children may inherit wealth that originated in the fraud and, if restitution claims lapse or cannot be traced, keep it. They inherit the schooling, the contacts, and the social position that the stolen money purchased. If the fraudster dies with unsatisfied civil judgments against him, the estate that compensates his victims is also the estate that would otherwise have been his children's inheritance. The child is not guilty, but the child's life can end up better or worse off because of the parent's act in a pattern that mirrors, in substance, what a law of inherited guilt would have done.

The doctrine of original sin invites the same thought from the other direction. Its modern critics object that no one should be condemned for the act of an ancestor or for a condition they never chose; its defenders reply that the doctrine concerns a transmitted condition of the will, not personal guilt as the law understands it. That exchange is usually treated as a specifically theological problem, but the asymmetry above suggests it may just be a sharper version of a structure secular practice already accepts.

Is there a principled difference between inheriting guilt and inheriting the benefits and burdens of guilt, or does the secular rejection of inherited guilt amount to refusing the word while accepting the thing? Give the strongest justification you can for treating guilt as non-transferable while allowing the material and social consequences of wrongdoing to pass down a line of inheritance, and test it against nearby cases: a fortune whose origin was slave-trading or forced expropriation, a hereditary disease a parent caused by exposure, a legal regime in which heirs do answer personally for the decedent's debts. In the end, say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up, and state what that verdict implies for the intelligibility of the doctrine of original sin.
