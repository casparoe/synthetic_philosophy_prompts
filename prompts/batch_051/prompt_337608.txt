Here is an argument I keep going in circles on, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a summary of the usual positions. (1) Suppose a whole-brain emulation reproduces a brain's causal organization at fine enough grain that, on any feasible test, it behaves indistinguishably from the original. (2) If two systems are behaviorally indistinguishable across all feasible tests and share the relevant causal architecture, then either they have the same mind or we have no evidentially grounded way of attributing any mind beyond our own. (3) So, in the non-branching case, the emulation has the same mind as the original. (4) Now branch: make two copies of the emulation at once, or run one emulation while the original continues to live. By the same reasoning, each copy has the same mind as the original, and therefore the same mind as the other. (5) But one mind cannot be numerically identical to two distinct subjects in two places at once. (6) So premise (2) must be false, and behavioral-plus-architectural indistinguishability is never enough for sameness of mind. (7) But that seems to prove too much in the other direction, because it makes every attribution of a mind to another human an inference beyond all possible evidence. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which step do you reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? If forced, I would rather keep some version of (2) for the non-branching case than keep the prohibition on one mind in two places, but I want to know what I am paying.
