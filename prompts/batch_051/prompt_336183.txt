We've been doing Friedman on method in my philosophy of economics course, and honestly I keep going back and forth. The view — the prof calls it the F-twist — is that you judge a theory by whether its predictions come out right, not by whether the assumptions are realistic. Part of me finds this really convincing. Every model idealizes, so if we made "realistic assumptions" a hard requirement we'd have no models left. It also gives a clean decision procedure: you don't get stuck arguing about whether firms "really" maximize, you just check whether the model predicts. And it explains why models with obviously false assumptions can still be useful — like the billiard player who doesn't solve the physics but plays as if he does.

But I've also got this really firm intuition that a model can predict well for the wrong reasons. Take a model that predicts something using variables that are causally inert but happen to correlate with the real causes in your sample; as long as the correlation holds it predicts fine, but it doesnt explain, and I don't want to call it as good as a model that has the mechanism right. More generally, it feels like explanation requires getting the actual causal structure, not just matching outcomes. Epicycles predicted planetary positions pretty well, but nobody thinks they explained the motions. The usual move I've come across is to distinguish harmless idealizations that just ignore causally negligible factors (frictionless planes) from misleading ones that posit the wrong mechanism. But I can't see how you draw that line without helping yourself to the very realism requirement Friedman was rejecting — you'd have to know which false assumptions don't matter, and that's exactly the hard part.

So I'm stuck between a tidy methodological rule that lets us be honest about idealization, and an intuition that predictive success can be bought with false assumptions and that this should count against a model when we're after explanation. I don't want to be told which side wins; I want a negotiated verdict. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing I give up (am I just confusing prediction with explanation?), and what would it take for the F-twist to be amended (maybe it's right for prediction but not for explanation; or maybe the false assumptions have to be, in some sense, causally harmless for the purpose at hand)? Is there a reading of "as good as" on which both of these survive — where Friedman is right about testing and I'm right about explaining — or is that just equivocating?
