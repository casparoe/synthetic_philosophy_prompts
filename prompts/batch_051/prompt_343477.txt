So my friend and I spent last night arguing about whether Newton and Leibniz both inventing calculus means the whole "who got there first" thing is basically a lottery, and whether the Darwin and Wallace story teaches the same lesson or a different one, and we ended up with completely different instincts about what credit in science is even for. I want to turn the argument into a little sorting quiz I can send to some friends, the kind where people answer concrete scenarios and it tells them what they actually think instead of what they call themselves.

Could you write it? Eight questions, three options each, and a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto five positions I want in play: strong inevitabilism (the discovery was in the air and priority is just a social convention), priority realism (first is first and credit should track it), a contribution view (what matters is who actually worked the thing out and made it usable, not who had the private idea), a pluralist view (it depends on the kind of discovery and how crowded the field was), and a constructivist view (what counts as the discovery and who counts as its maker get settled afterward by communities, prizes, and stories, with no fact of the matter before that). Dont organize the questions around those labels though; the questions should sort people by what they say about cases, so anyone can take it without knowing any jargon. The dimensions I want the questions to pull apart are inevitability versus singularity, private insight versus public contribution, and whether priority is a real relation or a story told afterward.

Prefer invented miniature scenarios over the famous episodes for the actual questions, so it works for people who dont know the history, but make them structurally like the classic simultaneous-invention situations: someone tells the key idea over coffee but never publishes, two labs arrive a week apart, a discovery everyone in the field says was bound to happen within a few years, a lone figure who had the whole thing privately for a decade. Build it from the logical space rather than from the names: show which distinctions the questions test, which answer combinations are coherent, which empty corners almost nobody occupies and why, and if two of the five positions can't be separated by eight questions tell me that plainly instead of forcing them apart. The whole thing should be continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold text or tables, like a long email, even if the scoring key ends up a bit awkward that way.
