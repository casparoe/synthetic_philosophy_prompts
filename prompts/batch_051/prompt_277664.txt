(a) State the core commitments of mathematical platonism and of nominalism, taking care to distinguish the existence of mathematical objects from the truth of mathematical statements. (b) Reconstruct the indispensability argument for platonism, making its premises explicit. (c) Present one nominalist reply and say whether you think the argument settles the debate.

Student's answer:

Platonism is the view that mathematical objects such as numbers, sets, and functions exist independently of human minds and languages, and that they are abstract objects — outside space and time and causally inert. Nominalism denies that there are any such objects; because of this, it is committed to saying that mathematical sentences like '2+2=4' are, strictly speaking, false even though they are useful in science and everyday life. The indispensability argument starts from the fact that our best scientific theories make indispensable use of mathematics: quantum mechanics quantifies over Hilbert spaces, general relativity over differentiable manifolds, and there is no adequate reformulation of these theories in purely nominalistic terms. Since those theories are empirically confirmed, and since they quantify over mathematical objects, we have reason to believe that those objects exist. The argument is best understood as an inference to the best explanation of the success of science: positing platonic objects explains why mathematically formulated theories are as predictively powerful as they are, in just the way that positing electrons explains the tracks in a cloud chamber. The best nominalist response is to treat mathematical language as a useful instrument rather than as literally referring. But instrumentalism still leaves the nominalist unable to explain why the mathematical instrument works so reliably across so many domains, so the indispensability argument ultimately favors platonism.
