I am preparing a job talk for a joint political philosophy and climate policy audience, and I want to be ready for the question period. The paper defends the claim that the central injustice in intergenerational climate change is better understood as domination than as a distributive shortfall. My argument runs roughly as follows: the distinctive wrong of present high-emissions choices is that earlier generations set long-lived parameters—the atmospheric carbon stock, sea-level trajectory, the mix of energy infrastructure, the siting of long-lived waste—that later generations cannot contest, and this unilateral, unchecked setting of the terms of future life is best captured by a diachronic extension of the neo-republican idea of freedom as non-domination. From this I draw a policy conclusion: decisions that lock in path-dependent, non-insurable burdens on future people are presumptively illegitimate even when standard discounting would count them as acceptable, and cost–benefit analyses that return a high "optimal" level of warming overstep their authority when they treat that presumptive constraint as a mere input.

The audience is mostly political philosophers and normative theorists, not climate scientists. About half work on distributive justice and think about intergenerational issues through carbon budgets, fair shares of a remaining emissions space, or harm-based duties. One senior person works on the non-identity problem and will press on whether future persons are the right relata here at all. Two are sympathetic to neo-republican ideas but have applied them only synchronically, within a single polity, and will be skeptical that the framework extends across time. The climate policy scholars in the room are sympathetic to integrated assessment models and will want to know what, concretely, my view rules in or out that a serious discounting framework does not. I will not be able to teach republicanism from scratch during Q&A.

Give me a prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by a combination of likelihood and damage, each phrased the way this specific audience would put it, each with a reply that would take about two minutes to deliver aloud and a note on where that reply is softest. Close with the question I am hoping nobody asks, and a brief note on how I might pre-empt it in the talk itself.
