Here's a counterfactual. Suppose that from tomorrow on, worldwide, anyone about to enter a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with a stranger can switch on a cheap, perfectly reliable lock: their move is now fixed at cooperate and can't be changed. The other person can see whether you've locked yourself before they choose, and they can lock themselves too or stay free. The payoffs of the underlying game are untouched — theres still no reputation, no repeat encounters, no third-party punishment; locking in only removes your own defection option. Predict what happens over the next fifty years to cooperation rates, to who uses the lock, and to how much people trust strangers who refuse to use it. Show the reasoning in stages: first work out whether locking in is a dominated strategy in a single, well-mixed population of self-interested players; then say which real-world features — repeat encounters elsewhere, assortative matching, gossip, or the lock working as a costly signal — would have to be present for it to catch on. Flag which predictions you'd be confident in and which are guesses, and what would prove you wrong.
