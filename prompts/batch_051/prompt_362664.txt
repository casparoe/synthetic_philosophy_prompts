I'm a PhD student in developmental psychology, and I ended up as the de facto referee after our lab meeting devolved into an argument I couldn't settle. A postdoc had presented a twin study (large sample, standard ACE model) that estimated the heritability of reading comprehension at .62 in the population they looked at. The fight wasn't really about the number, it was about what the number licenses. Elena said the number means our department's summer reading intervention for struggling middle schoolers is basically a bad bet and we should move the money elsewhere. Marcus disagreed, and they went back and forth for a while. I wrote down their moves as faithfully as I could, because I owe the lab a summary:

(1) Elena: .62 heritable means that, within that population, just under two-thirds of the individual differences in reading comprehension are genetic. So the rank order of who reads well is mostly fixed by genotype, and a summer program can only fiddle with the environmental leftover; it won't shift the rankings much, so funding it is a waste.

(2) Marcus: That's the old heritability-equals-immutability error. Height is highly heritable within cohorts—something like .8—yet average height has risen a lot across cohorts with better nutrition. High heritability doesn't show an environmental intervention can't move the mean.

(3) Elena: Average height across cohorts is the wrong analogy. The program isn't trying to shift a population mean over decades; it's trying to re-rank struggling kids next summer. Heritability is a within-population variance partition, so here's the one place it actually speaks. If the current spread of reading scores is mostly genetic, tutoring won't much change who is at the bottom.

(4) Marcus: Even on that retarget, it doesn't follow. The .62 was estimated under the existing range of environments in that sample, and a summer program is a new environment, not one baked into the estimate. You can have a trait stay highly heritable within both fertilized and unfertilized plots while fertilizer shifts the mean; if the response to the new environment interacts with genotype, ranks can change too, and h² is silent on either.

(5) Elena: But the "new environment" point only works if the intervention is genuinely outside the environmental range the sample already contains. These kids' homes already run from no books in the house to books everywhere and parents reading daily. If that existing environmental spread mattered much for rank differences, it would show up in the shared or nonshared environment components. By and large it doesn't—that's what .62 is saying. A summer reading camp is just repackaging a within-range environment, so its rank-shifting effect is already counted in the decomposition, and it came out small.

(6) Marcus: The E component is not an upper bound on what any particular environmental manipulation will do—including one inside the current range—because heritability partitions variance, not causal effects. Also, .62 from a twin model can absorb gene-environment correlation: a kid with a reading-related genotype may evoke more book talk from adults or seek out text-heavy niches, and the model can credit that correlated environment to genes. And if there's gene-by-environment interaction—different genotypes responding differently to tutoring—the estimate is silent on it.

Then somebody had to go teach and the meeting dissolved, and now I'm the one who has to summarize who's right about what. I'd rather not fake confidence.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the six moves, rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and explain why, bringing in the distinctions that seem to do the work: individual vs. population, mean vs. variance, existing vs. novel environments, gene-environment correlation and interaction, and what exactly the E component does and doesn't bound. Then say who is more right overall and what both of them are missing.

Just the JSON.
