I've been rereading William James's The Will to Believe, and there's a bit where he draws a distinction between two commandments for would-be knowers: to know the truth, and to avoid error. He insists they aren't two ways of stating the same commandment, but two separable laws. That's got me bothered, because ordinary intellectual life seems to treat the second law as much heavier than the first. If I say I've no idea what the capital of Burkina Faso is, nobody blames me for failing to know. But if I confidently say it's Accra, I'm an idiot. Both are failures of inquiry: one is a failure to acquire truth, the other a failure to avoid error. Yet only one gets punished.

Does accuracy-first epistemology have a principled way of justifying that asymmetry? In epistemic utility theory, we score credences for accuracy; avoiding error and seeking truth are supposedly captured by the one scoring rule. Can they be pulled apart as genuinely distinct desiderata with different weights, or is the ordinary asymmetry just about the social risks of asserting falsehoods? I want the strongest defence you can give for treating avoid-error as more important than seek-truth, and an honest verdict on whether James's two-separable-laws move survives inside a proper scoring rule.
