I have been trying to write a short discussion paper on whether the economic organisation of AI development is the fundamental obstacle to making it safe, and a colleague recently handed me an argument that I now find simultaneously compelling and overreaching. Our shared starting point is that no individual company sets out to be reckless; the question is whether the logic of the system pushes even well-intentioned actors toward unsafe outcomes. In numbered form, the argument runs as follows. (1) Transformative AI is being developed chiefly by private firms that must compete for investment, revenue, and market position. (2) All else equal, a firm that voluntarily absorbs substantial safety costs — longer evaluations, slower deployment, forgoing profitable applications, submitting to outside scrutiny — will deploy later and at higher cost than a rival that skips those precautions. (3) Under ordinary market selection, the more cautious firm is therefore systematically penalised: it loses customers, capital, or relevance, while the less cautious firm is rewarded. (4) If transformative AI is to be developed safely, the leading developers must exercise coordinated restraint across the industry. (5) Competitive markets systematically undermine that kind of restraint, because any firm that restrains itself unilaterally puts itself at a disadvantage, and joint restraint is unstable whenever defection is profitable. (6) So in an economy where AI development is organised along capitalist lines, the expected result is not merely a risk of unsafe development but a systematic tendency toward it. (7) Therefore capitalism itself — understood as the organisation of production around private, competitive, profit-seeking firms — must be regarded as the root cause of existential AI risk, and only a decisive break from competitive market incentives can adequately address it.

The difficulty is that I cannot tell whether this argument is too strong in a way that damages it, or simply too strong for my comfort. It appears to establish more than I think my colleague wants to claim. If the mechanism in (2), (3), and (5) is sound, then the same dynamic ought to afflict any collection of rivalrous actors pursuing a powerful technology — competing states in an arms race, separate military laboratories under a centrally planned economy, even hostile academic groups racing for priority — none of which is capitalism in the ordinary sense. So the argument seems to show that the real culprit is rivalry or decentralised competition under conditions where no one can bind everyone else, not private capital as such. Yet I can also imagine the reply that capitalism is precisely the institutional form in which such rivalry has been made the motor of technological change, which may be all that is needed to justify calling it the root cause.

I would most like to keep premise (4) and conclusion (7), but I suspect that keeping both costs more than I have so far admitted to myself. Which step, if any, should I reject, and what exactly do I give up by rejecting it? If you think the argument is valid as it stands, then say what its conclusion amounts to once its true scope is made explicit, and whether my worry that it proves too much is well founded. Please answer in British English.
