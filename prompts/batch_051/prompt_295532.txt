I'm a software engineer and I keep going in circles on something, so I want a straight answer from someone who does this kind of thinking. A while back I read about Nomic, the game where the rules explicitly let players change the rules as a move, and since then I've been modeling rule systems as code that can rewrite its own source while running. I came up with a concept I call the Boot Gap.

Here's the idea. Any rule system that can amend itself needs an initial rule that says how amendments work. That root rule either applies to itself, or it doesnt. If it applies to itself, then the system can lawfully amend the amendment rule into something that forbids amending it. At the moment you make the change you're acting legally, and the moment after you're in violation of the new rules: same actor, same system, just evaluated one commit apart. If instead the root rule doesnt apply to itself, then there's a hardcoded rule whose authority can't be derived from the system its part of — like a repo where the first commit was made with commands that aren't in the repo. Either way there's a gap between the rules and whatever gave them authority, and I claim every self-amending system has this gap. Adding more rules just moves the gap up a level, like adding a bootloader before the bootloader.

The three ways out I can see: hardcode the root rule and accept that the foundation is just posited; accept an infinite chain of amendment rules, which no finite rulebook can actually contain; or let the amendment rule apply to itself and eat the weird transitions. I think the third is the most honest, but it makes 'legal' look like a time-indexed property, and I'm not sure if that dissolves the paradox or just hides it.

So: is the Boot Gap a real thing, or am I confusing law with version control? Did someone already map this out under another name? And if you had to defend one of the three options, which is least broken and why — or is there a fourth option I'm missing?
