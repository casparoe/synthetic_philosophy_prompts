I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on bounded rationality and decision theory, and I am writing the final exam. Please draft one original essay question on satisficing that cannot be answered by reciting the textbook contrast between maximising and choosing a "good enough" option. The question should force students to work through the following pressure point: once search and deliberation costs are modelled explicitly, it looks as though the satisficer can be redescribed as an expected-value maximiser who chooses an optimal stopping rule, so either satisficing is optimisation in disguise or the claim that satisficing is a distinct account of rational choice needs an account of what resists the redescription. A strong question will make students say what a satisficing rule is a rule for choosing among, what information it uses, and what role the aspiration level plays; it will also make them assess adaptive aspiration levels and the step at which an agent revises its threshold. The question should be answerable in 90 minutes and should not require citations or prior reading of particular authors.

Please provide:
- the exam question itself;
- a model answer of roughly 800 words;
- a marking scheme worth 25 marks that distinguishes an excellent answer from a competent one by naming specific moves, such as distinguishing a stopping rule from a valuation rule, explaining what it would take for the optimisation redescription to succeed, giving a non-circular account of how an aspiration level is set or revised, and identifying what adaptive aspiration levels change and what they leave untouched.
