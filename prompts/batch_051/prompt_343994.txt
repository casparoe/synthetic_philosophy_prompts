Before I read any more theories about when statistical evidence is enough for legal proof, I want the specification first. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of naked statistical evidence must meet. I am not asking for a survey of authors or doctrines; I am asking for the criteria by which proposals should be judged.

The purpose I have in mind is a normative account of legal factfinding: a theory of when a court should be permitted to find a party liable, or a defendant guilty, on evidence that only places them in a class, and why. The theory must cover both the preponderance standard and the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard, or explain why the two standards should treat statistical evidence differently.

Anchor cases it must deliver a considered verdict on, with reasons:

- The blue bus case: the plaintiff is hit by a bus and shows only that the defendant company operates four-fifths of the buses on that route, with no witness, mark, or other trace linking this particular bus or driver to the accident.
- The gatecrasher case: a thousand people attend an event and only 499 tickets are accounted for; a spectator is selected at random to be sued, so the probability that anyone selected that way did not pay is slightly above one half.
- The criminal analogue: nearly all members of a well-defined group committed a crime, but no evidence distinguishes one member of the group from another.

Then say what else a theory must handle. I expect the list to include, with the case or consideration behind each:

- why bare class membership strikes many people as inadequate even when the stated probability passes the applicable standard, and whether that intuition should be endorsed or explained away rather than merely deferred to;
- why the same system does accept probability-laden evidence in various forms, such as DNA match statistics accompanied by other evidence, epidemiological evidence, and schemes that allocate liability by market share when the harm cannot be traced to a particular producer, so the theory cannot amount to the slogan that statistical evidence is never enough;
- the reference-class problem: any item of evidence can be described as placing a person in some class with some frequency, so the theory must say what distinguishes admissible probabilistic proof from naked statistical evidence without leaning on an unarticulated distinction between individualizing and statistical evidence;
- the relation between the proof standard and the kind of evidence: whether a probability is ever sufficient by itself for preponderance, and whether the criminal standard imposes a different rule of evidence rather than merely a higher threshold;
- any distinction the theory must not collapse, such as the difference between the probability that the defendant is the one who did it and the warrant for entering judgment against this particular defendant.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a private preference. Flag any pair of requirements that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most work by excluding the greatest number of otherwise attractive positions, such as pure expected-accuracy views, views that reduce the issue to incentives, and views that require the evidence to causally trace to the act. Keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on how familiar candidate views fare.

Finally, tell me which one requirement on your list you would be most willing to give up, and what argument or counterexample would persuade you to demote or drop it. This should also tell me where, in the overall list, your reasoning is most likely to fail.
