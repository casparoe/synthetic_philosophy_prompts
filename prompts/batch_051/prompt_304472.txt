Two people in my cohort — one's in clinical psych, the other's in a philosophy program — got into it at lunch yesterday about a patient with Capgras delusion, the one where a man insists his wife has been replaced by an identical impostor. I missed the beggining but I wrote the moves down afterwards because it was actually a decent argument.

(1) The psych student said: he can't really believe his wife is an impostor, because he doesn't act like it. He eats the food she cooks, sleeps in the same bed, doesn't call the police or change the locks. If he believed the woman in his kitchen was a stranger faking it, we'd see the ordinary actions that go with that. The philosophy student shot back: that's too strict. You believe you'll die someday, but you still floss and save for retirement; plenty of real beliefs sit behaviorally dormant until the right cue comes. And anyway the guy does sometimes flinch away from her or ask her to prove who she is.

(2) The psych student then brought in the two-factor theory: the first factor is the strange experience — the guy looks at his wife's face and the usual automatic feeling of familiarity doesn't fire, so the thought "this woman can't be my wife" suggests itself; the second factor is a broken belief-evaluation system, which is why the thought doesn't get rejected like a passing weird idea. And, the psych student said, notice the second factor cuts my way: the evaluation system is what would promote or reject candidate thoughts, and if it's broken, the thought never gets promoted to full belief — it stays as a kind of compulsive avowal the patient makes without it taking hold of his mind. The philosophy student said that's backwards: the filter's job is to keep bad candidates out, not to induct them in. If the filter fails, the thought is in — that's what a failure of rejection means. The delusion is a belief that got admitted without vetting.

(3) The psych student came back: but then why is the belief so circumscribed? He "believes" the woman is an impostor from ten to noon and then lets the same woman handle his medication; real beliefs integrate with the rest of what you accept, this one doesn't. The philosophy student said integration is a spectrum, not a switch: plenty of our beliefs are fragmentary or compartmentalized, and the distress plus the insistent assertion is enough to count as belief even if the guy's behavior is inconsistent.

(4) The psych student's last move was: fine, but if your standards for "belief" are that low, then a nightmare is a belief while it's running, and so is the thought "my wife is an impostor" that I can get myself half-scared by imagining — both cause distress and flinching, but neither is asserted in the cold light of day. The philosophy student said no, the difference is the patient asserts it in the cold light of day, over and over, and treats it as his settled view even if his actions don't all follow.

They had to leave for seminar. I'd like someone to adjudicate this move by move: at each step, who's right and who's stretching. I'm especially curious whether the two-factor theory actually settles the "is it a belief?" question one way or the other — the psych student treated factor two as if it demotes the thought, the philosophy student as if it lets a belief in unvetted. Which reading is closer to the theory, and does the theory even say anything about belief status? And what are both of them missing?
