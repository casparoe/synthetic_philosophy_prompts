I've been reporting on attempts to infer people's values from their behavior — the shared logic of inverse reinforcement learning and revealed-preference economics — and I've come to find the core position compelling: given enough choice data and a good enough model of noise, incentives, and situational constraints, behavior is in principle our best, perhaps our only non-question-begging, evidence of what a person really values. I want to know what would count against that view. Lay out its exposure: which empirical findings, formal results, and philosophical arguments — reward ambiguity, akrasia, adaptive preferences, context-dependent choice, or anything else — should most move a committed defender; which of these a defender should concede as genuine costs rather than dismiss; which could be reasonably set aside as tractable noise; and whether the view has been protected by qualifications such as discounting, framing effects, and noise terms to the point where no observable pattern of behavior could refute it, and if so whether that insulation is a problem. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among the economists, philosophers, and machine-learning researchers who work on this, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
