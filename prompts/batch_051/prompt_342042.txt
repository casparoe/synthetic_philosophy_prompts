I am a PhD student in the philosophy of language. In our conditionals reading group, the line 'the probability of a conditional is the conditional probability' kept doing three different jobs: one person read it as a constraint on rational credence, another as a semantic claim about what an indicative conditional says, and a third as merely a claim about how acceptable it is to assert the conditional. The discussion stalled when someone cited Lewis's triviality theorems: if conditionals express ordinary propositions whose probabilities obey the standard axioms, and the equation holds for the probability functions obtained by conditionalizing on the consequent and on its negation, then P(C|A) = P(C) for all A and C with P(A) > 0, which is absurd. But it was never clear which of the three readings is actually committed to the presuppositions that proof exploits, nor which readings survive its failure. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses hiding in that sentence, say which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, and which are outright untenable, and identify which reading the triviality proof genuinely refutes and which it leaves standing?
