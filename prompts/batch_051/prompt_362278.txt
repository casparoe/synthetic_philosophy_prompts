I keep a small group of rescued animals—some hens from a commercial egg operation, two old wool-breed sheep, and a goat from a place that shut down—and over the years I have noticed that I have strong but only half-articulated judgments about which uses of them are acceptable. The hens still lay most days; if I leave the eggs they break or rot, so I collect them, eat some, sell the rest to pay for feed and medicine, and crush the shells back into their feed. I think that is clearly fine. The sheep do not shed well on their own; they overheat and get skin problems if they are not shorn, so I shear them and sell the wool to cover their keep. That also feels fine. The goat once had a kid naturally, and while the kid was nursing I would not have touched her milk; after the kid was weaned and she was still producing, I began taking the surplus for cheese, and that felt fine too. I also spread the animals' manure on the vegetable garden without any unease. But when a fox killed one of the hens and left most of the body, I buried it; eating her would have felt wrong even though I eat chicken, and even though leaving the body seemed like pure waste. When one of the sheep died of old age, I paid to have the body cremated rather than use the hide or the meat, and I still cannot say why that felt different from using her wool. A neighbor says I am drawing a line around my own sentimentality: the egg and the wool are just parts of the animal, and using the hide of an animal who died naturally harms her no more than taking eggs from a living hen.

The closest I have come to a principle is something like this: one may use a byproduct of a rescued animal when it would otherwise be wasted, taking it causes no additional suffering, and doing so does not create demand for more animals to be bred or kept. That principle covers the eggs, the wool, the post-weaning milk, and the manure, but it also seems to cover the fox-killed hen and the old sheep's hide, since the animals are already dead, using those things harms no one, and leaving them is waste. I reject that result, so the principle must be missing something. I would most like to keep my verdicts on the eggs, the wool, and the fox-killed hen, and I am less certain about the sheep's body. What principle best captures these judgments, or shows me which of them I should give up? Please state it precisely, show how it handles each case, and apply it to a few cases I have not faced—for example, honey from a rescue hive, feathers a hen molts, and milk from a cow who was bred repeatedly before I took her in and is still producing.
