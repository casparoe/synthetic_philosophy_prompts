Two philosophers have spent a year in an adversarial collaboration on the question of whether an advance directive can legitimately bind a later self who now protests. One of them, the present-sovereignty theorist, holds that the competent later self's current wishes have final authority over any earlier instruction. The other, the whole-life agency theorist, holds that a properly formed advance directive can bind the later self's protest, because the earlier self exercised authority over a temporally extended life rather than merely over a momentary slice of it. They have used Book 12 of the Odyssey as a shared test case. Here is the passage they keep returning to, from the A.T. Murray translation:

First she bade us avoid the voice of the wondrous Sirens, and their flowery meadow. Me alone she bade to listen to their voice; but do ye bind me with grievous bonds, that I may abide fast where I am, upright in the step of the mast, and let the ropes be made fast at the ends to the mast itself; and if I implore and bid you to loose me, then do ye tie me fast with yet more bonds.

Draft the joint document they would sign after that year. It should include: (1) a numbered agreed-claims section, stating which features of the Odysseus case are uncontroversial and which general principles both camps accept; (2) a disagreements table in which each row separates a genuine disagreement from a merely verbal one, with the present-sovereignty view and the whole-life agency view each stated in wording the other side would accept as accurate; (3) the cruxes, ordered from the one that would most move at least one side to the one that would least, with each crux formulated as a sub-question such that settling it would require at least one side to revise its view; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about what that case would show recorded in advance. Do not adjudicate between the two positions.
