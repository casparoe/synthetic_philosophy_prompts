Suppose a specific technological development arrives in the middle of the debate over epistocracy. A free AI policy advisor becomes available on every phone and is genuinely good: it can explain the likely consequences of a proposed law, answer a layperson's follow-up questions in ordinary language, flag the biases it is prone to, and summarize technical evidence from both sides of a dispute. After an hour of back-and-forth, a previously low-information voter can deliberate at roughly the level of a competent graduate student in public policy, and the measured gap between the most and least sophisticated voters nearly disappears when both are allowed to use the tool.

Now consider Jason Brennan's influential case for epistocracy. Its central factual premise is that political knowledge and judgment are very unevenly distributed, and that the errors of uninformed or misinformed voters do not cancel out, so restricting or weighting the franchise toward those who clear a competence threshold would produce systematically better decisions. Does Brennan's argument still go through once this premise is no longer true? Or does the development turn the epistemic argument against universal suffrage into an argument for universal suffrage, since competence is now cheap and evenly distributed? Or does it merely move the problem: the scarce competence becomes knowing when to trust, challenge, and supervise the advisor, and the serious epistemic threat becomes whoever controls the advisor's training data, fine-tuning, and default framing? I am interested in what happens to the argument itself, not in any particular policy. Say which premise the development bears on, whether the argument goes through, needs repair, or is dead, and whether the repaired form can still be called an argument for epistocracy.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Write in American English.
