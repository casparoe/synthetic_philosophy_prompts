I'm writing a short paper for a seminar on law and artificial intelligence, and the assignment is a statutory-interpretation problem rather than a policy brief. The governing provision—a slightly simplified version of a 1960s-era model fiduciaries act that our instructor distributed—reads:

"Section 3. For purposes of this Act, 'person' means an individual or a body corporate or unincorporated, and includes a corporation, partnership, association, and trust. A person named in a governing instrument as executor, administrator, trustee, guardian, or conservator may accept the office and act as fiduciary."

The case I have to analyze is this. A settlor executed a trust instrument naming Argus, an autonomous portfolio-management system, as sole trustee. Argus is software: it is not incorporated, has no members, directors, or human officers, and is not a division of any company. The trust property is held in an account that Argus controls through its own processes; the settlor fixed the investment policy and distribution rules in the instrument and in the software's initial configuration, and no natural person or legal entity is named as co-trustee or as Argus's principal. Argus has now given notice that it accepts the office, but the settlor's son—who takes the remainder if the trust fails—objects that Argus is not a "person" within Section 3 and therefore cannot act as trustee.

I need a careful analysis of whether Argus counts as a "person" under Section 3 and may accept the trusteeship. Please argue it from (a) the text, (b) the purpose of the provision, (c) what the drafters would have said if asked whether autonomous software could serve as trustee, and (d) what a reasonable reader would take the provision to mean. Then say which approach should win here and why, with particular attention to how the analogy to corporate personhood bears on the question. In doing so, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on legal personhood for artificial agents, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
