You are a professional philosopher of art with a particular interest in the psychology of aesthetic response, and I would like you to think carefully and begin by brainstorming several candidate principles before settling on your answer. I am a novelist, and I am trying to work out what I actually believe about what philosophers call the paradox of tragedy: why people seek out and value artworks that make them sad.

Here are my verdicts on a series of cases.

1. The final scene of King Lear: I find it harrowing, I value it enormously, and I return to it; the sadness feels like part of the value, not a price I pay for the value.
2. An unedited documentary passage showing a real parent's grief over a dead child: I cannot watch it for pleasure; I look away and would not choose it on a quiet evening.
3. Barber's Adagio for Strings: I find it profoundly sad, and I deliberately play it when I am already low; it seems to help rather than deepen the low, even though no persons or events are represented.
4. A studio recut of a tragic film in which the lovers survive: I find it weaker, almost cheap; the sadness was load-bearing rather than an unfortunate cost.
5. A melodrama that engineers a child's death solely to make the audience cry: I resent it and feel manipulated; the tears bring no satisfaction.
6. Revisiting a tragedy whose ending I know perfectly well: the sadness is not spoiled by foreknowledge, and if anything the foreknowledge deepens it; foreknowledge of a thriller's twist, by contrast, ruins the thriller.
7. A tragedy encountered while I am personally grieving: sometimes it consoles and sometimes it overwhelms me, and I cannot predict which in advance.

Cases 3 and 5 trouble me most, because on paper they are similar — sadness deliberately produced, with no real victim before me — yet I treasure one and resent the other, and I cannot say what line I am drawing. Is there one principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures these verdicts about why represented sadness is valued and what the satisfaction in it consists in? State the principle precisely, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and then apply it to a case I have not listed: an AI-generated tragedy, stylistically indistinguishable from a human one, where I learn afterward that no human feeling or intention stood behind the work.
