I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I have agreed to lead a forty-five-minute seminar for policy reporters who are comfortable with quantitative argument but have no background in formal logic. The topic is Russell’s paradox and the limits of naive set theory. I want the session to be built around a specific passage from Bertrand Russell’s *Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy* (1919). In the passage I have in mind, Russell asks his reader to consider the class of all classes that are not members of themselves, and then shows that this class belongs to itself exactly when it does not. I do not want the barber analogy to serve as the carrying example. Do not explain the paradox to me; plan how I should explain it. What conceptual distinctions must be in place before I present the class of all classes that are not members of themselves, and in what order should I introduce them? What one concrete example should carry the session through to the contradiction, and what features must it have if it is not to obscure the logic? What misconceptions should I expect from this audience — for instance, that the problem is a trick of self-reference, that banning self-membership removes the difficulty, that the paradox shows there are no sets, or that it is only a puzzle about words — and where in the sequence should I defuse each? What should I openly refuse to explain at this level? Finally, what check for understanding would tell me that someone has grasped the paradox as a structural limit on naive comprehension, rather than merely been surprised by the contradiction?
