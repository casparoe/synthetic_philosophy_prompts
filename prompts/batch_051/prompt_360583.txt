I've been defending something I've started calling safe Pareto-improvementism in a reading group, and I want to stress-test it before I write it up. The view, roughly: for any policy that changes who gets what across a group, the only changes we can justify without a whole lot of downstream moral argument are safe Pareto improvements — changes that leave nobody worse off than they are right now on any dimension they actually care about, and that don't create new downside risks for anyone, including the people who benefit. I also hold the stronger corollary that when a safe Pareto improvement is available, declining to make it is itself a moral mistake, even if some non-Pareto change in the background would be better overall.

What would count against this view? I don't want the standard egalitarian line that Pareto improvements can lock in unfair distributions unless you can make it bite against my version specifically, safety clause included. I want the defeaters ranked by how bad they'd be — fatal, serious but survivable, merely awkward — and I want an honest account of which ones a defender of the view should grant and which ones they can reasonably wave off. And tell me whether the position as I've stated it is insulated enough that nothing could in principal refute it, and if so whether that's a real problem or just the normal condition for this kind of normative principle.
