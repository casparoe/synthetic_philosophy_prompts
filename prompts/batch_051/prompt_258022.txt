Been rereading William James’s Great Men and Their Environment — the one where he pushes back against the Spencerian move that treats individual variations as almost irrelevant because the environment supposedly generates them anyway. The bit I keep coming back to says, roughly, that individuals matter because they supply new variations, and the social environment then selects among them; it doesn’t produce the variations itself. That made me wonder about a journal policy, maybe for a journal of comparative or historical social science. Suppose every submission that argues a large event depended heavily on one particular person — say, no Hitler means no Nazi seizure of power — has to include a substitution test: the author must name at least two actual people who occupied similar structural positions at the time and explain concretely why neither would plausibly have done the parallel causal work. If the author can’t name two live candidates, the paper still gets published but gets a visible red tag saying unfilled substitution test, plus an invited reply slot for a structuralist critic. Analyze this as a mechanism, not as a moral or political argument. Exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion. Cover: what kinds of historical arguments this selects for; how authors will game the two-candidate rule; what happens to the named non-candidates; how reviewers and editors respond to the red tag; and whether any fix keeps the test from becoming either a pointless hoop or a cudgel.
