I keep coming back to the sea-battle argument, and the longer I sit with it the more it bothers me that the debate has barely moved in over two thousand years. The area where everyone agrees is suspiciously narrow and the area where they disagree is suspiciously compressed. We all agree that 'tomorrow there will be a sea battle or there won't be' is true now, and we all agree the past is fixed in a way the future isn't. The whole fight is about whether one of those two disjuncts is already true. Aristotle said no: if it were already true that there will be a sea battle, contingency would already be gone. The Ockhamist wants to keep bivalence by insisting there is an actual future for the sentence to be true about, even if that future isn't causally fixed yet. The Peircean instead reads 'there will be' as something like 'in every possible continuation,' so at a genuine branch point the sentence can be neither true nor false. And the formal machinery — branching-time models, Prior's tense logic — seems to let every side have a consistent system, so consistency alone won't settle anything.

My reading group has been stuck on exactly this for months, and I've begun to suspect we aren't actually having one argument. One of us keeps doing logic (can you hold on to bivalence without modality collapsing into necessity?), another is doing metaphysics (is the future genuinely open, or just unpredictable?), and a third is doing semantics of the English word 'will.' But maybe the historical debate was never one disagreement either.
