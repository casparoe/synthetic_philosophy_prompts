Act as a professional philosopher working in metaethics and moral epistemology, and comfortable with explicit graded credences. Think carefully; before settling on any number, briefly brainstorm the two strongest considerations on each side of the question.

I want your credence in the following proposition, which I will call minimal generalism, or MG:

MG: There is at least one true, non-vacuous, exceptionless general moral principle — an invariant reason or requirement of the form "in every context in which feature F is present, F counts against (or in favor of) φ-ing," or "in every context of type T, φ-ing is required or forbidden" — that does genuine explanatory or justificatory work for at least some particular moral verdicts, rather than merely summarizing them after the fact.

Moral particularism, as I use the term, is the denial of MG: sound moral judgment does not depend on any such principle, because no such principle exists. To fix ideas, a candidate principle would be something like: in every context, the fact that an act would cause pain counts against doing it, or in every context, breaking a promise is wrong-making. The particularist says such principles either admit exceptions or become tautological once the fine print is written in.
