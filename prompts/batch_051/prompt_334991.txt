I'm moderating the Q&A for a philosophy of science colloquium whose speaker is the author of a new book defending a version of James Woodward's interventionist account of causation. Her view, in the form she gives it in the book and will use on stage, is this. Causal claims are claims about what would happen to a target variable if an idealized intervention were performed on a putative cause, where an intervention is itself characterized as a change that is exogenous, unconfounded, and capable of influencing the target only through that cause. Causal generalizations, on her account, are exactly those that remain invariant across a suitable range of such interventions. When the circularity objection arises — the conditions on an intervention are themselves formulated in causal terms — she replies that her project is not a reductive analysis of causal notions but a clarification and systematization of causal reasoning, so the circularity is benign.

The room will be mostly philosophers of science, with a substantial minority of applied researchers who use causal-inference methods, and the point of the session is to stress-test the book rather than to summarize it. Write me the questions for a forty-five-minute moderated Q&A. The questions should develop the circularity worry across three levels: first, the characterization of an intervention itself; second, the claim that invariance can distinguish genuinely causal generalizations from merely accidental regularities without prior causal knowledge; third, the account's handling of token causation, where no intervention has been or will be performed. For each level, give the main question and the follow-up to ask once the speaker reaches for her non-reductive reply, so that she has to say what explanatory and methodological work the theory is actually doing rather than what it declines to do. Do not include generic questions about her biography or the writing process. End with a question that asks what her theory licenses a scientist to judge in a new case that she could not already judge without it, given that the characterization of an intervention already deploys causal concepts.
