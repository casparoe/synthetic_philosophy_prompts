You are a professional philosopher specializing in moral theory and the philosophy of consent. I am going to report a disagreement between two of my friends, Elena and Marcus, which I overheard but could never settle myself. Please read it carefully and charitably. Before giving any verdict, briefly brainstorm the candidate distinctions that might be doing the work here (for example: the act itself versus its background; lies about risk versus lies about identity; what the speaker invites the other to rely on versus what they merely fail to correct; the opacity of inner states). Then give a point-by-point adjudication. Treat this as a question of moral philosophy, not positive law.

The case they were arguing about is this. Daniel met Alex at a conference and told Alex, falsely, that he was single. Daniel is in fact married. Alex slept with him, and later said Alex would never have slept with him had Alex known he was married.

Here is the exchange as I remember it.

Elena said Daniel did not have valid consent. He procured sex on terms Alex had not agreed to: the person agreeing to sex with a single man is not agreeing to become an affair partner, to complicity in infidelity, to secrecy. Marcus said that overmoralizes. Consent, he said, is to the sex itself — the body, the encounter, the time. Marital status is background. The sex was freely chosen and wanted. The lie is a separate wrong, but it does not turn consensual sex into nonconsensual sex.

Elena then asked him to consider a nearby case. Suppose Daniel had falsely said he had had a vasectomy. Same structure — a lie about a fact that would have changed Alex's decision — but now the lie reaches the physical risk of the act. Marcus granted the point, but said it only showed that lies which alter the risk profile of the encounter cut deeper: Alex cannot consent to a risk profile he has deliberately misrepresented. That is the act itself, not background. Marital status changes nothing about what physically happened.

Elena replied that the distinction between "what physically happened" and "background" already assumes that the body exhausts the act. But people do not consent to bare physical motions; they consent to acts under descriptions. For many people, sex with a married person is a different act from sex with a single person, because it makes them an affair partner and subjects them to a shape of secrecy and cost they never chose. If Alex would not consent under the true description, how is consent under the false one valid? Marcus said this proves too much. People almost never consent after being told everything, and all sorts of false beliefs — about age, height, income, past partners, even a false star sign — can change a person's decision. If every causal falsehood that mattered to the other party invalidates consent, then nobody could ever know whether their partner's consent was valid, and the ordinary minor deceptions of early dating would all count as sexual violation. That cannot be right.

Elena conceded the over-inclusion worry but denied that it killed her point. The test, she said, is not whether some belief happened to matter, but whether the falsehood changed the description under which consent was reasonably sought and given. Daniel did not merely fail to correct a guess; he actively held out a picture of himself as single, knowing Alex would refuse an affair, and invited Alex to rely on that picture for the purpose of Alex's decision. Consent attaches to a description of the encounter, not to a bare event. Marcus answered that "description" is doing all the work: almost anything can be re-described as part of the act. Unless Elena can say which descriptions are consent-relevant, she is either smuggling in a background moral theory of sex or her view goes silent on the very cases, like the vasectomy, that she thought were clear.

Then Marcus pressed the hardest case. What about someone who says "I see a future with you," or "I love you," falsely, and secures sex that way? Does that vitiate consent? Elena hesitated, then said: in principle it can, if the other person was consenting to sex-with-someone-who-loves-me; but in practice, people should treat professions of feeling as something opaque, unstable, and unwise to rely on. Marcus said: there it is — the line between what we are reasonably invited to rely on and what we should treat as opaque is doing the work, and that line is exactly what you have not specified. Elena replied that no theory of consent avoids drawing some such line; his own risk-profile line is just as normative, and it is worse at explaining why Alex was wronged. Neither would grant the other the gap between cases.

I would like an impartial adjudication. Please:

(1) Go through the exchange in order and say who is right about which specific claims and who is overclaiming.

(2) Identify the distinctions that are actually bearing weight, and assess whether each can bear the weight the parties place on it.

(3) Say what, if anything, both parties are missing that would break the impasse.

(4) Give an overall verdict: who is more right, and why, with the strongest versions of both positions on the table. Do not split the difference where one side is wrong; say so explicitly.
