An institute that funds foundational work at the intersection of computation and the natural sciences is planning a five-year program on the ideas of Stephen Wolfram's A New Kind of Science. The program's premise is that the principle of computational equivalence is not a single thesis but a bundle of independent claims that are usually run together, and that their scientific value differs much more than is typically acknowledged. You are part of the advisory group asked to turn this premise into a research agenda.

First, separate the bundle into its main component claims — for example, a claim about how readily simple rules achieve universal computation, a claim about the absence of stable intermediate levels of computational sophistication in natural systems, a claim that universal computation marks a ceiling on behavioral sophistication, and any further components you can articulate. Second, rank these component claims by how much scientific significance each would have if it were made precise and established. Third, for the claim you rank first, describe what a three-to-five-year investigation could realistically accomplish: what evidence would raise confidence in it, what would count against it, and what would reveal it to be a purely mathematical observation with no empirical bite.

The institute is not interested in exegesis of Wolfram's text; it wants to know which version of the principle is worth building a scientific program around and why. Defend your ordering against the objection that you are simply favoring the claim that is easiest to test.
